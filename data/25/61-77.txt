=61=

This verse draws attention to the signs in the sky: "Blessed is the One Who has placed constellations in the sky, as well as a ˹radiant˺ lamp and a luminous moon." The verse again opens with the declaration of blessing, praising Allah who placed constellations — the great star patterns — in the sky, and who set within it a radiant lamp, the sun, and a luminous moon. These celestial bodies, arranged in perfect order and beauty, are among the most visible and universal of Allah's signs, seen by all of humanity across all ages.

The verse distinguishes between the sun, described as a "radiant lamp" (sirāj), and the moon, described as "luminous" (munīr). The sun is a source of light, radiating its own brilliance, while the moon reflects light, shining with a borrowed glow. This precise distinction, revealed in the Qur'an fourteen centuries ago, is itself a sign, for it describes with accuracy a truth about the sun and moon that was not commonly understood at the time. The sun and moon, in their complementary roles, provide the light that sustains life and the rhythm that marks time.

The verse teaches the believer to reflect upon the signs of the heavens and to recognise in them the power and wisdom of the Creator. The constellations, the sun, and the moon are not random features of the universe but deliberate creations, arranged with precision and purpose. The believer is encouraged to look up at the night sky with a heart full of wonder and gratitude, seeing in its beauty the hand of their Lord. The verse also serves as a refutation of those who worshipped the sun, the moon, and the stars, for these celestial bodies are themselves creations, signs pointing to the One Who created them.

=62=

This verse continues the description of Allah's signs: "And He is the One Who causes the day and the night to alternate, ˹as a sign˺ for whoever desires to be mindful or to be grateful." The verse describes the alternation of day and night — the continuous succession of light and darkness, activity and rest — as a deliberate sign from Allah, designed for the benefit of those who wish to be mindful and grateful. The cycle of day and night is so constant and universal that it is often taken for granted, yet it is one of the most profound of Allah's signs.

The verse identifies two responses to this sign: mindfulness (dhikr) and gratitude (shukr). The alternation of day and night provides the opportunity for both — the day for the remembrance of Allah through work and worship, and the night for reflection, prayer, and gratitude for the blessings received. The person who reflects upon the cycle of day and night is moved to remember their Lord, who set this rhythm in motion, and to give thanks for the countless blessings that each new day brings.

The verse teaches the believer to see the ordinary as extraordinary, to recognise the signs of Allah in the everyday rhythms of life, and to respond to them with mindfulness and gratitude. The alternation of day and night is a reminder of the passing of time, of the brevity of life, and of the constant presence of Allah's mercy. The believer is encouraged to use each day and each night in the remembrance of Allah, to fill the hours with worship and gratitude, and to live with the awareness that the One Who alternates the day and night is the One to Whom all will return.

=63=

This verse introduces the description of the true servants of the Most Compassionate: "The ˹true˺ servants of the Most Compassionate are those who walk on the earth humbly, and when the foolish address them ˹improperly˺, they only respond with peace." This begins one of the most beautiful passages in the Qur'an, which describes the qualities of those who have truly devoted themselves to Allah. The first quality mentioned is humility: they walk upon the earth gently and modestly, without arrogance or ostentation, their hearts free of pride and their demeanour marked by dignity and grace.

The verse then describes their response to provocation: "when the foolish address them ˹improperly˺, they only respond with peace." The "foolish" (jāhilūn) refers to those who are ignorant and ill-mannered, who may insult, mock, or provoke the believers. The servants of the Most Compassionate do not respond in kind, do not descend to the level of their provocateurs, and do not engage in futile quarrels. Instead, they respond with "peace" (salām) — a word of kindness and restraint — or simply with a peaceful withdrawal, preserving their dignity and refusing to be drawn into conflict.

The verse teaches the believer the virtue of humility and the discipline of restraint. True servitude to Allah is reflected not only in worship but in character — in the way one walks, speaks, and responds to others. The believer is called to cultivate humility in their demeanour, to guard their tongue against harshness, and to respond to ignorance with grace and peace. This is the hallmark of those who are truly devoted to the Most Compassionate, and it is a quality that requires constant effort and self-discipline to attain.

=64=

This verse describes the second quality of the true servants of the Most Compassionate: "˹They are˺ those who spend ˹a good portion of˺ the night, prostrating themselves and standing before their Lord." While the rest of the world sleeps, these servants devote a portion of the night to prayer, alternating between prostration and standing before their Lord. The night prayer is described here as one of the defining characteristics of the believers, for it is in the stillness of the night, away from the distractions of the day, that the heart finds its deepest connection with Allah.

The verse mentions the two primary postures of prayer — prostration (sujūd) and standing (qiyām) — for these are the positions of the night prayer, in which the believer stands to recite the Qur'an and prostrates in humility before their Lord. The night is a special time for worship, when the soul is quiet, the world is still, and the believer can commune with Allah in solitude and sincerity. The willingness to sacrifice sleep for the sake of worship is a sign of true devotion, for it demonstrates that the love of Allah takes precedence over the comfort of rest.

The verse teaches the believer the virtue of the night prayer and the value of voluntary worship. The servants of the Most Compassionate are not content with the obligatory prayers alone but seek to draw closer to their Lord through additional devotion, especially in the quiet hours of the night. The believer is encouraged to cultivate this habit, to dedicate a portion of the night to standing and prostrating before Allah, and to taste the sweetness of that private communion which is reserved for those who truly love their Lord. This quality, together with humility, forms the foundation of the character described in the verses that follow.

=65=

Among the most striking qualities of the true servants of the Most Merciful is their constant prayer that Allah avert from them the punishment of Hell. This verse captures their supplication: “Our Lord, avert from us the punishment of Hell; indeed, its punishment is ever adhering.” The word translated as “ever adhering,” gharām, conveys the idea of a clinging, unrelenting debt or burden from which there is no escape. Once it takes hold, it does not release its grip. The believer therefore does not feel a false sense of security in their own good deeds. They do not rely upon their prayer, their fasting, or their charity as though these were guarantees. Instead, their hearts remain in a state of humility and fear, always turning to Allah and seeking His protection from His own just wrath.

This fear is not the fear of one who despairs; it is the fear of one who knows the weight of divine justice and who recognizes their own shortcomings. The servants of the Most Merciful are described elsewhere in the Qur'an as those who give what they give while their hearts tremble, knowing that they will return to their Lord. Their prayer for deliverance from Hell is not a sign of weak faith but of profound awareness. It reflects a heart that has truly understood the reality of the Hereafter and the seriousness of standing before Allah.

At the same time, this verse teaches that fear and hope are inseparable in the life of faith. The same servants who tremble at the thought of Hell are the ones who have placed their complete trust in Allah's mercy. They ask Him to avert the punishment precisely because they believe He alone has the power to do so, and because they have tasted the sweetness of His mercy in this world. The supplication itself is an act of worship, a recognition of absolute dependence upon the Creator.

This quality stands in sharp contrast to the arrogance of those who feel entitled to salvation. The believer never assumes safety. The believer continues to knock at the door of Allah's mercy until the very end, for they know that no one enters Paradise by their deeds alone, but only by the encompassing mercy of Allah. This verse thus encapsulates the spiritual posture of the believer: humble, watchful, hopeful, and ever turning towards the One who holds all affairs.

=66=

This verse completes the supplication that the servants of the Most Merciful make concerning Hell, declaring it to be “an evil settlement and residence.” The two terms used, mustaqarr and muqām, point to two dimensions of the abode. Mustaqarr refers to the place where one settles and comes to rest, while muqām refers to the place where one dwells and remains. Together they convey that Hell is not merely a passing moment of suffering, nor a temporary prison from which release is hoped for. It is a permanent dwelling, a fixed abode whose horror does not diminish with time and whose inhabitants find no comfort, no refuge, and no escape.

By describing Hell as an evil settlement, the verse drives home the reality that a place of residence is not judged by its size, its adornment, or its apparent comforts, but by the peace and security it provides. A palace filled with anxiety is a prison, while a modest home filled with contentment is a paradise. Hell is the worst of dwellings because it combines every form of physical torment with the spiritual torment of despair, regret, and separation from Allah's mercy. There is no companionship there except the company of the accursed, no rest except the deepening of punishment, and no hope to lighten the heart.

The believer who reflects upon this verse is moved to pray for protection, and this is exactly what the preceding verse models. The awareness that Hell is an evil settlement produces a healthy fear that restrains the soul from sin and propels it towards obedience. It also produces gratitude, for the believer realizes that every moment of comfort, safety, and peace in this world is a gift from Allah that could easily be withdrawn. The servants of the Most Merciful hold these twin realities together: they tremble at the prospect of an evil settlement, and they therefore cling to the One who can keep them from it.

This verse is also a reminder that the final home is what truly matters. A person may spend their entire life securing a beautiful dwelling in this world, yet if they neglect their eternal abode, they have secured nothing of lasting value. The wise believer invests first in the Hereafter, for that is the residence that will never end.

=67=

This verse describes the balanced character of the servants of the Most Merciful in their spending: “those who, when they spend, are neither extravagant nor miserly, but hold a just balance between the two.” The Qur'an here sets forth a complete economic ethic in a single sentence. Extravagance, isrāf, is spending beyond what is appropriate, pouring wealth into excess, luxury, and waste. Miserliness, iqtār, is withholding what should rightly be given, hoarding wealth out of greed and fear of poverty. Both are condemned, and between them lies a middle path that the verse praises as qawām, a firm and upright balance.

This balance is the mark of a heart that is neither enslaved by the love of wealth nor neglectful of its responsibilities. The extravagant person spends to impress, to indulge, and to satisfy fleeting desires, often leaving the rights of others unmet. The miser hoards out of a mistaken belief that their wealth will secure their future, forgetting that true security lies with Allah alone. The balanced believer, by contrast, recognizes that wealth is a trust from Allah, a means rather than an end, and that it must be spent in a manner that pleases its true Owner.

The teaching of this verse extends far beyond money. It is a principle that governs every resource Allah has placed in human hands: time, health, energy, talent, and influence. Each can be wasted through excess or withheld through stinginess, and each must be used with moderation and wisdom. The believer is neither a spendthrift nor a hoarder, neither a workaholic nor a slacker, neither indulgent nor neglectful.

This balanced path is what Islam calls the “middle community,” the ummah of moderation. It is a path that requires constant self-examination, for the line between generosity and extravagance, and between prudence and miserliness, is subtle and easily crossed. The servants of the Most Merciful therefore weigh their spending carefully, seeking always the pleasure of Allah and the welfare of others over the whims of the self.

=68=

This verse enumerates three grave sins from which the servants of the Most Merciful keep themselves absolutely pure: they do not call upon any deity besides Allah, they do not take a life that Allah has made sacred except with legal right, and they do not commit fornication or adultery. These three sins touch the very foundations of faith, life, and family — the three pillars upon which a sound human society rests. By singling them out, the verse highlights the severity of violating the rights of Allah, the rights of human beings, and the sanctity of the moral order.

The first sin, shirk — associating partners with Allah — is the gravest of all wrongs because it corrupts the very purpose of existence. The second, unlawful killing, violates the sanctity of life that Allah has declared inviolable, and is only permitted in cases where the law itself requires it, such as just retribution. The third, unlawful sexual relations, tears at the fabric of the family, destroys lineage, and spreads corruption through society. Together, these sins represent the fullest expression of a soul that has abandoned its Creator, trampled the rights of others, and surrendered to base desire.

The verse then warns that “whoever does that will meet a penalty” — that is, a multiplied punishment and disgrace, as the following verse makes clear. The warning is general, reminding every human being that these are not light matters that can be dismissed or minimized. The believer recognizes the gravity of these prohibitions and builds a life that keeps far from them, not merely avoiding the act itself but also the steps that lead towards it.

Yet the tone of the passage is not one of despair, for the very next verses open the door of repentance wide. The mention of these sins here, within a description of the servants of the Most Merciful, teaches that true servitude is recognized not by never having fallen, but by turning back to Allah sincerely when one does. The servants of the Most Merciful are those who purify their lives of these sins, and who hasten to repentance whenever they slip.

=69=

This verse details the consequence awaiting the one who persists in the grave sins mentioned in the previous verse: their punishment will be multiplied on the Day of Resurrection, and they will remain in it forever, in disgrace. The multiplication of punishment points to the compounded nature of these sins. Each of them is not a single transgression but a chain of harms that ripple outward — corrupting the self, harming others, and inviting others towards evil. The one who commits them without repentance accumulates a burden that grows heavier with every passing day, and the punishment is intensified in proportion to the evil committed and the defiance shown.

The word “disgrace” is significant. It teaches that the punishment of the Hereafter is not merely physical but also spiritual and emotional. The wrongdoer is not simply pained; they are humiliated, stripped of every pretense of honor, and exposed before all creation. In this world, a person may sin in secret and preserve a respectable appearance before others. But on the Day of Resurrection, secrets are laid bare, and the sinner stands naked of all pretense. The arrogance that once shielded them from repentance becomes their deepest humiliation.

The verse also underscores the permanence of this state for those who die upon these sins without repentance. The Hereafter is not a place of second chances. It is the harvest of what was sown in this life, and the door of return closes when the soul departs. This is why the Qur'an repeatedly urges humanity to repent now, while the opportunity remains, before death arrives and the multiplied punishment becomes unavoidable.

The contrast with the servants of the Most Merciful is vivid. They live in constant fear of this very end, which is why they pray to be averted from Hell and keep themselves far from these sins. The verse thus serves both as a warning to the heedless and as a reinforcement of the believer's resolve to remain upright.

=70=

After the stern warning of the previous verses, this verse opens the widest door of hope: “Except for those who repent, believe, and do righteous work, for them Allah will replace their evil deeds with good deeds. And Allah is ever Forgiving and Merciful.” This is one of the most consoling verses in the entire Qur'an, for it addresses even those who have fallen into the gravest sins — shirk, murder, and adultery — and assures them that sincere repentance can transform their entire record. No sin is too great for the mercy of Allah when the heart turns back to Him in truth.

The verse lists three conditions of true repentance: repentance itself, which is the turning away from sin with remorse; faith, which is the renewal of the heart's bond with Allah; and righteous action, which is the proof that the repentance is genuine and not merely a passing emotion. These three together form a complete transformation of the person. Repentance removes the sin, faith restores the connection to Allah, and good deeds build a new life upon a new foundation. One is not sufficient without the others.

The most extraordinary promise here is that Allah will “replace their evil deeds with good deeds.” The scholars explain that this transformation occurs in the record itself: the sins that once stood against a person are erased, and in their place are written the good deeds they performed after repenting. Some also understand it to mean that Allah changes the very nature of the person, replacing their inclination towards evil with a love for good, and turning their past disobedience into a source of humility and gratitude that drives them forward.

The verse closes with the reminder that Allah is ever Forgiving and Merciful. His forgiveness is not reluctant or conditional upon the person being already pure. Rather, it is His mercy that initiates the process, reaches down to the fallen servant, and lifts them up. This is the essence of the Islamic conception of Allah: a Lord whose mercy precedes His wrath and who rejoices at the return of His servant more than a traveler rejoices at finding his lost camel in the desert.

=71=

This verse reiterates and reinforces the theme of the previous one, declaring that “whoever repents and does righteous work has truly turned to Allah with an acceptable repentance.” The emphasis here is on the completeness and sincerity of the return. The word used for repentance, matāban, carries the sense of a full, decisive, and wholehearted turning — not a half-hearted gesture or a momentary regret, but a complete reorientation of the entire being towards Allah. It is a repentance that changes the direction of a life.

The verse teaches that repentance is not merely the utterance of words of forgiveness, nor a ritual formula repeated without reflection. True repentance involves a genuine remorse over the sin, an immediate cessation of it, and a firm resolve never to return to it. Where the sin involved the rights of another person, repentance also requires restoring those rights or seeking their pardon. When all of these are present, the repentance is described as the turning that Allah accepts, and the one who repents becomes, in the words of the Prophet, like one who has no sin at all.

The pairing of repentance with righteous work is again significant. Repentance is the removal of evil, while righteous work is the establishment of good. A person cannot claim to have truly repented while their life remains empty of good deeds, for the absence of good is itself a form of spiritual stagnation. The believer who has repented fills the space left by sin with worship, charity, kindness, and remembrance of Allah, so that the soul is rebuilt rather than merely emptied.

This verse is a source of immense hope for every believer who struggles with sin. It assures the penitent that Allah does not merely tolerate the one who returns but welcomes them with love. The door of repentance remains open until the soul reaches the throat at death, and even a lifetime of error can be erased in a single sincere moment of turning. The servants of the Most Merciful are precisely those who live in this constant state of returning to their Lord.

=72=

This verse describes another noble trait of the servants of the Most Merciful: “those who do not bear false witness, and when they pass near vain talk, they pass by with dignity.” The prohibition on false testimony is placed here because honesty is the foundation of all social trust. False witness is singled out because it is uniquely destructive: it perverts justice, wrongs the innocent, shields the guilty, and corrupts the very institutions meant to protect society. A single lie uttered under oath can destroy a life, break a family, or overturn a rightful verdict. The believer therefore guards their tongue with the utmost care and never lends it to falsehood.

The second part of the verse extends this principle from the court of law to the arena of daily life. Vain talk, laghw, refers to speech that is empty, frivolous, or harmful — gossip, mockery, foul language, and idle chatter that wastes time and hardens the heart. The servants of the Most Merciful do not engage in such speech, nor do they linger among those who do. When they encounter it, they pass by “with dignity,” meaning they neither participate nor react with equal vulgarity. They rise above it, preserving their honor and their time.

This verse captures a complete etiquette of speech. The believer's tongue is an instrument of worship, used for truth, remembrance, and kindness, not for falsehood, gossip, or abuse. The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught that a person may utter a single word without thinking, and it will cause them to plunge into the Fire further than the distance between the east and the west. Conversely, a single word of goodness may raise a person in the sight of Allah.

The dignity with which the believer passes by vain talk is itself a form of da'wah, an invitation to goodness. It silently demonstrates that a life of purpose has no room for triviality, and it protects the believer from the slow erosion of the soul that comes from constant exposure to empty and harmful speech.

=73=

This verse describes the receptiveness of the servants of the Most Merciful to divine guidance: “those who, when reminded of the verses of their Lord, do not fall upon them deaf and blind.” The image is powerful. The Qur'an here contrasts the believer with the disbeliever who, when the signs of Allah are recited, behaves as though they were deaf and blind — unhearing, unseeing, and utterly unresponsive. The believer, by contrast, is fully awake. When the verses of their Lord are recited, they listen with attention, reflect with their minds, and respond with their hearts and limbs.

The phrase “fall upon them deaf and blind” suggests a deliberate, even violent refusal. It is not that such people cannot hear or see; it is that they choose to block out the truth, collapsing into heedlessness as a person collapses into sleep. The believer refuses this collapse. They remain upright and alert, greeting the verses of Allah as a traveler in the desert greets water — with thirst, gratitude, and full engagement. This is the opposite of the arrogance that turns away from the truth because it threatens one's desires or status.

The verse also teaches that the Qur'an is meant to be received as a living address, not a static text. Each recitation is a fresh reminder, and each reminder demands a response. The believer does not hear the same verse a hundred times with a heart that has grown dull. Rather, each hearing is an opportunity for renewed understanding, deeper humility, and greater closeness to Allah. This is why the companions of the Prophet would weep, tremble, and be transformed when they heard the Qur'an recited.

This receptiveness is both a gift and a discipline. It is a gift in that Allah opens the hearts of His sincere servants to His light. But it is also a discipline, cultivated through presence of mind, humility, and a genuine desire to be guided. The servants of the Most Merciful strive to keep their inner ears and eyes open, so that no verse of their Lord ever passes them by without leaving its trace upon their souls.

=74=

This verse records one of the most beautiful supplications of the servants of the Most Merciful: “Our Lord, grant us from among our spouses and offspring comfort to our eyes, and make us leaders of the righteous.” The phrase “comfort to our eyes” (qurrata a'yun) is an idiom for the deepest joy and contentment — the delight that makes a person's eyes light up when they see their loved ones upon goodness. The believer does not pray merely for a beautiful spouse or successful children. They pray for spouses and offspring who will be a source of spiritual joy: righteous, pious, and pleasing to Allah, for it is this that brings true peace to the heart.

The supplication reflects a profound understanding of what constitutes happiness in family life. A spouse who shares one's faith, who encourages one towards good, and who is a partner in worship is a far greater treasure than one who is merely attractive or wealthy. Children who grow up upon the path of righteousness are the most precious legacy a parent can leave behind. The believer therefore asks Allah to make their family a source of comfort in this life and of reunion in the Hereafter.

The second part of the supplication, “make us leaders of the righteous,” elevates the prayer from the personal to the communal. The believer does not ask merely to be good, but to be an imam — a model, a guide, and an example whom others follow in goodness. This is a request for excellence, for the highest station of the righteous, in which one's own righteousness becomes a light that guides others. It reflects a heart that is not content with personal salvation but yearns to contribute to the guidance of humanity.

This supplication is a complete vision of a blessed life: a righteous family that brings joy, and a life that inspires righteousness in others. It is a prayer that the believer should make constantly, for it encompasses both the private and the public dimensions of faith, and it asks Allah for the very best in both this world and the next.

=75=

Having described the beautiful qualities of the servants of the Most Merciful throughout this passage, the Qur'an now reveals their reward: “Those will be rewarded with the highest chambers in Paradise because of their patience, and they will be met therein with greetings and peace.” The “highest chambers” (ghurfah) refer to the elevated, lofty dwellings of Paradise, reserved for those who excelled in faith and character. They are mansions built in the heights of Paradise, from which their inhabitants look down upon the gardens and rivers below, and above which there is nothing but the Throne of the Most Merciful.

The reward is explicitly tied to patience. This is because every quality described in the preceding verses — moderation in spending, purity from grave sins, honesty, dignity in the face of vanity, receptiveness to guidance, and devotion in prayer — requires patient, persistent effort against the inclinations of the self. Patience is the thread that binds all virtues together. It is the strength to restrain desire, to endure difficulty, to forgive others, and to remain steadfast upon the path of Allah over a lifetime. Paradise is described elsewhere as the reward of the patient, and here the highest chambers of Paradise are promised to those who embody patience in its fullest sense.

The verse then describes how they will be received: with greetings and peace. The angels will greet them with salām, and they will be met with words of honor, welcome, and reassurance after the trials of this world. This greeting is the culmination of their lives of peace — lives in which they spread peace among people, avoided conflict and falsehood, and remained in a state of inner tranquility with their Lord. Now that peace is returned to them in its most perfect form, multiplied and made eternal.

This verse connects the struggle of this world directly to the reward of the next. Every act of patience is an investment in that high chamber, and every difficulty endured for the sake of Allah is a brick in that lofty dwelling. The believer is thus encouraged to persevere, knowing that what awaits them is far greater than anything they have left behind.

=76=

This verse concludes the description of the reward of the servants of the Most Merciful by declaring that they will abide in Paradise forever, and that it is an excellent settlement and residence. The word khālidīn, abiding forever, is the ultimate promise: this bliss is not temporary, not conditional, and not subject to decline. Every joy in this world is shadowed by the fear of its ending, but the joy of Paradise is eternal, growing ever more perfect without the slightest diminution. This is the truest and most complete happiness, for it combines the perfection of the place with the permanence of the state.

The phrase “excellent settlement and residence” deliberately echoes the earlier description of Hell as an “evil settlement and residence.” The Qur'an thus places the two final destinations side by side, inviting the listener to compare and to choose. Hell is the worst of homes, where the inhabitants find torment, humiliation, and despair without end. Paradise is the best of homes, where the inhabitants find peace, honor, and delight without end. The believer who has been described throughout this passage is the one who has chosen wisely, and whose choice is now confirmed and rewarded.

The repetition of the word “settlement” and “residence” emphasizes that human beings are, by nature, seekers of a home. Everyone desires a place of belonging, security, and rest. The servants of the Most Merciful have understood that the true home is not the house of this world, which is temporary and will inevitably be left behind, but the abode of the Hereafter, which is prepared for them by their Lord. Their lives in this world are spent in preparation for that home, and their hearts are attached to it rather than to the fleeting dwellings of this life.

This verse thus brings the entire passage to a beautiful resolution, assuring the believer that their patience, their purity, and their devotion will culminate in an eternal home of excellence, a dwelling whose pleasure never fades and whose inhabitants never depart.

=77=

This final verse of the sûrah is addressed to the Prophet, peace be upon him, with a message for all of humanity: “Say, 'What would my Lord care for you were it not for your supplication? But you have denied, so the denial will be incumbent upon you.'” The verse shatters any illusion of human importance that is not grounded in the worship of Allah. Human beings, in themselves, are of no consequence to their Lord. Their worth lies entirely in their relationship with Him, expressed through supplication, worship, and the turning of the heart towards Him. Without this, a person is, in the divine scales, of no more weight than dust.

The word “supplication” here is understood by the scholars to encompass both du'ā' in the specific sense of calling upon Allah for one's needs, and ibādah in the broader sense of worship as a whole. The servant's value is found in their recognition of their need for Allah and their humble turning towards Him. It is this that elevates the human being above the heedless and makes them precious in the sight of their Lord. When a person abandons this, they abandon the very thing that gives their existence meaning.

The verse then declares that since the disbelievers have denied the truth, the consequence of that denial will be incumbent upon them — literally, it will cling to them and be “attached” to them without escape. The Arabic word lizām conveys the idea of something that adheres firmly and cannot be shaken off. Their denial will follow them into the Hereafter and become the very thing that seals their fate, just as their rejection of the Prophet's warning will become their eternal regret.

This verse is a fitting conclusion to a sûrah that has contrasted the arrogance of the disbelievers with the humility of the servants of the Most Merciful. It reminds every reader that true honor lies not in wealth, lineage, or worldly status, but in the sincere worship of Allah. The sûrah ends where true religion begins: with the recognition that we are nothing without our Lord, and that our only value lies in turning to Him.
