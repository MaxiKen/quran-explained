=41=

This verse records the mockery directed at the Prophet: "When they see you ˹O Prophet˺, they only make fun of you, ˹saying,˺ 'Is this the one that Allah has sent as a messenger?'" The disbelievers of Mecca, upon seeing the Prophet, would mock him, pointing at him and asking with derision whether this was truly the one Allah had sent as a messenger. Their mockery was a reflection of their arrogance and their inability to reconcile their worldly standards of status and power with the humble figure of the Prophet.

The mockery reveals the disbelievers' flawed criteria for judging a messenger. They expected a messenger to be someone of wealth, power, and worldly prominence, and they could not understand how Allah could choose a man of modest means and simple life. Their question — "Is this the one?" — was not a genuine inquiry but a sarcastic dismissal, an attempt to belittle both the Prophet and the message he carried. They judged the message by the messenger's outward appearance, failing to see that the truth of a message is not determined by the status of its bearer.

The verse teaches the believer about the nature of the opposition that the truth faces. Throughout history, the messengers of Allah were mocked by those who measured worth by worldly standards, and the Prophet Muhammad was no exception. The believer is reminded not to judge the truth by outward appearances, and not to be shaken by the mockery of those who cannot see beyond the surface. The verse also consoles the believer with the knowledge that the mockers, in their arrogance, are mocking not a mere man but the chosen messenger of Allah, and that their mockery will turn to regret on the Day of Judgment.

=42=

This verse continues the disbelievers' mockery: "He would have almost tricked us away from our gods, had we not been so devoted to them." The disbelievers, still speaking among themselves, claimed that the Prophet had nearly succeeded in turning them away from their idols, and that only their devotion to their gods had saved them from his influence. Their words, intended as mockery, are in fact an unintentional admission of the power of the Prophet's message — a message so compelling that it nearly overcame their attachment to their false gods.

The verse then delivers the response to their mockery: "˹But˺ soon they will know, when they face the punishment, who is far astray from the ˹Right˺ Way." The disbelievers considered the Prophet to be astray and themselves to be upon the right path, but the verse warns them that the truth will soon be revealed. When they face the punishment of the Hereafter, they will know who was truly astray. The mockery of this world will give way to the clarity of the Hereafter, where the reality of their error will be undeniable.

The verse teaches the believer about the ultimate vindication of the truth. The disbelievers mocked now, but the day will come when the roles will be reversed, and the truth will be manifest for all to see. The believer is encouraged to remain patient in the face of mockery, trusting that the truth will prevail and that the mockers will eventually recognise their error, even if that recognition comes too late. The verse also warns against the arrogance that dismisses the truth, for the one who mocks the truth today will face its consequences tomorrow.

=43=

This verse poses a rhetorical question: "Have you seen ˹O Prophet˺ the one who has taken their own desires as their god? Will you then be a keeper over them?" The verse describes a person who has elevated their own desires to the status of a deity, worshipping their whims and inclinations instead of Allah. This is a profound diagnosis of the root of disbelief, for the one who rejects the truth is often not rejecting it for intellectual reasons but because the truth conflicts with their desires, and so they make their desires the ultimate authority in their lives.

The question "Will you then be a keeper over them?" addresses the Prophet, asking whether he is responsible for such a person. The answer is implicit: the Prophet's duty is to convey the message, not to compel belief or to be a guardian over those who choose to worship their desires. The one who has made their desires their god has chosen their path, and they bear the responsibility for that choice. The Prophet is reassured that he is not accountable for those who refuse to be guided.

The verse teaches the believer about the danger of letting desires rule the heart. Every sin, every rejection of the truth, and every turning away from Allah is ultimately rooted in the worship of desire — the choice to follow what one wants over what one knows to be right. The believer is called to dethrone their desires and to place Allah at the centre of their life, making His pleasure the standard by which every desire is measured. The verse also reminds the believer that guidance cannot be forced, and that the responsibility for each soul rests ultimately with itself.

=44=

This verse continues the diagnosis of the disbelievers' condition: "Or do you think that most of them listen or understand? They are only like cattle—no, more than that, they are astray from the ˹Right˺ Way." The verse asks whether the Prophet thinks that most of the disbelievers truly listen to the message or understand it, and then answers the question: they are like cattle, following blindly without comprehension, and indeed they are even more astray than cattle, for cattle at least respond to their shepherd, while these people refuse to respond to the guidance of their Lord.

The comparison to cattle is not meant to insult but to diagnose. Cattle follow their immediate desires — food, water, comfort — without reflection or understanding, and the disbelievers were similar in that they lived only for their immediate worldly desires, without reflecting on the purpose of their existence or the reality of the Hereafter. Yet the verse adds that they are "more than that" — even more astray than cattle — because they possess the faculties of hearing and understanding that cattle lack, and they misused those faculties, choosing to ignore the truth that was made clear to them.

The verse teaches the believer about the importance of using the faculties of hearing and understanding for their true purpose. These faculties are gifts from Allah, given so that human beings may listen to the truth, reflect upon it, and be guided by it. The one who uses them only for worldly pursuits, ignoring the guidance of their Lord, has sunk below the level of the animals. The believer is urged to listen with an attentive heart, to understand with a reflective mind, and to respond to the truth with action, so that they may not be among those who are astray from the right path.

=45=

This verse draws attention to a sign in nature: "Have you not seen how your Lord extends the shade—He could have simply made it ˹remain˺ still if He so willed—then We make the sun its guide." The verse invites reflection upon the shadow, which Allah extends gradually across the earth and then withdraws as the sun moves. The shadow is a subtle but powerful sign of Allah's wisdom, for it moves and changes in perfect order, governed by laws that no one controls.

The verse notes that Allah could have made the shade stationary, fixed in one place, but He did not; instead, He made the sun its guide, causing the shade to lengthen and shorten, appear and disappear, in a continuous cycle. This movement of shade and light is a sign of the divine wisdom that governs all of creation, and it points to the One Who set the sun and the earth in their precise orbits. The changing shade is a daily reminder, visible to all, of the power and design of the Creator.

The verse teaches the believer to contemplate the signs of Allah in the natural world, for the universe is filled with evidence of His existence, power, and wisdom. The movement of the shade, which most people take for granted, is in fact a carefully orchestrated sign, as precise as the orbit of the planets. The believer is encouraged to move beyond a superficial awareness of nature to a deep reflection upon its meaning, seeing in every natural phenomenon the hand of the Creator and the wisdom of His design. This reflection is a means of strengthening faith and deepening gratitude.

=46=

This verse completes the description of the shade: "causing the shade to retreat gradually." After Allah extends the shade, He causes it to withdraw gradually, little by little, as the sun rises higher in the sky. The gradual retreat of the shade is a sign of the gentle, measured wisdom of Allah, who does not act abruptly but governs the world through gradual, orderly processes. The shade does not vanish suddenly but retreats step by step, in a manner that is imperceptible yet inexorable.

The gradual movement of the shade serves as a metaphor for many of the processes of life. The passing of time, the changing of seasons, the growth of a child, and the unfolding of Allah's decrees all occur gradually, according to His wisdom. The believer is reminded that Allah's plans unfold in their own time, and that patience is required while waiting for the fulfilment of His promises. Just as the shade retreats gradually, so too do the affairs of life move according to a divine timetable that is neither rushed nor delayed.

The verse teaches the believer to recognise the wisdom of gradualism in Allah's creation and decree. The one who reflects upon the gradual retreat of the shade learns to trust in the gradual unfolding of Allah's plan, to be patient in times of waiting, and to appreciate the order and rhythm that govern all of existence. The verse also serves as a reminder of the passing of time, for just as the shade retreats, so too does the span of one's life retreat, moment by moment, towards its appointed end, and the wise person uses that time in the remembrance of their Lord.

=47=

This verse draws attention to another sign: "He is the One Who has made the night for you as a cover, and ˹made˺ sleep for resting, and the day for rising." The verse describes three of Allah's blessings: the night, which He made as a cover for rest; sleep, which He made for repose and restoration; and the day, which He made for rising and activity. These are among the most fundamental and universal of Allah's gifts, experienced by every human being every single day, yet so often taken for granted.

The description of the night as a "cover" is evocative, for the darkness of night wraps the earth like a garment, providing concealment and rest from the labour of the day. Sleep is described as a time of "resting" (subāt), a state in which the body and mind are restored and refreshed. The day is made for "rising" (nushūr), the time of awakening and activity, when people rise to pursue their livelihoods and their duties. The alternation of night and day, sleep and wakefulness, is a sign of Allah's mercy and wisdom, providing the rhythm that sustains human life.

The verse teaches the believer gratitude for the everyday blessings of Allah. The night, sleep, and the day are not accidental features of existence but deliberate gifts, designed by the Creator for the well-being of His servants. The believer is encouraged to reflect upon these blessings, to recognise the wisdom behind them, and to express gratitude for them through the worship and obedience of their Lord. The verse also reminds the believer that sleep is a kind of death and waking a kind of resurrection, pointing to the greater resurrection that awaits, when all of humanity will rise from their graves to face their Lord.

=48=

This verse continues the enumeration of Allah's signs: "And He is the One Who sends the winds ushering in His mercy, and We send down pure rain from the sky." The verse describes the winds, which Allah sends as heralds of His mercy, preceding the rain and announcing its arrival. The winds gather the clouds, carry the moisture, and prepare the atmosphere for the rainfall that will revive the earth. They are described as "ushering in His mercy," for the rain they bring is a manifestation of Allah's mercy upon His creation.

The verse then describes the rain itself: "and We send down pure rain from the sky." The word "pure" (ṭahūr) describes water that is itself clean and that also purifies, for rain is the source of the fresh water that cleanses the earth, sustains life, and provides for every creature. The purity of rain is a sign of the purity of Allah's provision, and its descent from the sky is a sign of His power to provide for His creation from above.

The verse teaches the believer to see the mercy of Allah in the natural world. The winds and the rain are not mere weather patterns but deliberate acts of divine mercy, sent down by the One Who cares for His creation. The believer is encouraged to reflect upon the rain, to recognise in it the provision of their Lord, and to respond with gratitude and humility. The verse also serves as a reminder of the spiritual rain — the revelation of the Qur'an — which descends from Allah to revive hearts just as the physical rain revives the earth, and the believer is urged to open their heart to both.

=49=

This verse completes the description of the rain's effect: "giving life to a lifeless land, and providing water for countless animals and humans of Our Own creation." The rain descends upon the dead, barren earth and gives it life, causing vegetation to spring forth where before there was nothing. This revival of the lifeless land is one of the most powerful signs of Allah's ability to resurrect, for the One Who brings the dead earth to life can just as easily bring the dead to life on the Day of Judgment.

The verse then mentions the rain's role in providing water for "countless animals and humans of Our Own creation." The water that falls from the sky sustains an innumerable multitude of living beings — animals of every kind and humans of every nation — all of whom depend upon it for their survival. The word "countless" emphasises the vastness of Allah's provision, which extends to every creature He has created, without exception and without limit. The mention that all are "of Our Own creation" reminds us that every living being is the direct creation of Allah, and that His provision encompasses them all.

The verse teaches the believer about the interconnectedness of creation and the comprehensive mercy of Allah. The rain that falls upon a distant land is part of a divine system that sustains all life, and the believer is reminded that their own provision comes from the same source that provides for every creature. The verse also points to the resurrection, for the revival of the dead earth through rain is a sign and a proof of the revival of the dead on the Day of Judgment. The believer is urged to reflect upon this sign and to strengthen their certainty in the promise of the Hereafter.

=50=

This verse explains a deeper purpose behind the distribution of rain: "We certainly disperse it among them so they may be mindful, but most people persist in ungratefulness." The rain is not sent uniformly upon all lands but is distributed and dispersed by Allah according to His wisdom, falling upon some places and withheld from others, in varying amounts and at varying times. This distribution is a test, for it is meant to awaken gratitude in the hearts of those who receive it and to remind them of their dependence upon their Lord.

The verse states that this dispersal is "so they may be mindful" — that is, so that people may reflect upon the source of their provision and remember the One Who provides. When a person receives rain and its blessings, they are meant to turn in gratitude to Allah, recognising that the provision came not by their own power but by His mercy. Yet the verse notes that "most people persist in ungratefulness," for despite the clear signs in the rain and its distribution, most people remain heedless and ungrateful, taking the blessings for granted and attributing them to natural causes rather than to their Lord.

The verse teaches the believer about the test of provision and the virtue of gratitude. Blessings are not distributed equally in this world, and this inequality is itself a sign and a test, meant to cultivate gratitude in those who receive and patience in those who are tested with less. The believer is reminded to respond to Allah's blessings with gratitude, to recognise His hand in every provision, and to avoid the ingratitude that afflicts so many. The verse also encourages the believer to share their provisions with others, for the dispersal of blessings is a reminder that provision ultimately belongs to Allah, who gives to whom He wills.

This verse addresses a hypothetical: "Had We willed, We could have easily sent a warner to every society." The verse reminds the Prophet and the believers that Allah, in His power, could have sent a separate warner to every community, each with its own prophet to deliver its own warning. The fact that He chose instead to send a single messenger, the Prophet Muhammad, with a universal message for all of humanity, is a matter of divine wisdom and a testament to the comprehensive nature of his mission.

The verse highlights the unique position of the Prophet Muhammad as the final messenger, sent not to one people or one time but to all of humanity until the end of time. His message is the culmination of all previous revelations, complete and sufficient for every people and every age. This is a great honour for the Prophet and his followers, for they are the bearers of the final, universal message, entrusted with conveying it to the world.

The verse teaches the believer to appreciate the completeness of the Qur'anic message and the weight of the responsibility that comes with it. Since Allah chose to send a single messenger to all of humanity, the believers are the inheritors of that universal mission, and they carry the duty of conveying the message to others. The believer is encouraged to learn the message deeply, to live by it faithfully, and to share it with wisdom and compassion, recognising that they are part of the community entrusted with the final revelation to all of humanity.

The verse teaches that the greatest struggle against falsehood is not through coercion or force but through the power of the Qur'an — its arguments, its wisdom, its warnings, and its guidance. The Qur'an is the believer's most powerful tool in calling people to the truth, and the struggle to convey it and uphold it is the noblest of struggles.

The verse teaches the believer about the nature of true striving in the path of Allah. The believer is called to stand firm upon the truth, to resist the pressure to compromise, and to engage in the struggle of conveying the Qur'an with wisdom, patience, and perseverance. This struggle is waged through knowledge, through example, through gentle persuasion, and through steadfastness in the face of opposition. The believer is reminded that the Qur'an is not merely a book to be read but a force to be wielded, a light that dispels darkness, and a truth that overcomes falsehood.

This verse draws attention to another sign: "And He is the One Who merges the two bodies of water: one fresh and palatable and the other salty and bitter, placing between them a barrier they cannot cross." The verse describes the meeting of two bodies of water — one fresh and sweet, the other salty and bitter — and the invisible barrier that Allah has placed between them, preventing them from mixing and corrupting each other. This phenomenon is visible in many places on earth, where rivers of fresh water flow into the sea without the salt water overcoming the fresh.

The "barrier they cannot cross" (barzakh) is a sign of Allah's precise and delicate control over creation. The two bodies of water meet, yet each retains its own character, separated by a barrier that neither can breach. This is a testimony to the power and wisdom of the Creator, who set the laws that govern the natural world and maintain the balance upon which life depends. Without this barrier, the fresh water that sustains life would be overwhelmed by the salt, and the earth would lose its ability to support living beings.

The verse teaches the believer to reflect upon the balance and order in creation, and to recognise in it the hand of the All-Wise Creator. The barrier between the two seas is a sign that nothing in creation is random or uncontrolled, but all is governed by precise divine laws. The believer is reminded that the same Lord who maintains the barrier between the seas also maintains the balance in their own life, and that true security lies in trusting His wisdom and following His guidance. The verse also serves as a metaphor for the separation between truth and falsehood, which Allah keeps distinct so that the sincere may be guided.

This verse continues the enumeration of Allah's signs: "And He is the One Who creates human beings from a ˹humble˺ liquid, then establishes for them bonds of kinship and marriage. This humble origin is itself a sign, for it demonstrates that the great complexity of the human being, with all its faculties and capacities, comes from the most lowly of beginnings, through the power of Allah alone.

The verse then mentions the bonds that Allah establishes among human beings: "bonds of kinship and marriage." From the single drop of fluid, Allah creates a human being and then establishes for them the bonds of lineage and marriage, through which families, communities, and entire societies are formed. These bonds — the ties of blood and the ties of marriage — are among the greatest of Allah's gifts, for they provide the love, support, and continuity that sustain human life across generations.

The verse concludes: "For your Lord is Most Capable," affirming the power of Allah to create, to connect, and to provide. The believer is taught to reflect upon their own origin and upon the bonds that connect them to others, and to recognise in both the power and mercy of their Lord. The verse also encourages gratitude for the gift of family, and a sense of responsibility towards the bonds of kinship and marriage, which are to be honoured and maintained as signs of Allah's wisdom. The humble origin of every human being is a reminder of humility, while the bonds of family are a reminder of the love and interdependence that Allah has woven into creation.

The one who worships besides Allah is entrusting themselves to that which is utterly powerless, while the one who disbelieves is actively taking a stand against their Creator. The believer is called to purify their worship, directing it solely to Allah, the only One Who can benefit or harm, and to avoid the state of those who collaborate against their Lord. The verse is a warning against the heedlessness that leads a person to side against the very source of their existence.

This verse clarifies the role of the Prophet: "And We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ only as a deliverer of good news and a warner." The verse defines the mission of the Prophet with two terms: he is a "deliverer of good news" (bashīr) and a "warner" (nadhīr). As a deliverer of good news, he brings the glad tidings of Paradise, forgiveness, and the mercy of Allah to those who believe and do good. As a warner, he cautions against the punishment of Allah for those who disbelieve and do wrong. These two roles together constitute the essence of the prophetic mission.

The word "only" is significant, for it defines the limits of the Prophet's responsibility. He is sent only to deliver the message — to bring good news and to warn — and not to compel belief, not to judge the hearts, and not to guarantee the outcome. This clarification is a source of comfort for the Prophet, relieving him of the burden of those who reject his message, for his duty is fulfilled in the faithful delivery of what he has been sent with, while the response of each soul is its own responsibility.

The verse teaches the believer about the balance of hope and fear that characterises the Islamic message. The message of Islam is neither one of pure threat nor one of pure leniency; it is a message of both good news and warning, calling people to Allah through the promise of His mercy and the caution against His punishment. The believer is encouraged to embody both aspects, to be a source of hope for the struggling and a source of warning for the heedless, and to recognise that the ultimate responsibility for guidance lies with Allah, while their duty is the faithful and compassionate delivery of the message.

The believer is encouraged to purify their own intentions, to serve the cause of Allah without expectation of worldly return, and to recognise that the path to their Lord is open to them and to all who wish to take it. The verse also reassures the believer that the message of Islam is not a burden imposed upon them but an invitation, freely offered, to the path of their own salvation.

The verse teaches the believer to place their trust in the Ever-Living, to glorify His praises, and to find in His complete awareness a source of both accountability and mercy.

The verse concludes that this command, rather than guiding them, only increased their distance from the truth, for the truth, when met with arrogance, drives the arrogant further away.

The verse teaches the believer about the nature of humility and the danger of arrogance. The prostration to Allah is the ultimate symbol of submission, and it is this submission that the arrogant soul finds unbearable. The believer is called to embrace prostration — both physical and spiritual — as the path to closeness to Allah, and to beware of the arrogance that rejects the truth because it demands humility. The verse also highlights the profound privilege of the believer, who knows ar-Raḥmān and prostrates to Him willingly, finding in that prostration not humiliation but elevation and peace.
