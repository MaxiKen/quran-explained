=41=

After presenting the celestial signs, the surah moves to a historical sign on water—a sign of mercy and deliverance intertwined with warning. “Another sign for them” links directly to the sequence of signs in the earth, the night, the sun, and the moon. Now the Quraysh are reminded of the story of Noah, which they knew as part of their ancestral tradition but had ceased to reflect upon. The verse says, “We carried their ancestors,” referring to those who were saved in the Ark with Noah.

This is a profound point: the Quraysh themselves are descendants of the few believers who boarded the Ark, because all humanity after the flood descends from Noah and those with him. They owe their very existence to that merciful carriage. The Ark was “fully loaded” (al-fulk al-mashḥūn), meaning it was packed with the believers and a pair of every animal species, carrying the seeds of a new world. The event is presented not merely as a story but as a personal sign for the Meccans. Their own lineage traces back to a moment of divine rescue from a catastrophic flood that drowned the deniers. This should stir gratitude, awe, and fear. If Allah saved their forefathers by carrying them on water, He can just as easily carry them on their sea journeys, and He can also drown them if they persist in denial. The Ark, a seemingly fragile wooden vessel, withstood the raging flood by Allah’s command. It stands as an enduring symbol that safety lies not in material strength but in following God’s messenger. For the Prophet Muhammad, the parallel was clear: his followers, though small and weak in Makkah, were like the passengers on the Ark, destined for salvation, while the disbelieving majority faced destruction if they did not board the ship of faith.

=42=

The sign is not limited to the historical Ark; Allah reminds the Quraysh that He has created for them “similar things to ride in.” This refers to the ships, boats, and vessels that humans build and use to traverse the seas. The Quraysh were a trading people who undertook two major caravan journeys mentioned in Sūrah Quraysh, but they also knew maritime travel and the ships that docked at the ports of the Red Sea.

The verse teaches that human ingenuity in shipbuilding is itself a divine gift. The wood, the metals, the knowledge of engineering, and the buoyancy of water are all created by Allah. When a person boards a ship, they are placing their trust in a vessel that floats by the laws Allah has ordained.

The phrase “similar things” extends beyond watercraft to include all modes of transportation—camels, horses, and later cars and planes—anything that carries people and their goods. All of them are signs of Allah’s provision and teaching. This connects to the earlier theme of gratitude: people use these conveyances daily without reflecting on the One who made them possible.

The verse also contains a subtle warning: just as Allah provided ships, He can also take away their utility in an instant. The ships that sail smoothly can become fragile shells in a storm. The sign, therefore, reminds the traveler that every journey is by Allah’s leave, and that the same Lord who gave the means of transport can also decree its end. It encourages the believer to recite the travel supplication and to journey with consciousness of their utter dependence on the Sustainer.

=43=

This verse pulls back the curtain on human vulnerability. After speaking of the ships that people enjoy, Allah reveals how easily He could transform that blessing into destruction. “If We willed, We could drown them” is a direct statement of absolute power. No ship is unsinkable by its own nature; the sea that carries it can also swallow it by a single divine command.

The verse then paints a terrifying picture of helplessness. “Then no one would respond to their cries” (ṣarīkh) describes a drowning person screaming for help. In that moment, all human strength, technology, and emergency systems become useless. There would be no coast guard, no rescue team, no power on earth that could save them if Allah did not will it. “Nor would they be rescued” (lā yunqadhūn) means they would not be saved or delivered from their plight.

This image is meant to shake the heedless heart. The disbelievers who feel secure in their worldly means are reminded that their security is an illusion. The same sea that floats their trading vessels could be the grave of all their hopes. The verse is not meant to cause despair but to redirect reliance from the creation to the Creator. When one truly internalizes that no one can rescue them from a divinely willed calamity, the only logical response is to turn to the One who controls all things and to seek His protection before it is needed.

The verse also carries echoes of the flood of Noah, where cries for help were unavailing after the decree was issued. It connects the sign of the Ark to the present and the future, weaving a coherent warning.

=44=

The verse immediately provides the exception, the source of all safety. People are not drowned “except by mercy from Us.

” The word “mercy” (raḥmah) encompasses Allah’s compassion, protection, and forbearance. Every safe voyage, every ship that reaches its harbor, does so purely because of Allah’s mercy, not because of human skill or favorable winds alone.

The second part of the verse, “allowing them enjoyment for a little while” (matāʿan ilā ḥīn), places this mercy in the context of the temporary worldly life. The safe passage, the successful trade, the return to family—all of this is a short-term enjoyment.

The word “little” (ḥīn) can mean a specified term, which is the lifespan of a person or the duration of this world. The enjoyment is a test and an opportunity to be grateful. When people survive a storm or complete a journey safely, they are given more time.

The question is how they will use that time. Will they use the saved moments to turn to Allah in gratitude and obedience, or will they remain heedless and return to their old ways? The Quraysh were granted safety in their caravans and respect among the tribes precisely so that they might worship the Lord of the Kaʿbah, yet they filled His house with idols.

This verse reminds them that the postponement of punishment is a mercy, but it will not last forever. The little while is running out. The believer, therefore, sees every day of safety as a gift to increase in good deeds. The verse ties the maritime sign directly to the urgency of repentance: the Ark of salvation through faith is still open, but the time of enjoyment granted is limited.

=45=

The surah now shifts to describe the disbelievers’ reaction to clear advice. The phrase “Still they turn away” is implied and captures their habitual rejection. When they are told to “beware of what is ahead of you and what is behind you,” they ignore the warning. “What is ahead of you” refers to the Hereafter—the approaching Day of Judgment, the Reckoning, Paradise, and Hell. “What is behind you” refers to the historical punishments that befell previous nations like ʿĀd, Thamūd, and the people of Noah. These two sources of warning—the past and the future—combine to give a complete picture: look at what happened to those who denied the truth before you, and look at what awaits you if you follow their path. The purpose of this warning is stated at the end: “so you may be shown mercy.” The warnings are not sent to satisfy divine anger but to guide people to the mercy of Allah. The very act of warning is a form of mercy.

Yet the disbelievers turn away. Their turning away is not due to a lack of clarity; the warning is clear and comprehensive. It is due to arrogance, love of worldly life, and the false sense of security that the previous verses dismantled.

This verse teaches the proper response to admonition: the believer should not turn away from reminders about the Hereafter and the lessons of history but should receive them with an open heart, hoping for Allah’s mercy. For the Prophet, the verse explains the frustrating reality that even clear, merciful warnings are often met with rejection.

=46=

This verse generalizes the disbelievers’ attitude. The word “sign” (āyah) in this context includes the verses of the Quran, the natural signs in the horizon and within themselves, and the miracles that they witnessed.

The phrase “from their Lord” underscores the source of the signs; they are not from a human being but from their Creator, Sustainer, and ultimate Judge. This makes their turning away all the more severe. The action “they turn away” (aʿraḍū) implies a deliberate, conscious rejection, not a passive neglect. They physically and mentally turn aside, refusing to engage with the truth, mocking it, or busying themselves with other things.

The use of “whenever” (wa mā taʾtīhim) indicates that this is a persistent pattern. It is not a one-time refusal but a habitual response. Every new sign becomes another occasion for rejection.

This is the tragic state of a heart that has become sealed due to continuous sin and arrogance. The verse also highlights a profound irony: the signs are given to guide them, but their rejection of the signs becomes the cause of their further misguidance and the justification for their punishment. This pattern is a warning for all believers to honour the signs of Allah when they come, whether it is a verse recited, a sermon heard, or a reflection upon creation. One should immediately turn towards the sign with acceptance and reflection, not away from it with indifference.

The verse also served to comfort the Prophet; the rejection he faced was not personal but a symptom of a deeper spiritual sickness that had afflicted many before them.

=47=

This verse presents a concrete example of the disbelievers’ arrogance and twisted logic. They are invited to spend in charity from the wealth that Allah has provided them. The command to donate is a test of gratitude and compassion.

However, the disbelievers respond with a sarcastic argument that sounds logical on the surface but is deeply flawed. They say, “Why should we feed those whom Allah could have fed if He wanted to?

” This is an attempt to use divine decree as an excuse for miserliness and neglect. They argue that if Allah had willed, He could have made the poor rich, so the fact that they are poor must be what Allah wants, and they should not interfere. This argument is a gross misuse of the concept of predestination. Allah’s will includes the creation of rich and poor as a test: the rich are tested with their wealth to see if they will give, and the poor are tested with their poverty to see if they will be patient. The disbelievers twist this test into a justification for oppression. They then add, “You are clearly astray,” accusing the believers of being misguided for advocating charity.

This is a complete inversion of morality: generosity is called misguidance, and selfishness is presented as wisdom. The verse exposes the mindset that leads to economic injustice and the dehumanization of the poor. It also shows that mocking the commands of Allah only compounds one’s guilt.

For believers, it is a reminder to never use theological arguments to escape moral obligations, and to expect that doing good may invite ridicule from those who love this world.

=48=

The disbelievers shift from mocking charity to mocking the promise of the Hereafter. Their question is not a sincere inquiry but a challenge dripping with disbelief and sarcasm. “When will this threat come to pass?” refers to the Day of Judgment, the resurrection, and the punishment they were warned about. By demanding a specific date, they think they are exposing the believers as frauds. If the believers cannot name the hour, the disbelievers assume the whole thing is a myth.

This is a classic tactic of those who reject the unseen: they demand empirical proof on their own terms. The question also reveals their underlying assumption that because the punishment has not yet arrived, it never will. They fail to understand that the delay is a mercy and a test, not a sign of falsehood.

The phrase “if what you say is true” is a conditional challenge meant to belittle the believers. The verse captures the psychological state of the Meccan elite: they were so entrenched in their worldly power and so accustomed to immediate results that they could not fathom a delayed but certain reckoning.

For believers, this verse is a reminder not to be disheartened by such mockery. The timing of the Hour is known only to Allah, and its suddenness is part of its terror and wisdom.

The answer to their question is about to be given in the next verses, not with a date, but with a description of the suddenness that will leave them no time to prepare or escape.

=49=

The answer to their mockery is a chilling warning. They are not awaiting a date they can mark on a calendar; they are awaiting “a single Blast” (ṣayḥatan wāḥidah).

This is the first blow of the Trumpet, a terrifying cosmic shout that will bring the world to an end. It echoes the “one blast” that destroyed the town earlier in the surah, connecting the present warning to the past parable.

The phrase “while they are entrenched in worldly disputes” paints a vivid picture of the moment it strikes. They will be going about their daily lives, arguing over business deals, debating politics, haggling in the marketplace, or quarrelling with their families, completely oblivious to what is about to happen.

The word “entrenched” (yakhiṣṣimūn) implies intense engagement in conflict and worldly affairs. There will be no special preamble, no warning signal that gives them time to repent. The Blast will seize them in the midst of their heedlessness.

This is a direct response to their question: they want to know when the threat will come, but when it comes, they will have no chance to ask. The verse is a powerful reminder to the living that worldly disputes, which seem so important, will be rendered utterly meaningless in an instant. The believer should not be among those who are caught up in such disputes at the expense of preparing for the Hereafter. It also assures the Prophet that the judgment is in Allah’s hands and will come at a time the mockers least expect.

=50=

The suddenness of the Blast is further emphasized. After it strikes, they will not have the opportunity to make a last will or to give final instructions about their wealth and dependents.

This detail is particularly sharp because the Arabs took great pride in leaving wills and being remembered through their inheritance arrangements. The Blast will cut off all such worldly concerns in an instant.

Moreover, “nor can they return to their own people” means they will not be able to go back to their families or homes. This could refer to the fact that they are instantly killed and thus cannot return from wherever the Blast struck them, or that after the Blast, the world as they know it ends, and the phase of this life is permanently closed. There will be no second chance, no reprieve to make amends or to say goodbye. This verse reinforces the finality of the Blast. The opportunity to do good, to repent, to correct one’s affairs, ends with death. The Islamic teaching that one should have a will ready and that one should settle debts and disputes promptly is rooted in this awareness. Life is uncertain, and the end can come at any moment.

The verse also serves as a warning to those who delay their repentance, thinking they have plenty of time. When the collective Blast comes, it is too late for everyone. The scene transitions now from the end of the world to the resurrection.

=51=

After the first Blast which brings death to all, the second blowing of the Trumpet will occur. This is the blast of resurrection. The suddenness is captured by “then—behold!” (fa-idhā hum), a dramatic Quranic expression that depicts the scene as instantaneous. The dead will rise from their graves and “rush” (yansilūn) toward their Lord.

The word “rush” implies speed, urgency, and an irresistible force drawing them to the place of gathering. They will not have time to hesitate or hide. Some will emerge in a state of confusion, others in fear, all moving toward the plain of assembly.

The phrase “to their Lord” indicates the ultimate destination. They are not rising to resume their earthly lives but to stand before their Creator for judgment. This verse directly answers the earlier mockery about the bones being revived.

The image is clear: the graves will split open, and humanity will stream forth like locusts, as described in other surahs. For the Meccans, who buried their dead in the desert sands and saw no possibility of return, this was an extraordinary claim. But the One who created them the first time can certainly recreate them.

The verse turns a theoretical belief into a vivid, almost cinematic reality, urging the listener to live as if they can already see that day. The rushing to the Lord is a moment of utter accountability, and the believer hopes to be among those who rush with anticipation, not with terror.

=52=

The resurrected disbelievers express their shock and sudden recognition. Their first cry is one of distress: “Woe to us!” (yā waylanā), a profound expression of regret and grief. They then ask, in their disorientation, “Who has raised us up from our place of rest?” The “place of rest” (marqad) refers to their graves, which they had assumed would be their final, eternal resting place. They had believed that death was the end, a long sleep from which there would be no awakening. Now that they are awake, they are stunned.

The question is not asked for information but out of bewildered horror. Then immediately, without waiting for an answer, their own conscience or the angels supply the answer: “This must be what the Most Compassionate warned us of; the messengers told the truth! ” The use of “the Most Compassionate” (ar-Raḥmān) is poignant. They now recall the very attribute of Allah that they were invited to trust. They had mocked the warnings of a merciful Lord, and now they see that those warnings were utterly true.

The final admission, “the messengers told the truth,” is the bitter confession of those who have lost everything. In the world, they called the messengers liars; now, with the punishment before their eyes, they must acknowledge the truth. But this declaration of faith at that moment is of no benefit to them, because faith after seeing the punishment is not accepted. The verse stirs a deep fear of being among those who recognize the truth only when it is too late.

=53=

This verse reiterates the simplicity and suddenness of the resurrection event. The entire cosmic process of gathering humanity from the first to the last is accomplished with “one Blast” (ṣayḥatan wāḥidah). There is no lengthy procedure, no complicated machinery of resurrection; a single divine command uttered by the angel Isrāfīl upon the Trumpet is sufficient.

The phrase “then at once” (fa-idhā hum) again creates the effect of a scene change that happens in the blink of an eye. “They will all be brought before Us” emphasizes the totality and the destination. No one, whether believer or disbeliever, high or low, will be left behind. They will be “brought before Us” meaning presented before Allah for the reckoning.

This is a scene of complete exposure, where all of humanity stands in the court of the King of all kings. The repetition of the Blast imagery throughout this passage serves to dismantle the disbelievers’ argument that resurrection would require a great, observable, and perhaps impossible effort. Allah is declaring that it is astonishingly easy for Him. A single command, “Be,” and it is.

This verse also ties back to the earlier destruction of the town with one blast. The same effortless power that destroys can also recreate.

For the believer, the simplicity of the resurrection magnifies Allah’s greatness and serves as a constant reminder that the Day is not a distant possibility but an imminent reality that can arrive at any moment.

=54=

This verse establishes the absolute justice of the Day of Judgment. The first principle is that “no soul will be wronged in the least” (lā tuẓlamu nafsun shayʾan).

This is a comprehensive guarantee against any injustice. No one will receive a punishment greater than they deserve, no sin will be invented for them, and no good deed will be diminished.

This is a direct response to the fear that God might act arbitrarily. Allah, being the Most Just, will not wrong anyone by an atom’s weight.

The second principle is that “nor will you be rewarded except for what you used to do.” This is the principle of recompense based on deeds. Paradise is not earned by mere claim or lineage; it is granted through divine mercy, but entry and the ranks within it correspond to the deeds performed with sincere faith. This verse eliminates two common misconceptions.

The first is that a merciful God will simply forgive everyone regardless of their actions, making moral striving pointless. The second is that the judgment might be arbitrary or biased. Instead, the Quran presents a perfectly just system where every individual’s own actions—recorded in the perfect Record mentioned in verse 12—determine their outcome. This motivates the believer to fill their scale with good deeds and to avoid any transgression. It also warns the oppressor that every act of injustice will be accounted for, and the oppressed will receive their rights. The justice of that Day is a source of profound peace for those who were wronged in this world without redress.

=55=

The surah now contrasts the fate of the disbelievers with the state of the people of Paradise. The word “Indeed” (inna) gives emphasis and certainty. The residents of Paradise are described as being “busy enjoying themselves” (fī shughulin fākihūn).

The word “busy” might seem strange, as we associate busyness with work and stress, but here it refers to being delightfully occupied with pleasures that never grow tiresome. They are so engrossed in the joys of Paradise—its foods, drinks, companions, sights, and conversations—that they are completely free from any thought of the worldly suffering or of the fate of the condemned. Their busyness is not a chore; it is pure, rapturous enjoyment. Some commentators note that this implies they are too occupied with bliss to even notice the punishment of the disbelievers, which is a mercy for them, as seeing the suffering of others might cause them grief. They are in a state of perfect, all-encompassing happiness. This verse answers the deep human longing for a life free from anxiety and boredom. In this world, even moments of pleasure are tinged with the awareness that they will end. In Paradise, the enjoyment is continuous and ever-renewed. The verse invites the listener to desire that station and to work for it. It also implicitly contrasts with the believers in this world who were “busy” with worship and good deeds; their worldly busyness has now been transformed into eternal delight.

=56=

The description of the residents of Paradise becomes more detailed. “They and their spouses” (azwāj) refers to their wives or companions in Paradise.

This promise includes the reunion of righteous spouses who both enter Paradise, as well as the spouses specially created for the believers in Paradise. The concept of azwāj indicates companionship, love, and the fulfilment of human relational needs in a pure and elevated form. The setting is described as “in cool shade” (ẓilāl). The shade in Paradise is not a dim shadow but a perfectly comfortable, protective covering that shields them from any excessive heat or glare, while allowing delightful light. For the Arabs, who lived under a scorching sun, the image of eternal, pleasant shade was particularly evocative of luxury. They will be “reclining on canopied couches” (ʿalā al-arāʾiki muttakiʾūn).

The word arāʾik refers to raised, curtained couches or thrones, indicating honour, comfort, and royal treatment. Reclining is a posture of relaxation and security, as opposed to the anxious standing and rushing of the Day of Judgment. The canopies suggest privacy and elegance. This picture of marital harmony, physical ease, and luxurious surroundings shows that Paradise is not merely a spiritual state but a tangible, physical reality that satisfies the senses in the most beautiful way. It also gives believers a standard to look forward to, encouraging them to maintain their faith and chastity in this world for the sake of that eternal companionship.

=57=

The provision of Paradise is highlighted. Fruits are specifically mentioned, echoing the earlier earthly sign of dates and grapes. The fruits of Paradise are similar in name to those of the world, but their taste, quality, and abundance are unimaginably superior. More importantly, they will have “whatever they desire” (mā yaddaʿūn). This opens the door to the fulfilment of every lawful longing and wish. Whatever the soul craves for pleasure, beauty, or delight, it will be granted.

This is the ultimate freedom from lack and frustration. In this world, human desires are often unfulfilled or, if fulfilled, they bring with them consequences and dissatisfaction. In Paradise, desire and fulfilment are perfectly aligned, and there is no harm in anything they wish for.

This verse also contains a deep spiritual meaning: the highest desire of the believers is to see Allah and to be close to Him, and that too will be granted. The phrase “whatever they desire” thus encompasses both the sensory delights and the spiritual ecstasy of divine proximity. The verse is a powerful motivator. It tells the believer that no sacrifice made for Allah in this life is in vain, because in the Hereafter, the doors of pure and complete satisfaction will be opened wide. The contrast with the disbelievers, who will have nothing they desire and will be in total deprivation, is stark and deliberate.

=58=

This verse crowns the description of Paradise with its most sublime blessing. The greeting “Peace” (salām) is not merely a word spoken by the inhabitants to one another but a direct salutation from the Merciful Lord Himself.

This is a mark of unparalleled honour and intimacy. Allah, the Lord of all worlds, addresses the believers with a word that conveys safety, serenity, and complete well-being. It means that they are now permanently safe from any harm, punishment, sorrow, or divine displeasure. The greeting is from “the Merciful Lord” (ar-Raḥmān), emphasizing that this peace flows directly from His attribute of mercy. The very Lord whom they worshipped and trusted in the unseen, the Lord they called upon in their distress, now welcomes them with this blessed word. Some scholars note that when the believers hear this greeting from Allah, they will experience a joy that surpasses all other pleasures of Paradise. It signifies the fulfillment of the verse “They will have whatever they wish for, and We have more” (50:35). The greeting of Salām also confirms that the relationship between the Lord and His servants has reached a state of complete mutual pleasure. There is no anger, no reproach, only peace. This verse gives the believer in this world a taste of that peace through the remembrance of Allah, knowing that the ultimate Salām awaits them. It also serves as a final, sharp contrast to the state of the disbelievers, who will now be addressed in a very different manner.

=59=

The scene shifts dramatically. While the believers are enjoying peace and bliss, the disbelievers are given a harsh command.

The phrase “Step away” (imtāzū) means to separate, to be distinguished, or to stand apart. On the Day of Judgment, the disbelievers will be sorted from the believers and ordered to distance themselves. This separation is a humiliation and a confirmation of their status as outcasts from Allah’s mercy. The address “O wicked ones” (ayyuhā al-mujrimūn) labels them by their identity: they are criminals. Their crime is not merely a minor offense; it is the major crime of disbelief, associating partners with Allah, rejecting His messengers, and leading a life of transgression.

This command to separate is a public display of their disgrace. In the world, they mingled with the believers, perhaps even mocking them, but on this Day, the believers are honoured and they are shunned.

The verse also indicates that there is no place for the wicked near the righteous. The communities that were once mixed are now divided for eternity. This separation begins at the moment of judgment and continues into their respective final abodes. The command is a powerful warning that association with believers in this life is a blessing that will be withdrawn from those who reject faith. It also comforts the believers who were ridiculed and marginalized; they will see their mockers publicly shamed and separated from them, while they are received with honour. The verse initiates the direct speech that will be addressed to the disbelievers in the following verses.

=60=

After the command to separate, Allah directly addresses the disbelieving humans as “O Children of Adam.” This address ties the warning back to the very beginning of creation, to the original enmity between Adam, peace be upon him, and Satan. Allah reminds them of a command that was embedded in the human conscience and conveyed through all the prophets: “Did I not command you not to follow Satan?” This question is a form of rebuke that carries the weight of undeniable truth. It reminds them that they were not left without guidance; the command was clear. The description of Satan as “your sworn enemy” (ʿaduwwun mubīn) is crucial. An enemy who is open and clear in his hostility should be fought against, not befriended or obeyed.

Yet humanity repeatedly fell for Satan’s deceptions, treating him as a hidden advisor when he is in fact the most obvious enemy. This verse explains the root cause of the disbelievers’ ruin: they followed Satan’s whispers and footsteps instead of the divine command.

The question format leaves them speechless; they have no excuse. They cannot say they were not warned, because the warning was given through the primordial covenant and through the succession of messengers. For the living, this verse is a stark reminder to examine one’s own life: are we, knowingly or unknowingly, following the path of an enemy who desires our eternal destruction? The only safe course is to adhere strictly to Allah’s command and to recognize Satan’s enmity in every temptation.
