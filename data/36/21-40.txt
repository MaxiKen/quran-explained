=21=

The believing man now gives his people a clear and practical instruction. He tells them to follow the messengers he is defending, and he highlights two compelling qualities that should make them attractive to any reasonable person.

The first quality is that these messengers “ask no reward of you.” They do not seek any financial payment, material gain, or social status in return for their preaching. Their call is entirely free of worldly self-interest.

This is a powerful proof of their sincerity, because a person who invents a religion or a political movement typically wants followers for power, wealth, or influence. The messengers, however, had nothing to gain from the people; they endured rejection and threats while asking for nothing. This same quality was evident in the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, who never accepted payment for his message and lived a life of simplicity and sacrifice.

The second quality is that they are “rightly guided” (muhtadūn). This means they are not lost or wandering in error; they walk upon the Straight Path that leads to Allah and His pleasure. They are not merely good-intentioned people but divinely guided prophets whose own lives reflect the truth they preach. By linking sincerity with divine guidance, the believing man provides a complete argument: those who ask for nothing and are manifestly guided by God deserve to be followed. For the Quraysh hearing this parable, the lesson was unmistakable. Muhammad, peace be upon him, was known to them as al-Amīn, the trustworthy, and he never asked for a wage. His life, character, and message all testified that he was upon guidance. So the verse invites everyone to evaluate the callers to faith by these two standards: do they seek our wealth, and are they themselves following the truth? If the answer is no to the first and yes to the second, then the rational response is to follow them. The believer’s counsel here is as urgent as ever, for humanity continues to be surrounded by leaders and ideologies that demand sacrifice from followers while offering no divine guidance.

=22=

After calling his people to follow the messengers, the believing man now explains his own personal conviction in the form of a logical question. “Why should I not worship the One Who has originated me?” The verb “originated” (faṭaranī) refers to the act of bringing something into existence from nothing, shaping it with purpose and precision. He is pointing to the very fact of his own creation as the ultimate reason for worship. Every human being knows they did not create themselves; their existence is owed to a Creator. Reason dictates that the one who gives life and form deserves gratitude, obedience, and devotion. To worship anything else would be an injustice to the Creator. He then immediately connects the beginning of life to its end: “and to Whom you will be returned. ” This clause brings in the reality of the Hereafter, which was the central doctrine the messengers were calling to. If people will be returned to their Lord for judgment, then worshipping Him is not only a matter of gratitude but also an urgent necessity. The shift from “me” to “you” is subtle but powerful. In the first part he speaks of his own origin, and in the second he reminds his people that they too will return to God. He is telling them that his decision to worship the Creator is the only sensible path, and that their own eventual return should make them reconsider their rejection. This verse is a concise summary of pure monotheism rooted in creation and accountability. It echoes the Quranic argument that those who reflect on the heavens, the earth, and their own souls will inevitably recognize the Creator’s right to be worshipped. For the contemporary reader, this verse offers a refreshingly direct way to think about faith: since we did not bring ourselves into being, and since we will one day face our Maker, why would we refuse to worship Him alone?

=23=

The believing man continues his logical exposition by rejecting idolatry in the strongest terms. He asks rhetorically, “How could I take besides Him other gods?

” The question implies that such an action would be utterly irrational and self-destructive. He then outlines two fatal flaws in the idolatrous belief system. First, the so-called gods “whose intercession would not be of any benefit to me” are completely powerless. The Meccans and many polytheists throughout history believed that their idols could intercede with the supreme God and bring them closer to Him. The Quran repeatedly refutes this, asserting that intercession belongs entirely to Allah, and no one can intercede except with His permission and for those with whom He is pleased. An idol that is deaf, dumb, and lifeless cannot help anyone. Second, those false gods “nor could they save me if the Most Compassionate intended to harm me” are utterly incapable of providing protection or rescue. If Allah wills to bring a calamity, illness, or punishment, no idol, saint, or angel can avert it. Only the Almighty Himself can remove harm.

The use of the divine name “the Most Compassionate” (ar-Raḥmān) here is striking. It reminds the listener that the true God is not a distant, wrathful being but the source of all mercy. Turning away from the Merciful Lord to lifeless objects is both folly and ingratitude. The believer’s argument demolishes the entire structure of polytheism by exposing its practical uselessness in the moments that matter most: when one needs intercession and when one needs rescue. His words teach that pure monotheism is not merely a theological preference but the only worldview that makes sense in the face of human vulnerability and need.

=24=

This short verse is the logical conclusion to the previous one. The believing man states that if he were to take other gods besides Allah, he would be “clearly astray” (ḍalālin mubīn).

The word “astray” here means far removed from the truth, lost in error with no excuse. “Clearly” indicates that such misguidance would be manifest and undeniable to any sound mind. He is not saying that he might be slightly mistaken; he is affirming that idolatry, after having known the truth, would place him in an obvious and profound error.

This is a declaration of unwavering certainty. The believing man has examined the evidence of creation, the sincerity of the messengers, the powerlessness of the false gods, and the certainty of return to the Lord, and he has reached a firm conviction. He can see the Straight Path, and he knows that deviating from it would be a catastrophic loss. In a society that normalizes idolatry, such a confession requires immense courage. He is essentially telling his people that their way of life is clearly misguided, while he is upon obvious truth.

This verse teaches the importance of drawing a clear line between truth and falsehood. Faith should not be a matter of vague feelings but of clear-sighted certainty that distinguishes right from wrong without ambiguity. The believer’s courage is rooted in this clarity; when one knows that a certain course of action leads to manifest ruin, one avoids it at all costs.

For those who hear the Quran’s call, the verse poses an honest question: after all the proofs have been laid out, would not choosing a path other than God’s guidance be a clear, self-destructive straying?

=25=

With all his reasoning laid out, the believing man now makes an open and bold declaration of faith. He says, “I do believe in your Lord,” directly addressing his people. Notice that he does not say “my Lord” in a way that separates himself from them, but “your Lord,” which carries a profound rhetorical purpose. By saying “your Lord,” he reminds them that the God he believes in is not a foreign deity or an unknown being; He is their own Lord as well. The Creator who originated them, sustains them, and to whom they will return is the very God they should be worshipping. He is inviting them to recognize the Lord who already owns them, rather than asking them to accept something alien. Then he adds, “so listen to me.

” This is not an arrogant command but a heartfelt plea. He knows his life is in danger, and he has rushed to speak before it is too late. He wants them to pay attention, not to his personal status, but to the truth he is conveying. He is willing to be heard, even if it costs him his life. This verse demonstrates the highest form of sincere concern for others. The believing man does not keep his faith private; he risks everything to share it, hoping to save his people from destruction.

The sequence of his speech, ending with this public testimony, is a masterclass in prophetic-style preaching: he first removed obstacles by pointing to the messengers’ sincerity, then reasoned with the intellect using creation and accountability, then demolished false beliefs, and finally called them to listen to the truth. His example remains a timeless inspiration for believers who live among those who reject faith, showing that love for one’s people means calling them to their true Lord, even in the face of hostility.

=26=

The narrative takes a sudden and tragic turn, but immediately transforms into a scene of triumph. The people, enraged by his bold testimony and his defense of the messengers, martyred him. The Quran does not detail the manner of his killing, because the focus is not on the brutality of the disbelievers but on the honour Allah bestowed upon him instantly after his death.

The phrase “he was told” refers to the angels conveying Allah’s greeting as his soul departs: “Enter Paradise! ” There is no waiting, no intermediate state of punishment or delay; the gates of eternal bliss are opened for him at once. This is the immense reward for those who give their lives in the path of truth.

Yet even in that moment of supreme personal joy, his first thought is not about his own comfort but about his people who killed him. He says, “If only my people knew. ” His words are not a curse or a cry for revenge; they express a wish that his people could see what he is witnessing. He longs for them to know the forgiveness and honour that await those who believe, so that they too might be saved. This selfless compassion reflects the highest character of a true believer. Even death at the hands of his own people does not extinguish his love for them. The verse is also a powerful response to anyone who thinks that dying for faith is a loss; in reality, it is an immediate gateway to unimaginable reward. The believer’s wish highlights the profound gulf between the perception of the disbelievers and the unseen reality of the Hereafter. For the Prophet Muhammad and his followers who faced persecution in Makkah, this story offered deep consolation: the martyr is not defeated but instantly honoured, and his concern for his people remains alive in the presence of his Lord.

=27=

The believing man completes his wish by specifying what he longs for his people to know. He wants them to see “how my Lord has forgiven me.” This forgiveness means that all his previous sins, if any, have been completely erased by Allah’s mercy, and he now stands pure and unburdened.

The emphasis on “my Lord” is deeply personal; he is boasting, not in himself, but in the generosity of his Master. He also wishes they could see that Allah has “made me one of the honourable” (al-mukramīn). This honour is the highest form of divine hospitality, which includes a lofty station in Paradise, respectful treatment from the angels, and the pleasure of Allah. The contrast is staggering: his people dishonoured him, tortured him, and killed him, yet Allah has granted him true and eternal honour. This demonstrates that human disrespect and persecution mean nothing when compared to the honour that comes from God.

The verse also reveals the psychology of the believer who faces death for his faith. He does not feel that he is losing anything; he feels that he is gaining everything. His concern is entirely directed toward his people who remain behind, still living in the deception of this world. This teaches that those who are certain of the Hereafter do not cling to this life, nor do they harbor bitterness against their oppressors, because they know the ultimate justice of Allah. The believing man’s statement is preserved in the Quran for all time, so in a sense his wish was granted: countless people across centuries have come to know of his forgiveness and honour, and his story has guided many to faith. His death was not the end; it was the beginning of an enduring legacy.

=28=

After the martyrdom of the believing man, Allah describes how He dealt with the people of the town. The verse states that no heavenly army, no angels in ranks descending from the sky, was sent to destroy them.

This statement is significant because it demolishes the arrogant assumption that the punishment of God requires a dramatic, overwhelming military force. The Quraysh often challenged the Prophet to bring down the punishment, imagining it would involve visible armies of angels or stones from the sky. Allah declares that He had no need to mobilize such forces.

The phrase “nor did We need to” underscores the absolute ease with which divine punishment can be executed. Allah’s power is not dependent on means; He does not require soldiers, weapons, or preparation. The townspeople who seemed powerful and secure were completely vulnerable.

This verse also contains a subtle element of mercy and justice. By not sending an army of angels, Allah shows that the punishment is not a retaliation on a personal scale; it is a cosmic decree that needs no theatrics. It is swift and final, leaving no room for negotiation or escape. For the believers who were oppressed, the verse reassures them that their Lord does not need human help or angelic legions to establish justice; He can avenge His righteous servants in a single instant.

This is a profound source of comfort for anyone suffering injustice, teaching that patience is not weakness but trust in the One whose power is absolute and whose timing is perfect.

=29=

Allah now reveals the exact nature of the punishment that wiped out the town. It was merely “one mighty blast” (ṣayḥatan wāḥidah).

The word ṣayḥah refers to a loud, terrifying cry or shout, often associated with the angel’s trumpet or a divine command that shatters life. This single blast was enough to render them “extinguished at once” (khāmidūn).

The Arabic verb khamada refers to a fire being completely put out, leaving only cold ashes. The imagery is vivid: the people who were burning with arrogance, activity, and plots against the messengers were suddenly reduced to silence and stillness, like a fire that has been extinguished. There were no survivors to mourn, no resistance, no lingering struggle. The blast came, and everything was over. This sudden destruction teaches that divine punishment does not follow human schedules or warnings that can be negotiated. When it comes, it is swift and final. It also highlights the fragility of human power. The town that dared to threaten and kill the messengers could not withstand a single divine command. The verse is a direct warning to the Makkans who mocked the Prophet and demanded the punishment. Allah is telling them that if He wills, their destruction is equally easy.

Yet, in His mercy, He delayed it to give them a chance to repent. The story of this town serves as a mirror, showing the Quraysh their own potential fate if they persist in their arrogance. For all readers, the verse is a reminder not to be deceived by the apparent security of a life lived in defiance of God; the end can come at any moment, and no one can resist it.

=30=

After recounting the destruction, Allah expresses a lament over the condition of such servants. The exclamation “Oh pity” translates “yā ḥasratan,” a word that conveys deep regret and sorrow.

This is not the regret of a human who has lost something, but a divine expression intended to emphasize the tragedy of human stubbornness. The phrase “such beings” refers to those who consistently reject God’s messengers. Their tragedy is that “no messenger ever came to them without being mocked.

” This is a pattern that spans human history. From Noah to Moses to Jesus to Muhammad, peace be upon them all, every prophet faced mockery from his people. The mockery is not a reflection on the messengers but on the mockers, whose hearts are so blinded by arrogance and love of worldly life that they cannot take the truth seriously. They ridicule what they do not understand, and in doing so, they seal their own doom. The verse is a profound consolation for the Prophet Muhammad, who was called a poet, a madman, and a sorcerer. It tells him that he is walking a path well-trodden by the noblest of human beings, and that the mockery he faces is a sign of his truthfulness, not an indication of failure. At the same time, the verse is a stark warning to those who mock: they are placing themselves in the category of those over whom divine pity is expressed, a pity that will turn into severe punishment if they do not change. The lesson for all generations is to take the call of faith seriously and never treat messengers of truth as objects of ridicule, for in doing so, one mocks the One who sent them.

=31=

Now the surah turns to directly address the Meccan deniers, drawing their attention to history. The question “Have the deniers not considered” challenges them to use their reason and observation. The “peoples We destroyed before them” refers to the powerful nations of the past, such as the people of Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, and the Pharaoh’s host. These were civilizations far stronger and more established than the Quraysh. “Who never came back to life again” in this context primarily means that they did not return to their worldly life and power. Their cities lie in ruins, their wealth has vanished, and no one from among them has ever come back to rebuild their dominion.

This is a clear sign that worldly might does not guarantee permanence. The verse implicitly refutes the materialist mindset that sees only the present world. It asks the Quraysh to travel and look at the ruins they pass on their trade routes, to see the fate of those who denied the truth.

However, the phrase also carries a deeper significance concerning the denial of resurrection. The Meccans used to say, “When we are dead and become dust, will we really be raised again?” Here, the verse acknowledges that indeed these destroyed nations have not come back to this life, but that is because a greater return is yet to happen—the return to Allah on the Day of Judgment.

The next verse immediately clarifies this. The historical reflection is meant to shake them out of their heedlessness and prepare their hearts for the truth.

=32=

This short verse completes the thought of the previous one and strikes at the heart of the disbelievers’ error. The destroyed nations did not return to the life of this world, but “they will all be brought before Us.

” The word “all” is comprehensive, including not only the past generations but also the Quraysh and all humanity until the end of time. The phrase “brought before Us” means gathered for the ultimate judgment, standing before their Lord with no escape. This verse corrects a common misconception: that death is the final end. The disbelievers assumed that since the destroyed nations had vanished without returning, there was no afterlife. Allah responds that their failure to return to this world is not because they have been annihilated into nothingness; they are in the intermediate realm of the grave (barzakh), and they will certainly be resurrected and presented for accountability. For the Quraysh, this was a thunderous warning. They felt secure in their trade, their social status, and their idols. They mocked the idea of resurrection. This verse tells them that the very nations they considered gone forever are in fact awaiting the same gathering that they themselves will face. The verse connects the historical signs in the earth with the reality of the Hereafter, reinforcing the central theme of the surah. It also provides a motivation for believers: the apparent triumph of disbelievers is temporary; they will all stand before the Just Judge, and on that day the truth will be made plain.

Thus, the reflections on history are not meant for mere storytelling but to awaken a living consciousness of the ultimate return.

=33=

With this verse, the surah transitions into a powerful series of signs in the natural world that prove Allah’s power to resurrect. The “sign” (āyah) is in the “dead earth”—barren, lifeless soil that seems utterly incapable of producing anything. The Meccans saw this with their own eyes in the desert landscape. Then Allah says, “We give it life,” referring to the transformative miracle of rain that causes seeds to sprout and vegetation to burst forth.

This is a direct parallel to resurrection: just as the earth is dead and then brought to life, the human body that has decayed into dust can be brought back to life by the same divine power. The specific mention of “producing grain from it for them to eat” shifts the focus from abstract miracle to personal benefit. The grain that sustains their daily life—the bread they eat—comes from this divinely orchestrated revival of the earth. The sign is not distant or theoretical; it is right before them at every meal. If they can accept that Allah revives the dead earth every season, why do they find it strange that He can revive the dead on the Day of Judgment? The verse subtly shames the disbelievers for enjoying the fruits of this annual resurrection while denying the greater resurrection. It also cultivates gratitude in the believer, who is reminded that every morsel of food is a sign of God’s mercy and power. This method of reasoning from the observable to the unseen is a hallmark of the Quran, appealing to both the intellect and the heart.

=34=

The description of the earth’s revival continues with more specific blessings. After mentioning grain as a staple, Allah draws attention to “gardens of palm trees and grapevines.” These represent high-quality, delightful provisions. Dates and grapes were the most cherished fruits in the Arabian environment, providing not only nutrition but also pleasure and economic benefit.

The mention of “springs to gush forth” highlights the source of this life: fresh water that Allah causes to burst from the ground. Without water, the earth remains dead, no matter how rich the soil. The gushing springs are signs of a deliberate, caring design. The water flows to nourish the trees, and the trees in turn provide fruit for human beings.

This is not a random natural process but a carefully arranged cycle of mercy. The verse invites reflection on the fact that human beings do not control the rain, the springs, or the growth of these gardens; Allah alone manages them. The Quraysh, who prided themselves on their agricultural oases like Ta’if with its vineyards, knew full well that their efforts alone could not produce a single grape if the water and the growth were withheld.

Thus, the sign is twofold: it demonstrates the power to give life, and it demonstrates the mercy that provides for human needs and delights. This combination of power and mercy is central to the Quranic argument for tawḥīd and resurrection. The natural world becomes a book of signs for those who look with the eyes of faith.

=35=

This verse states the purpose of the gardens and the fruits: they are there so that people may eat. The phrase “which they had no hand in making” (mā ʿamilathu aydīhim) is particularly striking. Human beings plant seeds, tend the soil, and irrigate the land, but they do not create the fruit. The actual life within the seed, the transformation of soil and water into a sweet date or a juicy grape, is entirely beyond human ability. Human labour is only a secondary cause; the primary cause is the creative act of Allah. This humbles human pride and reminds people that their food is ultimately a gift, not a purely human product. Then comes the penetrating question: “Will they not then give thanks?” Gratitude (shukr) is the proper response to recognizing that all blessings come from God. Lack of gratitude is a sign of arrogance and a root cause of disbelief. The disbelievers enjoy Allah’s provisions yet refuse to worship Him alone, which is the height of ingratitude. The verse therefore links the physical act of eating to the spiritual duty of thankfulness. True thankfulness is not just saying “alhamdulillah” with the tongue; it is to use the energy gained from food in obedience to the Provider, and to direct worship only to Him. The sign of the earth and its fruits becomes a moral test. Will the human being merely consume like an animal, without reflection, or will he recognize the Giver and submit to Him?

For the believer, this verse transforms the everyday act of eating into an act of worship and a reminder of the ultimate resurrection, when they hope to eat from the fruits of Paradise.

=36=

After detailing the earth’s produce, the surah lifts the gaze to a broader principle of creation: everything is created in pairs. This verse begins with “Glory be,” declaring Allah’s absolute perfection and transcendence above any deficiency or partner. The fact that He created all things in pairs is a profound sign of His wisdom and oneness. The verse lists three categories. First, “what the earth produces” refers to the plant kingdom, where male and female elements exist even in vegetation, enabling reproduction and the production of fruit. Second, “their genders” refers to human beings and animals, where the pairing of male and female is immediately obvious. This pairing is not only for reproduction but also for companionship, love, and the completion of life’s functions. Third, “what they do not know” extends the principle to realms of creation that were beyond the knowledge of the seventh-century Arabs, and indeed some of which are still being discovered. Modern science has revealed pairing at the atomic level, with positive and negative charges, and the pairing of matter with antimatter. The verse is not a scientific textbook, but it hints at a universal law of complementarity that points to a single, wise Creator.

The pairing of things refutes the polytheistic idea of a multiplicity of independent gods, because a perfectly complementary system requires a single will to design and harmonise it. It also demonstrates the Creator’s power to bring life out of combinations, which supports the possibility of resurrection. The call to glorify Allah here inspires awe; the more one learns about the paired nature of the universe, the more one recognizes the majesty of its Maker.

=37=

The surah now moves from the sign in the earth to the sign in the heavens, beginning with the night. The verse describes a daily miracle that people often take for granted. “We strip from it daylight” uses a vivid metaphor: daylight is like a covering that is gradually peeled away, leaving the darkness of the night. The verb “strip” (naslakhu) implies a slow, inevitable, and silent removal. Before one realizes it, the bright day has disappeared, and darkness has enveloped the world. “Then —behold!—they are in darkness” creates a sense of sudden awareness. The transition from day to night is so smooth that people go about their business until suddenly they find themselves surrounded by darkness.

This is a sign of Allah’s absolute control over time and light. No human power can hold back the night or extend the day by a single moment. The regularity of this alternation is a mercy, providing a cycle of activity and rest. The night, with its darkness, forces human beings to pause and recognize their vulnerability. It is also a reminder of the darkness of the grave and the darkness that would cover the soul without the light of revelation. For the Meccans who denied the resurrection, the daily death of the day and its rebirth the next morning was a clear sign that the One who can strip away the light and bring it back can also take away life and restore it. The verse invites contemplation of the ordinary in order to perceive the extraordinary power behind it.

=38=

From the general phenomenon of night and day, the surah focuses specifically on the sun. The statement “The sun travels” (ash-shamsu tajrī) indicates that the sun is in motion, not stationary as some ancient civilizations believed. It moves in a precise, calculated course. “For its fixed term” (li-mustaqarrin lahā) has been interpreted in two primary ways. One is that the sun runs toward an appointed end point, a final resting place where its motion will cease at the end of the world. The other is that it travels a fixed, predetermined orbit or course for a specified period of time, as part of its daily and annual cycles. Both interpretations emphasize that the sun’s movement is not random or eternal; it is subject to the decree of its Creator.

The verse then adds, “That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing. ” The sun’s precise orbit, which provides exactly the right amount of heat and light to sustain life on earth, is the work of the Almighty (al-ʿAzīz), whose power is irresistible and who set the sun on its path with authority. It is also the design of the All-Knowing (al-ʿAlīm), whose knowledge encompasses every detail of the cosmic system, ensuring that the sun’s motion serves a greater purpose and does not deviate. The verse refutes sun-worshippers who divinized the sun; it declares that the sun is a created entity, obeying the command of its Lord. For the deniers of resurrection, the sun’s obedience to its fixed term is a sign that the universe is governed by a command, and that same command can just as easily bring about the resurrection.

=39=

After mentioning the sun, the surah turns to the moon, describing its divinely appointed phases. “We have ordained precise phases for it” (qaddarnāhu manāzil) refers to the lunar mansions or stations that the moon passes through night after night, causing its visible shape to change from a thin crescent to a full moon and back again. These phases are perfectly measured, allowing people to calculate time, months, and seasons. The lunar calendar, which governs Islamic worship including Ramadan and Hajj, is a direct result of this precise ordainment. The description “until it ends up like an old, curved palm stalk” (al-ʿurjūn al-qadīm) is a magnificent metaphor drawn from the Arab environment. After a date cluster has been harvested, the remaining stalk dries, becomes yellow, curves, and withers. The waning crescent moon in its final nights resembles that old, bent palm stalk in shape, colour, and frailty.

This image conveys the cycle of the moon’s life: it is born as a crescent, grows to fullness, then withers and fades away, only to be renewed again. This cycle of renewal is another sign of resurrection. If the moon can reappear after it has withered to a thin curve, then the human being can also be brought back to life after death. The precise phases are a manifestation of Allah’s perfect wisdom; they are not random but ordained for human benefit. The verse encourages the believer to see the moon not just as a celestial object but as a reminder of the Creator’s meticulous planning and the certainty of the appointed term for all things.

=40=

This verse beautifully concludes the description of the celestial bodies by highlighting the perfect order and absence of conflict in the universe. “It is not for the sun to catch up with the moon” means that the sun cannot overtake the moon to eliminate the night or to encroach upon the moon’s distinct dominion. Their functions are separate and complementary. The sun, with its blazing light, rules the day, while the moon, with its gentle reflected light, governs the night and the calendar. “Nor does the night outrun the day” means that the night cannot prematurely overtake the day or extend beyond its appointed period. The alternation of night and day is so precise that neither transgresses its boundary.

The final statement, “Each is travelling in an orbit of their own” (kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūn), is a profound declaration of cosmic order. The word “falak” refers to a rounded course or orbit, and “yasbaḥūn” means they are swimming or gliding smoothly. The sun, the moon, the night, and the day are all moving in paths ordained by Allah, without collision, without deviation, and without the need for human intervention. This cosmic harmony is a sign that the universe has a single, All-Powerful Controller. If there were multiple gods, there would be chaos and competition. The orderliness thus testifies to the oneness of Allah. It also shows that just as Allah has set precise laws for the celestial bodies, He has set a moral law for humanity and a fixed term for every soul, after which will come the judgment. The believer who contemplates the sky can feel at peace, knowing that the same God who keeps the stars in their orbits holds the affairs of their life in perfect wisdom.
