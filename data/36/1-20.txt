=1=

The surah opens with these two Arabic letters, Yâ’ and Sĩn, which belong to the category of the disjointed letters that appear at the beginning of a number of Quranic chapters. Their precise meaning is known fully only to Allah, yet scholars have drawn several important lessons from them. One widespread understanding is that these letters serve as a dramatic wake-up call, grabbing the attention of the Arab listeners who were masters of the language. When they heard these individual letters recited, they would fall silent, and then the powerful message would follow. The letters are composed of the very alphabet they used daily, yet they could not produce anything like the Quran. This immediately challenges anyone who doubts the divine origin of the Book: here are the letters you know, so if you claim the Quran is human speech, then bring something like it. This interpretation makes the disjointed letters a miraculous challenge. Some early commentators also noted that “Yâ-Sĩn” is one of the names of the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, meaning “O human being” or “O master,” as a term of endearment. Others related it to the phrase “Yâ insân” in certain dialects.

However, the majority of reliable scholars view it primarily as part of the Quran’s miraculous structure, a sign that this revelation cannot be matched. The Meccan context of the surah is vital here; the Quraysh were denying the prophethood and the Quran. Starting with mysterious letters followed by an oath on the wise Quran immediately undermines their claim that the Prophet was a poet or a madman. It forces the listener to reflect: how can an unlettered man bring forth a book of such depth that it stuns the eloquent Arabs, and it is made of the very letters they use?

Thus, the opening is not merely a set of symbols; it is a profound statement about the source and authority of what follows.

=2=

Allah takes an oath by the Quran, describing it as “rich in wisdom,” which translates the Arabic word Ḥakīm. An oath in the Quran is a means of drawing solemn attention to the thing sworn by, as well as to the statement that comes after it. The Quran is called Ḥakīm for several complementary reasons. It means that the Quran is full of profound wisdom, containing guidance that directs human beings to what is right in belief, conduct, and society. It also means that the Quran is firmly constructed and free from any flaw, contradiction, or inconsistency—it judges between truth and falsehood with perfect precision.

Moreover, Ḥakīm can imply that the Quran is the source of wise judgment, teaching its followers how to distinguish right from wrong and how to live in a way that leads to success in this life and the Hereafter. By swearing by the wise Quran, Allah prepares the listener to accept the following declaration about the Prophet’s messengership. The oath also functions as a response to the Meccan disbelievers who dismissed the Quran as poetry, magic, or ancient fables. Swearing by something in this manner was a recognised Arabic rhetorical device that conveyed the speaker’s absolute seriousness and confidence in the truth of what was being said. The choice of the Quran as the object of the oath highlights its supreme status. It is not an ordinary book; it is the speech of the All-Wise, and its wisdom is evident to those who approach it with an open heart. This verse, therefore, establishes the Quran as the ultimate criterion and alerts the listener that what follows is grounded in unshakeable truth.

=3=

Here the object of the oath in the previous verse is revealed. The solemn statement is a direct address to the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, affirming with the utmost emphasis that he is indeed one of the messengers sent by Allah.

The use of “truly” captures the Arabic particle inna, which conveys certainty and removes any doubt. This verse came in response to the polytheists of Makkah who denied his prophethood, calling him a poet, a soothsayer, or a man possessed. By linking this affirmation to the oath on the wise Quran, Allah is declaring that the Quran itself is the decisive proof of his messengership. The content, the wisdom, and the miraculous nature of the Quran all testify that the one who brought it cannot be an impostor.

Furthermore, the phrase “one of the messengers” reminds both the Prophet and his audience that he is not a solitary figure making an unprecedented claim; he stands within a long line of chosen men who received revelation and were sent to guide their peoples. This connection to the chain of prophethood was important because the Arabs had some historical awareness of earlier prophets, and it countered the accusation that Muhammad, peace be upon him, had invented something new.

The verse also provides comfort and reassurance to the Prophet himself, who faced relentless rejection. Allah himself testifies that he is truly a messenger, which is the greatest honour and the strongest source of strength in the face of hostility.

=4=

This phrase continues the description of the Prophet’s status. He is not only a messenger, but he is firmly established “upon the Straight Path” (ʿalā ṣirāṭin mustaqīm). This path is the clear, direct way of monotheism, justice, and righteousness that leads to Allah’s pleasure and eternal salvation. The wording indicates that the Prophet himself is guided and upright in his own person, in his conduct, and in the message he delivers. This refutes any claim that he was misguided or teaching error. The Straight Path is the same path that all the prophets called to: the worship of Allah alone without partners, the belief in the unseen, and the observance of moral and spiritual duties. It is the path mentioned in Sūrah al-Fātiḥah, for which every believer prays many times a day. By describing the Prophet as being upon this path, Allah is telling the disbelievers that rejecting him is tantamount to rejecting the path of guidance itself. For the believers, it is a reminder to follow the Prophet’s example because he is walking on the most certain and correct way.

The phrase also connects to the nature of the Quran revealed to him, which is a book of guidance that maps out that very same Straight Path for all who wish to follow it. Therefore, the messenger, the message, and the way of life are all perfectly aligned in truth.

=5=

This verse explains the source of the message the Prophet brings. It is a revelation (tanzīl) sent down from the Almighty (al-ʿAzīz), the Most Merciful (ar-Raḥīm).

The word “revelation” implies that the Quran is not the Prophet’s own words or ideas; it is divinely sent down in stages, originating from the highest authority. The pairing of the two divine names, the Almighty and the Most Merciful, carries deep significance. “The Almighty” stresses Allah’s irresistible power, honour, and supremacy. This assures the Prophet that the One who sent the revelation is fully able to protect him, defeat his enemies, and establish the truth. “The Most Merciful” highlights that this revelation is primarily an act of immense mercy. The Quran was not sent to destroy people arbitrarily but to guide them out of darkness into light, to warn them of what harms them, and to give them glad tidings of forgiveness and reward. The Meccan polytheists acknowledged Allah as the Creator but associated partners with Him, and some of them even claimed it was unfitting for Allah to send a human messenger. By affirming that the revelation is from the Almighty, Most Merciful, the verse overturns their false assumptions: the Almighty does whatever He wills, and His mercy is precisely why He sends guidance. This combination of might and mercy gives the believer both awe and hope.

Thus, rejecting this revelation is not merely refusing a man’s advice; it is turning away from the powerful and merciful Lord.

=6=

The purpose of the revelation is now stated: the Prophet is sent to warn the Arabs. The description “a people whose forefathers were not warned” refers to the long interval (fatrah) between the time of Prophet Jesus, peace be upon him, and the advent of Prophet Muhammad. For centuries, the Arabs had not received a messenger with a fresh scripture. They had lost the pure teachings of Ishmael and Abraham and had fallen into idolatry, superstition, and tribal vendettas. Their immediate forefathers lived and died without a warner, so over generations, they became heedless (ghāfilūn) of the truth about God, the resurrection, and accountability. This historical context shows that the sending of the Prophet was an extraordinary act of mercy after a prolonged period of spiritual neglect.

The verse also contains an important logical point: being heedless due to lack of warning does not mean they are innocent if they then reject the clear warning when it arrives. Their heedlessness was the very condition that necessitated a warner. Now that the warner has come with clear proofs, they have no excuse. This also answers those who might ask why Allah would punish a people without sending a messenger; the messenger has now been sent, and his warning is clear. The verse thus establishes the Prophet’s mission as a revival of the Abrahamic faith and a final call before accountability.

=7=

This verse delivers a severe verdict concerning the majority of the Meccan disbelievers. The “decree” refers to the word of punishment that has become binding upon them.

This is because Allah, with His perfect knowledge, knows that they will persist in their disbelief and arrogance, refusing to believe even after the clear warning and signs. The phrase “has already been justified” translates the Arabic ḥaqqa, meaning it has been established as true and necessary. This does not imply that they were forced to disbelieve; rather, it is a statement of divine foreknowledge and the consequence of their own freely chosen path. When a person continuously rejects the truth with full awareness and deliberate stubbornness, a point is reached where the heart becomes sealed and the decree of punishment becomes inevitable.

However, the verse says “against most of them,” not all. This indicates that a minority among them would eventually accept faith, and indeed many of the early Muslims were from the Quraysh. This nuance is important because it shows that the door to guidance remains open for those whose hearts are not hardened. The verse serves as a sobering warning to the listener: do not be among those upon whom the decree of torment is justified. It also comforts the Prophet, explaining that the stubborn rejection of many is not due to a deficiency in his message, but due to a decree that follows their own arrogance.

=8=

This verse presents a powerful metaphor for the spiritual state of the disbelievers. They are likened to people with iron shackles encasing their necks, extending from the chin upwards so that their heads are forcibly raised and they cannot lower them.

The image is one of complete restriction, humiliation, and an inability to see what is directly in front of them. In a spiritual sense, the shackles represent the chains of their own stubbornness, pride, and sins that have taken hold of their hearts. Because their heads are forced up, they cannot bow in humility to Allah, nor can they look down to reflect upon the signs on the earth. They walk with arrogant upturned faces, blind to the truth that lies at their feet. Some commentators also connect this imagery to the Hereafter, where the disbelievers will be literally shackled, but here the phrasing “it is as if” indicates a parable depicting their current psychological and spiritual imprisonment. Their refusal to submit has ironically enslaved them, making it impossible for them to humble themselves. This metaphor is not about physical coercion but about the natural consequence of persistent rejection; when a person keeps rejecting faith, Allah places a barrier in their perception, making guidance inaccessible. It is both a warning and an explanation for why they do not respond to the Prophet’s call.

=9=

The imagery continues, describing how these disbelievers are completely surrounded and blocked from perceiving the truth. A barrier (saddan) stands in front of them, and a barrier behind them, and they are also covered from above. This triple obstruction means that no matter which direction they turn, they cannot reach or see the light of guidance. The barrier in front prevents them from moving forward toward faith; the barrier behind prevents them from returning to the truth they have abandoned; and the covering over them prevents heavenly light from reaching their hearts. The result is that “they fail to see,” not because their eyes are physically blind, but because their inner sight has been rendered useless.

This is a divinely imposed consequence of their own persistent choice to reject the signs. Allah does not wrong them; they wrong themselves, and then as a just recompense, they are left to wander in confusion. The metaphor also extends to the Day of Judgment, where the disbelievers will find barriers between them and Paradise, but in this world the barriers are spiritual. The lesson for the believer is to fear the state of the heart that leads to such blindness, and to constantly ask Allah for light and guidance. It also clarifies to the Prophet why his sincere efforts do not seem to penetrate their hearts: they have been sealed off by their own doing.

This is not a reason to give up on calling people, for one does not know whom Allah will guide, but it explains the reality of those who have reached a point of no return.

=10=

After depicting their sealed state, Allah plainly tells the Prophet that for these hardened individuals, his warning makes no difference. Their disbelief is so entrenched that no amount of preaching, argument, or miracle will bring them to faith, because the decree has been issued based on Allah’s knowledge that they will never choose belief.

This statement must be understood in context: it applies to those specific obstinate leaders among the Quraysh like Abū Jahl, not to every single Meccan, for we know many later accepted Islam. The verse serves multiple purposes. It consoles the Prophet, freeing him from excessive grief over their rejection, because the outcome is not due to any shortcoming on his part. It is also a warning that there is a limit to the heart’s capacity to accept guidance; if a person continually pushes away the truth, a time comes when the heart is irreversibly sealed. The verse does not encourage abandoning the duty of warning—the command to warn is still in place—but it provides a realistic perspective. A messenger’s task is to deliver the message with clarity, not to force acceptance. This truth is echoed elsewhere in the Quran, such as “You cannot guide whom you love, but Allah guides whom He wills. ” Thus, the verse balances the Prophet’s deep concern for his people with the reality that guidance is ultimately in Allah’s hands.

=11=

In contrast to the previous verses, this verse identifies the audience who will truly benefit from the warning. The “Reminder” is the Quran itself, and those who “follow” it are the ones who not only hear it but also act upon its teachings. Their key quality is that they stand in awe of the Most Compassionate (ar-Raḥmān) “without seeing Him,” meaning they have a profound inner consciousness of Allah’s presence, majesty, and mercy, even though He is hidden from their physical sight.

This is the essence of true faith: a heartfelt fear and reverence of God that is not dependent on visible phenomena but is rooted in an awareness of the unseen. Because they possess this reverent fear, their hearts are receptive to warning and eager to obey.

The command then shifts to positive encouragement: “So give them good news of forgiveness and an honourable reward.” The Prophet is not only a warner but also a bearer of glad tidings. Forgiveness (maghfirah) means that their sins will be completely erased, and the generous reward (ajrun karīm) refers to the delights of Paradise that are honourable and eternal. This verse beautifully balances warning with hope, and it teaches that the true followers are those who combine study of the Quran with a living consciousness of their Merciful Lord. It also clarifies that effective preaching depends on the spiritual receptivity of the listener, not merely on the eloquence of the preacher.

=12=

This verse makes a definitive statement about resurrection and divine record-keeping, which were central points of contention for the Meccans. “It is certainly We Who resurrect the dead” uses the emphatic “certainly” (innā) to counter their denial of life after death. By presenting resurrection as an act of Allah, the verse links it to His power and will. Then it moves to the recording of deeds: “what they send forth” refers to the good and bad actions that people perform during their lives and send ahead to the Hereafter. “What they leave behind” includes the lasting effects of their deeds, such as beneficial knowledge they taught, charitable endowments they established, or even the evil consequences of their misdeeds that continue after their death. Everything is meticulously “listed” in a “perfect Record,” which is the Preserved Tablet (al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ), a comprehensive register that contains the decree of all things. This recording is not limited to major events; every thought, word, and action is accounted for.

The mention of this record serves as a powerful motivation for mindfulness. If a person truly believes that every deed is being written and will be presented, they will be more careful. It also reassures the believers that no good deed is lost and no injustice will go unpunished. The connection to the surrounding verses is clear: after telling the Prophet who will benefit from his warning, Allah reminds him that the resurrection and judgment are certain and that all human actions are preserved, reinforcing the urgency of his message.

=13=

Now the surah moves to a historical parable designed to mirror the situation in Makkah. The Prophet is commanded to set forth an example (mathalan) for his people.

This is the story of a town—widely understood by commentators to be Antioch, in present-day Turkey—to which Allah sent messengers. The story is not told in exact historical names and dates, because its purpose is not a history lesson but a timeless illustration of the pattern of prophetic rejection and divine punishment. The fact that the Quraysh are asked to consider this example is meant to jolt them into reflection: their own treatment of the Prophet is exactly like the behavior of those who were destroyed.

The sequence of the story will show the sending of messengers, the people’s rejection, the threat of violence, the support of a lone believer from the outskirts, and the final sudden destruction. Every element of the parable resonates with the Prophet’s experience in Makkah. This method of teaching through a narrative example is highly effective because it engages the listener’s imagination and makes the warning less abstract. It also provides a prophetic pattern of patience and trust in Allah’s plan.

=14=

Allah initially sent two messengers to the people of the town. The townspeople rejected them outright, refusing to believe in their message. Rather than destroying them immediately, Allah showed mercy by strengthening the two with a third messenger, increasing the evidence and the force of the call.

This sequence demonstrates a divine principle: Allah gives people every opportunity to accept the truth before punishment descends. The sending of a third messenger after the rejection of the first two is a sign of immense patience and an escalation of the argument against them. The three messengers then gave a unified, unambiguous declaration: “We have indeed been sent to you.” The words “have indeed been sent” (innā ilaykum mursalūn) are emphatic, asserting the certainty of their divine mission. The identity of these messengers is not definitively stated in the Quran or in an authentic hadith, though some narrations from the People of the Book mention that they were disciples of Jesus sent to Antioch.

However, what matters is the lesson: when people persistently reject repeated calls to the truth, they are heading toward a fate like that of the people of this town. The Meccans were now hearing the same message from Prophet Muhammad, after previous prophets had come to humanity, making their rejection all the more blameworthy.

=15=

This verse captures the classic arguments of disbelievers against God’s messengers throughout history. First, they rejected the messengers on the grounds that they were ordinary human beings who ate, drank, and walked in the markets. They believed that if God were to send a messenger, it should be an angel.

This is the same objection the Quraysh raised: “Why has an angel not been sent down to him? ” Second, they made a sweeping theological claim: “the Most Compassionate has not revealed anything.” They denied the very concept of divine revelation, effectively closing the door to any prophetic claim. Their use of the name “the Most Compassionate” is ironic; they acknowledge God’s mercy with their tongues but deny that His mercy entails sending guidance to humanity. Third, they accused the messengers of deliberate falsehood: “You are simply lying. ” This triple rejection—attacking the messenger’s human nature, denying revelation itself, and labeling the messengers liars—reflects a deep-seated arrogance and a refusal to be accountable to a human like themselves. The Quran highlights this mindset to expose its irrationality: why would God leave humanity without guidance, and if He sent angels, how would humans interact with them? The human nature of messengers is in fact a mercy, as they serve as relatable role models.

=16=

Faced with accusations of lying, the messengers did not resort to angry retorts or personal attacks. Instead, they calmly referred the matter to Allah, saying, “Our Lord knows.

” This is the response of true prophets and sincere callers to God: when their truthfulness is questioned, they appeal to the divine knowledge as the ultimate witness. They are not merely claiming to be messengers; they are certain that Allah knows their mission, and this certainty is their strength. The statement implies that even if the people do not believe them, their Lord’s knowledge is sufficient for them, and He will ultimately vindicate the truth. This teaches a profound lesson in dealing with rejection: one’s concern should not be to convince every stubborn opponent but to maintain sincerity before God, who knows the reality of one’s intentions and mission. The phrasing “truly been sent” (la-ilaykum lamursalūn) is again emphatic, underscoring their unshakeable confidence.

This verse also shows the moral high ground of the messengers; they do not descend into the language of abuse but leave judgment to the All-Knowing. For the Prophet Muhammad and his followers, this is a model of patient reliance on Allah when accused of falsehood.

=17=

Here the messengers define the exact scope of their responsibility. Their task is not to compel belief, to punish the rejecters, or to force the truth into people’s hearts. Their sole obligation is to convey the message (al-balāgh) in a clear manner (al-mubīn).

The phrase “clearly” means that they must present the revelation without distortion, ambiguity, or concealment, using plain language and sound arguments, so that no one can claim they did not understand. This principle is central to Islamic theology: the job of prophets, and by extension all who call to Islam, is to deliver the message faithfully. Guidance and acceptance are entirely in Allah’s hands.

This teaching frees the caller from the psychological burden of feeling responsible for the outcome. It also serves as a final witness against those who reject; once the message is delivered clearly, they have no excuse before God. The messengers’ statement also implicitly warns the people that persistence in disbelief after such a clear delivery will bring about severe consequences, but those consequences are from Allah, not from the messengers. The Meccan pagans needed to understand that Muhammad’s sole duty was communication, and their continued rejection did not harm him but spelled their own doom.

=18=

The townspeople’s response escalated from verbal denial to superstitious accusation and physical threats. They claimed that the messengers were a bad omen (taṭayyur), believing that their presence had brought misfortune, division, or calamity. This was a common pagan superstition: if something disliked happened after a person’s arrival, that person was considered an ill omen. The Quraysh said similar things to Prophet Muhammad, blaming him for their hardships. The messengers’ call to abandon idols and reform society threatened the status quo, and the pagans interpreted the resulting social tension as bad luck rather than as a natural consequence of their own wrongdoing. Then they delivered a brutal ultimatum: stop preaching or be stoned to death, followed by a “painful punishment”—possibly torture or humiliating treatment before death. This reflects the typical pattern of tyrannical societies; when they cannot answer the message with reason, they resort to violence and intimidation. The verse highlights the severity of the persecution faced by the messengers, a situation the Prophet Muhammad and his early followers knew well. The threat of stoning and painful punishment reveals the depth of their hatred for the truth, and it sets the stage for the sudden entrance of a courageous believer who would speak up in defense of the messengers.

=19=

The messengers refute the superstition of bad omens with a profound spiritual truth. They declare that any perceived ill fortune is not caused by their presence but “lies within yourselves.” That is, the real source of misfortune is the people’s own disbelief, sins, and moral corruption. Their attachment to idolatry and their rejection of God’s guidance invite divine displeasure and calamity. The messengers then pointedly ask: is your charge of bad omen simply because we reminded you of the truth? This rhetorical question exposes the irrationality of their stance. The reminder (dhikr) is in fact a great good, a lifeline sent by the Merciful to save them, yet they treat it as a curse.

Finally, the messengers label them a “transgressing people” (qawmun musrifūn), meaning they have crossed all boundaries in their arrogance, superstition, and hostility. This response teaches that those who call to Allah should not be intimidated by false accusations of bringing bad luck; rather, they should redirect people’s attention to the real causes of their problems—their own transgression and distance from God. The messengers’ words are firm yet truthful, and they serve as a final warning before the narrative’s dramatic turn.

=20=

Just when the situation seemed hopeless and the messengers faced death threats, a lone supporter emerged. A man came from the farthest part (aqṣā al-madīnah) of the town, which suggests he was from the outskirts, perhaps a poor or ordinary person, not from the elite. He came “rushing” (yasʿā), meaning he hurried with urgency and concern, having heard that the messengers were being threatened. His haste shows his courage and the depth of his faith; he did not wait to see what others would do but immediately ran to speak the truth. His address, “O my people! Follow the messengers,” is simple, direct, and full of sincere care. He does not claim authority or status; he appeals as a fellow citizen who desires their well-being. Commentators identify this man as Ḥabīb an-Najjār, a carpenter who had believed in the message. His intervention is a timeless example of an individual standing up for truth against an entire society, willing to risk his life.

The mention that he came from the farthest end of the city symbolizes that faith sometimes springs from the margins, from those whom society overlooks, not from the powerful elite. His example serves to encourage every believer who feels alone in their environment to speak out and call their people to what is right, for one sincere voice can leave an enduring legacy.
