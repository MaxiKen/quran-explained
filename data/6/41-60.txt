=41=

The verse opens with a decisive “No!” that answers the rhetorical question posed in the previous verse. The Prophet had been instructed to ask the polytheists whom they would call upon in overwhelming distress; this word confirms what they knew deep within themselves. “He is the only One you would call” strips away their pretenses. In moments of extreme fear—a storm at sea, a terminal illness, an earthquake—the human soul instinctively turns to the Supreme Power alone. The idols, the saints, the intermediaries are all forgotten.

This is the fitrah, the primordial nature, asserting itself when the layers of cultural conditioning are peeled away by terror. Then the verse adds, “And if He willed, He could remove the affliction that made you invoke Him.” This underscores Allah’s complete power over the situation. Not only is He the only one called, but He alone can lift the calamity. The false gods have no power to harm or benefit.

The final clause reveals a tragic irony: “Only then will you forget whatever you associate with Him in worship.” When the affliction is removed, many people return to their old ways, forgetting the sincere monotheism they displayed in distress. They re-assign partners to Allah after He saved them.

This is a recurring Quranic observation about human ingratitude, as in Surah Yunus when people call upon Allah on a ship, then after being saved, they revert to shirk. The verse is a piercing critique of the Meccan polytheists and all who treat faith as a temporary emergency measure rather than a lifelong commitment. It teaches that true worship must be constant, in ease as well as in hardship, because the One who saves is the same One who sustains.

=42=

After exposing the polytheists’ emergency monotheism, Allah draws historical parallels to show that the pattern of denial and warning is ancient. “Indeed, We have sent messengers before you” consoles the Prophet by placing him in the line of previous prophets. His rejection is not novel. “To other people” refers to nations like ‘Ad, Thamud, and the people of Midian. The specific detail is given: “who We put through suffering and adversity for their denial.” When those nations rejected their messengers, Allah sent afflictions—drought, famine, disease, loss of crops—as a wake-up call. The words “suffering” (ba’sā’) and “adversity” (ḍarrā’) denote different types of hardship, economic and physical. The purpose is explicitly stated: “so perhaps they would be humbled. ” The Arabic term yataḍarraʿūn means to humble oneself, to supplicate with humility, to turn back. Hardship is not always a punishment in itself; it can be a merciful tool to break arrogance and bring hearts back to Allah.

The verse reveals a divine educational method: when people become proud and heedless, a trial is sent to soften their hearts, to make them recognize their dependence. Unfortunately, as the next verses show, many did not respond.

This verse also addresses a common question about suffering: why does Allah afflict people? Here the purpose is clear—it is an invitation to return, a warning before the ultimate punishment.

For believers, it provides a lens to view their own trials as opportunities to draw closer to Allah. For the Prophet, it reassured him that his mission followed the same path as those before him, and the initial reactions of his people mirrored those of earlier nations.

=43=

Following the statement that suffering was sent to humble them, this verse laments the actual response. “Why did they not humble themselves when We made them suffer?” is a question of disappointment and rebuke. The opportunity was given, the remedy was applied, yet they failed to respond correctly. Instead of turning to Allah in repentance, they persisted in their arrogance.

The verse then diagnoses the cause: “Instead, their hearts were hardened.” The heart, when repeatedly exposed to warnings without acceptance, becomes callous. The hardening is a spiritual sclerosis that prevents the penetration of truth. Then, “Satan made their misdeeds appealing to them. ” Satan beautified their sins, making disbelief and oppression seem attractive, logical, or even virtuous. He adorned their pride as dignity, their rejection as intellectual independence.

This is a deadly combination: a hardened heart that cannot feel the pain of guidance, and an external whisperer who paints evil as good. The verse shows the dual source of misguidance—internal (hardened hearts) and external (Satan’s embellishment). It also explains why some people, despite severe trials, do not change; the trial does not automatically produce humility unless the heart is receptive.

This is a warning to the believers to guard against the first stages of hardening: neglecting prayer, ignoring the Quran, and rationalizing sins. Once the heart hardens, even calamities cease to move it.

The verse also reminds us of Satan’s continuous efforts to deceive, and the need for constant vigilance and repentance to keep the heart soft and alive to Allah’s reminders.

=44=

This verse describes a terrifying divine strategy called istidrāj (gradual leading to destruction). “When they became oblivious to warnings” means they ignored the earlier afflictions and the calls of the messengers, sinking deeper into heedlessness. At that point, “We showered them with everything they desired.” The doors of worldly blessings were opened: wealth poured in, health returned, crops flourished. This was not a reward but a test. They mistook it for divine favor, thinking their condition proved their rightness. “But just as they became prideful of what they were given” captures their reaction—arrogance, self-congratulation, and a sense of invincibility. They attributed their success to their own efforts or to their false gods, never looking to Allah. Then, abruptly: “We seized them by surprise.” The punishment came without warning, in the midst of their enjoyment. The suddenness is a key element; they had no time to repent, no chance to escape. The result: “then they instantly fell into despair! ” The word “despair” (mublisūn) denotes a state of utter hopelessness, shock, and silence. All their pride evaporated, replaced by helplessness. This pattern is a stark warning to the Meccans, who were enjoying prosperity while denying the Prophet. It teaches that material abundance is not a reliable sign of Allah’s pleasure; sometimes it is a prelude to destruction. The believer must always view blessings with gratitude and humility, not arrogance, and must never feel secure from Allah’s plan. The suddenness of the seizure reminds us that death can come at any moment, and we must always be prepared.

=45=

The outcome of the previous process is stated with finality. “So the wrongdoers were utterly uprooted” means their entire civilization was cut off, destroyed to the last remnant.

The Arabic word quṭiʿa dābir conveys the image of cutting something from its roots, leaving no trace. Their power, wealth, and lineages were completely wiped out.

This is the ultimate fate of those who persist in wrongdoing and reject divine guidance. After this declaration of justice executed, the verse abruptly shifts to praise: “And all praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds.” This shift is deeply significant. The destruction of the wrongdoers is an act of divine justice and a manifestation of Allah’s lordship over creation. It frees the earth from corruption and provides a lesson for future generations.

Therefore, it is a blessing that merits praise. It might seem harsh to modern sensibilities, but from a Quranic perspective, the removal of entrenched evil is a cause for gratitude. The believers who suffered under those oppressive nations are vindicated, and the divine moral order is restored.

The phrase “Lord of all worlds” emphasizes that His care and authority extend over all creation, not just one nation. He nurtures the righteous and eliminates the corrupt for the greater good of humanity.

This verse teaches believers to see the big picture: historical events of destruction are not random but are part of Allah’s plan, and they should evoke praise and reflection. It also comforts the oppressed that their oppressors’ prosperity is temporary, and ultimate victory belongs to Allah’s justice.

=46=

The surah returns to a direct argument using the human senses. The Prophet is to ask them to imagine a scenario where Allah “takes away your hearing or sight.” These are two of the most vital faculties; without them, life becomes profoundly difficult and disconnected.

The question “who else other than Allah could restore it?” challenges the polytheists to name any false deity that could return lost hearing or sight. The idols were carved from stone; they could not hear, see, or act.

This is a brutally logical blow to shirk. The mention of sealing the hearts adds a spiritual dimension. If Allah “seals your hearts,” spiritual perception is lost, leading to misguidance and eternal ruin. Again, only Allah can reverse that. Then the verse includes a divine comment to the Prophet: “See how We vary the signs, yet they still turn away.” Allah points out that the Quran presents arguments in diverse forms—historical, psychological, cosmic—all pointing to the same truth.

Yet the disbelievers persist in their aversion. This phrase not only consoles the Prophet but also shows that the problem is not a lack of evidence but a refusal to look.

The verse teaches believers to be constantly grateful for their senses, recognizing them as trusts from Allah that can be taken at any moment, and to use them in obedience. It also emphasizes that even the most obvious reasoning cannot benefit a heart that has decided to turn away.

=47=

Continuing the series of probing questions, the Prophet is instructed to present another frightening scenario. “Imagine if Allah’s punishment were to overwhelm you” refers to the divine chastisement that can strike a community.

The phrase “with or without warning” covers two possibilities: a punishment that comes openly, preceded by clear signs, or one that arrives suddenly, catching people in their heedlessness. The disbelievers often demanded to see the punishment, mocking the Prophet to bring it on. This question makes them consider: if it does come, who will actually suffer? The rhetorical answer is immediate: “who would be destroyed other than the wrongdoers?” The punishment, though terrifying, is not indiscriminate. It specifically targets those who have earned it through their persistent injustice and denial.

This is a principle of divine justice: Allah’s punishment only annihilates those who are guilty; the believers and the innocent are saved. The verse offers a subtle warning that their mockery and demands might just result in their own destruction while leaving others unharmed. It reinforces the earlier statement that the Prophet’s role is only to warn, and he cannot control the timing. For the believers, this verse is a reassurance that Allah’s justice is precise; they need not fear that they will be swept away with the unjust when collective punishment comes, as long as they are righteous. It also calls the polytheists to self-reflection: they label themselves as the wrongdoers by implication if they acknowledge that the punishment will fall on the wrongdoers, yet they continue their ways.

=48=

After the intense warnings, the Quran clarifies the true function of messengers to correct any misunderstanding about their role. “We have sent messengers only as deliverers of good news and as warners.” They are not sent to compel belief, to bring about punishment on demand, or to control people’s destinies. Their function is to convey the message, giving glad tidings of Paradise to those who believe and warnings of Hell to those who reject. This clarifies the limits of the Prophet’s responsibility, echoing verse 35’s message that he cannot force guidance.

The verse then states the outcome for the positive respondents: “Whoever believes and does good, there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.” The combination of “believes” and “does good” underscores that faith and righteous deeds are inseparable for salvation. “No fear” refers to the future—they will not fear the punishment of the Hereafter or the terrors of the Day of Judgment. “Nor will they grieve” refers to the past—they will not regret what they left behind in the world or any missed opportunities, because what awaits them is far better.

This is the ultimate security and peace. This verse provides a beautiful balance: the earlier verses emphasized destruction and punishment, and now the door to mercy and tranquility is opened. It shows that Allah’s mercy precedes His wrath, and that the primary purpose of the messengers is to guide to eternal happiness. It is a powerful invitation and a source of immense hope for all who hear and respond.

=49=

The counterpart to the previous verse is now given succinctly. “But those who deny Our signs” refers to the people who reject the Quranic revelations and the prophetic miracles. Their denial is active, and it stems from a moral choice. “Will be afflicted with punishment” indicates a certain and severe consequence. The nature of the punishment is not specified here but is understood as the torment of Hellfire, and possibly worldly destruction as seen in earlier verses.

The verse adds a critical nuance: “for their rebelliousness” (bi-mā kānū yafsuqūn). This word “rebelliousness” (fisq) implies a departure from the path of obedience, a willful transgression. The punishment is not arbitrary but is a direct result of their own actions. They left the bounds of faith and morality, so they deserve the penalty. This verse reinforces the principle of causality in divine justice: people are not punished for simple ignorance but for their defiant rejection after the truth has been made clear. It also connects back to the function of messengers as warners; the warning is given, so when punishment comes, no one can claim injustice. The contrast between the two outcomes—complete security for the believer and painful punishment for the denier—is stark and motivates the reader to choose faith and obedience. The lesson for believers is to avoid fisq in all its forms, as it leads to ruin both in this world and the next.

=50=

The disbelievers often criticized the Prophet for being an ordinary human who ate, walked in the markets, and did not control the treasures of the world. They wondered why an angel was not sent. Here, Allah instructs him to openly declare the limits of his human role. “I do not say to you that I possess Allah’s treasuries” means he does not control the stores of provision, rain, wealth, or mercy. He cannot grant worldly riches or miraculously provide for people. “Or know the unseen” addresses their demand for predictions and secrets; he knows only what Allah reveals to him. “Nor do I claim to be an angel” rejects any supernatural status beyond prophethood. He is a human being receiving revelation. This honesty sets him apart from false claimants who pretend to divine powers. Then, “I only follow what is revealed to me” sums up his mission: complete adherence to the divine guidance. After this clarification, he is instructed to ask a rhetorical question: “Are those blind to the truth equal to those who can see?” The “blind” are the disbelievers whose spiritual sight is blocked, and the “seeing” are the believers who recognize truth.

The question challenges the listener to use their reason. “Will you not then reflect?” is an appeal to intellect.

This verse teaches the essential Islamic position on prophethood: the Prophet is a human being chosen to convey revelation, not a demigod. It emphasizes that the value lies in the message, not in any material power. It also invites reflection on the clear distinction between guidance and misguidance.

=51=

Following the clarification of the Prophet’s role, he is given a specific instruction on who to focus his warning upon. “Warn with this Quran” identifies the tool of warning: the Quran itself, with its arguments, stories, and promises. The target audience is described as “those who are awed by the prospect of being gathered before their Lord.” These are people whose hearts tremble at the thought of standing before Allah on the Day of Judgment. They already have a natural fear of accountability, making them receptive to the message.

This is in contrast to those who mock the idea of resurrection. The verse adds a crucial detail about that gathering: “when they will have no protector or intercessor besides Him.” On that Day, no friend, relative, idol, or angel can protect or intercede without Allah’s permission. The only true protector and authorized intercessor is Allah Himself. This isolation is terrifying, and the warning aims to create mindfulness (taqwā).

The phrase “so perhaps they will be mindful” indicates the hope and purpose behind the warning—it is not to condemn but to guide people to taqwā, which leads to salvation. This verse directs the Prophet’s efforts toward those who show signs of fear and sincerity, not to waste energy on the hardened rejecters who mock. It also highlights a core theme: the Quran is a book of warning and mercy for those who have a living conscience.

For the believer, it cultivates a constant awareness of that inevitable gathering and motivates preparation through righteous deeds.

=52=

This verse addresses a specific historical incident that carries an enduring moral lesson. The Meccan chiefs, like Abu Jahl and others, would mock the poor and socially low-ranking believers such as Bilal, Suhayb, and Ammar. They suggested that the Prophet should send these humble people away if he wanted the nobles to sit with him. The Prophet, eager to gain the influential leaders’ acceptance, considered doing so. Then this verse was revealed. The instruction is firm: “Do not dismiss those poor believers who invoke their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure.” Their wealth and status are irrelevant; their devotion, sincerity, and constant remembrance of Allah make them worthy of honor. “You are not accountable for them whatsoever, nor are they accountable for you” severs any imagined social or spiritual hierarchy that would justify dismissive behavior. The Prophet cannot bear their sins, nor they his. The value of a person is not determined by tribe or wealth. The command is repeated with a strong warning: “So do not dismiss them, or you will be one of the wrongdoers.” The warning, directed at the Prophet, shows that even the greatest of creation cannot compromise on this principle. It establishes the radical equality of believers in Islam: faith, not social standing, is the criterion of honor. This verse was a profound shock to the Meccan aristocracy and a source of immense comfort to the poor believers who were given dignity by Allah. For all times, it forbids any form of class discrimination within the Muslim community and reminds leaders to honor the sincere, regardless of their worldly position.

=53=

The surah now explains the wisdom behind the social tension just mentioned. “In this way We have tested some by means of others” reveals that the division between rich and poor, powerful and weak, is a divine trial. The wealthy are tested by their wealth to see if they will be grateful and humble, and the poor are tested by their poverty to see if they will be patient and steadfast. The specific test here is that the disbelieving nobles saw the poor believers and wondered in arrogance, “Has Allah favoured these poor believers out of all of us? ” They could not understand why Allah would guide the lowly and leave them, the respected leaders. Their question reflects their materialistic worldview; they assumed that divine favor correlates with worldly status. The verse answers with a rhetorical question: “Does Allah not best recognize the grateful?” Allah knows who truly appreciates His blessings and who will be thankful when guidance comes. The poor believers were grateful; they responded to the truth without arrogance. The rich disbelievers were ungrateful, despite having more material blessings.

Thus, Allah’s favor is not based on lineage or wealth but on a heart’s capacity for gratitude and faith. This verse teaches believers not to envy the worldly success of the disbelievers, for it is a test, and not to look down on the poor faithful, for they may be the truly favored. It also warns the affluent that their wealth is a test, and ingratitude can lead to being excluded from the greatest favor—guidance.

=54=

After rebuking any thought of dismissing the poor believers, the Prophet is taught the proper way to receive them. “When the believers in Our revelations come to you” refers to those sincere, often marginalized, believers who approach seeking guidance and reassurance.

The first instruction is to greet them with “Peace be upon you!” This salutation (salam) is not merely a courtesy but a declaration of safety, honor, and divine blessing. It recognizes their dignity as believers. Then, they are to be given a message of profound hope: “Your Lord has taken upon Himself to be Merciful.” This echoes verse 12 and emphasizes that mercy is a divine commitment, a self-imposed obligation. The greeting then expands into a general promise: “Whoever among you commits evil ignorantly or recklessly then repents afterwards and mends their ways, then Allah is truly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. ” This covers sins committed out of ignorance (inadvertently) or recklessly (knowingly but driven by desire). The condition for forgiveness is sincere repentance (tawbah) and reforming one’s conduct (iṣlāḥ). The names Al-Ghafūr (All-Forgiving) and Al-Raḥīm (Most Merciful) seal the promise. This verse serves multiple purposes: it honors the weak believers, removes any despair they might feel over past sins, and establishes a culture of mercy and hope. It also stands in stark contrast to the harsh exclusivity of the pagan elite. For all Muslims, it is a reminder that the door of repentance is always open, and that when a sinner returns, they should be met with peace, not condemnation.

=55=

This short but profound verse summarizes the purpose of the preceding detailed instructions and arguments. “This is how We make Our signs clear” refers to the entire method of the Quran—the use of rational proofs, historical examples, moral precepts, and social rulings. The clarity of the signs leaves no excuse for those who contemplate them. The purpose is then stated: “so the way of the wicked may become distinct. ” The Arabic word for “wicked” is mujrimūn, meaning criminals, those who persist in sin and denial. The clarity of Allah’s guidance serves to separate the path of the criminals from the path of the righteous. When the truth is made plain, people are no longer confused; they can see clearly what is right and what is wrong.

This distinction forces a choice and makes accountability just. The wicked are exposed, not because Allah unveiled their secrets unjustly, but because their own choices stand in sharp contrast to the luminous signs. This verse encapsulates a major function of revelation: to draw a clear line between guidance and misguidance, between the believers and the criminals. For the believers, it is a reassurance that the truth is evident and that the confusion sown by the disbelievers cannot obscure it.

For the disbelievers, it is a warning that their path has been clearly marked as destructive, and they are without excuse. The verse transitions well to the next declaration of pure monotheism, as the way of the wicked includes shirk.

=56=

The Prophet is commanded to declare his complete dissociation from idolatry. “I have been forbidden to worship those you invoke besides Allah” establishes a clear, divine prohibition.

The word “forbidden” shows that the matter is non-negotiable; it is a direct command from Allah. The beings they invoke—idols, angels, jinn—are categorically rejected as objects of worship. Then, a second declaration: “I will not follow your desires.” Here, the root of polytheism is identified: it is not based on reason or revelation but on personal whims, cultural traditions, and unchecked desires. The Meccans followed the religion of their forefathers out of emotional attachment, not truth. The Prophet declares independence from such subjective inclinations. The reason is given: “for I then would certainly be astray and not one of those rightly guided.” If he were to follow their desires, he would lose the guidance he has received and join the ranks of the misguided. This humble admission—that even the Prophet is susceptible to misguidance if he abandons revelation—shows his complete reliance on Allah.

The verse teaches the believer that the primary conflict is between revelation and human desire. True religion demands submission to what Allah has commanded, even when it contradicts popular opinion or personal inclination. It is a declaration of intellectual and spiritual freedom from the tyranny of societal norms, placing divine revelation as the ultimate criterion.

=57=

The Prophet asserts his firm position of evidence-based faith. “Indeed, I stand on a clear proof from my Lord” refers to the Quran and the revelation, which provide undeniable evidence of monotheism, resurrection, and the prophethood of Muhammad.

This is not blind faith but certainty grounded in clear signs. The contrast is stark: “yet you have denied it. ” The disbelievers reject the clear proof, not because it is unclear but because of stubbornness.

The verse then addresses their recurring challenge: they would mockingly ask for the punishment to be hastened. “That torment you seek to hasten is not within my power.” The Prophet is not the dispenser of punishment; he is a warner. This limitation is again emphasized, reinforcing his humanity and the fact that divine decisions are with Allah alone. “It is only Allah Who decides its time.” The timing of the punishment, whether in this world or the next, is according to His wisdom. “He declares the truth” means He speaks the absolute truth in His revelation and His judgments. “And He is the Best of Judges” (khayru al-fāṣilīn) means He alone can perfectly distinguish between truth and falsehood, and between the guilty and the innocent. The verse serves to refocus attention on the message rather than the messenger’s personal power. It is a confident declaration of the truth’s origin and a warning that the judgment, when it comes, will be decisively from the Best of Judges.

=58=

Adding to the previous point, the Prophet is instructed to explain the implication of the limits of his power. “If what you seek to hasten were within my power”—that is, the punishment they mockingly demand—“the matter between us would have already been settled.” The human inclination, when faced with persistent rejection and mockery, is to retaliate. If the Prophet had the power to call down divine punishment, he might have already done so, ending the conflict immediately.

However, this is not his role. The delay is not because the disbelievers are right, but because Allah has a plan.

The phrase “But Allah knows the wrongdoers best” explains why the matter is not yet settled. Allah knows their inner states, their potential for future repentance, or the children who might be born believers from them. He also knows the precise moment when punishment will serve the greater purpose of justice and warning.

The verse teaches patience and trust in Allah’s timing. Even when the believers are eager for vindication, they must leave the judgment to Allah. The Prophet’s restraint is a model for all Muslims: personal feelings of anger or the desire for revenge must be subdued in favor of divine wisdom. It also warns the disbelievers that the punishment is not being withheld because of any inability or because they are safe; it is being delayed by the knowledge of the All-Wise.

=59=

This magnificent verse expands on the theme of Allah’s exclusive knowledge, which underpins His role as the Best of Judges. “With Him are the keys of the unseen—no one knows them except Him.” The “keys” (mafātiḥ) represent exclusive access to the unseen realms: the time of the Hour, the falling of rain, what is in the wombs, what a soul will earn tomorrow, and where a person will die, as mentioned in a hadith. No prophet, angel, or any created being possesses this knowledge independently. This refutes the claims of soothsayers and those who attribute knowledge of the unseen to saints or idols.

The verse then elaborates on the breadth of His knowledge: “And He knows what is in the land and sea.” Every creature, every event, every hidden treasure in the earth or the oceans is within His awareness. Then a striking image: “Not even a leaf falls without His knowledge. ” A single leaf, among trillions, dropping from a tree in a remote forest, is fully known to Him—its timing, its place, its trajectory. Even more hidden: “nor a grain in the darkness of the earth.” A tiny seed buried deep in the soil, invisible to all, is clearly seen by Allah. The verse culminates: “or anything—green or dry—but is written in a perfect Record. ” The “perfect Record” is the Preserved Tablet, where everything is inscribed.

The mention of “green or dry” covers all states of existence, from life to death. This verse instills immense awe and trust in Allah. No suffering, no good deed, no injustice is ever lost or unnoticed. It provides profound comfort to the oppressed and a terrifying warning to the oppressor. The divine knowledge ensures that the judgment, when it comes, will be perfectly just.

=60=

Building on the theme of divine knowledge and control, this verse moves to a daily miracle that humans often overlook: sleep. “He is the One Who calls back your souls by night” describes sleep as a form of death. During sleep, the soul is taken by Allah, partially and temporarily, removing consciousness and awareness. “And knows what you do by day” extends His knowledge to all actions during wakefulness. “Then revives you daily” refers to the act of waking up, which is like a mini-resurrection, a daily proof of Allah’s power to bring the dead back to life. This daily revival serves a purpose: “to complete your appointed term.” Each day of life is a portion of the fixed lifespan decreed by Allah, and waking up each morning is a chance to fulfill that term. Eventually, “To Him is your ultimate return” points to the final death from which there is no waking until the Day of Resurrection. “Then He will inform you of what you used to do” is the final accounting. Every deed, public or private, will be presented. The verse links the daily cycle of sleep and wakefulness to the ultimate cycle of death and resurrection, making it a tangible sign. It encourages the believer to recite the supplications of sleeping and waking, mindful that sleep is a minor death and waking is a new lease on life. The verse fosters constant awareness of the final return and motivates accountability throughout the day. It also beautifully concludes the discussion of Allah’s knowledge by showing that His recording of deeds is not distant but intimately woven into the fabric of daily life.
