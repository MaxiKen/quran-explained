=141=

“He is the One who produces gardens—trellised and untrellised—date palms, crops of differing tastes, olives, and pomegranates, similar yet different. Eat from their fruit when they bear, give its due on harvest day, and do not waste. Surely He does not love the wasteful.”

After exposing invented restrictions, the Quran presents Allah’s actual agricultural ethic: enjoy, share, and do not waste. Provision is not divided between God and idols or reserved through superstition.

Ma‘rushat are gardens trained upon trellises or carefully cultivated; untrellised growth stands without that support. Human cultivation and wild or differently structured growth both depend upon Allah’s design.

Dates, grains, olives, and pomegranates offered the first audience staple food, oil, sweetness, and variety. “Similar and different” describes forms that share soil, water, leaves, or appearance while producing distinct taste and benefit. Familiar food becomes a sign.

“Eat” affirms lawful enjoyment. Ascetic refusal is not automatically gratitude. Farmers and families may receive the fruit of labour while remembering that labour did not create rain, seed, or life.

“Give its due at harvest” establishes the poor person’s claim before wealth disappears into storage. Scholars discussed whether this is agricultural zakah later specified by the Sunnah or a broader harvest charity. Fixed rates differ according to irrigation and crop rules; qualified guidance is needed.

Giving happens at harvest, when produce and need are visible. Delaying obligations while spending freely on oneself reverses the verse’s order.

Israf includes consuming, giving, or using beyond proper bounds. Charity itself should not irresponsibly leave dependants without rights, and farming should not waste water, soil, food, or labour.

Modern food loss between farm and market makes this command communal as well as personal. Better storage, fair purchasing, gleaning, and redistribution can turn abundance away from landfill and toward gratitude.

Verse 142 moves to livestock used for carrying and those kept close to the ground. Both are provision, and satanic footsteps must not turn lawful benefit into false taboo.

=142=

“Among livestock are animals for carrying loads and others kept close to the ground. Eat from what Allah has provided for you, and do not follow Satan’s footsteps. Surely he is your clear enemy.”

Hamulah includes larger animals used for transport and burdens, such as camels. Farsh can refer to smaller livestock close to the ground, animals used for food, or material from hides and wool spread as furnishing. The words cover varied benefits from one divine provision.

The pagans had exempted selected animals from work or food for idols. Allah restores the normal rule: livestock He provided may be used within lawful limits.

Permission does not justify cruelty. The Prophet, peace be upon him, forbade overloading animals, starving them, or using them as targets. A burden-bearing animal has a right to food, rest, and humane treatment.

“Eat” includes receiving provision gratefully, not making meat the centre of diet or consuming without regard to health, environment, and affordability. Legal permissibility and responsible use remain related but distinct.

Satan’s footsteps are gradual. A cultural preference becomes sacred, an invented restriction becomes proof of piety, and eventually an idol or elite controls Allah’s provision. False religion often develops by small normalised steps.

Seeing Satan as clear enemy should not create obsession with unseen causes. Practical errors in farming or health need practical diagnosis. His role is temptation; humans remain responsible for systems they choose.

Verses 143-144 now examine four livestock species as eight paired animals—male and female sheep, goats, camels, and cattle. The questions expose that pagan restrictions cannot identify any consistent sex, womb, or revealed command as their basis.

=143=

“Allah created eight animals in pairs: two from sheep and two from goats. Ask them, ‘Has He forbidden the two males, the two females, or what the wombs of the two females contain? Tell me with knowledge, if you are truthful.’”

The eight are male and female of four domesticated species. This verse begins with sheep and goats, animals central to food, wool, milk, and livelihood.

The logical questions test the claimed prohibition. If all males are forbidden, why do they permit some? If all females, why others? If everything in the womb, both sexes of offspring should follow one rule. Their actual taboos shift according to birth sequence and invented category.

“Tell me with knowledge” demands evidence. Halal and haram cannot rest upon folklore, priestly interest, or an ancestor’s unexplained rule. Divine prohibition requires revelation or a valid derivation from it.

This method teaches careful questioning rather than mere ridicule. Ask what exactly is forbidden, which category includes it, and what proof makes the distinction. Contradiction often exposes fabrication.

The verse does not say human beings may impose no regulations involving sex or breeding. Veterinary welfare, conservation, ownership, and agricultural policy can require evidence-based rules. They must not be presented as Allah’s eternal prohibition without authority.

Consumers likewise need factual clarity. A cultural dislike or health recommendation may be useful without becoming haram. Precise language protects revelation.

The paired creation also points to provision and reproduction under Allah. The pagans benefited from offspring while attributing authority over the womb’s product to idols.

Verse 144 completes the set with camels and cattle, asks whether they witnessed Allah issue such a command, and condemns fabrication that misleads without knowledge.

=144=

“And two from camels and two from cattle. Ask, ‘Has He forbidden the two males, the two females, or what the wombs of the two females contain? Were you present when Allah commanded you to do this?’ Who is more unjust than one who fabricates lies against Allah to mislead people without knowledge? Surely Allah does not guide wrongdoing people.”

Camels and cattle complete the eight. They carried people, provided milk and meat, and represented substantial wealth. Invented restrictions therefore had significant economic effects.

The same questions reveal inconsistency, then a sharper challenge asks whether they witnessed divine command. They possess neither scripture nor prophetic testimony. Their confident rule has no source beyond claim.

Witnessing revelation is not the only way Muslims know law; authentic transmission from the Prophet and sound derivation also establish it. The question targets people with no revelation or chain at all, not later evidence-based scholarship.

Fabrication is “more unjust” because it harms both Allah’s exclusive right to legislate and people misled into deprivation or shirk. A false religious rule can outlive its inventor for generations.

The phrase “without knowledge” condemns reckless certainty. Influence magnifies duty to verify. A viral speaker who casually declares products, marriages, or people forbidden can create real harm even without malicious intent.

Allah’s not guiding wrongdoers corresponds to persistence. A person who discovers an error, corrects it publicly, and repents is not required to remain trapped in the category.

The verse also challenges blind proof by popularity. Many witnesses repeating one inherited claim do not equal one witness to revelation.

Verse 145 replaces invented prohibitions with a concise revealed list: carrion, flowing blood, swine flesh, and what is dedicated sinfully to another, while preserving necessity.

=145=

“Say, ‘In what has been revealed to me, I find nothing forbidden for a person to eat except carrion, flowing blood, the flesh of swine—for it is impure—or what is sinful because it was dedicated to other than Allah. But whoever is compelled by necessity, neither desiring it nor exceeding need, will find your Lord All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.’”

The list gives foundational prohibitions in the revelation available at this Makkan stage. Al-Ma’idah later details animals killed by strangling, blows, falls, goring, and predation, all related to unlawful death unless reached for valid slaughter.

Maytah is an animal dead without lawful slaughter. Daman masfuhan is blood poured or flowing, not every trace remaining within meat. Swine flesh is forbidden, and dedication to another makes otherwise lawful meat fisq.

The verse’s “I find nothing” refutes pagan additions rather than limiting every possible prohibition forever to these words without Sunnah. The Prophet prohibited additional harmful creatures and clarified food law through the authority the Quran gives him.

Necessity protects life. A person facing genuine starvation or comparable danger may consume what is needed when no lawful alternative exists. He must not seek the situation, desire the forbidden as such, or exceed survival need.

Al-Ghafur and al-Rahim show concession is not reluctant defeat of the law. Mercy is built into divine legislation. Preserving life serves the Lawgiver.

Questions about medicines, derivatives, contamination, and emergency supply require both factual experts and qualified jurists. A prohibited ingredient can involve transformation, dose, alternatives, and necessity beyond what a label reveals.

The broad lawful field should reduce obsessive restriction. Clear prohibitions are respected without turning every uncertainty into haram.

Verse 146 explains that some additional foods were forbidden specifically to Jews as recompense for transgression, not as universal proof supporting pagan taboos.

=146=

“For those who were Jews, We forbade every animal with an undivided hoof, and We forbade them the fat of cattle and sheep except what is attached to their backs or intestines or mixed with bone. In this way We repaid them for their transgression, and We are certainly truthful.”

The exact zoological meaning of dhi zufur has been rendered as animals with claws or undivided hoofs. The verse’s purpose is not a modern taxonomy lesson but a historical dietary restriction upon a particular community.

Cattle and sheep fat was restricted with named exceptions: fat attached to backs, entrails, or bone. These details show the law was specific rather than a vague rejection of all animal fat.

The restriction was jaza’ for baghy or rebellion, as 4:160 also says wholesome things were forbidden because of wrongdoing. It was not evidence those foods were inherently evil for every nation.

Muslims should not mock Jewish dietary observance. Earlier law came from Allah, and conscientious followers acted within what they understood as covenant. The Quran’s theological explanation can be stated respectfully.

Nor can believers infer that every hardship or food allergy is punishment. Only revelation identifies this communal restriction and cause. Personal medical limitations have natural causes and can carry reward through patience.

“We are truthful” answers distorted histories and pagan use of earlier restrictions as precedent. Allah knows what was originally lawful, what was later prohibited, and why.

The final Islamic law restores broad permission while retaining its own prohibitions. Ease creates responsibility to avoid inventing new burdens.

Verse 147 tells the Prophet how to answer denial: Allah possesses vast mercy, yet His punishment cannot be turned away from persistent criminals.

=147=

“If they deny you, say, ‘Your Lord possesses vast mercy, but His punishment cannot be averted from the criminal people.’”

Denial does not lead immediately to a speech of rage. The Prophet begins with sa‘at al-rahmah—vast mercy. The door remains open to those currently rejecting him.

Mercy appears in provision, delay, revelation, concession, and forgiveness. Its vastness exceeds the sinner’s history when repentance is sincere. No living opponent should be treated as incapable of guidance.

The second half prevents mercy from becoming presumption. Ba’s is severe consequence, and no idol, wealth, or argument can repel it once Allah decrees it for persistent criminals.

The balance corrects two distortions: despair that imagines sin larger than Allah’s mercy, and entitlement that uses mercy to continue sin without return.

Mujrimin are not people who merely made one mistake. The context describes fabrication, child killing, shirk, and determined denial. Final punishment corresponds to persistence and proof.

Callers can model this order—invite through mercy and warn honestly. Threat without hope misrepresents the Lord; comfort without accountability leaves people in danger.

Victims should not be pressured to waive rights because “Allah is merciful.” Divine mercy includes justice, protection, and repentance that repairs harm.

Verse 148 exposes the polytheists’ next excuse: if Allah had willed, they and their ancestors would not have practised shirk or forbidden anything. The Quran answers that this is conjecture, not knowledge.

=148=

“The polytheists will say, ‘Had Allah willed, neither we nor our forefathers would have associated partners, nor would we have forbidden anything.’ Those before them argued similarly until they tasted Our punishment. Ask, ‘Do you have any knowledge you can present to us? You follow only conjecture and do nothing but guess.’”

Their premise contains a truth misused: nothing exists outside Allah’s universal will. The false conclusion is that whatever He permits to occur must be approved and commanded.

Allah allows theft, disbelief, and murder within a world of testing while explicitly forbidding them. Existence is not endorsement. His creational will and His revealed religious command must be distinguished.

They invoke ancestors to deepen the excuse. If generations practised it under divine sovereignty, they argue, it must be acceptable. Historical duration still does not become revelation.

Earlier peoples used comparable fatalism, but punishment showed they remained accountable. If decree erased responsibility, no warning or consequence would have meaning.

The challenge is epistemic: produce knowledge. They cannot see the decree before choosing, possess no revealed approval, and rely only on assumption. Qadar is known as doctrine, not as a private excuse for a specific sin.

Believers can also misuse “Allah willed it” after wrongdoing. The proper response is repentance and repair. Decree consoles after unavoidable loss; it does not justify voluntary disobedience.

This distinction also protects effort. Illness and poverty occur by decree, yet treatment and work are commanded. Means themselves belong to qadar.

Verse 149 states that Allah owns the conclusive proof. If He had willed to compel guidance, He could have guided everyone, but the test and evidence establish responsibility.

=149=

“Say, ‘Allah alone has the conclusive proof. Had He willed, He could have guided all of you.’”

Al-hujjah al-balighah is proof that reaches its goal and leaves no valid counterargument. Allah created reason, sent messengers, revealed signs, and clarified the difference between permission and approval.

His ability to guide everyone is not disputed. He could create creatures without the option of disbelief or compel visible submission. The existence of rejection therefore never indicates a rival power defeated Him.

He willed an accountable human life in which guidance is offered, chosen, and granted. Human choice remains inside divine sovereignty rather than outside it.

The verse does not explain every philosophical question about decree in two sentences. It establishes boundaries: Allah is fully powerful, humans really respond, and His proof and judgment are perfect.

Compelled movement would not carry the same moral meaning as faith in the unseen. Angels already obey without human appetite; humanity’s test has its own wisdom.

The conclusive argument belongs to Allah, not to every preacher. A caller can misunderstand, communicate poorly, or fail to answer. Humility about personal argument should coexist with confidence in revelation.

Nor may the verse justify coercive conversion. If Allah did not make compulsory faith the structure of the test, humans cannot create genuine belief through force.

Verse 150 asks the polytheists to produce witnesses for their food prohibitions and forbids the Prophet from joining false testimony or following deniers’ desires.

=150=

“Say, ‘Bring your witnesses who can testify that Allah forbade these things.’ If they falsely testify, do not testify with them. Do not follow the desires of those who deny Our signs, disbelieve in the Hereafter, and set up equals with their Lord.”

The challenge moves from abstract claim to evidence. Who heard a messenger, received a book, or possesses a reliable chain from Allah? Customary repetition is not testimony to revelation.

Witnesses can coordinate around falsehood. Number and solemn tone do not transform fabricated evidence. The Prophet must refuse participation even if joining would ease social conflict.

“Do not testify with them” protects religious integrity and legal ethics. A believer cannot endorse a false statement for family, faction, diplomacy, or a supposedly good outcome.

Their ahwa’ connect food rules to a larger worldview: denial of signs, the Hereafter, and tawhid. Once no final account or sole Lawgiver is recognised, desire can legislate while calling itself sacred.

The verse does not require rejecting every factual statement from a disbeliever. Testimony is assessed by its field and legal rules. The prohibition concerns following their unsupported religious desire and false witness.

Modern claims of halal and haram should identify source and reasoning. “A scholar said” needs a name and context where stakes are high; viral graphics are not witnesses.

The Prophet’s refusal is active but not abusive. He does not need to insult witnesses; he states the evidence is false and remains separate.

Verse 151 replaces invented food taboos with foundational moral commands: no shirk, excellence to parents, protection of children, avoidance of indecency, and sanctity of life.

=151=

“Say, ‘Come, let me recite what your Lord has truly forbidden to you: do not associate anything with Him; treat parents with excellence; do not kill your children because of poverty—We provide for you and for them; do not approach indecencies, whether open or hidden; and do not take a life Allah has made sacred except with legal right. This He has commanded you so that you may understand.’”

The invitation “come” contrasts with superstition. Allah’s prohibitions protect worship, family, dignity, and life rather than serving invented idols.

Shirk comes first because tawhid grounds every right. Without the true Lawgiver, powerful people can redefine life and morality around interest.

Parents receive ihsan—beautiful care, speech, and support—not unconditional obedience in sin. Abuse does not become lawful because the abuser is a parent; boundaries and safety can coexist with whatever kindness remains possible.

Child killing for poverty is answered by provision for parents and children. The order “for you and for them” addresses existing fear directly. Poverty requires solidarity and lawful planning, never murder of dependants.

The verse condemns infanticide. Complex questions of abortion involve stages of pregnancy, maternal danger, fetal condition, and juristic detail; they should not be collapsed into slogans without qualified medical and religious counsel.

Do not “approach” indecencies: paths, coercion, secrecy, exploitation, and normalisation matter before the final act. Open and hidden forms are both known to Allah.

Sacred life may be taken only bil-haqq through narrowly defined law, such as valid judicial retribution or lawful defence. Individuals, families, and mobs cannot appoint themselves court and executioner.

Understanding should see these commands as coherent protection, not arbitrary burden. Verse 152 continues with orphan property, honest measure, just speech, and covenant.

=152=

“Do not approach an orphan’s property except in the best manner until they reach maturity. Give full measure and weight with justice—We do not burden any soul beyond its capacity. When you speak, be just, even concerning a close relative. Fulfil Allah’s covenant. This He has commanded you so that you may remember.”

“Do not approach” protects the property before actual theft. Guardians avoid conflicts of interest, speculative use, and mixing that makes accountability impossible. Investment or spending must genuinely serve the orphan.

Maturity includes legal age and sound judgment as detailed in 4:6. Control cannot continue indefinitely because a guardian enjoys it. Property should be transferred with records and witnesses.

Full measure and weight govern markets. Modern equivalents include digital scales, billing, wages, product quantity, data reporting, and financial disclosure. Small hidden reductions across many customers become large injustice.

“We do not burden beyond capacity” acknowledges honest measurement has practical limits. It excuses unavoidable minor error after reasonable care, not deliberate calibration in one’s favour.

Justice in speech includes testimony, reporting, evaluation, and family disputes. Kinship may intensify love or conflict, but neither may alter fact. Fairness against one’s side is covenantal worship.

Allah’s covenant encompasses faith and revealed duties; human covenants remain binding where lawful. A promise to commit sin must be broken, while rights affected by breaking require proper repair.

“Remember” suggests conscience recognises much of this morality but needs revelation against desire and forgetfulness. Systems, audits, and education help remembrance become durable practice.

Verse 153 gathers these commands into Allah’s one straight path and warns against many diverging roads that fragment people away from it.

=153=

“Indeed, this is My path, perfectly straight, so follow it. Do not follow other paths, for they will separate you from His path. This He has commanded you so that you may become mindful.”

The straight path includes the preceding creed and ethics: tawhid, parents’ rights, children’s lives, chastity, protected blood, orphan wealth, fair trade, truthful speech, and covenant. Spirituality and social justice are one road.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, drew a straight line and branching lines, explaining that the straight line was Allah’s path and a devil called from each side road. The image makes fragmentation visible.

“Other paths” include false religions and desires that depart from revelation. The verse should not be used to declare every juristic school or legitimate interpretation a separate condemned sect. Valid diversity can remain within one evidentiary path.

Nor does unity mean submission to one modern organisation. Groups are measured by Quran and Sunnah; no party owns the straight line.

Side roads often begin near the path and borrow its language. A movement may preach tawhid while violating protected life or claim justice while abandoning worship. The previous commands provide a test.

Following requires action, not admiration of straightness. Prayer, restitution, and restraint place feet upon what creed identifies.

Taqwa is the purpose: awareness that prevents drifting at each branch. Good companions and knowledge help, but the traveller must keep asking Allah for guidance.

Verse 154 links these principles to Musa’s scripture, given as completion, detailed guidance, and mercy so his people might believe in meeting their Lord.

=154=

“Then We gave Musa the Scripture, completing Our favour upon those who did good, explaining what they needed in detail, and as guidance and mercy, so that they might believe in the meeting with their Lord.”

Thumma connects the universal moral commands with the historical gift of Torah. The essential path did not begin with the Quran; Allah taught tawhid and justice to earlier communities.

“Completion for the one who did good” has been understood as completing favour upon Musa because of his excellence or upon righteous recipients. In either case, revelation crowns ihsan with structured guidance.

“Tafsil of everything” means everything required for that scripture’s guidance and law, not every scientific or technical fact. Similar Quranic phrases should be read according to purpose rather than as encyclopaedic claims.

The Torah was huda and rahmah in its revealed form. Muslims honour it as Allah’s Book to Musa while accepting the Quran’s correction of later concealment and alteration.

Belief in meeting Allah is named as a goal because law without accountability can become external identity. The Hereafter gives hidden honesty weight.

The verse also answers pagans who thought detailed revelation alien to Abrahamic faith. Musa’s Book already joined law, guidance, mercy, and resurrection.

Muslims should not speak contemptuously about earlier revelation or its sincere followers. Finality of Quran does not erase gratitude for light that Allah truly sent before it.

Verse 155 turns directly to the Quran as a blessed Book. Follow it and maintain taqwa so that mercy may be received.

=155=

“This is a blessed Book We have revealed, so follow it and be mindful of Allah, so that you may receive mercy.”

Mubarak means abundant and lasting in good. Quran blesses through truth, recitation, understanding, healing, law, remembrance, and action—not as a charm detached from its message.

The imperative “follow” explains how blessing is received. A beautifully displayed copy cannot substitute for honest trade, prayer, family justice, and tawhid. Recitation should lead toward obedience.

Taqwa guards application from desire. People can quote scripture to control others while exempting themselves. Consciousness of Allah makes the reader a subject of the verse before making him a judge over neighbours.

Mercy is the intended destination. Following does not purchase mercy as an equal wage; it takes the road Allah graciously attached to it.

The verse is concise because the previous commands already demonstrate following. Quranic relationship is not mysterious: learn, believe, act, repent, and return.

Blessing also appears communally through literacy, ethical institutions, care for vulnerable people, and resolution of disputes. A reciting society that normalises oppression has not realised the Book’s barakah fully.

Translation and technology can widen access, but context and sound teaching remain needed. Ease of quotation can produce fragmentation as well as benefit.

Verses 156-157 explain that the Book removes two Arab excuses: earlier scriptures belonged only to Jews and Christians and were inaccessible, or Arabs would have been better guided if they had received one.

=156=

“Now you cannot say, ‘Scripture was revealed only to two communities before us, and we were unaware of what they studied.’”

The two communities are Jews and Christians. The Arab polytheists could claim earlier books came in other languages and histories, leaving them outside direct scriptural responsibility.

The Quran arrives in clear Arabic through a messenger whose life they know. The excuse of having no accessible revelation is removed for the first audience.

“Unaware of their study” acknowledges real barriers of language and access. Allah’s justice considers whether a person could understand, not merely whether a book existed somewhere in the world.

This principle should humble modern callers. A mistranslated fragment, abusive representation, or inaccessible technical lecture may not equal clear conveyance. Allah alone knows when proof reaches an individual fully.

The verse does not belittle Torah and Gospel as private texts for two tribes. It explains the Arabs’ claim and the mercy of receiving their own final revelation, which also confirms earlier truth.

Receiving Quran creates responsibility. Arabic speakers cannot turn linguistic access into pride while neglecting meaning. Greater access can establish a stronger case against heedlessness.

Later non-Arab Muslims receive its message through preserved Arabic, translation, scholarship, and global teaching. Universality does not require every person to become ethnically Arab.

Verse 157 blocks the second boast: “If a book came, we would be better guided.” Now clear proof, guidance, and mercy have arrived, so denial and obstruction become greater wrong.

=157=

“Nor can you say, ‘If Scripture had been revealed to us, we would have been better guided than they were.’ A clear proof, guidance, and mercy have now come to you from your Lord. Who is more unjust than one who denies Allah’s signs and turns away from them? We will repay those who turn away with a severe punishment for their rejection.”

It is easy to imagine oneself faithful in a test never faced. The Arabs could criticise earlier communities and claim superior memory or loyalty. The Quran turns the hypothetical into present responsibility.

Bayyinah makes truth clear; huda shows the road; rahmah invites rescue. Revelation is evidence, direction, and mercy together. Rejecting it loses all three.

Sadafa means turning away and can include turning others. The wrong is not merely failing to understand immediately but active rejection and obstruction after clarity.

The verse warns Muslims against the same comparative pride. Saying “we would never act like earlier nations” is hollow if Quranic duties are ignored when costly.

Severe punishment corresponds to repeated turning. Allah knows the difference between confusion seeking an answer and arrogance blocking the path.

Clear proof does not mean every individual receives it identically. Human judgment should avoid claiming final accountability over people whose access is unknown. The general warning remains true.

Communities can become barriers through poor translation, racism, scandal, or coercion. Those failures do not make revelation false, but may add responsibility to its custodians.

Verse 158 asks whether deniers wait for angels, the Lord’s judgment, or major signs. Once a decisive sign arrives, new faith and newly activated goodness no longer benefit someone who refused before.

=158=

“Are they waiting for angels to come to them, or for your Lord’s command to arrive, or for some major signs of your Lord? On the Day a major sign of your Lord comes, faith will not benefit a soul that did not believe before or had earned no good through its faith. Say, ‘Keep waiting; we too are waiting.’”

The verse names thresholds after which the test closes: angels arriving at death or judgment, Allah’s decisive command, and major signs that make the unseen unavoidable. Waiting for compulsion defeats the purpose of faith.

Authentic hadith identifies the sun rising from the west as a major sign after which new repentance and faith are not accepted. Other major signs belong to the Hour, but date-setting and sensational prediction remain forbidden.

The clause about one who “earned no good through its faith” warns that a dormant claim activated only after the decisive sign cannot suddenly create the life of obedience previously neglected. Classical explanations connect both unbeliever and complacent claimant to the closed threshold.

This does not mean believers are saved only by a quantity of deeds independent of mercy. Genuine faith normally bears some response, including repentance, while Allah knows inability and hidden good.

Death is each person’s practical threshold even before cosmic signs. A lifetime of delay can end without the dramatic event someone imagines will force change.

“Wait” is an ultimatum after proof, not passive uncertainty. The Prophet continues obeying while events disclose whose expectation was true.

The verse should motivate present action rather than obsessive study of portents. Pray, return rights, believe, and serve while the door is open.

Verse 159 separates the Prophet from those who fragment religion into sects. Their judgment belongs to Allah, who will disclose their deeds.

=159=

“Surely you have nothing to do with those who divide their religion and become sects. Their affair rests only with Allah; then He will inform them of what they used to do.”

The Prophet brought one path of tawhid and submission. People who fracture it around desire, rivalry, and selective belief cannot claim his endorsement merely by using religious names.

Shiya‘ are factions defined by partisan allegiance. The sin is not every organised group or disagreement, but making faction superior to revelation and severing the unity faith requires.

Valid schools of law developed methods for sincere interpretation and often recognised one another. Treating all madhhabs as condemned sects misuses the verse. Difference becomes blameworthy through arrogance, falsehood, and broken brotherhood.

Likewise, unity does not mean suppressing evidence or protecting abuse. A community can disagree firmly while maintaining justice and shared Islam.

“You have nothing to do with them” clears Muhammad of later distortions. It also warns factions not to claim exclusive prophetic ownership while contradicting his character.

Their affair belongs to Allah. Believers assess doctrines and outward acts where needed but should avoid assigning every individual an eternal fate. Intention, proof, and ending are His knowledge.

The promise that He will inform them means slogans will not settle the dispute. Contributions to division, hidden ambition, and sincere efforts will all be disclosed.

Practical unity grows through shared worship, accurate disagreement, refusal of takfir without proof, and cooperation in agreed good.

Verse 160 closes this passage with the scale of divine generosity: one good deed receives at least tenfold, while an evil deed receives only its equivalent, and no one is wronged.

=160=

“Whoever comes with a good deed will receive ten times its like, while whoever comes with an evil deed will be repaid only with its equivalent. None will be wronged.”

The minimum multiplication of accepted good is ten. Other texts describe seven hundredfold and reward without measure according to deed, intention, need, and Allah’s favour. Generosity exceeds strict equivalence.

An evil deed is written as one and not multiplied in basic recompense, though its seriousness can differ by sacred time, place, harm, persistence, and influence. “Equivalent” means no unjust addition.

The Prophet explained that intending a good deed can earn reward even before completion, and performing it brings multiplication. Intending evil and leaving it for Allah can be rewarded, while involuntary thoughts are pardoned.

“Comes with” suggests the deed reaches judgment. Repentance may erase sin, oppression may transfer good deeds, and disbelief can nullify saving reward. The final scale includes what remains after Allah’s full justice and mercy.

No one is wronged through a missing good, inflated evil, or another person’s guilt. Human courts lose evidence; Allah’s record and knowledge do not.

The verse encourages small good. A greeting, removed hazard, honest correction, or quiet charity can become far larger than its visible size. Delay waiting for a grand project may waste daily multiplication.

It also blocks despair. The scale is tilted toward mercy, but not toward carelessness. One evil can gravely harm people even if counted without numerical multiplication, and rights still require repair.
