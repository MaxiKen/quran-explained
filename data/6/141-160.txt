=141=

After exposing the absurdity of the pagan prohibitions, the surah redirects attention to Allah’s generous provision. “He is the One Who produces gardens—both cultivated and wild” points to the variety in creation. Cultivated gardens are those tended by human hands, with trellises and organized rows, while wild gardens spread without support, yet both flourish by Allah’s design.

This distinction shows His power working in both the structured and the seemingly chaotic, all under His command. Then specific plants are mentioned: “palm trees, crops of different flavours, olives, and pomegranates—similar in shape, but dissimilar in taste.” The date palm, staple of Arabia, yields sweet fruit; the crops include grains and vegetables of diverse tastes; olives provide oil and nourishment; pomegranates are a fruit that is alike in appearance yet varies in flavor from sweet to tart. This variation within similarity is a subtle sign: the same earth and water produce an astonishing range of flavors, colors, and textures. It is a daily miracle that points to the creative will of a Designer.

The verse then gives two commands: “Eat from the fruit they bear and pay the dues at harvest.” The first is permission to enjoy the lawful provision without the superstitious restrictions the pagans invented.

The second command establishes the obligation of charity: giving a portion of the harvest to the poor, an early form of zakāh on agricultural produce. The timing is specified—“at harvest”—meaning immediately, not delayed, so the poor receive their share fresh. This intertwines physical enjoyment with social responsibility, ensuring the blessings circulate.

Finally, “but do not waste. Surely He does not like the wasteful.” Extravagance and squandering are forbidden. The believer must balance eating with giving and avoid excess. Wastefulness is not merely poor management; it is a moral failure that Allah dislikes.

This verse teaches a complete economic ethic: recognize the divine source of provision, enjoy it lawfully, share with the needy promptly, and abstain from waste. It directly counters the pagan practices of dedicating crops to idols and imposing arbitrary restrictions, replacing them with gratitude, charity, and moderation.

=142=

The surah now addresses the livestock that the pagans had arbitrarily classified. “Some cattle are fit for labour, others are too small.

” The Arabic ḥamūla refers to large animals capable of bearing burdens and transport, like camels and adult cattle. Farsh refers to smaller animals that are too young or too weak for heavy work, kept closer to the ground, like calves and lambs. Both categories are part of Allah’s creation and provision, serving different purposes. The pagans had invented rules—some cattle were “exempted from labour” or dedicated to idols—but here Allah clarifies the natural function: large animals for work, smaller for other benefits like food. The command “Eat of what Allah has provided for you” is a clear permission to consume the meat and other products of these animals, directly countering the false prohibitions. The provision is from Allah, not from idols, so human rules that forbid what He has made lawful are invalid.

The verse then warns: “and do not follow Satan’s footsteps.” The footsteps of Satan are the gradual, subtle steps that lead away from Allah’s commands—first a doubt, then a small innovation, then a major deviation. The pagan practices of prohibiting certain cattle and dedicating them to idols were precisely such satanic deceptions, dressed in the guise of piety.

The verse concludes, “Certainly, he is your sworn enemy. ” Satan’s enmity is not passive; he has pledged to mislead humanity. One of his primary strategies is to make people alter what Allah has permitted and forbidden, thereby corrupting the religion.

This verse reminds believers that dietary and agricultural laws must be sourced from revelation alone, not from custom or satanic whispers. It also emphasizes the importance of seeing Satan as a clear enemy whose suggestions must be actively resisted.

The mention of cattle as a provision also reinforces the theme of gratitude: the animals that carry burdens and provide food are gifts, not objects of superstitious reverence.

=143=

The Quran now deconstructs the irrationality of the pagan prohibitions regarding livestock by engaging in a logical challenge. “Allah has created four pairs” refers to eight domestic animals mentioned in pairs: sheep, goats, camels, and oxen (the latter two in the next verse).

This verse begins with “a pair of sheep and a pair of goats”—the male and female of each species are a pair within their kind, making four individuals. The Prophet is instructed to ask the pagans a precise question about their arbitrary rules: “Has He forbidden to you the two males or the two females or what is in the wombs of the two females? ” Their prohibitions were often based on gender—they allowed males for food but forbade females, or forbade the young in the womb. By posing the question, Allah exposes the lack of any consistent divine basis. If Allah had forbidden something, was it the males specifically? Or the females? Or the unborn? The pagans could not produce any coherent revelation to support their claims; their rules were contradictory and varied from tribe to tribe.

The phrase “Tell me with knowledge, if what you say is true” is a challenge to provide genuine revealed proof. It calls them out for speaking about Allah without authority. This method of questioning demonstrates the Quranic principle that religious claims must be backed by evidence, not speculation. It teaches believers to examine the basis of religious rulings critically, adhering only to what is authentically from Allah.

The verse also shows the mercy of Allah in providing livestock for human benefit without arbitrary restrictions, freeing people from the chains of man-made religious systems. The challenge remains open for all who invent prohibitions in the name of God: produce your evidence or acknowledge your falsehood.

=144=

Completing the four pairs, the verse adds “a pair of camels and a pair of oxen.” The camel was the most valuable animal for the Arabs, and the ox was essential for agriculture. Together, these eight animals constituted the backbone of their pastoral economy. The same question is repeated: “Has He forbidden to you the two males or the two females or what is in the wombs of the two females? ” The repetition hammers home the point that across all types of livestock, the pagan prohibitions lack any logical or revealed foundation. Then an even sharper question is added: “Or were you present when Allah gave you this commandment?

” This is a sarcastic challenge; they were not witnesses to any revelation, so where did they get these laws? It exposes the absurdity of claiming divine authority for something that was never revealed.

The verse then delivers a powerful rhetorical condemnation: “Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah to mislead others without any knowledge?” Fabricating laws and attributing them to God is the height of wrongdoing because it corrupts religion, oppresses people by prohibiting what Allah made lawful, and leads multitudes astray. Such people lack “any knowledge”—their claims are rooted in ignorance, custom, and satanic whispers.

The final sentence, “Surely Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people,” means that those who persist in such fabrications will be left astray and will not find the path to truth. This verse completes the logical dismantling of the pagan dietary system. It reinforces the principle that only Allah has the right to legislate, and that speaking about religion without knowledge is a grievous sin. It also serves as a protection for the believers, encouraging them to demand proof for any religious ruling and to reject what is based on mere assumption.

=145=

After refuting the pagan prohibitions, the Prophet is instructed to state the actual divine prohibitions regarding food, which are remarkably few. “I do not find in what has been revealed to me anything forbidden to eat except...” indicates that the default ruling for food is permissibility; only specific items are haram.

The first prohibition is “carrion” (maytah): any animal that dies naturally without proper slaughter. The second is “running blood” (daman masfūḥan): blood that is poured out, not the trace amounts in meat.

The third is “swine—which is impure” (rijs): the pig is inherently unclean, and its consumption is forbidden. The fourth is “a sinful offering in the name of any other than Allah”: any meat slaughtered with a name other than Allah invoked, which is a direct violation of tawhid. These four categories encapsulate the core dietary laws of Islam, later expanded in Surah Al-Ma'idah to include strangled animals, those killed by a blow, by a fall, by goring, or by wild beasts unless properly slaughtered.

The verse then provides the exception of necessity: “But if someone is compelled by necessity—neither driven by desire nor exceeding immediate need—then surely your Lord is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. ” If a person is facing starvation and has no other food, they may eat the forbidden items, but only enough to survive. The conditions are strict: they must not desire the forbidden food or exceed the minimum needed.

The mention of Allah’s forgiveness and mercy at the end emphasizes that He does not wish hardship; the prohibition is lifted in genuine need. This verse is a foundational text for Islamic dietary law, establishing the principle that what is halal is vast, what is haram is limited, and mercy prevails in emergencies. It liberates the believers from the complex, arbitrary food taboos of the pagans and other religious systems, calling them back to the pure, simple, and rational guidance of Allah.

=146=

In connection with the dietary laws, the Quran mentions some specific prohibitions that were imposed on the Jews as a punishment. “For those who are Jewish, We forbade every animal with undivided hoofs.” Animals with undivided hooves, like camels and certain birds, were forbidden to them, unlike the general Islamic ruling that permits them. “And the fat of oxen and sheep except what is joined to their backs or intestines or mixed with bone. ” The pure fat of cattle and sheep was also prohibited, except the fat attached to the back, the internal fat around the intestines, or fat intermixed with bone that could not be easily separated. These additional restrictions were not part of the original Abrahamic law but were imposed later as a consequence of their transgressions. The verse explains the reason: “In this way We rewarded them for their violations.

” The word “rewarded” is used ironically; their defiance of divine commands led to a tightening of the permissible, making their lives more difficult. This is detailed in Surah Al-Nisa’ where Allah says that for the wrongdoings of the Jews, He made unlawful for them certain good things that had been lawful to them.

The verse concludes, “And We are certainly truthful.” This assertion is a response to any denial from the Jews who might claim these prohibitions were original or that they did not transgress. Allah affirms the truth of this historical account.

The mention of this serves a dual purpose: it shows the Meccans that their arbitrary prohibitions resemble the punishment of the Jews and are not a mark of honor, and it warns the Muslims that disobedience can lead to restrictions being placed upon them as a trial. It also demonstrates the principle that the original law given to the prophets was one of ease and mercy, and it was only human stubbornness that brought about harsher rulings.

=147=

Addressing the Prophet after recounting these legal and historical truths, Allah prepares him for the persistent denial of his opponents. “But if they deny you” refers to the polytheists who reject the message despite all the clear arguments. The Prophet is instructed on what to say: “Your Lord is infinite in mercy.

” This statement opens with hope, reminding both the Prophet and the deniers that Allah’s mercy is vast and encompassing. It implies that their rejection does not immediately bring destruction because Allah, in His mercy, grants them respite and the opportunity to repent.

However, the second part balances this with a severe warning: “yet His punishment will not be averted from the wicked people.” The “wicked people” (al-qawm al-mujrimūn) are those who persist in their crimes of shirk, denial, and oppression. While Allah’s mercy is vast, it does not nullify His justice. When the time for punishment arrives, no one can deflect it. The verse combines the attributes of mercy and justice in a single statement, reflecting the Quranic balance. It consoles the Prophet that the delay in punishment is not weakness but a manifestation of divine mercy, while also reassuring him that the criminals will not escape forever. For the believers, this verse instills a balanced understanding of Allah: we should never despair of His mercy, but we must also never feel secure from His punishment.

The verse also teaches a method of da‘wah: when met with denial, respond by reminding people of Allah’s mercy as an invitation, but also warn of the consequences for those who insist on wickedness. It is a statement that leaves the door open while drawing a clear line.

=148=

The surah now exposes a common but flawed argument used by the polytheists to justify their shirk and prohibitions. They would say: “Had it been Allah’s Will, neither we nor our forefathers would have associated others with Him in worship or made anything unlawful.” They argued that since Allah is all-powerful and allowed them to practice polytheism, it must mean He was pleased with it.

This is a classic attempt to use divine decree as an excuse for sin. The verse immediately refutes this by drawing a historical parallel: “Likewise, those before them rejected the truth until they tasted Our punishment.” Previous nations used similar arguments, but their rationalization did not save them when the punishment arrived. The divine will does not indicate approval; it merely encompasses the permission for them to exercise their free will, leading to accountability. Then the Prophet is instructed to challenge them directly: “Do you have any knowledge that you can produce for us? ” They are asked to bring forth any proof, any revelation, any evidence that their practices were sanctioned by Allah. They have none.

The verse concludes with a blunt assessment: “Surely you follow nothing but false assumptions and you do nothing but lie.” Their entire religious system is built on conjecture and inherited falsehood, not on truth. This verse is a crucial lesson in theology: divine decree is never a valid excuse for disobedience. The fact that Allah allows something to happen does not mean He commands it or is pleased with it. It also teaches the importance of grounding faith in revealed knowledge rather than supposition or cultural tradition. The believers are called to base their beliefs on certainty, not assumption.

=149=

After exposing the emptiness of the polytheists’ argument, the Prophet is instructed to deliver the final word on the matter. “Allah has the most conclusive argument” (al-ḥujjah al-bālighah) means that the definitive, overwhelming proof belongs to Him. He has sent messengers, revealed books, and placed signs in creation and in the human soul. No one can argue against Allah and succeed. The polytheists’ use of divine decree as a justification is itself a proof against them, because that decree includes the sending of messengers and the granting of intellect and free will; their rejection is their own choice, not a compulsion from Allah.

The verse continues: “Had it been His Will, He would have easily imposed guidance upon all of you.” Allah is fully capable of making all human beings believers. He could create them without free will, or compel their hearts to accept faith. The fact that He did not do so is part of His wisdom. He willed to test humanity by giving them choice, making the path of guidance clear, and then rewarding or punishing based on their response.

This is the essence of the test. Their argument that He could have forced them misses the point of life’s purpose.

The verse teaches that Allah’s will is not arbitrary; it includes the establishment of a system of accountability. If He had wished to bypass this system, He could have. But His wisdom dictated a world of free will, where the conclusive argument is established through revelation, leaving no excuse.

This is a powerful response to fatalism: yes, Allah could have made everyone guided, but He chose to make guidance a choice, and that very choice is the basis of His conclusive argument. It leaves the deniers with no defense.

=150=

The challenge to the polytheists is now intensified. They are commanded: “Bring your witnesses who can testify that Allah has forbidden this.” The “this” refers to the cattle and crops they had declared unlawful. They are asked to produce reliable witnesses—whether angels, prophets, or scriptures—who can confirm that Allah decreed those prohibitions. Of course, they have none. Their only “witnesses” are their own customs, their forefathers, and the devils who whispered to them.

The verse then instructs the Prophet: “If they falsely testify, do not testify with them.” Even if they bring forward someone who claims to witness, it is false, and the Prophet is forbidden from endorsing their fabrication. He must remain completely separate from their lies. The command extends to all believers: never validate a false religious claim, even by passive acceptance.

The verse then adds a broader principle: “And do not follow the desires of those who deny Our proofs, disbelieve in the Hereafter, and set up equals with their Lord.” This identifies the three characteristics of such people: they reject the signs of Allah, they deny the afterlife, and they commit shirk. Their desires are the source of their false laws, and following those desires leads to straying. This verse is a comprehensive directive for maintaining the purity of faith. It commands the believers to demand evidence for religious rulings, to refuse to participate in falsehood, and to avoid being swayed by the whims of those whose worldview is opposed to revelation. It highlights that false religion often stems from human desires rather than divine instruction. The believer must be vigilant to adhere strictly to what Allah has revealed, no matter how many people claim otherwise.

=151=

After exposing the false prohibitions, the Prophet is commanded to recite the true, fundamental prohibitions from the Lord. The opening “Come! Let me recite to you what your Lord has forbidden to you” is an invitation, making the listener lean in.

This is not a list of arbitrary dietary rules but the foundational moral code. The first and most critical prohibition is “do not associate others with Him in worship.” Tawhid is the cornerstone; shirk is the gravest sin.

The second is “honour your parents.” The Arabic iḥsānan implies excellence, kindness, and care, far beyond mere obedience. Parents are next to Allah in the hierarchy of rights.

The third is “do not kill your children for fear of poverty.” This directly condemns the horrific pagan practice of infanticide, especially of daughters. The reassurance follows immediately: “We provide for you and for them. ” Allah is the Provider; fear of poverty is a false justification for murder. The fourth is “do not come near indecencies, openly or secretly.” This prohibits all forms of sexual immorality and shameless behavior, whether public or private. The phrasing “do not come near” blocks the paths leading to sin. The fifth is “do not take a human life—made sacred by Allah—except with legal right.” This establishes the sanctity of life and restricts lawful killing to specific judicial cases, such as retribution for murder.

The verse concludes, “This is what He has commanded you, so perhaps you will understand.” These prohibitions are meant to be pondered; they appeal to reason and protect the essential fabric of human society. This verse is one of the most comprehensive summaries of Islamic ethics, linking the rights of Allah with the rights of fellow humans. It demonstrates that true religion is not about superstitious food taboos but about justice, mercy, and monotheism.

=152=

The enumeration of divine commandments continues with a focus on social justice and integrity. “And do not come near the wealth of the orphan—unless intending to enhance it—until they attain maturity.” The orphan’s property is sacred. The guardian is forbidden from touching it for personal benefit; the only exception is to manage it in a way that improves or protects it. Once the orphan reaches adulthood and demonstrates sound judgment, the wealth is handed over. This protects the most vulnerable members of society.

The next command is “Give full measure and weigh with justice.” Fair dealing in trade and business is a religious obligation. Cheating in weights and measures is not merely a civil offense but a sin against Allah.

The phrase “We never require of any soul more than what it can afford” provides a merciful balance: striving for perfect justice is required, but Allah does not burden a soul beyond its capacity. If a slight error occurs despite sincere effort, there is leniency. Then, “Whenever you speak, maintain justice—even regarding a close relative. ” Speech must be truthful and just, even if it means testifying against one’s own family. Personal relationships must not distort the truth. The command “And fulfil your covenant with Allah” encompasses all pledges made to God, including the primordial covenant of faith, as well as oaths and vows. Breaking a covenant is a sign of hypocrisy. The verse closes with “This is what He has commanded you, so perhaps you will be mindful.

” The word “mindful” (tadhakkarūn) implies that these truths are already inscribed in the human conscience; the command serves as a reminder. This verse establishes the fundamental principles of economic justice, honesty in speech, and loyalty to commitments. It forms the ethical backbone of a righteous community, protecting the weak and demanding accountability from all.

=153=

After listing the major commandments, Allah identifies them as the Straight Path. “Indeed, that is My Path—perfectly straight.” The pronoun “that” encompasses all the preceding commands: monotheism, honoring parents, protecting life, chastity, justice, and fulfilling covenants.

This is the Path of Allah, described as “perfectly straight” (mustaqīman)—without deviation, leading directly to Him. The command “So follow it” is an exclusive directive; cling to this Path alone. The warning immediately follows: “and do not follow other ways, for they will lead you away from His Way.” The “other ways” (al-subul) refer to the various paths of innovation, sects, and false religions that branch off from the straight path. The Prophet once drew a straight line in the sand and said, “This is the path of Allah. ” Then he drew lines branching off to the right and left, saying, “These are other paths, and on each path is a devil calling to it.” The message is clear: unity on the straight path is essential, and division into divergent ways leads to misguidance. The verse closes with “This is what He has commanded you, so perhaps you will be conscious of Allah.” Taqwā, God-consciousness, is the ultimate goal of these commandments. By following the straight path, the believer develops a constant awareness of Allah, which in turn protects them from straying. This verse is a powerful call for Muslim unity based on the core divine commands, avoiding sectarianism and innovation. It teaches that true guidance is not found in the multiplicity of human opinions but in the single, clear path laid out by Allah in the Quran and taught by the Prophet.

=154=

The surah now connects the previous commandments with the earlier scriptures, showing the continuity of divine guidance. “Additionally, We gave Moses the Scripture” refers to the Torah, bestowed after the great favors shown to the Children of Israel, such as their deliverance from Pharaoh. The Torah is described as “completing the favour upon those who do good.” The iḥsān—excellence in faith and conduct—was manifest in the righteous among them, and the Scripture was a perfection of the blessing upon them. “Detailing everything” means the Torah contained detailed laws for their specific time and nation, explaining the halal and haram, the moral and the legal. “And as a guide and a mercy” indicates that its purpose was to lead them to righteousness and to provide them with a merciful framework for life. The ultimate goal was “so perhaps they would be certain of the meeting with their Lord.” The Torah’s teachings, its promises and warnings, were all aimed at instilling in their hearts the certainty of the Day of Judgment and the return to Allah. This verse implicitly corrects the Jewish claim that they alone have the truth and that nothing else is needed. The Torah was a mercy for its time, but it pointed to the same realities: monotheism, moral law, and the Hereafter. By mentioning Moses, the Quran reminds the Meccans that the Torah, which they had heard about from the Jews, contained these very commands, and the Quran confirms and completes them. It also teaches that all scriptures share the essential purpose of guiding to certainty in the meeting with the Lord, which is the anchor of moral life.

=155=

From the mention of the Torah, the attention shifts to the Quran itself. “This is a blessed Book We have revealed.

” The word “blessed” (mubārak) means it is full of continuous, abundant goodness. It brings spiritual healing, guidance, light, and reward. Its blessings extend to every aspect of life, both individual and communal. The fact that it is revealed by Allah is the source of its blessing; it is not of human authorship. The command “So follow it” is comprehensive—recite it, understand it, implement its rulings, and embody its ethics. Following the Book is not a passive acknowledgment but an active conformity of one’s life to its guidance. “And be mindful of Allah” (ittaqū) means to develop taqwa, a protective consciousness of Allah that prevents sin.

The sequence is crucial: following the Book naturally leads to taqwa, and taqwa reinforces the commitment to the Book. The purpose is stated: “so you may be shown mercy.” The mercy here is the ultimate divine mercy—forgiveness in this life, a good life under the shade of guidance, and entry into Paradise in the Hereafter.

The phrase “be shown mercy” (turḥamūn) uses the passive voice, indicating that mercy is a gift from Allah, not something one earns automatically, though it is connected to following the Book and having taqwa. This verse serves as a powerful summary of the believer’s relationship with the Quran: it is a blessed divine gift that demands adherence, cultivates piety, and leads to the all-encompassing mercy of Allah. It connects the surah’s earlier discussions of guidance and misguidance to a clear, actionable conclusion.

=156=

The Quran now anticipates and blocks the excuses of the Arab polytheists regarding their lack of a revealed scripture. “You pagans can no longer say...

” The Arabic phrase “an taqūlū” indicates that the Quran’s revelation removes a specific claim. The excuse was: “Scriptures were only revealed to two groups before us”—the Jews and the Christians—“and we were unaware of their teachings. ” The Arabs could argue that they had no prophet of their own, no book in their own language, and that the scriptures of the earlier communities were in foreign languages and had been distorted or were inaccessible.

Therefore, they might claim they were not accountable. This verse cuts off that excuse. The Quran has now been revealed in “a clear Arabic tongue,” addressed directly to them, through a prophet from their own lineage. The excuse of ignorance is no longer valid. The revelation is fresh, complete, and preserved. This demonstrates the justice of Allah: no people will be punished without a clear messenger speaking their language. The Arabs, who were previously in a state of jāhiliyyah, have now been given the ultimate guidance. For later generations, the principle holds: once the message of Islam reaches a person clearly and without distortion, the excuse of ignorance is removed.

The verse also implicitly urges the pagans to seize the opportunity; the door of mercy has opened, and they should not let pride or tradition cause them to be among those who reject this final, clear scripture.

=157=

A second possible excuse is blocked. The Arabs might have said: “If only the Scriptures had been revealed to us, we would have been better guided than they.” This reflects a certain arrogance; they believed that because of their intelligence, their memory, or their perceived virtues, they would have followed a scripture more faithfully than the Jews and Christians who fell into corruption. But this was an untested hypothesis. Now the scripture has indeed come, and their reaction is the true test. “Now there has come to you from your Lord a clear proof—a guide and mercy.” The “clear proof” (bayyinah) is the Quran itself, supported by the Prophet’s miracles and character. It is a “guide” leading to the straight path and a “mercy” rescuing from destruction. The arrival of this proof transforms the hypothetical into the actual. Their response will now reveal their true character.

The verse then poses a rhetorical question: “Who then does more wrong than those who deny Allah’s revelations and turn away from them?” Denial and turning away after such a clear proof is the ultimate wrongdoing because it is a conscious rejection of the truth that has been made manifest. The threat follows: “We will reward those who turn away from Our revelations with a dreadful punishment for turning away.” The punishment is explicitly linked to the act of turning away (ṣadd), which implies not just disbelief but actively distancing oneself and others from the truth. This verse serves as a profound reflection on human self-justification. People often imagine they would be better believers if only circumstances were different. Allah provides the circumstances and the guidance, and then one’s true nature is exposed. It is a call to humble oneself and accept the mercy rather than clinging to excuses and pride.

=158=

After blocking the excuses, the verse warns about the finality of the test. “Are they awaiting the coming of the angels, or your Lord Himself, or some of your Lord’s major signs?” The disbelievers were demanding visible, dramatic signs instead of accepting the Quran. They wanted angels to appear, or for Allah to manifest openly, or for the major signs of the Hour to arrive. This was not a sincere request but a form of procrastination and mockery. The verse warns that when these major signs do arrive, it will be too late. “On the Day your Lord’s signs arrive, belief will not benefit those who did not believe earlier or those who did no good through their faith.” This refers to the established divine law that when the great, compelling signs—such as the rising of the sun from the west or the appearance of the major portents of the Hour—manifest, the door of repentance closes. Faith at that moment is like a drowning person’s desperate cry; it is not genuine free-willed acceptance but a forced recognition, and it is not accepted. Similarly, for those who already believed but did not accumulate righteous deeds, the sudden arrival of these signs will be a time of judgment, not an opportunity to make up for past negligence. The Prophet is then instructed to deliver a parting statement: “Say, ‘Keep waiting! We too are waiting.’” This is a final ultimatum, leaving the matter between them and the future. It is not passive but a statement of firm expectation: we are waiting for Allah’s promise to unfold, and you will see who was right. This verse is a powerful motivator to seize the present moment for faith and good deeds, without delay.

=159=

The surah now addresses a critical issue: religious division. “Indeed, you are not responsible whatsoever for those who have divided their faith and split into sects.

” The phrase “divided their faith” (farraqū dīnahum) refers to those who took the originally pure religion and fragmented it, creating differing denominations, each claiming to have the truth while deviating from the original message. This was a characteristic of the People of the Book, but it is also a warning for the Muslim community. The Prophet is absolved of any responsibility for their division; his duty was to deliver the unified, clear message.

The verse then states, “Their judgment rests only with Allah.” The judgment of such people, who cause divisions and sects, is a matter for Allah alone. He will decide their fate based on their intentions and the extent of their deviation. The Prophet and the believers are not to usurp this role by condemning individuals to Hell or making absolute pronouncements about their eternal destiny; they should refer the matter to Allah. “And He will inform them of what they used to do” means that on the Day of Judgment, Allah will reveal to them the reality of their deeds and their consequences. This verse serves as a strong prohibition against sectarianism and the fragmentation of the Muslim ummah. It calls for unity upon the Straight Path mentioned in verse 153 and warns that creating sects is a serious deviation that falls under divine judgment. It also teaches humility in dealing with those who differ: ultimate judgment is with Allah, and while we uphold the truth, we do not assume the role of the divine judge over individuals.

=160=

The batch ends with a verse of immense hope and justice. “Whoever comes with a good deed will be rewarded tenfold.

” The Arabic “bi’l-ḥasanah” (a good deed) refers to any righteous act performed with sincerity and in accordance with the Sunnah. The minimum multiplication is ten times, but the Quran elsewhere indicates it can be multiplied up to seven hundred times or even more, depending on the quality of the deed, the purity of intention, and Allah’s boundless generosity. This multiplication is a manifestation of divine mercy and encouragement. “But whoever comes with a bad deed will be punished for only one.” The bad deed (sayyi’ah) is punished with its exact equivalent, without any amplification.

This is pure justice. The verse does not mention that a bad intention without action is punished, whereas a good intention is rewarded even if not acted upon, as known from hadith. The balance here is staggering: the mercy outweighs the justice by a factor of ten.

The verse concludes, “None will be wronged.” There is no injustice in this system; no one will be punished more than they deserve, and no one will be deprived of the reward they have earned. This verse is one of the most hope-inspiring in the Quran. It reveals that the scales are heavily tilted in favor of the believer. It encourages us to never belittle any good deed, knowing that its return can be multiplied far beyond our imagination. At the same time, it warns against bad deeds, but without despair, as the punishment is limited to the act’s measure. This beautiful principle rounds off the passage with a call to action grounded in trust in Allah’s overwhelming mercy and perfect justice.
