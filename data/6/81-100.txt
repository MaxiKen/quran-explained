=81=

After Abraham's confident dismissal of the idols' power to harm, he flips the argument entirely. The question "And how should I fear your associate-gods" is a rhetorical challenge that exposes the absurdity of their threats. The idols are lifeless and powerless; fearing them is irrational. But then Abraham points to a much more terrifying reality: "while you have no fear in associating others with Allah. " The polytheists were calm and untroubled about committing shirk, the gravest sin, yet they expected Abraham to fear their fabricated deities.

The phrase "a practice He has never authorized" highlights that their worship lacks any divine sanction; it is based on pure conjecture and inherited custom. Abraham then poses a penetrating question: "Which side has more right to security?" This invites the people to think logically: who truly deserves to feel safe—the one who worships the Creator who controls all things, or the one who worships created objects that can neither harm nor benefit? The implied answer is obvious, but Abraham pushes them further: "Tell me if you really know!

" This is a call to produce genuine knowledge, not blind tradition. The verse demonstrates that true security is tied to truth. A believer whose heart rests in tawhid experiences inner peace, while the polytheist, despite outward bravado, lives with anxiety, trying to appease many gods, never sure of their acceptance. Abraham’s argument is a masterpiece of shifting the fear from the powerless to the real source of power. It connects directly to the following verse, which supplies the divine verdict on who exactly merits that security and guidance.

=82=

This verse delivers the definitive answer to Abraham's question. "It is only those who are faithful and do not tarnish their faith with falsehood" identifies the people entitled to security. The word "falsehood" (ẓulm) has a specific meaning here. When this verse was revealed, the companions were deeply distressed, wondering which of them had not mixed faith with some form of wrongdoing. The Prophet clarified this by referring them to the verse in Surah Luqman: "Indeed, shirk is a great wrongdoing. " The "falsehood" here refers specifically to shirk, associating partners with Allah, which is the ultimate injustice that corrupts pure faith. Faith that is mixed with shirk is like pure water mixed with poison—it is spoiled. Those who maintain pure tawhid, untainted by any form of idolatry, are "guaranteed security." This security encompasses freedom from fear in this world and the Hereafter; they will be safe from eternal punishment and from the terrors of the Day of Judgment.

Additionally, they "are rightly guided." Guidance is not merely intellectual knowledge but the divine enablement to walk the straight path that leads to salvation. The verse thus ties security and guidance together as fruits of unblemished faith. It serves as a powerful reassurance for the believers facing persecution in Mecca: their present fear is temporary, but their ultimate security is assured. For all generations, it underscores the absolute necessity of guarding one's tawhid from any adulteration, whether it be blatant idol worship, showing off, or relying on causes rather than the Causer. True, lasting peace is the exclusive reward of the monotheists.

=83=

After recounting Abraham's logical triumph, Allah identifies its source. "This was the argument We gave Abraham against his people." The rational proof that demolished the astral worship and established monotheism was not merely Abraham's personal brilliance; it was a divine gift, a clear evidence bestowed upon him by Allah. This teaches that true wisdom and effective reasoning in matters of faith are granted by Allah, not acquired solely through human intellect. Then the verse states a universal principle: "We elevate in rank whoever We please. " The elevation here refers to ranks in knowledge, wisdom, prophethood, and honor. Abraham was elevated above his people because Allah willed it, based on His wisdom. This counters the Meccan chiefs' assumption that leadership and status should belong to them by lineage or wealth. Spiritual elevation is a divine choice, often granted to those who appear lowly in the world's eyes.

The verse concludes with "Surely your Lord is All-Wise, All-Knowing," affirming that this selection is based on perfect wisdom and comprehensive knowledge. Allah knows who is deserving of spiritual rank and who will use that elevation for guidance. The verse links back to the earlier discussion about the poor believers being favored with faith, while the arrogant leaders were bypassed. It reassures the Prophet and his followers that their guidance is a sign of divine elevation, and the rejection of the Quraysh is a result of Allah's just decree, rooted in His infinite knowledge of hearts.

=84=

From the story of Abraham's argument, the surah broadens to mention the blessings Allah bestowed upon him and his progeny. "And We blessed him with Isaac and Jacob" refers to the gift of righteous offspring, a direct answer to Abraham's prayers. Isaac was his son, and Jacob his grandson, both prophets. Then the verse expands the circle: "We guided them all as We previously guided Noah." The chain of guidance is continuous. Noah is mentioned as the earlier ancestor from whom this line of prophets descends. Then a list of illustrious prophets from his descendants is given: David, Solomon, Job, Joseph, Moses, and Aaron. Each of these was a beacon of guidance, facing different trials and demonstrating distinct virtues—David's kingdom and psalms, Solomon's wisdom and dominion, Job's patience, Joseph's chastity and forgiveness, Moses' deliverance of a nation, and Aaron's eloquent support. The verse declares, "This is how We reward the good-doers. " The reward for their excellence in faith and deeds was not only Paradise but also prophethood, guidance, and a noble mention for all generations. It underscores that being a "good-doer" (muḥsin) attracts divine favors that extend beyond oneself to one's progeny.

This is a source of immense hope for believers: sincere goodness is recognized and rewarded by Allah in this world and the next, and it can bless one's family for generations. The mention of these prophets also serves to remind the Quraysh that the same message they reject was carried by these towering figures they claimed to respect.

=85=

Continuing the roll of honored prophets, the Quran mentions another group connected to the Israelite lineage. "Likewise, We guided Zachariah, John, Jesus, and Elias." Zachariah was the righteous priest and guardian of Mary, who prayed for a son and was given John in his old age. John (Yahya) was a prophet of purity, wisdom, and asceticism, preparing the way for Jesus. Jesus (Isa) was the Messiah, born miraculously, given the Gospel, and a sign for all people. Elias (Ilyas) was sent to the people of Baalbek who worshipped a false idol, and he called them back to monotheism. The verse describes them all as "of the righteous" (min al-ṣāliḥīn).

The term "righteous" encompasses both correct belief and excellent conduct. They were not merely guides in words but embodied the message in their lives. This grouping highlights that prophethood is a chain of righteousness spanning different times and circumstances, all united in the call to worship Allah alone. Significantly, the verse places Jesus among the prophets, subtly correcting the later Christian elevation of him to divinity. He was a righteous prophet, guided by Allah, not a god. For the believers, this litany of names serves to strengthen their connection to a global history of faith, showing that the struggles they face in Mecca are part of a timeless story. It also demonstrates the diversity of prophetic missions—some had kingdoms, others lived in asceticism, some were persecuted, others were believed—yet all were righteous and guided.

=86=

The list of prophets extends to include more familiar names. "We also guided Ishmael, Elisha, Jonah, and Lot." Ishmael (Ismail) was the first son of Abraham, the ancestor of the Arabs, who was dedicated to Allah and assisted his father in building the Kaaba. Elisha (Al-Yasa‘) was a prophet who continued the mission of Elias among the Israelites. Jonah (Yunus) was sent to Nineveh; his story of being swallowed by the whale and later repenting is a profound lesson in patience and divine mercy. Lot (Lut) was the nephew of Abraham, sent to the people of Sodom, whose story is a warning against moral corruption.

The verse adds "favouring each over other people of their time." This indicates that Allah granted them a special rank and excellence above their contemporaries, not necessarily above other prophets, but over the people to whom they were sent. It underscores that prophethood is a divine choice that elevates an individual from among their own folk, yet they remain human.

This distinction is important: the prophets were not superhuman beings but chosen humans honored with divine favor. The Meccans, who were familiar with the stories of Ishmael and Lot, are reminded that these respected figures were guided by Allah alone, and their guidance was not due to idol worship but to pure monotheism. This implicitly calls the Quraysh to follow the religion of their ancestor Ishmael rather than the innovations they had introduced.

=87=

After listing specific prophets, the verse broadens the scope of divine favor to include the wider circle of the prophets' families. "And We favoured some of their forefathers, their descendants, and their brothers." This indicates that not only the prophets themselves but also many among their ancestors, children, and siblings were recipients of divine favor—whether by receiving faith, righteousness, or even prophethood itself. The line of prophethood often ran through blessed families, such as the family of Abraham, the family of Imran, and the family of David.

The phrase "We chose them" emphasizes active divine election. They were selected by Allah's knowledge and will, not by human election. Then, "and guided them to the Straight Path" clarifies the content of this favor: they were given unwavering direction to the path of pure monotheism and righteous action. The Straight Path (al-Ṣirāṭ al-Mustaqīm) is the same path that the Prophet Muhammad called to, linking all prophetic missions into one continuous highway of divine guidance. This verse counters any notion that prophethood is a random event; it is part of a deliberate, familial pattern, yet still a choice based on Allah's wisdom. For the believers, it fosters respect for the prophetic families and an understanding that closeness to a prophet is an honor, but true nobility comes from being chosen for guidance. It also suggests that the righteous environment of a prophetic household contributes to the guidance of relatives, though ultimate guidance is from Allah.

=88=

The surah now explicitly attributes all the previous guidance to its divine source. "This is Allah’s guidance with which He guides whoever He wills of His servants." The entire chain of prophets and their followers were guided solely by Allah’s will and grace.

The phrase "whoever He wills" reaffirms that guidance is a divine gift, not an automatic inheritance or a reward for human effort alone. It is given according to Allah's wisdom and mercy. This serves as both a reminder of humility for the guided and a warning against assuming one's own righteousness is the cause. Then a stark warning follows: "Had they associated others with Him in worship, their good deeds would have been wasted." Even these great prophets—if hypothetically they had committed shirk—would have found all their righteous acts nullified. This emphasizes the absolute centrality of tawhid. Good deeds, no matter how numerous or impressive, are invalid if the foundation of faith is corrupted by associating partners with Allah.

This is a direct message to the Meccans: their claims of following Abraham are worthless as long as they worship idols, because Abraham himself was a pure monotheist, and if he had been a polytheist, his works would have been in vain. The verse instills in the believer a vigilant protectiveness over their tawhid, recognizing that it is the condition for any deed to be accepted. It also highlights the severity of shirk, which can obliterate a lifetime of devotion.

=89=

The verse summarizes the blessings bestowed upon the mentioned prophets. "Those were the ones to whom We gave the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood." The Scripture refers to the revealed books like the Torah, Psalms, and Gospel. Wisdom (ḥukm) encompasses sound judgment, understanding of the law, and the ability to adjudicate justly. Prophethood is the highest honor. These three gifts together represent the complete package of divine guidance. Then the discourse shifts to the present: "But if these pagans disbelieve in this message"—referring to the Quran brought by Muhammad—"then We have already entrusted it to a people who will never disbelieve in it.

" This is a powerful warning and a promise. The pagans of Mecca are told that their rejection will not harm the truth; if they turn away, Allah has prepared another community that will accept and preserve the message. This primarily refers to the Companions—the Muhajirun and Ansar—who embraced Islam wholeheartedly and carried it forward. It also encompasses all subsequent generations of sincere believers until the Day of Judgment.

The phrase "never disbelieve in it" indicates their firm, unwavering faith. This verse comforted the Prophet by assuring him that the truth would find its worthy bearers, even if his own tribe rejected it. It also teaches that the cause of Islam is not dependent on any particular nation or group; if some abandon it, Allah raises others to uphold it, guaranteeing the preservation of His religion.

=90=

After listing eighteen prophets, Allah commands the Prophet Muhammad to emulate them. "These prophets were rightly guided by Allah, so follow their guidance." The guidance referred to is the core message of tawhid, upright character, patience, and trust in Allah. The Prophet is being told to follow in their footsteps, making his own mission a continuation of theirs. The command "follow their guidance" implies that the essential religion is the same; the details of laws differ, but the foundational creed and ethics are one.

This is a source of unity and strength. Then the Prophet is instructed to declare his disinterest in material gain: "Say, 'I ask no reward of you for this Quran—it is a reminder to the whole world.'" He seeks no financial compensation, no status, no power.

This is a hallmark of a true prophet, as all messengers declared the same. The Quran is a "reminder" (dhikrā), a universal call to awaken humanity to the truth they instinctively know. "To the whole world" (lil-ʿālamīn) emphasizes that the message is not limited to the Arabs but is global and for all times. This verse links the Meccan surahs' repeated denial of any material motive behind the Prophet's call, underscoring his sincerity. It also places a moral obligation on those who hear the message: if the messenger asks nothing, what excuse is there to reject it? The universal scope of the reminder challenges all people to reflect.

=91=

The verse addresses a blasphemous claim made by some of the Meccans or certain Jews who denied the very concept of divine revelation. "They have not shown Allah His proper reverence when they said, 'Allah has revealed nothing to any human being.'" This statement grossly underestimates Allah's mercy and wisdom; He is too exalted to leave humanity without guidance. The rebuttal is immediate: "Who then revealed the Book brought forth by Moses as a light and guidance for people?" The Torah, known and acknowledged by the Jews, was a clear scripture. The description "which you split into separate sheets—revealing some and hiding much" critiques their selective handling of the scripture, displaying parts and concealing others to suit their interests. Then the Prophet is reminded of a favor: "You have been taught through this Quran what neither you nor your forefathers knew." The Quran brought knowledge that was entirely new to the Arabs—about the unseen, the Hereafter, and the stories of previous prophets.

This is evidence of revelation. The command "Say: Allah revealed it!" leaves no ambiguity; the origin of the Quran is divine.

The verse concludes with "Then leave them to amuse themselves with falsehood," signifying that after clear argument, further debate with obstinate deniers is futile. This verse defends the institution of prophethood itself, and by extension, the authenticity of the Quran, while exposing the intellectual dishonesty of those who acknowledge earlier revelations but deny the final one.

=92=

After refuting the denial of revelation, the Quran describes itself. "This is a blessed Book which We have revealed—confirming what came before it." The word "blessed" (mubārak) means it is full of lasting goodness, benefit, and spiritual power. It confirms the original scriptures, verifying the truth contained in them and correcting the distortions. The purpose is explicit: "so you may warn the Mother of Cities and everyone around it. " The "Mother of Cities" (Umm al-Qurā) is Mecca, the most sacred and central city for the Arabs. The warning extends to all people around the world, encompassing all humanity. This demonstrates the global mission of the Prophet.

The verse then identifies the true believers: "Those who believe in the Hereafter truly believe in it and guard their prayers. " Belief in the Hereafter is presented as the key that unlocks genuine faith in the Quran; one who denies the afterlife has little incentive to heed its warnings. Guarding prayers (yuḥāfiẓūn) means observing them consistently, with their proper conditions, times, and spiritual presence. Prayer is the practical manifestation of faith. This verse connects the universal message of the Quran with the personal responsibility of maintaining the prayer, a theme that runs throughout the surah. It also indicates that the Meccans' rejection was rooted in their disbelief in the Hereafter, which made them indifferent to the Quran's warnings.

=93=

The surah identifies the worst type of wrongdoer. The rhetorical question "Who does more wrong..." lists three categories: the one who fabricates lies against Allah by inventing false doctrines; the one who falsely claims prophethood; and the one who arrogantly claims they can produce something like the Quran. All three involve a direct assault on divine truth. Then the scene shifts dramatically to the moment of death. "If only you could see the wrongdoers in the throes of death" evokes a terrifying picture. The angels, described as "stretching out their hands," are harshly demanding the soul.

The phrase "Give up your souls!" (akhrijū anfusakum) suggests the violent, painful extraction of the soul from a disbelieving body that resists. The angels announce the verdict: "Today you will be rewarded with the torment of disgrace." The punishment is explicitly linked to two crimes: "telling lies about Allah" and "being arrogant towards His revelations. " Their arrogance was not mere indifference; it was a willful, scornful rejection of the signs. The disgrace matches their crime—they sought honor in falsehood and false claims, so they are rewarded with humiliation. This verse serves as a severe warning to those who misuse religious authority, claim inspiration for personal gain, or mock the Quran. It also provides a graphic reminder of the death of the wrongdoer, which every listener will face, urging immediate repentance and sincerity.

=94=

Continuing the address to the wrongdoer at the moment of resurrection, or as the soul departs, the verse emphasizes the total isolation and loss. "Today you have come back to Us all alone as We created you the first time" describes the stark reality of the Hereafter. Every human is brought back naked, without possessions, status, or companions, just as they were born.

The phrase "leaving behind everything We have provided you with" refers to all the worldly wealth, property, and relationships that preoccupied them. They were merely temporary trusts; the true owner was always Allah, and now they are returned. "We do not see your intercessors with you—those you claimed were Allah’s partners in worship. " The idols, saints, or beings they relied on to intercede for them are conspicuously absent. The claim of their partnership was a lie that is now exposed.

The verse concludes, "All your ties have been broken and all your claims have let you down." The bonds of kinship, friendship, and social alliance are severed; the arguments and justifications they used have crumbled. The scene is one of complete abandonment. This verse is a powerful call to detach the heart from worldly dependencies and to place all hope and trust in Allah alone. It reminds believers that the only thing that will accompany them to the grave is their deeds, and that false sources of security will vanish at the moment of truth.

=95=

After the scenes of judgment, the surah returns to the signs in creation, calling the disbelievers to reflect on the most basic processes of life. "Allah is the One Who causes seeds and fruit stones to sprout." A tiny seed or a hard fruit stone is buried in the earth, seemingly lifeless. Then, through the intricate process of germination, it splits, pushes out a shoot, and becomes a plant. No human power can make a dead seed come alive; the farmer merely plants and waters, but the spark of life is from Allah.

This is one instance of the general principle: "He brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living." A living tree grows from a dead seed; a living animal gives birth to young, yet that animal will die; a dead earth is revived with vegetation. This continuous cycle of life and death is entirely under Allah's control. The exclamation "That is Allah!" affirms His identity as the sole agent behind these phenomena. Then the question: "How can you then be deluded from the truth?" Despite witnessing these clear signs daily, people are turned away from worshipping Him alone to worship idols or to materialism. The verse appeals to reason: the One who does these things is the true God; how can you be so deceived as to worship others? It invites a simple, honest assessment of reality, stripping away the layers of cultural conditioning and stubbornness.

=96=

The focus shifts from the life cycle of plants to the cosmic rhythms. "He causes the dawn to break" (fāliq al-iṣbāḥ) beautifully describes the way the first light of dawn splits the darkness of the night. This daily miracle is so routine that it is taken for granted, yet it is a profound sign of a deliberate Creator who brings light out of darkness. "And has made the night for rest" indicates the wisdom behind the darkness: the night is a covering, a time of stillness and sleep, during which bodies and minds recover. If it were always day, life would be unsustainable. Then, "and made the sun and the moon to travel with precision. " The sun and moon move in calculated orbits, with exact timing, producing years, months, days, and seasons.

The word "precision" (ḥusbān) implies a strict accounting; there is no randomness, no deviation. These celestial bodies are not gods, as some worshipped, but servants of Allah, moving by His command.

The verse concludes, "That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing. " The term "design" (taqdīr) refers to the precise measure and determination of all things. The names Al-ʿAzīz (the Almighty) and Al-ʿAlīm (the All-Knowing) combine power with knowledge: the universe is not a product of brute force but of a perfectly calculated plan executed by infinite power. This verse encourages believers to contemplate the sky at dawn and dusk, seeing in them the fingerprints of their Lord, and to align their lives with His rhythms of worship and rest.

=97=

Continuing the cosmic signs, the verse highlights a practical use of the stars. "He is the One Who has made the stars as your guide through the darkness of land and sea." In the vast, featureless deserts and oceans, especially at night, the stars are a timeless navigational tool. Bedouins and sailors relied on constellations to find their direction when all other landmarks were obscured. This function is not accidental; it is a deliberate provision from Allah. The stars serve both as beauty and as utility.

The verse then states, "We have already made the signs clear for people who know." The "signs" include the stars and all previously mentioned phenomena, and they are "clear" (faṣṣalnā) meaning they are detailed and explained so that those with knowledge—those who reflect and use their intellect—can benefit and recognize the Creator.

The phrase "people who know" (yaʿlamūn) distinguishes those who use their observation to reach truth from those who are heedless. The stars, which the polytheists sometimes worshipped or used for astrology, are here designated as mere signs and tools, pointing to the One who created them. This corrects the human tendency to worship the means while forgetting the Provider.

For believers, it teaches that all of creation is at the service of humanity by Allah's command, and using natural phenomena for guidance should increase gratitude and monotheism, not superstition.

=98=

The surah now turns to the origin of humanity itself. "He is the One Who originated you all from a single soul" reminds us that all humans, despite their diversity of races, languages, and nations, originate from one soul—Adam. This foundational truth demolishes all claims of racial superiority or inherent nobility. All are equal in origin, and the One who created from a single soul can just as easily resurrect.

The verse then mentions two appointed stations: "a place to live and another to be laid to rest. " The "place to live" (mustaqarr) is the womb where the embryo settles and develops, or some scholars say it refers to the dwelling on earth. The "place to be laid to rest" (mustawdaʿ) is the grave where the body is deposited after death, or the loins of men before birth. In either interpretation, Allah has assigned for each soul a temporary abode in this world and a resting place after death, from which it will be raised.

The verse adds, "We have already made the signs clear for people who comprehend. " The word "comprehend" (yafqahūn) implies a deeper understanding, a grasping of the implications. The origin from a single soul and the movement through stages of existence are signs for those who use their intellect to see beyond the material surface. This verse strengthens the argument for resurrection: the One who originated you from a single soul and moved you through stages can certainly gather you again.

=99=

This verse is a rich, detailed canvas of divine provision through rain. "He is the One Who sends down rain from the sky" reminds us that the water cycle is initiated by Allah's command. From that water, "all kinds of plants" emerge.

The verse then zooms in: "producing green stalks from which We bring forth clustered grain." The image of tender green shoots giving way to ears of corn packed with grain is a process of intricate engineering. "And from palm trees come clusters of dates hanging within reach," a bounty that is easily accessible, a deliberate design for human convenience. Then, "gardens of grapevines, olives, and pomegranates" are mentioned, fruits that are "similar in shape but dissimilar in taste." The genetic variation producing different flavors from similar forms is a sign of creative brilliance. The command "Look at their fruit as it yields and ripens!" invites active observation: watch the stages of growth, the transformation from flower to fruit to ripeness.

The verse concludes, "Indeed, in these are signs for people who believe. " Unlike the previous verses that addressed "people who know" or "comprehend," here it addresses "people who believe," because faith elevates the observation of nature to a spiritual experience. The believer sees the hand of the All-Merciful Provider in every fruit, which increases his love and gratitude. This verse links the life-giving rain, the diversity of food, and the call to contemplation, making everyday sustenance a continuous sign.

=100=

After this magnificent display of divine power and wisdom, the verse points to the shocking irrationality of shirk. "Yet they associate the jinn with Allah in worship, even though He created them." Some Arab pagans worshipped the jinn, believing them to be partners or intermediaries. Others claimed that the jinn were related to Allah, perhaps His offspring. The absurdity is highlighted by the fact that the jinn themselves are created beings; they are not gods, nor are they uncreated. How can a creature be a partner to its Creator?

Additionally, "they falsely attribute to Him sons and daughters out of ignorance." The "sons" refer to claims like the Jews saying Ezra is the son of Allah or the Christians saying the Messiah is the son of God, and the "daughters" refer to the Arab pagans who claimed the angels were Allah's daughters. All these attributions are based on pure conjecture and inherited falsehood, with no evidence.

The phrase "out of ignorance" explains the root cause: they are not guided by knowledge but by speculation. The verse ends with a declaration of transcendence: "Glorified and Exalted is He above what they claim!" Allah is far above having a need for offspring, a consort, or any partner. He is self-sufficient and unique.

This verse concludes the section on signs with a powerful denunciation of shirk, tying back to the very first verses of the surah. It serves as a warning that despite the overwhelming clarity of the signs, some will still invent falsehoods, and that Allah is free from all such fabrications.
