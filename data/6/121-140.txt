=121=

After commanding the believers to eat only from what is slaughtered in Allah’s name, the surah now explicitly forbids the opposite. “Do not eat of what is not slaughtered in Allah’s Name” is a direct prohibition. The meat of an animal over which the name of Allah was deliberately omitted, or over which a name other than Allah was invoked, becomes unlawful.

This is not a minor dietary preference; the verse explains the reason: “For that would certainly be an act of disobedience” (fisq). The word fisq denotes a departure from obedience, a crossing of the boundaries set by Allah. Consuming such meat is not just a physical act but a spiritual transgression because it involves partaking in something dedicated to other than God, which violates the very core of tawhid.

The verse then reveals a deeper layer of this prohibition. The pagan Arabs would argue with the Muslims, saying that eating meat from an animal that died naturally was more sinful than eating meat they had slaughtered in the name of their idols. This was a satanic argument intended to confuse the believers and make their own idolatrous practices appear reasonable. The verse identifies the source: “Surely the devils whisper to their human associates to argue with you.” The devils among the jinn inspire their human allies to craft arguments that challenge the divine command, attempting to make the unlawful seem logical or superior. The warning is severe: “If you were to obey them, then you too would be polytheists. ” Obedience in this matter—accepting their argument and eating the meat dedicated to idols—amounts to endorsing shirk and thus sharing in their polytheism. This demonstrates that dietary laws are tightly connected to the purity of faith. Compromising on such a command under social pressure or clever argumentation leads directly to a corruption of monotheism.

The verse teaches believers to be vigilant against subtle arguments that seek to dilute divine commands, and to recognize that obedience to Allah is non-negotiable, even when the disbelievers present seemingly persuasive reasoning. It also establishes the principle that consuming what is haram can affect one’s spiritual state and even one’s identity as a believer, making it a matter of life and death for the soul.

=122=

The surah presents a powerful metaphor contrasting the believer and the disbeliever. “Can those who had been dead, to whom We gave life” refers to the spiritual state before and after faith. A person without guidance is spiritually dead—lacking the true purpose of existence, disconnected from Allah. When Allah grants them faith, it is a resurrection of the soul. This life is accompanied by “a light with which they can walk among people.” The light is the Quran, knowledge, and the clarity of faith that illuminates the path of life. The believer walks with confidence, distinguishing right from wrong, seeing the reality behind appearances, and navigating the darkness of worldly temptations. The metaphor then asks if such a person can be compared to “those in complete darkness from which they can never emerge. ” The disbeliever is immersed in multiple layers of darkness—the darkness of ignorance, of shirk, of unchecked desires, and of confusion. Crucially, this darkness is described as one from which they “can never emerge,” because they have no light source. They are trapped. The rhetorical question expects an emphatic “no”; they are utterly unequal.

The verse then explains the psychology behind the disbeliever’s state: “That is how the misdeeds of the disbelievers have been made appealing to them.” The darkness is not merely a lack of light; it is a condition where evil deeds are beautified. They love their darkness because it makes their sinful lifestyle seem attractive. Their spiritual sight is so corrupted that they perceive their darkness as light, their misguidance as truth. This verse serves as a profound reminder of the transformative power of faith. It encourages believers to recognize the immense value of the light they have been given and to walk by it in every aspect of life. It also explains why the disbelievers resist guidance: they are not merely uninformed but are psychologically attached to the darkness that allows them to pursue their desires without restraint. The only way out is a divine gift of life and light, which must be cherished and protected.

=123=

After describing the disbelievers’ state, the Quran reveals a universal pattern of social corruption. “And so We have placed in every society the most wicked to conspire in it.

” The phrase “the most wicked” (akābirahā) refers to the arrogant leaders, the wealthy elite, and the influential criminals who rise to prominence in communities that have turned away from divine guidance. Their role is to “conspire” (yamkurūn), engaging in plots—devising schemes to oppose the prophets, oppress the believers, maintain their power, and spread corruption. The verb indicates a cunning, behind-the-scenes planning. This placement is part of Allah’s decree; He allows such people to dominate as a test for the society and as a natural consequence of collective moral decay.

However, the verse immediately undercuts their perceived power: “Yet they plot only against themselves, but they fail to perceive it.” Their conspiracies, no matter how elaborate, ultimately bring about their own destruction and increase their own sin. They may think they are outsmarting the believers or defeating the truth, but in the grand scheme of divine justice, every scheme backfires on its architects. They are digging their own graves.

The phrase “they fail to perceive it” highlights their blindness; they are so intoxicated by their apparent success that they cannot see the ruin they are heading towards. This verse provides profound comfort to the oppressed believers throughout history. The powerful and corrupt who appear to be winning are, in reality, self-destructing. The believers need not despair; the cycle is in Allah’s hands. It also serves as a warning to those in positions of influence: using power to plot against the truth is a sure path to personal and collective doom, regardless of short-term gains.

=124=

The surah now exposes a specific arrogant demand of the Meccan leaders. “Whenever a sign comes to them,” whether a Quranic verse or a visible miracle, they do not reflect but issue a precondition: “We will never believe until we receive what Allah’s messengers received.” They envied the Prophet and claimed that if prophethood was a real honor, it should have been bestowed upon them—the wealthy and powerful—not upon an orphan. They saw prophethood as a worldly rank to be competed for, not as a divine selection based on spiritual qualification. The response is definitive: “Allah knows best where to place His message.

” The placement of prophethood is a matter of divine wisdom and knowledge. Allah chooses for His mission the purest, most capable, and most sincere souls, regardless of their social status. The demand of the Meccans was rooted in arrogance and ignorance of the nature of divine election.

The verse then delivers a threat: “The wicked will soon be overwhelmed by humiliation from Allah and a severe punishment for their evil plots.” The word “humiliation” (ṣaghār) indicates a disgrace that crushes pride. This was fulfilled in this world with the defeat at Badr and the conquest of Mecca, and ultimately in the Hereafter with the Fire. Their “evil plots” include their mockery, their scheming against the Prophet, and their arrogant demand for prophethood.

This verse teaches that spiritual gifts cannot be earned by worldly status, and that envying the chosen servants of Allah leads to humiliation. It also reinforces the principle that Allah’s choice is based on deep wisdom, and believers should submit to divine decisions without questioning why a certain person was honored with knowledge or leadership.

=125=

This verse provides one of the most eloquent descriptions of the spiritual states of guidance and misguidance. “Whoever Allah wills to guide, He opens their heart to Islam.

” The opening of the chest (sharḥ al-ṣadr) is a divine expansion that creates a sense of ease, acceptance, and joy in submitting to Allah. The believer finds the commands of Islam natural, the beliefs satisfying, and the worship a delight.

This is a gift from Allah, not a mere intellectual decision. The Prophet himself experienced this opening, as mentioned in Surah Al-Sharḥ, when his chest was expanded and his burden lifted. Conversely, “whoever He wills to leave astray, He makes their chest tight and constricted as if they were climbing up into the sky.” The simile is strikingly accurate. As a person climbs to a high altitude, the air becomes thin, breathing becomes labored, and the chest feels painfully tight. Similarly, the disbeliever feels Islam as a suffocating burden. Every command feels restrictive, every act of worship is heavy, and the heart is in a state of discomfort and rejection.

The verse concludes, “This is how Allah dooms those who disbelieve. ” The “doom” (rijs) here refers to spiritual punishment—the inner torment, confusion, and anxiety that accompany disbelief. This verse has immense psychological depth. It explains why two people can hear the same message and react completely differently: one feels embraced, the other feels choked. It teaches that guidance is ultimately in Allah’s hands, and one should constantly pray for an open chest. It also warns that persisting in disbelief leads to a state of inner constriction that makes the truth ever more intolerable.

=126=

After describing the spiritual states of guidance and misguidance, the verse presents the content of that guidance. “That is your Lord’s Path—perfectly straight.

” The word “that” refers to Islam, the opening of the heart, and the way of the prophets described earlier. It is the Path of “your Lord,” linking the path directly to the Creator and Sustainer, which adds a tone of intimacy and ownership. The path is “perfectly straight” (mustaqīman), without any crookedness, ambiguity, or contradiction. It leads directly to Allah and to eternal bliss.

The verse then states, “We have already made the signs clear to those who are mindful.” The signs—the Quranic verses, the cosmic phenomena, the stories—are explained in detail, leaving no obscurity.

However, only “those who are mindful” (yadhdhakkarūn) truly benefit. Mindfulness here means those who remember Allah, who have taqwa, and who actively engage their minds and hearts with the signs. For the heedless, the signs remain invisible. This verse reinforces the idea that the truth is not hidden; it is manifest and accessible. The responsibility lies with the individual to be among the mindful who recognize and follow the straight path. It is a powerful reassurance for the believers that they are walking a clear, divinely designated road, and it calls them to deepen their mindfulness so they can see the signs ever more clearly.

=127=

The destination of those who follow the straight path is now revealed. “They will have the Home of Peace with their Lord.” The “Home of Peace” (Dār al-Salām) is one of the names of Paradise. It is a place free from all turmoil, fear, pain, and sadness.

The word “peace” (salām) implies not just the absence of conflict but a positive, all-encompassing state of well-being, security, and harmony. The addition “with their Lord” (ʿinda rabbihim) is the ultimate honor. The peace is not merely in the gardens and rivers but is rooted in the nearness to Allah and His pleasure.

This is the supreme bliss, as indicated in another verse: “For them is whatever they wish, and with Us is more. ” Then, “Who will be their Guardian” (huwa waliyyuhum) signifies that Allah is their protector, friend, and patron. This guardianship is not a future event alone; it begins in this world and reaches its perfection in the Hereafter. The reason is given: “because of what they used to do.” Their deeds, performed with faith and sincerity, were not the absolute cause—for Paradise is ultimately by Allah’s mercy—but they were the conditions that Allah, in His wisdom, has established for receiving this reward. This verse fills the believer’s heart with hope and motivation. It beautifully combines the promise of a perfect abode, the supreme company of the Lord, and the acknowledgement that one’s efforts on earth are recognized and rewarded. It is a summary of the believer’s deepest aspirations.

=128=

The surah shifts to a dramatic scene on the Day of Judgment involving both jinn and humans. The command “Consider” invites deep reflection. “The Day He will gather them all together” includes every jinn and human being. Allah will address the jinn first: “O assembly of jinn! You misled humans in great numbers.” The jinn, particularly the devils among them, are primarily responsible for whispering, deceiving, and leading humans astray. They will be confronted with their crime. The response of the human associates—those who followed the devils and enjoyed the sinful life—is given: “Our Lord! We benefited from each other’s company.” The relationship was mutually exploitative. The devils provided temptations and adorned sins, and the humans provided obedience and worship. Both sides enjoyed the worldly gains. But the admission continues: “but now we have reached the term which You appointed for us.” Death and the Day of Judgment have arrived, and the time for benefit is over; now comes regret. Allah’s verdict is severe: “The Fire is your home, yours to stay in forever, except whoever Allah wills to spare.” The exception refers to those who had committed shirk but later repented, or those whose sins were not so grave, but the general principle is eternal dwelling.

The verse concludes with “Surely your Lord is All-Wise, All-Knowing,” emphasizing that this judgment is based on perfect wisdom and comprehensive knowledge. This verse unveils the hidden dynamics of misguidance and accountability. It warns humans not to take the devils as allies, for their partnership ends in mutual destruction. It also provides a sobering view of the unseen world and the ultimate consequences of following satanic whispers.

=129=

Following the scene of the jinn and humans blaming each other, the Quran states a universal principle of divine justice. “This is how We make the wrongdoers destructive allies of one another.” The wrongdoers, both among jinn and humans, are paired together in this world and in the Hereafter. They become each other’s companions, supporters, and partners in crime.

The word “destructive” or more literally “allies” (awliyāʾ) implies a close bond, but it is a bond that leads to mutual ruin. The reason is “because of their misdeeds.” Their shared love for evil and their common rebellion against Allah naturally bring them together. It is a form of punishment: the wrongdoer is denied the company of the righteous and is instead surrounded by similarly corrupt souls who reinforce his misguidance and eventually lead him to Hell.

This principle applies at all levels: corrupt leaders attract corrupt followers, sinful friends encourage each other’s sins, and deviant ideologies cluster together. The verse shows that the social environment of a wrongdoer is not coincidental; it is part of the divine decree that like attracts like, serving as both a test and a gradual descent into destruction.

For the believer, this is a warning to choose friends and associates carefully, as companionship can either be a source of salvation or a chain leading to the Fire. It also explains why breaking away from a sinful environment is often so difficult, and why doing so is an essential part of repentance.

=130=

Another dialogue from the Day of Judgment is presented. Allah will question both groups together: “O assembly of jinn and humans! Did messengers not come from among you?” The messengers were all human, but their message addressed both jinn and humans. The jinn heard the Quran and some believed, while others rejected.

The question is rhetorical; it establishes the proof that no one was left without warning. The messengers “proclaimed My revelations” and specifically “warned you of the coming of this Day of yours.” The Day of Judgment is called “this Day of yours” because it is the day that was appointed for them, now a present reality. The response of the sinners is not denial but confession: “We confess against ourselves!” They admit their guilt openly. The reason for their failure is then given: “For they have been deluded by their worldly life.” The pleasures, distractions, and temporary nature of the world deceived them, making them forget this inevitable meeting.

The verse ends with “And they will testify against themselves that they were disbelievers.” Their own tongues will bear witness to their kufr. There will be no false oaths like they made in the world; the truth will be undeniable. This scene demonstrates the perfect justice of Allah: punishment is never inflicted without clear warning and the establishment of proof. It also serves as a powerful reminder to not be deluded by the world, for its charm will one day vanish, leaving only accountability.

=131=

After narrating the questioning about the messengers, the Quran explains the wisdom behind sending them. “This sending of the messengers is because your Lord would never destroy a society for their wrongdoing while its people are unaware of the truth.” The pronoun “this” refers to the whole institution of prophethood and revelation. Allah’s justice does not allow Him to punish a people without first sending a warner to make them aware of the truth and establish the proof against them.

This is a fundamental principle of divine justice. No nation is destroyed for their shirk or sins unless a messenger has come to them and they have willfully rejected.

The word “unaware” (ghāfilūn) denotes a state of heedlessness where the truth has not been clearly presented. This verse provides immense comfort: it affirms that Allah’s mercy and justice precede His wrath. It also refutes the notion of arbitrary punishment.

For those who have never received a clear message of Islam—the “people of the interval” (ahl al-fatrah)—scholars say they will be tested on the Day of Judgment according to their circumstances. The verse also implies that once the message has reached a person clearly, the excuse of ignorance is removed. This places a great responsibility on those who have the message to convey it, but also assures that accountability is always based on knowledge. It is a verse that balances hope and fear, showing that Allah’s way is perfect fairness.

=132=

The concept of individual accountability and varying degrees of reward and punishment is now introduced. “They will each be assigned ranks according to their deeds.

” The word “ranks” (darajāt) indicates that the Hereafter is not a binary state of Paradise or Hell alone. Within Paradise, there are elevated levels; within Hell, there are descending depths. Every person’s rank will be precisely calibrated to their faith and actions. No two people are exactly alike in their record or their recompense.

The phrase “according to what they did” underscores perfect justice. The verse then reassures: “And your Lord is not unaware of what they do. ” The Arabic ghāfil, “unaware,” is negated; Allah is never heedless or forgetful. Every deed, no matter how small, is recorded and will be reflected in the rank. This verse encourages the believers to strive for the highest ranks, knowing that every extra effort, every additional act of worship, every sacrifice, will elevate them. It also warns the wrongdoers that their deeds are not lost; they are accumulating a precise measure of punishment.

The concept of ranks also highlights the vastness of Allah’s mercy and justice, as the variety of human actions necessitates a corresponding variety in outcomes. It provides a deep sense of hope for the believers who might worry that their small deeds are insignificant, and fear for the sinners who assume their sins will go unnoticed.

=133=

After discussing the ranks, the Quran reminds humanity of their absolute dependence on Allah. “Your Lord is the Self-Sufficient, Full of Mercy.

” The name Al-Ghanī means He is completely free of need. He does not require human worship, obedience, or even existence. His Mercy (Raḥmah) is what leads Him to provide, guide, and forgive.

The verse then issues a stark warning to the arrogant: “If He wills, He can do away with you and replace you with whoever He wills.” The entire human race could be wiped out, and a new creation—obedient and grateful—could be brought forth.

This is not difficult for Him. The phrase “just as He produced you from the offspring of other people” reminds the Quraysh and all humans that they themselves are replacements of previous generations. They walked upon the earth after the ‘Ad, Thamud, and countless others had perished. This historical precedent makes the threat tangible and immediate. This verse strikes at the root of human arrogance. People often act as if their existence is indispensable to the universe, forgetting that they are entirely replaceable. The only reason they continue to live and receive blessings is Allah’s mercy, not any inherent worth. This realization cultivates deep humility, gratitude, and a sense of urgency to fulfill the purpose of existence before one is replaced. It also warns societies that if they become corrupt and disobedient, Allah can easily substitute them with a better people, as happened throughout history.

=134=

After the warning of replacement, the verse delivers a concise but powerful affirmation of the certainty of the Hereafter. “Indeed, what you have been promised will certainly come to pass.” The “promise” encompasses the resurrection, judgment, Paradise, and Hell.

The use of the emphatic particle “indeed” (inna) and the future tense with certainty (la-ātin) leaves no room for doubt. The Arabic construction conveys that it is not just a possibility but an unstoppable reality marching towards them. Then, “And you will have no escape.

” The word bi-mu‘jizīn means you cannot frustrate, elude, or overpower Allah’s plan. No amount of flight, wealth, or scheming can avert the coming of that Day or one’s personal appointment with death and accountability. This verse is a direct response to those who mocked the warnings and acted as if they could outrun divine justice. It serves as a powerful spiritual anchor for the believers, helping them to live in the light of this certainty, making choices based on an unshakeable conviction in the ultimate outcome. It also strips away the false security of the disbelievers, confronting them with a truth that their denial does not negate. The simplicity and forcefulness of the verse make it one of the most impactful reminders in the Quran.

=135=

The Prophet is instructed to deliver a final, clear ultimatum. “O my people! Persist in your ways, for I too will persist in mine.

” This is not an approval of their misguidance but a declaration of mutual determination and a separation of paths. The verb “persist” (i‘malū ‘alā makānatikum) literally means “work according to your position,” implying that they should continue in their disbelief, idolatry, and opposition, while the Prophet will continue in his monotheism, worship, and calling. It is a statement of unshakable resolve.

The verse then directs attention to the outcome: “You will soon know who will fare best in the end.” The “end” (ʿāqibah) refers to the final outcome in this world and the Hereafter.

The question is not who is currently winning in terms of wealth or power, but who will have the praiseworthy end. The verse concludes with the fundamental principle: “Indeed, the wrongdoers will never succeed.” Success (falāḥ) is the ultimate positive outcome—eternal happiness and divine pleasure—and it is categorically denied to the wrongdoers. This verse serves as a powerful model for dealing with persistent rejection. After exhausting all arguments and efforts, the believer draws a clear line, entrusts the outcome to Allah, and continues on the path with patience and certainty. It also warns the disbelievers that their temporary worldly gains are not a sign of success; the real verdict is yet to come.

=136=

The surah returns to exposing the irrational and unjust practices of the pre-Islamic Arabs. “The pagans set aside for Allah a share of the crops and cattle He created.” They acknowledged that Allah was the Creator, so they designated a portion of their harvest and livestock as an offering to Him, and another portion to their idols.

However, their practice was riddled with injustice. The verse quotes them: “This portion is for Allah, so they claim, and this one for our associate-gods.” The hypocrisy is exposed: “Yet the portion of their associate-gods is not shared with Allah while Allah’s portion is shared with their associate-gods.” If the portion set for the idols fell into the portion set for Allah, they would return it to the idols’ share; but if the opposite happened, they would leave it, claiming Allah was self-sufficient and could afford the loss.

Thus, they treated Allah—in their own distorted logic—as the lesser priority, while favoring the idols. The verse concludes with an exclamation of disgust: “What unfair judgment!” This shows their gross misplacement of honor and their corrupted sense of justice. This practice illustrates how shirk perverts human reason and ethics. It also demonstrates that even when people profess belief in Allah, if they associate partners, their judgment becomes twisted, giving preference to created things over the Creator. The verse condemns such behavior and serves as a warning against any similar modern tendencies where worldly matters are prioritized over the rights of Allah, or where compromises are made that dishonor the divine while respecting the false.

=137=

The surah moves to an even more heinous practice. “Likewise, the pagans’ evil associates have made it appealing to them to kill their own children.” The “evil associates” are the devils among the jinn and humans who beautified this atrocity. The pagan Arabs, especially some tribes, would bury their daughters alive out of fear of poverty or shame. This act was made to seem acceptable or even honorable in their culture. The verse states its consequence: “only leading to their destruction as well as confusion in their faith.” The killing of children is a physical destruction of life and a spiritual destruction of the killers, who sink to the lowest level of inhumanity. Their faith becomes confused because they mix the remnants of Abrahamic monotheism with such barbaric practices, unable to see the contradiction.

The verse then adds the theological note: “Had it been Allah’s Will, they would not have done such a thing.” This, as before, affirms that everything occurs within Allah’s decree, but His will does not equal His approval; He allowed this as a test and a proof against them. The command “So leave them and their falsehood” instructs the Prophet to withdraw his concern from their fabricated religion. Their inventions are so far from the truth that no further engagement is warranted. This verse serves as a powerful condemnation of female infanticide, a practice Islam completely eradicated. It also shows how satanic beautification can make even the most monstrous acts seem normal, highlighting the need for divine revelation to correct moral perception.

=138=

The surah continues detailing the invented religious restrictions of the pagans. “They say, ‘These cattle and crops are reserved—none may eat them except those we permit,’ so they claim.” They designated certain animals and fields as sacred, forbidding their use to most people, based on their own whims, not divine command.

The phrase “so they claim” (bi-zaʿmihim) emphasizes the baselessness of this assertion. Then, “Some other cattle are exempted from labour,” meaning they were set free, not to be used for plowing or carrying, as an offering to idols. “And others are not slaughtered in Allah’s Name,” meaning they were slaughtered with the names of idols invoked, or they were not slaughtered at all because they were considered too sacred. All of this is summarized as “falsely attributing lies to Him.” They claimed these laws were from Allah while they were pure fabrications.

The verse ends with the warning: “He will repay them for their lies.” The recompense will be appropriate to the crime: they lied about Allah’s law, so they will face His punishment. This verse exposes how human beings can invent religious systems and attribute them to God, creating a false sense of piety while actually transgressing. It warns against the dangers of bidʿah—innovation in religion—where things not ordained by Allah are made compulsory or forbidden. The believers are reminded to seek only what Allah has clearly legislated, avoiding the pitfalls of self-invented religiosity.

=139=

Further absurdities of pagan laws are cited. “They also say, ‘The offspring of this cattle is reserved for our males and forbidden to our females; but if it is stillborn, they may all share it.’” This refers to another arbitrary rule: if a certain sacred animal gave birth to live young, only the men could eat it; the women were prohibited. But if the offspring was born dead, the prohibition was lifted, and women could partake.

This is a completely arbitrary, discriminatory rule with no basis in reason or revelation. It reflects the patriarchal and superstitious nature of their society, where women were often deprived unjustly. The verse again promises divine retribution: “He will repay them for their falsehood.

” The word “falsehood” (waṣfahum) points to their habit of ascribing descriptions and laws to Allah that He never authorized. The verse closes with “Surely He is All-Wise, All-Knowing. ” His wisdom and knowledge encompass the perfect legal system that ensures justice, unlike their fabricated rules which are rooted in ignorance and whims. This verse reinforces the Quran’s role in demolishing unjust social customs and establishing equity. It teaches that religion must be based on divine authority, not on human inventions that often serve the interests of the powerful. The believers are called to submit to Allah’s laws, which are just and wise, and to reject any human fabrications that contradict them.

=140=

The section on pagan injustices reaches its climax with a comprehensive condemnation. “Lost indeed are those who have murdered their own children foolishly out of ignorance.” This returns to the crime of infanticide, particularly female infanticide, describing it as “foolishly” (safahan), indicating a lack of sound reason, and “out of ignorance” (bi-ghayri ʿilmin), without any true knowledge. The combination emphasizes that the act is both brutal and baseless.

The verse then adds the second major crime: “and have forbidden what Allah has provided for them— falsely attributing lies to Allah.” This refers to all the arbitrary prohibitions on certain cattle, crops, and foods mentioned earlier. Together, these two categories—taking life unjustly and prohibiting the lawful—cover the major corruption of both the rights of humanity and the rights of Allah.

The phrase “falsely attributing lies to Allah” is the root cause. The verdict is final: “They have certainly strayed and are not rightly guided.” They are lost in misguidance, completely cut off from the straight path. This verse serves as a powerful summary of the pre-Islamic moral darkness and the liberating light Islam brought. It demonstrates the inseparable link between correct belief and ethical conduct. When people lie about Allah, their moral compass shatters, leading to the most horrific social practices.

The verse also highlights the mercy of Allah in sending the Quran to guide humanity out of such darkness, making the believers grateful for the clear guidance that protects life and upholds justice.
