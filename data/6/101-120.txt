=101=

After refuting the false attribution of sons and daughters, the Quran delivers a decisive rational argument. The description “He is the Originator of the heavens and earth” uses the divine name Badī‘, meaning the One who creates without any prior model, bringing existence out of nothing with perfect originality. This uniqueness excludes any possibility of an equal or a partner. The rhetorical question “How could He have children when He has no mate?” dismantles the concept of divine offspring through simple logic. In the created world, a child comes into being through a pair, a male and a female. Allah, being far above any gender, need, or companion, has no consort. The idea of a child for Him would imply a likeness to creation, which is impossible. The pagans who claimed angels were daughters of Allah, and later Christian concepts of divine sonship, are thus shown to be fundamentally inconsistent with His nature.

The verse continues “He created all things,” underscoring that everything other than Him is His creation. A child would be either part of the Creator or a created thing; if created, then the child is just another creature, not a deity, and attributing divinity to it is shirk. If uncreated, that would imply another eternal being, contradicting His oneness. The verse closes with “and has perfect knowledge of everything. ” His knowledge is all-encompassing, so He knows that He is self-sufficient and free from any need for offspring. Ignorance might cause a human to desire a child for support or legacy, but Allah’s perfect knowledge places Him above such deficiencies. This verse consolidates the argument for pure monotheism, leaving no room for any claim of divine progeny, and it also comforts the believers that the truth about Allah is both rationally sound and clearly presented in revelation.

=102=

Having established the logical impossibility of Allah having children, the surah now draws the natural conclusion for human conduct. “That is Allah—your Lord!” serves as a summarizing declaration. The attributes mentioned previously—the Originator, the All-Knowing, the One without consort—are the qualities of the true God whom you must recognize as your Lord.

The phrase “your Lord” personalizes the relationship: He is not a distant cosmic force but the sustainer and master of every individual. The central creed follows: “There is no god worthy of worship except Him.” This negation and affirmation are the essence of Islam. No other being—idol, angel, prophet, or celestial body—deserves worship. Then the verse provides the direct command: “He is the Creator of all things, so worship Him alone. ” The logical connector “so” (fa) indicates that His exclusive creation necessitates exclusive worship. Since He alone brought everything into existence, He alone merits devotion, obedience, and love. To direct worship to any created thing is a profound injustice.

The verse then adds a crucial divine name: “And He is the Maintainer of everything. ” Al-Wakīl means the One who is entrusted with the management of all affairs, the ultimate Trustee. He not only created but also sustains, provides, and governs. Knowing that Allah is al-Wakīl frees the believer from anxiety; one can rely completely on Him for every need without turning to intermediaries. This verse encapsulates the doctrine of tawḥīd al-ulūhiyyah (oneness of worship) built upon tawḥīd al-rubūbiyyah (oneness of lordship). It is a powerful call to abandon shirk and to place one’s full trust in Allah, recognizing that worship and reliance are the two inseparable wings of faith.

=103=

Following the command to worship Allah alone, the Quran addresses the nature of divine perception. The polytheists sometimes imagined that gods could be seen or represented by idols, while others demanded to see Allah directly.

This verse clarifies the transcendence of Allah. “No vision can encompass Him” means that no sight, whether in this world or the Hereafter, can fully comprehend His essence. Human eyes, limited as they are, cannot grasp an unlimited Being. Even the vision of Allah granted to the believers in Paradise, the greatest of all rewards, will not be an encompassing sight; they will see Him without being able to comprehend His infinite reality.

The second part, “but He encompasses all vision,” asserts that Allah sees everything that exists. Every glance, every sight, every object seen is fully within His knowledge and perception. Nothing escapes Him.

The verse concludes with two names: “For He is the Most Subtle, All-Aware.” Al-Laṭīf, the Most Subtle, indicates that He is too fine and transcendent to be perceived by the senses, yet He is also kind and gracious in ways that are barely perceived. Al-Khabīr, the All-Aware, emphasizes His deep, penetrating knowledge of all things, including the innermost thoughts. This combination provides immense comfort: the Lord we worship is beyond physical limitation, yet He is intimately aware of our states. The verse refutes anthropomorphism, the idea that Allah has a physical form like His creation. It also consoles those who long to see their Lord; though they cannot encompass Him, they will be granted the joy of looking upon His countenance, while He remains exalted above all comparison.

=104=

The discussion shifts from divine perception to the spiritual insights provided to humanity. “Indeed, there have come to you insights from your Lord” addresses all people.

The word baṣāʾir, “insights,” is the plural of baṣīrah, meaning deep spiritual perception, clear proofs, and illuminating knowledge. These insights refer to the Quranic revelations and the signs in creation that open the heart’s eye. They are from “your Lord,” a title of mercy and care, indicating that these insights are gifts of guidance.

The verse then presents the free choice: “So whoever chooses to see, it is for their own good.” Accepting the truth and gaining spiritual sight benefits only the person themselves; Allah is not in need of our worship. “But whoever chooses to be blind, it is to their own loss. ” Willful ignorance harms only the one who rejects. The blindness here is not physical but spiritual, a refusal to acknowledge what is clear. The consequences of this blindness fall entirely on the individual. The Prophet is then instructed to declare: “And I am not a keeper over you. ” His role is not to compel belief or to guard people from punishment. He has delivered the message, and now each soul is responsible for its choice. This verse beautifully balances the provision of divine guidance with human accountability. It teaches that while Allah provides all the tools for spiritual sight, the decision to see or remain blind rests with the individual.

This principle liberates the believers from excessive worry over those who reject, placing the burden of choice where it belongs.

=105=

The Quran now describes the divine method in presenting the signs and the disbelievers’ persistent accusations. “And so We vary our signs” refers to the diverse ways in which Allah explains the truth—using parables, historical narratives, rational arguments, and cosmic observations. The purpose is to reach hearts and minds with different levels of understanding.

Yet the disbelievers respond with a specific allegation: “to the extent that they will say, ‘You have studied previous scriptures.’” The Meccans, unable to deny the power and depth of the Quran, tried to dismiss it by claiming that the Prophet had learned its contents from Jewish and Christian sources. They pointed to his interactions with individuals who had knowledge of the earlier books. This was an attempt to deny the miraculous nature of the revelation. The verse refutes this implicitly by the very fact of its variation and profundity. The Prophet was unlettered, raised among them, and had never studied with any teacher. The historical context of his life was well-known, making their accusation baseless.

The verse adds: “and We make this Quran clear for people who know.” The clarity is there, but it is perceived only by those who possess knowledge and sincerity. The verb “We make clear” (nufaṣṣilu) indicates detailed explanation.

This is a recurring theme: the truth is manifest, but only the knowledgeable benefit. The verse encourages believers to deepen their understanding, knowing that the Quran’s signs are multifaceted and can answer every doubt, while the rejecters will always resort to unfounded excuses.

=106=

After detailing the accusations of the disbelievers, the Prophet is given direct instruction. “Follow what is revealed to you from your Lord” is a command to adhere strictly to the Quran and the divine inspiration. The source is “your Lord,” emphasizing the intimate and reliable nature of this guidance. The command is immediately reinforced by the central truth: “there is no god worthy of worship except Him. ” This phrase, woven into the command, clarifies that the core of what is revealed is pure monotheism. The Prophet’s mission is to live by and convey this message. Then the instruction adds: “and turn away from the polytheists.” This does not mean abandoning the duty of conveying the message, but rather turning away from their arguments, their mockery, and their attempts to dilute the truth. After clearly presenting the message, the Prophet should not engage in endless, fruitless debates or become entangled in their emotional manipulations. This turning away is a form of protecting one’s spiritual focus and leaving the judgment to Allah. It also serves as a non-verbal declaration that their position is unacceptable and will not be validated through continued engagement. This verse provided immense strength to the Prophet during the difficult Meccan period, when persistent opposition could have been draining. It teaches believers that while da‘wah is essential, there comes a point where one must prioritize one’s own faith and detach from the hostile environment, trusting that Allah will deal with the rejecters.

=107=

The command to turn away is further explained by this profound theological statement. “Had Allah willed, they would not have been polytheists” affirms that everything occurs within the sphere of Allah’s will. Their shirk does not happen outside His control or against His ultimate decree.

However, this does not mean He is pleased with it; rather, He has granted them free will, and their choice to associate partners is known and permitted by His wisdom. This knowledge relieves the Prophet of the mistaken belief that he must force them to believe. The verse immediately adds, “We have not appointed you as their keeper, nor are you their maintainer.” A “keeper” (ḥafīẓ) is someone who guards and records, and a “maintainer” (wakīl) is a trustee managing affairs. The Prophet is neither responsible for watching over their deeds to ensure they believe, nor is he entrusted with the power to control their destiny. His role is exclusively to warn and deliver.

This is a profound comfort for anyone engaged in calling to Islam: the outcome is not in your hands. You are not required to produce results, only to convey.

The verse also corrects any tendency to despair or frustration when people reject. It places the ultimate matter of guidance firmly with Allah, encouraging the believer to continue their efforts with patience, while leaving the hearts of the listeners to the One Who controls them.

=108=

This verse provides a crucial ethical principle for dealing with opposition. At times, the believers, out of zeal for their faith, would curse the idols of the pagans. This would provoke the pagans to retaliate by cursing Allah, the Most High, in a fit of ignorant anger. Allah forbids this, saying, “Do not insult what they invoke besides Allah or they will insult Allah spitefully out of ignorance. ” The prohibition is not out of respect for the false gods but to protect the sanctity of Allah’s name from being reviled. It teaches that a greater harm must be prevented even if it means restraining oneself from a lesser good.

The verse then provides a deeper insight: “This is how We have made each people’s deeds appealing to them.” The pagans’ devotion to their idols is part of the test; their false religion is beautified in their eyes, which explains their hostile reaction. The believers are reminded that ultimate judgment rests with Allah: “Then to their Lord is their return, and He will inform them of what they used to do.” They need not take justice into their own hands by insulting; Allah will settle the account on the Day of Judgment. This verse is a timeless guide for Muslims living among non-Muslims or in pluralistic societies. It commands respectful speech even towards what is deemed false, not out of compromise, but out of wisdom and concern for the honor of Allah. It encourages focusing on positive presentation of Islam rather than attacking others’ beliefs, which can lead to a backlash that causes greater disrespect to the Divine.

=109=

The surah returns to the theme of the disbelievers’ insincere demands. The Meccans would take heavy oaths, swearing by Allah that if a tangible miracle—like a spring gushing from the earth or a visible angel—were shown to them, they would absolutely believe. They made such promises to justify their current rejection and to shift blame onto the Prophet. Allah exposes their falsehood. The Prophet is instructed to respond: “Signs are only with Allah.” Miracles are not at his disposal; they are under Allah’s sole command and wisdom. Then the verse turns to address the believers with a rhetorical question: “What will make you realize that even if a sign were to come to them, they still would not believe?” The believers might have hoped that such signs would convince the pagans, but Allah informs them that the problem is not a lack of signs but the hardness of the disbelievers’ hearts.

The question is phrased in a way that indicates it is almost impossible for the believers to fully grasp the depth of this stubbornness. This verse, along with the following ones, exposes the psychology of entrenched disbelief. It teaches that when people are committed to rejection, even the most extraordinary miracles will not change them, because they have already closed their hearts and will find some excuse to dismiss the sign. The lesson for the believers is to place their hope not in spectacular displays but in sincere hearts, and to trust that Allah’s withholding of certain signs is part of His perfect plan.

=110=

This verse explains the divine action that follows persistent rejection. “We turn their hearts and eyes away from the truth” describes a punishment that matches the crime.

The Arabic verb nuqallibu implies a continuous turning over, a constant state of confusion and inability to settle on the truth. Their hearts are turned so they cannot understand, and their eyes so they cannot see the signs correctly. The cause is explicitly stated: “as they refused to believe at first.” When the initial clear call came, they willfully rejected it. As a consequence, Allah deprived them of the ability to benefit from later guidance.

This is a form of spiritual law: the first conscious rejection initiates a hardening process, and if not repented, it leads to a sealed heart. The result is “leaving them to wander blindly in their defiance.” They are left to stumble in their transgression (ṭughyān), confused and lost, with no light to guide them. This verse should strike fear into the heart of every believer. It warns against the habit of turning away from a known truth. One might think, “I can accept later,” but the delay can cause the heart to lose its receptivity permanently.

The verse also clarifies that this divine “turning” is not arbitrary but a just response to human choice. It is a powerful motivator to respond immediately and sincerely when guidance reaches us, for the window of opportunity may not remain open indefinitely.

=111=

The Quran now presents the ultimate hypothetical scenario to shut the door on any excuse. The disbelievers asked for angels to come down, for the dead to rise and testify, for all conceivable signs to be gathered. Allah says that even if He granted all of that—angels descending visibly, the dead brought back to speak, and every single sign paraded before their eyes—they would still not believe. This definitive statement is based on Allah’s knowledge of their inner state. The only exception is “unless Allah so willed.” If Allah willed to override their stubbornness, they would believe, but in the absence of that will, their hearts remain impermeable.

The phrase “But most of them are ignorant” indicates that they do not understand the nature of faith. They think it is about external evidence, but it is about internal submission. Many people live under the illusion that if they saw a miracle, they would become devout, but the history of the prophets proves otherwise. The people of Thamud saw the she-camel emerge from the rock and still hamstrung it. This verse is a profound call to self-examination: am I waiting for some extraordinary event to change, while ignoring the clear signs already present? It teaches that guidance is a gift from Allah, and one must earnestly seek it rather than making demands.

The verse also comforts the Prophet, showing him the futility of trying to satisfy the endless requests of those who are not sincere.

=112=

After explaining the hopelessness of the hardened disbelievers, the Quran reveals a cosmic pattern. “And so We have made for every prophet enemies” indicates that opposition is a sunnah, a constant in the mission of prophethood. No prophet came without facing adversaries. These enemies are of two types: “devilish humans and jinn,” meaning rebellious human beings and satanic jinn who cooperate in opposing the truth. Their method is “whispering to one another with elegant words of deception.” They inspire each other with “zukhruf al-qawl,” speech that is adorned, glittering, and superficially attractive. This includes sophistry, propaganda, poetic mockery, and false promises. The intention is to deceive both themselves and others.

The verse then adds, “Had it been your Lord’s Will, they would not have done such a thing.” This affirms that their actions are within Allah’s control and permission, serving a divine purpose in testing the believers. Then comes the command: “So leave them and their deceit.” The Prophet is instructed to abandon being preoccupied with them and the falsehoods they fabricate. This does not mean ceasing to convey the message, but rather not letting their schemes disturb his heart. The verse gives immense comfort to the believers who face sophisticated, well-funded, and eloquent opposition. It tells them that this is nothing new; it is part of the test, and the proper response is to ignore their psychological warfare and continue steadfastly on the path.

=113=

The verse states the consequence, or rather the divine wisdom, behind allowing such devilish enemies and their deception. “So that the hearts of those who disbelieve in the Hereafter may be receptive to it.” The hearts already inclined towards rejecting the afterlife will naturally incline towards this deceptive, beautiful speech. It resonates with their desires and provides a comforting justification for their disbelief. The heart “inclines” (taṣghā) like a vessel tipping towards water; it absorbs the falsehood eagerly. “Be pleased with it” indicates that they not only accept it but find satisfaction and joy in it. They love to hear arguments that validate their lifestyle. “And be persistent in their evil pursuits” means they continue to commit the sins and wrongdoings they are already engaged in. The deception serves to reinforce their existing path.

This verse reveals a profound psychological and spiritual mechanism: the rejection of truth is not a neutral intellectual error; it is often driven by a desire to continue in sin and a love for worldly life. When people want to reject accountability, they seek out and embrace any argument that tells them they do not need to believe. The whispers of the devils provide exactly that. The verse is a warning to believers to be careful about what they expose their hearts to. Attractive falsehood can only take root in a heart that has already turned away from the Hereafter.

Therefore, maintaining a strong connection with the promise of the afterlife is the best defense against deception.

=114=

After the mention of deception and those who follow it, the Prophet is instructed to affirm his sole reliance on Allah’s judgment. The rhetorical question “Should I seek a judge other than Allah” is a powerful declaration of complete satisfaction with divine arbitration.

The word “judge” (ḥakam) refers to the one who decides matters of dispute, belief, and law. It would be absurd to turn elsewhere when Allah is the ultimate authority. The reason is immediate: “while He is the One Who has revealed for you the Book with the truth perfectly explained.” The Quran is a detailed, clarifying book that leaves no need for any other source of ultimate judgment.

The phrase “perfectly explained” (mufaṣṣalan) emphasizes that it contains clear guidance for all essential matters. The verse then points to the testimony of the People of the Book: “Those who were given the Scripture know that it has been revealed to you from your Lord in truth. ” The sincere among the Jews and Christians recognized the truth of the Quran based on the descriptions in their own scriptures. This knowledge serves as an additional witness.

Finally, the command “So do not be one of those who doubt” is directed to the Prophet and, by extension, to all believers. After such clear evidence and such recognition from knowledgeable people, there is no room for hesitation. The verse instills certainty and confidence in the divine origin of the Quran, urging believers to make it their ultimate reference point in all aspects of life.

=115=

This verse continues the exaltation of the Quran, providing the strongest guarantee of its integrity. “The Word of your Lord has been perfected in truth and justice.” “Perfected” (tammat) means it is complete, lacking nothing, and flawless. “In truth” (ṣidqan) means its narratives, promises, and theological statements are absolutely true. “And justice” (ʿadlan) means its laws, commands, and ethical rulings are perfectly just. The balance between truth and justice covers both doctrine and practice, ensuring that the Quran is both theologically correct and morally upright. Then the verse declares: “None can change His Words.

” This is a divine promise that no one can alter, distort, or replace the Quran. This includes both the literal text, which has been miraculously preserved, and the decrees and laws within it.

The final authority of the Quran remains unchanged until the end of time. The verse closes with two divine names: “And He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.” Al-Samīʿ hears all claims, objections, and supplications. Al-ʿAlīm knows every circumstance and intention. Because He hears and knows all, His Word is perfectly suited to address all human situations. This verse provides immense assurance to the believers that they have a reliable, complete, and just guide. It also warns those who attempt to tamper with the text or reinterpret it beyond its true meanings that their efforts will fail. The permanence of the Quran is one of the greatest miracles of the Prophet Muhammad.

=116=

After affirming the perfection of the Quran, the surah warns against the influence of the majority. “If you were to obey most of those on earth, they would lead you away from Allah’s Way.

” This is a timeless statement about the state of humanity. At the time of revelation, the majority of people were disbelievers, polytheists, or followers of distorted scriptures. But the principle extends beyond that moment: the truth is not determined by numbers. The “majority” is often driven by unexamined tradition, desires, and social pressure. Following them blindly would inevitably lead astray from the straight path.

The verse then gives the reason for their misguidance: “They follow nothing but assumptions and do nothing but lie. ” Their beliefs are not based on solid evidence but on conjecture (ẓann)—guesses, inherited myths, and wishful thinking. Their actions are characterized by fabrication and falsehood.

This is a stark critique of any society that turns away from revelation and bases its values on opinion polls, popular culture, or man-made ideologies. The verse is a warning to the Prophet and to all believers not to be swayed by the sheer weight of numbers. The path of true guidance may be walked by a minority, but it is the only path that leads to Allah. It encourages intellectual and spiritual courage to stand firm on the truth even when it is unpopular, and to evaluate all claims by the standard of revelation rather than by the number of their followers.

=117=

This short but powerful verse complements the previous one. After stating that the majority would lead one astray, Allah reassures that He is fully aware of everyone’s true state. “Your Lord knows best who has strayed from His Way.

” The phrase “your Lord” is gentle, reminding the Prophet that his Lord is in control. The straying is from “His Way,” the path He has set. Those who are astray are not unknown to Allah; He knows them individually. Likewise, He knows “who is rightly guided.” This knowledge is complete and perfect, encompassing not only outward actions but inner sincerity. The verse implies that believers should not be overly distressed by the misguidance of the multitudes. The matter rests with Allah, who will judge each person according to His knowledge. It also warns those who are astray that they cannot hide their condition. For the guided, it is a source of comfort: being among the minority does not indicate being wrong, for Allah acknowledges and knows the guided ones. This verse encourages focusing on one’s own guidance and leaving the ultimate assessment of others to Allah. It also instills humility; we do not know our own ultimate end, but we trust that the Knower of all will deal justly.

=118=

The surah transitions from the broad principles of guidance to a specific practical command regarding food. This was a direct challenge to the Meccan pagans who had invented their own dietary taboos and who slaughtered animals in the name of their idols. “So eat only of what is slaughtered in Allah’s Name” commands the believers to consume meat only from animals over which the name of Allah was intentionally pronounced at the time of slaughter. The mentioning of Allah’s name is an acknowledgment that the life being taken belongs to Him and is only taken by His permission. It purifies the act and distinguishes it from the pagan practice of dedicating slaughter to false gods.

The phrase “if you truly believe in His revelations” makes this dietary practice a litmus test of faith. True belief in the Quran’s verses must manifest in obedience to its commands, including the seemingly mundane act of eating.

This command does not imply that food not slaughtered by Muslims is inherently physically harmful; it is a matter of spiritual purity and obedience. The prohibition of meat slaughtered in other than Allah’s name is directly tied to the rejection of shirk. For the early Muslims in Mecca, adhering to this command often meant social and economic difficulty, as the meat available in the markets was often slaughtered by polytheists.

Yet, they obeyed because their faith in the revelations was genuine. This verse teaches that Islam is a complete way of life where even eating is an act of worship and a declaration of monotheism, and that faith demands making conscious choices about what enters one’s body.

=119=

This verse addresses any hesitation or confusion the believers might have had. “Why should you not eat of what is slaughtered in Allah’s Name” is a rhetorical question encouraging consumption without doubt, as long as the proper condition is met. The reasoning is that “He has already explained to you what He has forbidden to you.” The forbidden foods have been clearly outlined—carrion, blood, pork, and what is dedicated to other than Allah. Everything else is lawful, so there is no need to complicate the matter or invent additional prohibitions as the pagans did.

The verse then adds the important exception: “except when compelled by necessity.” If someone is in a life-threatening situation and the only available food is the forbidden, they may eat just enough to survive, without transgressing the limits. Then the verse warns about a source of confusion: “Many deviants certainly mislead others by their whims out of ignorance. ” These are the people—pagan leaders or later false scholars—who prohibit what Allah has made lawful and vice versa, following their personal desires rather than revelation. They do so without genuine knowledge, leading others astray.

The verse ends with the reassurance: “Surely your Lord knows the transgressors best.” Those who exceed the limits, whether by unjustly prohibiting the lawful or by transgressing in necessity, are fully known to Allah and will be held accountable.

This verse establishes the Islamic principle that in matters of halal and haram, the primary reference is divine revelation, not human conjecture. It grants ease and removes the chains of superstition.

=120=

After addressing the specific issue of food, the surah broadens the command to encompass all moral conduct. “Shun all sin—open and secret.

” The Arabic word for “sin” here is ithm, which encompasses every form of wrongdoing, whether against Allah or against fellow beings. “Open” (ẓāhir) refers to sins committed publicly, like theft, adultery, or open mockery of faith. “Secret” (bāṭin) refers to hidden sins, such as secret envy, concealed disbelief, plotting harm, or indulging in forbidden thoughts. The command demands comprehensive moral integrity: the external and the internal life must both be purified. Islam does not only care about public reputation; it is a religion that polices the conscience. The believer is accountable before Allah, who sees both the visible and the hidden.

The verse then states the principle of divine justice: “Indeed, those who commit sin will be punished for what they earn.” The punishment is a direct consequence of their own acquired deeds.

The word “earn” (yaksibūn) indicates that sins are actively acquired by one’s choices; they are not forced upon anyone. This emphasizes personal responsibility. The verse serves as a powerful deterrent, reminding the believers that no sin, no matter how secret, will escape Allah’s knowledge and just recompense. It also encourages a life of transparency and sincerity, where one’s private self is as righteous as the public self, because the true Judge is aware of all. This comprehensive command rounds off the ethical directives, tying the dietary law to the broader framework of taqwā.
