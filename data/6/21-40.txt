=21=

“Who could be more unjust than one who invents a lie against Allah or denies His signs? Surely the wrongdoers will never truly succeed.”

Inventing against Allah corrupts the highest source of guidance. A human preference is announced as divine law, an idol is called His partner, or a false promise of salvation is placed in His mouth. People may then obey the invention precisely because they fear God.

The Makkan forms included claiming idols were authorised intercessors and attributing livestock taboos to Allah. Earlier scriptural distortions and every later statement of “Allah says” without knowledge fall under the same warning according to their degree.

Denying His signs is the opposite route to the same injustice. The fabricator adds what Allah did not reveal; the denier removes what He did reveal. Both place the human ego over divine testimony.

The rhetorical “who is more unjust?” identifies this as among the greatest wrongs without requiring a ranking between every individual sin. Shirk violates Allah’s exclusive right, misleads other people, and ruins the soul committing it.

Believers should be careful with confident religious speech. A scholar may explain an interpretation and acknowledge disagreement; a non-specialist can say, “As far as I know.” Humble qualification is safer than turning uncertainty into divine certainty.

Digital tools can now generate polished religious answers and fabricated quotations within seconds. Fluency is not authority. Quran references, hadith sources, scholarly attribution, and context should be checked before sharing, especially when an answer affects marriage, money, safety, or another person’s reputation. The person who publishes machine-generated falsehood as Allah’s law remains responsible for careless attribution.

Good-faith scholarly error is not fabrication when reached through qualified effort. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said a judge who strives and is correct receives two rewards, while one who strives and errs receives one. Fraud, recklessness, and sincere interpretation are not the same.

Wrongdoers may gain money, followers, or political control, but this is not falah. True success includes a sound ending before Allah. Temporary reach can enlarge eventual loss.

Verse 22 takes the reader to that final court. All people are gathered, and the polytheists are asked where the partners they claimed have gone.

=22=

“On the Day We gather them all together, We will ask those who associated partners, ‘Where are those alleged partners you used to claim?’”

Gathering removes every hiding place and every social boundary. The first and last, leader and follower, public preacher and private doubter stand in one assembly. No group can create a separate court for its own theology.

Allah’s question does not seek information. It exposes the claim publicly and forces worshippers to confront the absence and powerlessness of what they trusted. A god that cannot appear, answer, or protect at the decisive moment was never a god.

“Your alleged partners” points to their claim rather than validating partnership. Allah owns every angel, prophet, jinn, and object they may have invoked. None shares His throne merely because humans assigned a title.

Some worshipped actual created beings while inventing divine status; others carved objects or followed imagined names with no corresponding power. In either case, the religious relationship was fabricated.

The scene also exposes functional partners. Wealth, ancestry, public approval, and rulers may not be called gods, but a person can rely upon them as if they override divine judgment. On the Day, every dependent support reveals its limit.

Lawful intercession does not contradict the verse. Intercession belongs to Allah’s permission and occurs only for those He accepts; it is not a power idols or saints independently own. Worship is never directed to them as the price of access.

The beings falsely worshipped do not all share one response. Lifeless idols cannot answer, while prophets and angels can publicly reject the worship assigned to them, as Isa does in 5:116. Satan may acknowledge that he only invited his followers. Every form of the scene reaches the same result: no partner possesses the independent status its worshipper claimed.

The question invites repentance now: identify what receives ultimate fear, hope, and obedience before the final question is asked. Verse 23 then records the polytheists’ only desperate response—they swear by Allah that they were never polytheists.

=23=

“Then their only desperate response will be to say, ‘By Allah, our Lord, we were never polytheists.’”

Fitnah here can mean their excuse, response under trial, or the outcome of their false worship. Every elaborate justification used in life collapses into a bare denial.

They swear by Allah and call Him “our Lord,” consistent with polytheists who recognised a supreme Creator while assigning partners in worship. Recognition of lordship did not make their practice tawhid.

Why lie before the One who knows? Terror may drive a futile attempt, or they may try to distance themselves from the shame once they see people of tawhid rewarded. Some commentators understood scenes of denial as an early stage before mouths are sealed and limbs testify.

The Quran describes several moments of the Day: people speak, deny, confess, and become unable to hide. These are not contradictions. A failed lie can precede forced recognition; a long Day contains stages.

Their denial also reveals worldly self-deception. They may have called idols only intercessors and refused the word “polytheist” for themselves. A person’s preferred label does not change the structure of worship.

Believers should be cautious about minimising sins through vocabulary. Interest renamed a service charge, abuse renamed discipline, or shirk renamed mere respect can remain what Allah judged when its reality is unchanged. Names matter less than facts.

The verse is not permission to interrogate hearts or accuse Muslims recklessly of shirk. Major theological rulings require knowledge, evidence, and distinction between act and person. Its first purpose is warning the worshipper.

Verse 24 tells the Prophet to observe how they lie against themselves and how every invented object of worship disappears from them.

=24=

“See how they lie against themselves, and how everything they fabricated will vanish from them.”

A lie against oneself denies one’s own history and moral reality. Their deeds are recorded, their objects of worship witnessed, and their own limbs may testify, yet they try to disown the identity they built.

What they fabricated “strays” or disappears. The idols provide no answer, imagined intercessory rights vanish, and worshipped servants reject being made gods. Falsehood appeared solid only while the world allowed it social support.

This is a deeper loss than discovering one mistaken fact. A whole structure of meaning, ritual, and alliance collapses precisely when the person needs it most. Only truth can survive the Day of Truth.

People can experience smaller versions in life: a fraudulent investment collapses, a manipulative leader abandons followers, or status disappears after scandal. Such moments can become mercy if they awaken repentance before final exposure.

The verse does not say every attachment is idolatry. Love of family, livelihood, and community is natural. Fabrication occurs when created things receive worship, divine claims, or confidence that belongs only to Allah.

Self-honesty is therefore part of tawhid. A believer should name motives, admit contradictions, and ask forgiveness instead of protecting a religious self-image. Truthful repentance now prevents futile denial later.

Verse 25 returns to their present listening. Some attend the Prophet’s recitation, yet coverings lie over their hearts and heaviness in their ears because of persistent refusal. Even when they come to argue, they dismiss the Quran as ancient legends.

=25=

“Among them are those who listen to you, but We have placed coverings over their hearts so they do not understand it and heaviness in their ears. Even if they saw every sign, they would not believe. When they come to argue with you, the disbelievers say, ‘This is nothing but legends of earlier peoples.’”

Listening is not always seeking. Some Makkan leaders heard the Quran to find material for objection, assess its influence, or maintain the appearance of fairness. The ear receives sound while the heart has decided against meaning.

The coverings and heaviness are divine judgment corresponding to repeated aversion. Allah does not block a sincere seeker arbitrarily. The previous verses describe denial, fabrication, and mockery; chosen resistance eventually becomes inability to benefit.

Waqar is heaviness rather than physical deafness. Disability carries no moral blame. The Quran criticises functioning faculties deliberately unused for guidance, not Deaf people or those with cognitive limitations.

“Every sign” would not convince a heart that has made rejection part of identity. The issue is not that evidence never matters, but that moral willingness is one condition for evidence to guide.

Asatir al-awwalin—stories written or transmitted by earlier people—dismissed Quranic narratives as recycled myth. Yet the Quran’s use of earlier history is open: it confirms true prophetic events, corrects distortions, and draws moral proof. Previous subject matter does not establish human authorship.

Argument itself can be beneficial. The verse condemns disputation that arrives with a verdict already settled, not respectful examination. The Prophet debated, answered questions, and invited reflection.

Modern listening can be similarly performative: playing a lecture only to clip one sentence or reading scripture solely for attack. Intellectual integrity requires context and readiness to revise one’s conclusion.

Recommendation systems can deepen the covering by repeatedly serving material that confirms mockery and never allowing a serious explanation to remain long enough for thought. Technology does not create the original refusal, but it can reward it. Deliberately seeking primary sources and the strongest version of an argument is one way to resist a self-made echo chamber.

Verse 26 says these opponents also turn others away while keeping themselves distant. Their campaign destroys only their own souls, though they fail to perceive it.

=26=

“They prevent others from the Quran and keep themselves away from it. Yet they destroy none but themselves, though they do not perceive it.”

The most direct reading takes the pronoun as referring to the Quran or Prophet: they block people from listening and refuse guidance personally. Rejection becomes activism against another person’s access.

Some early commentary connected the wording to Abu Talib, who protected Muhammad from harm while not following his religion: he restrained others from attacking him but remained distant from belief. Whether or not applied to that case, the first reading fits the surrounding deniers and their obstruction.

Misleading others multiplies responsibility. A person may publish distortions, intimidate seekers, pressure relatives, or use power to make religious practice costly. Followers retain agency, but the blocker bears a share of the path he built.

“They destroy only themselves” does not mean no victim suffers. Obstruction can cause real worldly and spiritual harm. It means the final burden returns to the obstructer, while Allah’s truth cannot be extinguished.

The failure to perceive makes opposition especially tragic. They may think they protect tradition, family unity, or social order while investing in their own loss. Sincere self-examination asks whether the defended interest is truly righteous.

Muslims can also drive people away through abuse, false teaching, racism, or hypocrisy. Although not the original subject, the warning applies by principle: do not make your conduct a barrier to the Quran and then blame seekers for remaining distant.

The door remains open to use influence in the opposite direction—provide accurate access, remove practical barriers, and protect freedom of conscience without coercion.

Verse 27 shows the deniers standing before the Fire and begging to return, promising belief and an end to denial. The sight they demanded arrives after the test has closed.

=27=

“If only you could see them when they are made to stand before the Fire. They will cry, ‘If only we could be sent back! Then we would never deny our Lord’s signs, and we would become believers.’”

The Fire turns abstract warning into direct sight. Social confidence, philosophical dismissal, and ridicule disappear. Their wish is not for more evidence in the same conditions but for an entire second life.

They promise two changes: stop denial and embrace faith. This shows they now understand the connection between creed and destination. The problem is timing—recognition under unavoidable sight is not the faith freely chosen in the unseen.

The Quran often asks the listener to imagine future scenes so they can act before seeing them. Revelation gives the benefit of foresight without requiring actual punishment. The wise person borrows certainty from Allah’s description.

“If only” is the language of irreversible opportunity. Small daily chances to repent can feel ordinary, but their value becomes enormous once they no longer exist. Delaying prayer, restitution, or shahadah assumes return is guaranteed.

The scene should produce compassionate urgency in da‘wah, not satisfaction. Believers want people rescued before they stand there. They also fear making promises to repent later while continuing known wrong.

The verse does not deny the possibility of sincere conversion near death before the final threshold, but repentance after the unseen becomes visibly certain is another matter. Allah knows the exact boundary.

A crisis in this life can still become a genuine beginning. Someone who prays in an emergency and then keeps praying after safety, returns rights, and reforms has turned fear into tawbah. The condemned promise is the one Allah knows would vanish immediately if the punishment vanished. Present conduct after relief is therefore a practical test of what crisis words meant.

Verse 28 reveals that their worldly concealment is now exposed and that, even if returned, their entrenched character would lead them back to prohibition. Their promise is judged false by the One who knows every possible path.

=28=

“Rather, what they used to conceal has now become clear to them. Even if they were sent back, they would return to what they had been forbidden. They are certainly liars.”

The Fire exposes what was buried: recognition of truth, knowledge of wrongdoing, or the reality of deeds concealed behind excuses. Judgment is disclosure as well as punishment.

Their promise may feel sincere under terror, yet Allah knows it would not survive restored distance, appetite, and social pressure. Knowledge of what they would do under a counterfactual situation belongs fully to Him.

Calling them liars does not require that they consciously formulate a lie in the moment. A promise can be false because the speaker’s settled character would betray it, even while fear produces temporary conviction.

This warns against repentance based only on crisis emotion. Illness, exposure, or loss may soften a person, but durable tawbah requires changed beliefs, habits, companions, restitution, and safeguards after the crisis passes.

The verse does not teach that ordinary people can tell a repentant sinner, “You would certainly return.” Humans do not know alternate futures. They judge sincere outward reform and leave hidden persistence to Allah.

It also explains why endless extensions of the test would not resolve rebellion. More time is useful for someone willing to change; it does not automatically cure a will attached to denial.

Believers can ask Allah for thabat—steadfastness—because momentary insight is not enough. Faith must remain when fear subsides and the world looks attractive again.

Verse 29 quotes the doctrine underlying their conduct: they claim there is only this worldly life and deny resurrection altogether.

=29=

“They say, ‘There is nothing beyond our worldly life, and we will never be resurrected.’”

The verse records a materialist conclusion in the language of the deniers. Some Makkan polytheists acknowledged a creator while rejecting bodily resurrection; worship and inherited ritual did not necessarily include final accountability.

If this life is the only horizon, then status, survival, and pleasure can easily become ultimate measures. Moral sacrifice for an unseen court appears irrational, and unpunished oppression seems finally successful.

Denial of resurrection often focused on decayed bones. The Quran answers throughout the surah by pointing to first creation, earth revived by rain, and the Creator’s unrestricted power. Originating life is sufficient evidence of ability to restore it.

The verse does not claim every atheist is immoral or every believer behaves well. People can inherit ethical habits and empathy, while believers can violate their creed. It identifies the metaphysical loss of final accountability, not a simplistic description of every individual.

“We will not be raised” is asserted without access to the unseen. The Quran asks what knowledge supports certainty about non-existence after death. Refusal of evidence is not itself evidence.

For believers, resurrection should shape more than speech. Hoarding, injustice, and obsession with reputation may reveal practical living as though only this world exists. Creed becomes real when the future meeting changes present choices.

Verse 30 answers their confident denial with the scene they refused: they stand before their Lord, confess under oath that resurrection is true, and are told to taste the consequence of disbelief.

=30=

“If only you could see when they are made to stand before their Lord. He will ask, ‘Is this not the truth?’ They will reply, ‘Yes, by our Lord!’ He will say, ‘Then taste the punishment because you disbelieved.’”

Standing before “their Lord” is unavoidable even though they refused His lordship’s implications. The One who created, sustained, and sent signs now judges. Their relationship to Him existed even while denied.

The question publicly establishes truth through their own confession. Allah does not need their admission, but it closes every worldly argument and displays perfect proof.

“By our Lord” mirrors the oath in verse 23. There they denied shirk; here they acknowledge resurrection. Their tongue shifts under undeniable reality, while the record remains stable.

Taste conveys direct experience. Punishment is no longer a proposition discussed from distance. Its stated cause is their disbelief—the settled rejection described through signs, mockery, and denial of meeting.

The verse should not be used to enjoy another’s imagined torment. It is addressed as warning now, while the listener can say “Yes” through faith and action without tasting punishment.

A believer also learns to confess truth before compulsion. Admitting a debt, sin, or doctrinal correction in life may be painful but remains an act of freedom that can lead to mercy.

Human courts sometimes force false confessions, so this divine scene cannot justify coercive interrogation. Allah already knows every fact and wrongs no one; human institutions require safeguards precisely because they do not.

Verse 31 calls deniers of the meeting losers and describes the Hour arriving suddenly. They carry their burdens and lament the opportunity they neglected.

=31=

“Those who deny the meeting with Allah have certainly lost. When the Hour suddenly overtakes them, they will cry, ‘Woe to us for having neglected it!’ They will carry their burdens upon their backs. Evil indeed is what they bear.”

The meeting with Allah means resurrection, judgment, and exposure before Him, not physical merging with the Creator. Denying it shaped a life unprepared for its arrival.

The Hour comes baghtatan—suddenly. Its cosmic arrival has signs, but each person’s death can also end preparation unexpectedly. The useful question is not whether one can calculate the date but whether one is ready today.

Farratna conveys negligence, wasting, and letting something valuable slip. They did not lack an opportunity; they treated it as disposable until it became the only thing desired.

Sins become awzar, heavy burdens. Deeds that felt light while repeated now have weight. Leaders who misled others may carry added burdens corresponding to their influence without reducing followers’ responsibility.

The image does not require speculation about a literal shape for every sin. It communicates crushing ownership: no friend or idol carries the account away. The Prophet taught each soul will meet consequences with complete justice.

Some burdens arise from rights owed to other people. The bankrupt person in the hadith arrives with worship but has insulted, struck, slandered, and taken wealth; good deeds are transferred to victims and their sins may be placed upon him when his good is exhausted. Settling harm now is therefore lighter than carrying a case with no currency except deeds.

“Evil is what they bear” contrasts with travellers choosing good provision. Taqwa is called the best provision for pilgrimage (2:197); sin is the worst luggage for the final journey.

The verse encourages periodic accounting before the surprise: settle debts, repair relationships, repent, and convert intentions into deeds. Regret without time cannot do what one honest action can do now.

Verse 32 places the world in proportion. Detached from the Hereafter it is play and distraction, while the final home is better for people of taqwa. Understanding should recognise the difference.

=32=

“The life of this world is nothing but play and distracting amusement, while the home of the Hereafter is far better for those mindful of Allah. Will you not understand?”

La‘ib is play whose arrangement ends with the game; lahw is distraction that occupies a person from something more important. Worldly competition resembles both when treated as final purpose.

The verse does not say earthly life has no serious duties. The Quran regulates family, trade, justice, worship, and care precisely because choices here have eternal consequence. Life is trivial only when detached from its purpose.

Lawful recreation can restore strength and relationship. The Prophet raced with Aishah, allowed appropriate celebration, and recognised bodily rights. Amusement becomes blameworthy when it displaces prayer, rights, or the final horizon.

The Hereafter is al-dar—the real home of permanence. Worldly housing is temporary even when owned for decades. A believer uses the temporary residence to prepare for the lasting one without neglecting current inhabitants.

“Better for the mindful” ties superiority to taqwa. The Hereafter is not merely longer; it is morally ordered and free from the injustice and decay of this world for those accepted.

The appeal to reason asks people to compare duration, quality, and certainty. Trading eternity for brief appetite is not sophisticated realism but failed calculation.

Modern economies can monetise attention until distraction becomes a full-time environment. The verse invites deliberate limits on screens, consumption, and status games so amusement remains servant rather than master.

Work, study, parenting, civic responsibility, and building beneficial institutions are not “mere play” when done lawfully and for a sound purpose. They can become worship through intention and conduct. The verse criticises a worldview with no horizon beyond consumption, not serious cultivation of the earth as Allah’s trustee.

Verse 33 turns tenderly to the Prophet: Allah knows their words grieve him. They do not truly establish him as a liar; the wrongdoers are rejecting Allah’s signs.

=33=

“We certainly know that what they say grieves you. It is not truly you they regard as false; rather, the wrongdoers are rejecting Allah’s signs.”

The Prophet’s sadness is acknowledged, not dismissed as weak faith. Slander, mockery, and rejection hurt a truthful human being, especially when he wants good for the speakers. Allah’s knowledge itself gives companionship.

Quraysh had known Muhammad as trustworthy before revelation. Many did not possess evidence that he had suddenly become a liar; they rejected what accepting the message would require. The attack on his person was a vehicle for resisting the signs.

The wording has also been read as saying they may not inwardly deny his honesty even while publicly treating him as false. Allah knows the exact degree of recognition; the central comfort is that the conflict concerns His revelation.

This does not mean every criticism of a Muslim caller is rejection of Allah. Callers can make errors, behave badly, and deserve correction. Only a true messenger can receive this assurance in its full sense. Later teachers should review themselves before assuming all opposition is persecution.

For believers unfairly mocked because of a sound practice, the verse separates personal worth from hostile speech. They can seek support, set boundaries, and continue without letting insult define them.

Grief should not become hatred of the people one hopes to guide. The Prophet maintained invitation and justice while Allah carried the deeper case.

Verse 34 reminds him that earlier messengers were also denied and harmed, yet remained patient until help came. No one can alter Allah’s words, and prophetic histories already demonstrate the pattern.

=34=

“Messengers before you were also denied, yet they patiently endured rejection and persecution until Our help came to them. None can change Allah’s words, and accounts of those messengers have already reached you.”

Prophetic history converts lonely pain into companionship. Nuh, Ibrahim, Musa, Lut, and others faced denial in different forms. Muhammad’s experience is not evidence his mission failed but evidence he walks their road.

Sabr includes continuing obedience, restraining forbidden response, and enduring harm without surrendering truth. It is not passive acceptance of every abuse; prophets migrated, sought protection, argued, and took lawful action.

Help comes “until”—after a period whose length the messenger does not control. Delay tests sincerity, develops communities, establishes proof, and unfolds wisdom beyond immediate sight.

“No one changes Allah’s words” includes His truthful promise of support and final judgment. Enemies can distort quotations or suppress teachers but cannot make divine truth false or prevent what He decrees.

This phrase should not be isolated into a simplistic discussion of textual preservation alone; in context it assures the Prophet that Allah’s promised pattern cannot be defeated. The Quran’s preservation is established through additional evidence as well.

Stories have “reached” him to strengthen, instruct, and warn. Quranic narrative is not entertainment or exhaustive biography. It selects what forms character for the current mission.

Believers should not demand that help match the form they imagine. A messenger may be saved, migrated, granted victory, or honoured through martyrdom and final vindication. Allah defines help within the complete outcome.

Stories of perseverance should not be used by leaders to keep vulnerable people indefinitely in avoidable harm. Prophets combined patience with migration, consultation, defence, and changed strategy. Sabr is steadfast obedience to Allah, which may require leaving an abusive setting rather than preserving another person’s comfort.

Verse 35 addresses the Prophet’s intense wish for another sign. Even if he could tunnel into earth or climb to heaven, human effort cannot manufacture guidance. If Allah willed compulsion, He could gather everyone upon guidance.

=35=

“If their turning away is unbearable to you, then if you are able, seek a tunnel into the earth or a stairway into the sky and bring them another sign. Had Allah willed, He could have gathered them all upon guidance. So do not be among those who ignore this reality.”

The sentence is a compassionate impossibility, not an actual command to dig or climb. The Prophet’s grief made him long for a sign opponents could not dismiss. Allah shows that no human search can provide what a resistant heart refuses.

A tunnel and stairway cover every direction of extraordinary effort. Even if he could retrieve a sign from hidden earth or heaven, guidance would still belong to Allah and moral response.

Allah could create compulsory belief, but He chose a world of accountable response. His will allows people to choose within His sovereignty and judges them perfectly. The existence of disbelief does not show inability.

“Do not be among the ignorant” is prophetic education, not an accusation of settled ignorance. Compassion must remain informed by the limits Allah set. A caller can want guidance deeply without imagining enough cleverness can control it.

This protects against manipulative da‘wah. Emotional pressure, misleading miracle claims, and hiding difficult teachings may produce conformity but not the faith Allah seeks.

It also protects parents and teachers from crushing guilt. They must learn, love, model, pray, and correct. Another person’s heart is not a project whose result proves their worth.

The verse does not excuse poor conveyance. One should improve language, remove unnecessary barriers, and answer questions. The limit appears after sincere means, not before effort begins.

Verse 36 distinguishes those who truly listen and respond from the spiritually dead. Allah will raise the dead and return them to Himself, even if they do not answer the Messenger now.

=36=

“Only those who listen with receptive hearts will respond. As for the dead, Allah will raise them, then to Him they will all be returned.”

Hearing in the Quran often means more than sound entering an ear. It is attentive reception willing to obey. A person may be physically far from the Prophet and truly hear; another may sit beside him and remain closed.

The “dead” are first a metaphor for hearts unresponsive to revelation, then the verse moves naturally to literal resurrection. The Prophet cannot revive a deliberately dead heart, but Allah raises every physical dead person for judgment.

Again, disability is not the moral image’s target. Deaf believers can receive and respond through sign, text, and heart. The blame concerns chosen refusal of meaning.

People who respond do so by Allah’s guidance while exercising real willingness. The verse neither makes human beings independent creators of faith nor puppets without responsibility.

Callers should seek receptive audiences without giving up too quickly on others. Hearts can revive before death, and many early enemies later listened. Final classification belongs to Allah.

“Returned to Him” means return to His judgment and dominion, not that creatures were pieces of His essence. Creator and creation remain distinct.

The verse warns unresponsive people: silence toward the call does not produce silence in the grave. The One they ignored will raise them.

Verse 37 records yet another demand for a sign. Allah has full power to send one, but most do not understand the wisdom and consequence governing requested miracles.

=37=

“They ask, ‘Why has no miraculous sign been sent down to him from his Lord?’ Say, ‘Allah certainly has power to send down a sign, but most of them do not know.’”

The Quran itself is a sign, and the Prophet’s truthfulness, creation, and earlier history provide evidence. Their demand means a sign selected on their terms, often with no honest commitment to believe afterward.

Allah’s ability is affirmed so withholding cannot be interpreted as weakness. He sent Musa’s staff, Salih’s she-camel, and signs to other prophets. Miracles belong to His choice, not an opponent’s control.

Most do not know the consequence of decisive requested signs. Earlier verses explained that rejection after overwhelming manifestation can end respite and bring judgment. What they call proof on demand may be a request for danger.

They also do not fully know what kind of sign would serve guidance. Spectacle can amaze without reforming. The Quran’s continuing verbal and moral sign addresses generation after generation rather than one crowd.

Believers should avoid promising miracles as a condition for faith. Claims about hidden codes, scientific predictions, or sensational events need rigorous truth. Allah’s power does not validate human exaggeration.

Sincere people may ask for evidence and should receive thoughtful answers. The verse describes persistent demanders, not a ban on apologetics, reason, or research.

Verse 38 points to a sign continually present: every land creature and flying bird forms communities like humans. None is omitted from the divine record, and all are gathered to their Lord.

=38=

“There is no creature moving on the earth nor bird flying with its wings except that they form communities like you. We have neglected nothing in the Record. Then they will all be gathered to their Lord.”

Animals are umam, communities or species ordered by Allah’s creation. They reproduce, communicate, seek provision, protect young, migrate, and occupy ecological roles. The verse invites respect without claiming animal societies are identical to human moral and legal communities.

“Flying with its wings” draws attention to an ordinary wonder that familiarity can hide. The sign is not a vague bird in poetry but embodied flight repeatedly sustained by laws Allah created.

Al-Kitab can be understood as the Preserved Record in which every creature and event is known and written. Some commentators also connect the phrase to the Quran lacking nothing needed for its guidance-purpose, not containing every technical fact as an encyclopaedia. The first reading fits the gathering of creatures strongly.

Authentic hadith teaches that justice will be settled even between a hornless sheep and one that gored it, after which animals become dust. Their gathering demonstrates the comprehensiveness of divine justice without assigning them the same eternal accountability as humans.

The verse undermines careless cruelty. Animals are not meaningless objects. The Prophet condemned starving, overburdening, and tormenting them and rewarded mercy to thirsty creatures.

This does not prohibit lawful food, work with animals, or responsible pest control. It requires need, excellence, and avoidance of unnecessary suffering. Human stewardship operates inside Allah’s ownership.

Ecological destruction can erase whole communities whose place people barely understand. Conservation, humane treatment, and careful science can express gratitude while specific legal policies require expertise.

Recognising animal communities also disciplines scientific and religious arrogance. Human beings can study migration, communication, and ecosystems without claiming every newly observed behaviour was explicitly predicted in technical detail by the verse. Its enduring claim is order, community, divine record, and gathering; research helps people appreciate those signs more accurately.

Verse 39 contrasts these living communities with people spiritually deaf and mute in layers of darkness. Allah allows whom He wills to stray and places whom He wills upon a straight path.

=39=

“Those who deny Our signs are deaf and mute in layers of darkness. Allah allows whoever He wills to stray and places whoever He wills upon a straight path.”

They are “deaf” to receptive hearing and “mute” regarding truthful confession. Their physical senses may function perfectly; the moral faculties refuse their purpose. People with actual hearing or speech disabilities are not demeaned by the metaphor.

Darknesses are plural: ignorance, desire, pride, inherited falsehood, and fear can overlap. Without the single light of revelation, a person may move energetically while lacking direction.

Guidance and misguidance occur under Allah’s will, but His will is not arbitrary. The surah has shown choices of fabrication, denial, mockery, and obstruction. He leaves persistent rejecters to the darkness they prefer and guides sincere seekers by mercy.

No person can demand guidance as a personal achievement. Knowledge, opportunity, courage, and a softened heart are gifts. Gratitude should replace superiority toward someone still searching.

At the same time, people are commanded to listen and respond. Divine decree does not excuse chosen denial because they do not know the decree before choosing. Revelation addresses their real agency.

Calling a person “spiritually dead” is not an ordinary Muslim’s licence to stop justice or invitation. The Quran knows final inner states; humans see limited outward evidence and continue hoping while life remains.

The straight path is made straight by its destination and Lawgiver, not by popularity. A lone guided person can be more correctly placed than a crowd in darkness.

Verse 40 appeals to the polytheists’ own crisis instinct. If Allah’s punishment or the Hour arrived, would they sincerely call anyone other than Allah?

=40=

“Say, ‘Consider this: if Allah’s punishment came upon you or the Hour overtook you, would you call upon anyone other than Allah, if you are truthful?’”

The question strips polytheism of ceremony. In overwhelming danger, when social performance and ordinary means fail, whom does the heart recognise as possessing ultimate rescue?

Makkan polytheists often called upon Allah sincerely during danger at sea, then returned to partners on land. Crisis uncovered a fitrah-level recognition that idols did not control the final outcome.

The verse does not claim every human verbally calls Allah in every emergency. People’s conscious responses differ. It challenges the addressed polytheists through their own acknowledged practice and the logic of ultimate power.

Calling emergency services, doctors, or nearby people remains lawful. The question concerns ultimate du‘a and rescue from a divine punishment or the Hour, matters no creature can independently avert. Means are used while the heart knows their limit.

“If you are truthful” asks them to make polytheism operational. If partners truly share divine control, this should be the moment to call them. Their disappearance from crisis exposes the claim.

Calamity can soften false self-sufficiency, but faith born in crisis must continue after relief. Otherwise danger becomes a temporary interruption rather than guidance.

Believers can apply the question before ordinary anxiety: have created means occupied the heart more than the One who controls them? The answer is not abandoning means but restoring prayer, gratitude, and proportion.
