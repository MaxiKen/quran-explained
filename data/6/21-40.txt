=21=

Following the declaration that the People of the Scripture recognized the Prophet yet many disbelieved, the Quran now poses a rhetorical question highlighting the gravest injustice. “Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah” refers to anyone who falsely attributes statements, laws, or partners to God.

In the context of the Meccans, this included claiming that idols were divine intercessors or that certain foods were forbidden by Allah when He had not forbidden them. It also encompasses the People of the Book who concealed prophecies about the Prophet or distorted their scriptures.

The second group mentioned are those “who deny His signs.” Denying clear revelations after they have come is a profound transgression because it is a rejection not born of ignorance but of arrogance.

The word “more wrong” (aẓlam) indicates that no one surpasses these people in wrongdoing, as they violate the most fundamental right of the Creator—to be recognized and worshipped alone—and they mislead others. The verse then delivers a definitive verdict: “Indeed, the wrongdoers will never succeed. ” Success here is both worldly and eternal. They might enjoy temporary prosperity, but ultimately they fail to achieve their purpose, face ruin, and earn Allah’s punishment.

The use of “never” conveys absolute certainty. This verse connects backward to the earlier mention of those who “ruined themselves” and forward to the scenes of judgment to come. It establishes a core Quranic principle that ultimate success is tied to truthfulness and faith, while fabrication and denial lead to inevitable loss. The lesson for believers is to be meticulous in what they ascribe to Allah, ensuring that their beliefs and practices are grounded in authentic revelation, and to never fall into the trap of denying a clear sign due to pride or worldly pressure.

=22=

The surah now transports us to the Day of Judgment to witness the humiliation of the polytheists. The opening word “Consider” invites the Prophet and the reader to visualize this scene vividly. “The Day We will gather them all together” underscores the universality of the resurrection; every human being from the first to the last will be assembled without exception. No one will be able to hide or escape. Then Allah will specifically question “those who associated others with Allah in worship.

” The question, “Where are those gods you used to claim?” is not a request for information but a rebuke and a mockery of their false beliefs. These “gods” could be idols, angels, jinn, prophets, saints, or celestial bodies. In the worldly life, they proudly claimed these beings could intercede, harm, or benefit. Now, in the divine presence, they are asked to produce them. The absence of these supposed deities will become glaringly obvious.

The question exposes the emptiness of shirk. It also serves to intensify the shame and regret of the disbelievers, as they realize they devoted their lives to figments of their imagination. This verse demonstrates Allah’s absolute justice: He does not punish without establishing clear evidence against the wrongdoers, and their own beliefs will be turned into a witness against them.

For the believer, this scene is a powerful deterrent against any form of shirk, whether overt idol worship or subtle reliance on other than Allah. It reminds us that on that Day, all false supports will vanish.

=23=

This verse reveals the desperate reaction of the polytheists when confronted. “Their only argument” indicates they have no logical defense, no justification for their worship.

The word “argument” can also be translated as “trial” or “excuse,” but here it is their futile attempt to escape. They will swear, “By Allah, our Lord! We were never polytheists.

” This is a blatant lie, and they know it. The scholars of tafsir offer several explanations for this denial. Some say that in the terror of that Day, they will think lying might save them. Others explain that they will genuinely believe, for a moment, that they were not polytheists because they considered their worship of idols as mere intermediation and not direct association, or they might forget their polytheism due to the overwhelming horror.

Yet another interpretation is that when they see the mercy of Allah, they will hope to escape by pretending to have been monotheists. The oath “By Allah, our Lord!” shows they still recognize Allah as the supreme Lord, which is consistent with the Meccan belief in Allah as the Creator despite their shirk. But their own tongues will betray them. The verse illustrates that on the Day of Judgment, false claims will be swiftly exposed. This lying cannot deceive Allah, the Knower of all secrets. The scene teaches that shirk is so deeply shameful that its perpetrators will try to disown it.

For the believer, it is a warning against self-deception and the danger of minimizing one’s sins, because on that Day, no lie will be of any avail.

=24=

Continuing the dramatic courtroom scene, Allah directs attention to the double disaster that befalls the polytheists. “See how they will lie about themselves” is an expression of astonishment. They denied being polytheists, thus lying against their own souls. This self-incriminating falsehood adds to their punishment because they not only committed shirk but now attempt to cover it with a lie before the All-Knowing.

The phrase “about themselves” implies that their lie is directed against their own reality; they are disowning the very deeds that their limbs will testify to. The second part, “and how those gods they fabricated will fail them,” highlights the desertion by their false deities. The idols, the angels, the prophets, or whatever beings they worshipped will disappear from their sight or dissociate from them.

The word “fabricated” (yaftarūn) indicates that these gods were creations of their own imagination, having no real existence or power. They will not be present to intercede or defend, leaving their worshippers utterly alone. This dual failure—the failure of their own lies and the failure of their fabricated gods—encompasses complete hopelessness. This verse powerfully illustrates that whatever is worshipped besides Allah will vanish when most needed, and the soul that relied on them will stand naked before its Creator. It’s a sobering reminder to examine our attachments: anything we prioritize over Allah, be it wealth, status, or human approval, will similarly abandon us on that Day.

=25=

After the scenes of judgment, the surah returns to the psychology of the disbelievers in this world. “There are some of them who pretend to listen to your recitation” describes the Meccan leaders who would physically attend the Prophet’s recitations, not to understand but to mock, to find fault, or to appear open-minded.

However, their outward listening is useless because of a divine impediment: “but We have cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend it—and deafness in their ears.” The “veils” (akinnah) are spiritual coverings placed as a result of their persistent arrogance and rejection. This does not mean Allah forced them to disbelieve; rather, when they continuously chose to turn away, Allah sealed their faculties, making it impossible for them to benefit. The “deafness in their ears” prevents the words from penetrating. The result is that “even if they were to see every sign, they still would not believe in them.” No miracle, no matter how clear, would change their stance because the problem lies in the heart, not in the evidence. They would even come to argue aggressively, calling the Quran “nothing but ancient fables.” This slander reflects their deep-seated denial; they dismissed the stories of resurrection, the narratives of past nations, and the moral teachings as old legends recycled. This verse offers a profound insight: repeated rejection of truth deadens the spiritual senses. It also comforts the Prophet, explaining that the disbelief he faced wasn't due to a lack of clarity in his message but to a spiritual sickness in his audience.

=26=

The previous verse described the disbelievers’ inner state; this verse exposes their outward actions and their consequences. “They turn others away from the Prophet” refers to their active efforts to prevent people from listening to Muhammad. The Meccan chiefs like Abu Lahab and Abu Jahl would warn visiting tribes, saying he was a sorcerer who divided families. They would station men at the outskirts of Mecca to turn back anyone coming to see the Prophet. “And distance themselves as well” indicates they also keep away from him personally, refusing to engage sincerely. They combine two evils: they block others from guidance and reject it themselves. Then the verse reveals the tragic irony: “They ruin none but themselves.” Despite their efforts, they cannot harm the Prophet or his mission in the long run, nor can they truly prevent those whom Allah wills to guide. The ultimate damage is self-inflicted. Their turning away distances them from mercy, and their obstruction adds to their burden of sin without actually achieving its aim. “Yet they fail to perceive it” shows their profound ignorance; they think they are successful in undermining the Prophet, but in reality, they are digging their own graves.

This is a timeless principle: those who actively oppose the truth and mislead others will bear the full consequences of their misguidance while the truth continues to spread by Allah’s will. It’s a warning to anyone who uses their influence to obstruct people from the path of Allah, reminding them that the only one truly harmed is themselves.

=27=

The surah once again pulls back the curtain to show a future scene of immense regret. “If only you could see” addresses the Prophet, and by extension every listener, to imagine the shocking sight. “When they will be detained before the Fire” depicts the moment the disbelievers are brought to the edge of Hell, made to stand and witness its horror before being cast in. The “detention” is a pause filled with terror. Upon seeing the Fire, their bravado evaporates, and they cry out in despair. Their words: “Oh! If only we could be sent back, we would never deny the signs of our Lord and we would surely be of the believers” express a deep, desperate wish for a second chance. They promise to abandon their denial and embrace faith wholeheartedly. This reaction reveals that at that moment, the truth becomes undeniably clear; all the doubts and objections they had in the world vanish when faced with tangible reality.

However, this wish is futile because the test period has ended. The verse exposes the psychology of delayed obedience: many people live in sin, assuming they can always repent later, but when death or judgment arrives, their sudden realization and promises are worthless. It also shows that the disbelievers’ problem was not a lack of evidence but stubbornness. The believers are reminded to seize the opportunity of life before the moment of irreversible regret. The verse sets up the divine response that follows, where their lies are unmasked.

=28=

Allah immediately refutes the disbelievers’ desperate plea. “But no!” (kallā) is a sharp negation, dismissing their claims. The reason they are making these promises is “because the truth they used to hide will become all too clear to them.” In the world, they concealed the truth of monotheism, the reality of the resurrection, and the veracity of the Prophet, burying them under ridicule and falsehood. Now, with the Fire before their eyes, that hidden truth is manifest, leaving no room for denial. Their declaration is not a sincere change of heart but a reaction to punishment. Then Allah reveals a profound truth about their nature: “Even if they were to be sent back, they would certainly revert to what they were forbidden.

” This is based on Allah’s eternal knowledge of their hearts. Their character, formed by a lifetime of arrogance and transgression, would lead them back to the same sins. The world would again tempt them, and their weak faith would again crumble.

The verse concludes, “Indeed they are liars!” This pronouncement confirms that their promise of belief is false. They are lying to themselves and to Allah, though they might not be conscious of it. This definitive statement ends any hope that such people would become righteous if given another chance. The lesson is stark: the disbelievers’ problem is not lack of time but a corrupted will and heart. This underscores the urgency of genuine repentance in this life, before the sealing of the fate and the removal of the opportunity to change.

=29=

To further explain the roots of their disbelief, the Quran quotes their fundamental doctrine. “They insisted” indicates stubborn adherence, not a casual opinion. The statement “There is nothing beyond this worldly life” is a clear expression of materialistic atheism or deism that denies the afterlife. They saw life as confined to birth, enjoyment, and death, with no accountability. The addition “and we will never be resurrected” emphasizes their strong rejection of any return. This was the belief of many Meccan polytheists, despite their nominal acknowledgment of Allah as Creator. They found the idea of bones being reassembled after decay absurd. Their denial of the Hereafter was the root of their moral recklessness; if there is no judgment, then there is no reason to restrain desires or adhere to moral laws. This verse contextualizes why they mocked the Quran’s warnings about resurrection. It also shows that belief in the Hereafter is the pivot of ethical responsibility. By quoting their own words, Allah preserves the record of their error and exposes its fallacy. The immediate next verse responds by painting the scene of their standing before the Lord, forcing them to confront the truth they denied. The contrast between their confident assertion and their humbled admission on Judgment Day serves as a powerful rhetorical device. The lesson for the reader is to recognize that the denial of the Hereafter is not a new phenomenon, and its consequence is eternal ruin.

=30=

Mirroring verse 27, the surah presents another vision of the Hereafter, this time with the direct interrogation by Allah. “If only you could see when they will be detained before their Lord!

” The emphasis on “their Lord” reminds them that the One they denied will be the One they stand before. The scene portrays a formal, inescapable encounter. Allah asks, “Is this Hereafter not the truth?

” The question is not for information; it is a rebuke, a way of making them admit their error with their own mouths. The polytheists, stripped of all pretense, will cry out, “Absolutely, by our Lord!” They emphatically confess, using an oath, that the Hereafter is indeed true. This admission mirrors their earlier lie when they swore they were not polytheists; now they are forced to swear to a truth they once denied. Then comes the devastating command: “Then taste the punishment for your disbelief.

” The word “taste” implies direct, personal experience of the torment they scoffed at. The punishment is directly linked to their disbelief (kufr), which encompassed denial of the resurrection, the signs, and the messengers. This verse demonstrates that divine justice is not arbitrary but follows clear establishment of proof and self-admission. It is a chilling warning that the pleasures of denial are temporary, and the consequence is an unending punishment.

The repetition of “if only you could see” serves to intensify the emotional impact, making the listener feel the horror of that moment.

=31=

After the previous scenes, Allah pronounces the verdict on those who denied the Hereafter. “Losers indeed are those who deny the meeting with Allah” uses the emphatic particle “indeed” to underscore the certainty of their loss. “The meeting with Allah” refers to the resurrection and judgment. Their denial was not a casual error but a deliberate rejection that defined their lives. “Until the Hour takes them by surprise” describes how the Day of Judgment will come upon them suddenly, without warning, while they are heedless. They will be caught unprepared, their entire worldly investment turning to nothing. Their cry, “Woe to us for having ignored this!” expresses intense regret and sorrow for their negligence.

The word “ignored” (farratnā) can also mean “wasted” or “neglected,” implying they let slip the opportunity to prepare. Then, a graphic image: “They will bear the burden of their sins on their backs.” This burden is not physical but the heavy weight of guilt, disgrace, and punishment.

In the Hereafter, sins will be an actual load that crushes the bearer. The concluding exclamation, “Evil indeed is their burden!” reinforces the horror of their state. This verse ties together the themes of heedlessness, sudden accountability, regret, and the unbearable weight of sins. It warns believers to live in constant awareness of the meeting with Allah, to never let worldly life distract them to the point of forgetting their ultimate return.

=32=

In the wake of the severe warnings, the Quran provides a balanced perspective by defining the true nature of this world. “This worldly life is no more than play and amusement.

” The word “play” (laʿib) refers to aimless activity, something done for distraction with no lasting consequence. “Amusement” (lahw) is entertainment that diverts from serious matters. This does not mean that life is devoid of purpose or that lawful pleasures are forbidden; rather, it compares worldly pursuits in their entirety, when disconnected from Allah and the Hereafter, to a game that ends when the players depart. They are temporary, trivial, and ultimately unsatisfying. Then comes the contrast: “but far better is the eternal Home of the Hereafter for those mindful of Allah.” The Hereafter is described as the “Home” (dār), a place of permanent settlement, in contrast to the transient tent of this world. It is reserved for “those mindful of Allah” (alladhīna yattaqūn), those who have taqwa—God-consciousness that leads to obedience and avoidance of sin.

The phrase “far better” emphasizes the immense difference in quality and duration. The verse ends with a rhetorical question: “Will you not then understand?” It appeals to human intellect, urging people to grasp this obvious reality and prioritize accordingly. This verse does not call for monasticism but for a reorientation of values, seeing the world as a field to sow seeds for the Hereafter, not as the ultimate goal. It provides profound comfort to the believers who may be suffering poverty or persecution, reminding them that their true reward is far greater.

=33=

After reiterating the fate of the deniers, Allah turns to console His Messenger directly. “We certainly know that what they say grieves you.

” The use of “We” and “certainly know” conveys immense empathy and intimacy. Allah is fully aware of the emotional pain the Prophet endured from their accusations—calling him a sorcerer, a madman, a liar. This acknowledgment is itself a balm for his heart. Then comes a crucial clarification: “It is not your honesty they question.” The Quraysh knew Muhammad as al-Amin (the Trustworthy) for forty years before his prophethood. Their rejection was not based on any doubt about his character. “It is Allah’s signs that the wrongdoers deny.” The root of their opposition was not a personal vendetta against the Prophet but a rebellion against the message itself, which threatened their power, their idols, and their way of life. They projected their denial onto the messenger, but the real target was the revelation. This insight relieved the Prophet of the burden of feeling that his personal inadequacy caused their rejection. It teaches all callers to Islam that rejection often stems from the content of the truth, not the personality of the caller. The verse is a profound source of comfort for anyone facing mockery or rejection for their faith: your worth is not diminished; the struggle is against the truth itself, and Allah knows your pain.

=34=

The consolation continues with a reminder of prophetic history. “Indeed, messengers before you were rejected” normalizes the Prophet’s experience. Rejection is part of the path of all messengers; Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus all faced intense denial. The key is their response: “but patiently endured rejection and persecution. ” The word “patiently endured” (fa-ṣabarū) highlights the virtue of sabr, which is not passive suffering but steadfast perseverance without complaint. They were not just rejected verbally but were “persecuted” (ūdhū), meaning they underwent physical harm, exile, and threats. They did not give up, and they did not take shortcuts. “Until Our help came to them” indicates that divine victory arrives after the period of testing, not before. The timing is according to Allah’s wisdom. Then the verse gives a firm assurance: “And Allah’s promise to help is never broken.” The promise of ultimate success for the messengers and their sincere followers is absolute and unchangeable. The Prophet is reminded, “And you have already received some of the narratives of these messengers. ” The Quran is full of their stories, each a proof of this pattern. This verse serves to strengthen the Prophet’s resolve, telling him that patience is the bridge to victory, and that the help of Allah is certain. For the believers of all times, it is a timeless principle: enduring hardship for the sake of truth with patience will eventually be met with divine aid, just as it did for the prophets.

=35=

This verse addresses the intense emotional strain the Prophet felt. “If you find their denial unbearable” recognizes his deep desire for his people to believe, almost to the point of despair. In a rhetorical challenge, Allah says: “then build—if you can—a tunnel through the earth or stairs to the sky to bring them a more compelling sign.

” This is not a command but a way of highlighting the futility of such efforts. Even if the Prophet were to perform extraordinary feats like burrowing into the earth or ascending into heaven to retrieve a sign, it would not compel their faith. The verse echoes earlier statements that no sign would make them believe. Then the fundamental truth is stated: “Had Allah so willed, He could have guided them all.” Guidance ultimately rests in Allah’s hands. He could have created all human beings as believers by compulsion, but He chose to give free will and make guidance dependent on sincere seeking and moral choice. The Prophet is then warned, “So do not be one of those ignorant of this fact.” This ignorance refers to not understanding the divine wisdom behind allowing disbelief and the limits of human effort in guiding others. The verse is a crucial lesson for all da‘wah workers: one cannot force guidance; the duty is to deliver the message, and the outcome is with Allah. It protects the believer from excessive grief over the rejection of others, recognizing that true guidance is a gift from Allah.

=36=

Following the lesson that guidance is not forced, the Quran identifies who actually benefits from the message. “Only the attentive will respond to your call.

” The word “attentive” (yastajību) implies a readiness to listen and obey. These are the people whose hearts are alive, open to truth, and not clouded by arrogance. They are contrasted with “the dead,” meaning the disbelievers who are spiritually dead. Their hearts are so hardened that they cannot perceive the truth; they are like corpses that cannot hear. The Prophet’s call is effective only on the living-hearted.

The verse then asserts, “As for the dead, Allah will raise them up, then to Him they will all be returned. ” This refers to the physical resurrection on the Day of Judgment. In this world, they are unresponsive, but they will certainly be brought back to life and face accountability.

The phrase “Allah will raise them up” shifts the focus from the Prophet’s inability to revive their hearts to Allah’s ultimate power over them. They might ignore the call now, but they cannot escape the resurrection. This verse both comforts the Prophet—explaining that the lack of response is due to the audience’s spiritual death, not his failure—and warns the disbelievers that their current heedlessness will end with a forced awakening. It also reinforces the Quranic parallelism between spiritual death in this world and physical resurrection in the next.

=37=

The disbelievers’ persistent demand for physical miracles is reiterated. “They ask, ‘Why has no other sign been sent down to him from his Lord?’” They wanted tangible signs like the she-camel of Salih or the staff of Moses, ignoring the Quran itself as the greatest sign. Their request was a pretext to reject, not a sincere search for truth. Allah instructs the Prophet to respond: “Allah certainly has the power to send down a sign. ” This affirms that the lack of the requested miracles is not due to any inability. Allah can produce any wonder at will. The crucial caveat, “though most of them do not know,” implies that they are ignorant of the consequences. As explained earlier in the surah, if such compelling signs were sent and they still rejected, immediate destruction would follow, as was the case with previous nations. Their not knowing refers to this divine pattern. The response thus shifts the focus from the demand to the wisdom behind its denial. It teaches that the sending of miraculous signs is tied to a much larger divine plan and that demanding them out of stubbornness is dangerous.

The verse also subtly points to the Quran itself as the enduring intellectual miracle, sufficient for those who reflect. The believers learn to trust that Allah’s withholding of certain visible miracles is often an act of mercy, allowing the test of faith to continue and giving time for sincere repentance.

=38=

This verse expands the horizon by linking the animal kingdom to human communities, offering both a sign of Allah’s knowledge and a preview of the resurrection. “All living beings roaming the earth” includes every creature that walks, crawls, or moves on the planet. “Winged birds soaring in the sky” specifically highlights birds, whose flight is a continuous miracle of aerodynamics.

The phrase “are communities like yourselves” states that they are species or groups with their own forms of social organization, languages, and provisions, all governed by Allah’s decree. This is a profound ecological insight: animals are not random but form ordered societies, just as humans do. The purpose of this statement is to demonstrate the comprehensive nature of divine care and knowledge. Then the verse states, “We have left nothing out of the Record.” The “Record” is the Preserved Tablet (al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ), in which everything is written—every creature’s life, provision, and destiny. This implies that the same God who records every animal also records every human deed.

Finally, “Then to their Lord they will be gathered all together” extends the gathering of humans on Judgment Day to animals as well. A hadith mentions that even the rights among animals will be settled, after which they will be turned to dust. This emphasizes the absolute justice of Allah and the comprehensive scope of the resurrection. The verse serves as a powerful reminder that no creature is forgotten, and thus no human deed is overlooked, reinforcing the certainty of accountability.

=39=

After presenting the signs in nature and the record, the verse describes the condition of those who reject them. “Those who deny Our signs are willfully deaf and dumb—lost in darkness.” The deafness means they do not hear the truth receptively; their ears are functional but they refuse to listen. The dumbness means they do not speak the truth; their tongues fail to proclaim faith or testify to what is right.

The phrase “lost in darkness” portrays their disorientation; without the light of revelation, they wander in multiple layers of darkness—the darkness of ignorance, desire, and confusion. The adverb “willfully” (ṣummun bukmun) implies that this state is a result of their own choice, not an innate defect. Then the verse addresses the theological question of guidance: “Allah leaves whoever He wills to stray and guides whoever He wills to the Straight Way.” This does not indicate arbitrariness. Allah’s will is informed by His wisdom; He leaves to stray those who persistently choose misguidance and are unworthy of guidance, and He guides those who sincerely seek the truth. The verse combines human responsibility with divine decree. It follows the earlier pattern of stating that the disbelievers’ faculties are sealed because of their own denial. This verse warns against the hardening effect of sin and encourages believers to be grateful for the gift of guidance, recognizing it as a mercy from Allah rather than a personal achievement. It also closes off any objection that Allah is unjust, as His will is always aligned with perfect justice and wisdom.

=40=

The surah now returns to a powerful rational argument that exposes the innate monotheism within the polytheists. The Prophet is instructed to confront them with a hypothetical scenario. “Imagine if you were overwhelmed by Allah’s torment or the Hour” confronts them with two terrifying possibilities: a sudden punishment in this world, like a natural disaster, or the arrival of the Last Hour with its cataclysms. In such moments of extreme distress, when all worldly supports vanish, who do people instinctively call upon?

The question is rhetorical: “would you call upon any other than Allah for help?” The Meccans, despite their idol worship, knew deep in their hearts that only the Supreme Creator could rescue them from overwhelming calamity. They would forget their idols and cry out directly to Allah.

This is a profound psychological truth observed across humanity; even atheists in extreme danger may involuntarily call out to a higher power. The challenge “Answer me if your claims are true!” dares them to deny this reality. If they were truthful in their polytheism, they would call upon their idols, but they never did in genuine crises. The verse dismantles shirk by appealing to the fitrah (primordial nature) that recognizes Allah alone as the ultimate rescuer.

The question hangs in the air, leading directly to the next verse where the answer is provided, reinforcing the futility of their false gods.
