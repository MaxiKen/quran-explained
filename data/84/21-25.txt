=21=

Verse twenty-one narrows the astonishing disbelief by describing a specific, observable behaviour: “and when the Quran is recited to them, they do not bow down in submission?” The Arabic wa idhā quri’a ‘alayhimu al-qur’ānu lā yasjudūn uses the present tense, indicating a persistent, ongoing refusal. The Qur’ān, with its majestic style, its powerful arguments, its vivid descriptions of the apocalypse and the rewards of the virtuous, is recited directly to them. The natural, expected response of a sound heart would be to fall into prostration, overwhelmed by awe, humility, and recognition of the divine source.

The word yasjudūn means to prostrate, to bow down with the forehead touching the ground, the ultimate physical expression of submission. In the context of the Meccan pagans, their refusal to prostrate was a symbol of their arrogance. They would even mock the believers for putting their faces on the ground. This verse is a profound indictment of their pride. The fact that the Qur’ān itself commands prostration in certain verses, and this surah itself is considered one of the places of sajdah by many jurists, makes their refusal all the more poignant. When the Prophet (ﷺ) recited the Qur’ān, the believers would weep and bow down, moved by its truth. The disbelievers would turn away, some even plugging their ears and making noise to drown it out, as mentioned elsewhere.

The question form “they do not bow down?” is a continuation of the astonishment expressed in the previous verse: what is the matter with them? The evidence is recited to them in the most beautiful and compelling Arabic, promising eternal bliss and warning of a painful punishment, confirming their own observations of the twilight and the moon, and yet they refuse to lower their necks in humility. This verse exposes the core disease: not a lack of evidence, but a refusal to submit the ego. The prostration of the body is a mirror of the prostration of the heart. Their hearts were too proud to bend, so their bodies remained stiff.

For the believer, this verse reinforces the importance of responding to the Qur’ān with immediate, heartfelt submission, whether through a literal prostration of recitation or a constant spiritual prostration in daily life. The verse challenges every listener: when the Word of Allah reaches your ears, does your heart bow down?

=22=

After the rhetorical questions, the sūrah answers its own query with a definitive diagnosis in verse twenty-two: “In fact, the disbelievers persist in denial.” The Arabic bali alladhīna kafarū yukadhdhibūn uses “In fact” (bal) to correct any assumption that their refusal to prostrate might be a simple mistake or a momentary hesitation. It is a deliberate, active, and persistent denial. The verb yukadhdhibūn is in the present tense, indicating an ongoing, repeated action. They do not merely “not believe”; they actively brand the truth as a lie.

The word is intensive, showing that they put forth effort in their denial, constructing arguments and mockery to reject the Qur’ān. The surah thus moves from the external actions (not prostrating) to the internal state (persistent denial). The root of their behaviour is kufr, a covering up of the truth they recognise deep inside.

This is the same denial described in the previous surahs, a denial that stains the heart and leads to the cry for destruction. The use of the definite article “the disbelievers” (alladhīna kafarū) points to a specific group characterized by this trait, primarily the Meccan leadership.

Yet the verse is a timeless description of all who reject the truth after it has been made clear. The persistence in denial is a conscious choice, not a passive state. Every time the Qur’ān is recited and they refuse, they reinforce their own hardness. This verse serves as a warning to believers to never become comfortable with rejecting any part of the truth. Even a minor, persistent denial of a clear teaching can lead one down this path.

The verse also provides a sort of closure to the Prophet’s heart; he is not to wonder in astonishment forever at their state. The matter is clear: they are a people who persist in calling the truth a lie, and their fate has been sealed by their own choices.

The following verses will reveal that their secrets are known and that a painful punishment awaits, but there is always the contrasting hope for the believers.

=23=

The inner dimension of the disbelievers’ denial is addressed in verse twenty-three: “But Allah knows best whatever they hide.” The Arabic wa Allāhu a‘lamu bimā yū‘ūn uses the superlative a‘lam, meaning Allah is All-Knowing, Most Knowledgeable.

The phrase “whatever they hide” translates bimā yū‘ūn, from the root wa‘ā, meaning to gather, to contain, to conceal. It refers to what they gather and hide within their hearts—their secret plots, their hidden grudges, their unspoken doubts, their concealed mockery, and the true reasons for their rejection. While their outward behaviour is denial and refusal to prostrate, their inner reality is a mixture of arrogance, fear of losing status, and often a secret acknowledgment of the truth that they suppress. The verse assures both the Prophet and the believers that nothing of this is hidden from Allah. The disbelievers might craft careful arguments in their private gatherings, they might hide their economic motives for rejecting the message, they might conceal the envy they feel towards the Prophet’s growing influence. All of that is laid bare before the Almighty.

The verse also carries a comforting message for the believers who were oppressed and forced to hide their own faith in the early days of Mecca. Allah knows what the oppressors hide in their hearts, and He will bring it to light on the Day of Judgment.

Moreover, the believers themselves may have hidden struggles, secret acts of worship, and silent patience; Allah knows that too. The phrase “knows best” implies not only knowledge but a perfect, complete understanding that will result in the most just recompense. The verse connects directly to the earlier statement that Allah has always been All-Seeing of them. His knowledge encompasses their hidden and their open.

Therefore, the delay in punishment is not out of ignorance; it is out of a divine wisdom that gives them time to turn back, and that records every hidden detail for the perfect moment of judgment. This verse should inspire a profound sense of accountability in the believer: if Allah knows what the disbelievers hide, He certainly knows what we hide. It is a call to purify the inner world.

=24=

After the exposure of their hidden state, the sūrah issues a startling command to the Prophet in verse twenty-four: “So give them good news of a painful punishment.” The Arabic fa-bashshirhum bi-‘adhābin alīm uses a sharp rhetorical device, the employment of the word bashshir, which means “give glad tidings” or “good news,” for the announcement of a terrible punishment.

This is a form of ironic inversion, common in the Qur’ān when addressing those who mock the concept of reward and punishment. The disbelievers used to sarcastically ask when the Day of Judgment would come, treating it as a joke. The response is: the only “good news” you will receive from the Messenger is the news of the painful punishment that awaits you.

This is not cruelty but a direct, unvarnished warning that aims to shake them from their heedlessness. The command places the Prophet in the role of a clear warner. Despite their mockery and denial, he is instructed to deliver this message plainly, leaving no ambiguity about their destination if they persist. The “painful punishment” (adhābin alīm) is the Hellfire described earlier, the blazing Fire from which there is no escape. The adjective “painful” emphasises the sensory, experiential reality of the suffering; it is not an abstract concept but an intense, felt agony.

The verse also serves to contrast with the “good news” that the believers receive—the glad tidings of gardens and forgiveness. The same tongue that delivers glad tidings to the righteous must also deliver this stark warning to the wicked.

For the believer, this verse is a reminder of the seriousness of the prophetic mission. The truth cannot be watered down to avoid offending sensibilities. The “good news” of punishment is a mercy in itself, a final alarm bell before the door of repentance closes. The verse leaves the disbelievers with a clear choice: either continue in denial and face this “good news” becoming a reality, or turn in repentance and exchange it for the true glad tidings of Paradise. The surah will conclude with the contrasting eternal reward of the believers, highlighting the completeness of the divine message.

=25=

The sūrah closes with a note of eternal hope and justice in verse twenty-five: “But those who believe and do good will have a never-ending reward.” The Arabic illā alladhīna āmanū wa ‘amilū al-ṣāliḥāti lahum ajrun ghayru mamnūn provides the beautiful exception, the other side of the coin.

The word illā (except/but) sets the believers apart from the disbelievers just described. Their two defining qualities are “believe” and “do good. ” Faith (īmān) and righteous action (‘amal ṣāliḥ) are inseparably paired throughout the Qur’ān. Belief alone is not sufficient; it must manifest in deeds. Deeds without faith are hollow and unrewarded. The combination is the key to the never-ending reward. The reward is described as ajrun ghayru mamnūn.

The word ajr means reward, wage, recompense. Ghayru mamnūn has two primary interpretations, both profound.

The first is that it is a reward that will never be cut off, never cease, never be interrupted. It is continuous and everlasting, in contrast to the fleeting pleasures of this world that are inevitably terminated by death.

The second meaning is that it is a reward given without reproach or reminder of favour, without the giver making the recipient feel indebted. Allah gives generously and does not mar His gift by mentioning it as a favour, or by causing any sense of burden. Both meanings are perfectly true and combine to paint a picture of a perfect reward: eternal in duration and pure in nature, free from any sting or diminishment. This reward is the bliss of Paradise, the easy reckoning, the joyful return to one’s people, the radiant faces, the sealed wine of Tasnīm. The sūrah thus ends by returning to the theme with which it began: the final outcome of the human journey. The labour towards the Lord will either end in a painful punishment or a never-ending reward. The twilight, the night, the moon—all pass through their stages and fade, but the reward of the righteous will never fade. This concluding verse is a profound source of comfort, motivation, and hope for every believer. It urges us to evaluate our lives: are we among those who believe and do good? If so, we have the glad tidings of a reward that will never be cut off.
