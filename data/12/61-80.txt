=61=

Faced with Joseph’s firm ultimatum that they would receive no more grain without Benjamin, the brothers immediately gave their word. They said, “We will try to convince his father to let him come.

” The Arabic verb used for “try to convince” carries the sense of employing persistent effort, coaxing, and persuasion. They knew their father Jacob’s heart was still broken from the loss of Joseph, and that prying Benjamin away from him would be an immensely difficult task.

Yet they pledged themselves to this mission. They added, “We will certainly do our best,” using a strong emphasis to assure Joseph of their determination.

This promise is significant when contrasted with their earlier deceitful assurances to Jacob about watching over Joseph. At that time they had said, “we will really watch over him,” but their hearts were filled with treachery. Now, years later, they were speaking to a powerful official without knowing he was their wronged brother. Their words, though still carrying the weight of their past sin, may reflect a degree of genuine resolve shaped by the hardships of the famine and the pressing need to save their families. The verse subtly invites us to consider whether their character had undergone any change. Were they the same envious brothers, or had time and suffering softened them? Joseph would test this very question through the subsequent events. Their promise also highlights the principle that when one is in need, one must make sincere efforts and commitments, not merely rely on empty words. From a spiritual perspective, every promise made to a fellow human being is a trust, and a believer must strive to fulfil it, knowing that Allah is witness to every word spoken.

=62=

After securing the brothers’ promise to bring Benjamin, Joseph took a hidden step of profound generosity and psychological wisdom. He ordered his servants to secretly return the payment the brothers had brought for the grain, placing their money back into their saddlebags. The purpose is stated clearly: “so that they would find it when they returned to their family and perhaps they would come back.” Joseph had two motivations. First, he was secretly providing for his family, ensuring they would not suffer financial strain despite the famine. This was a silent act of charity to his own father and kin, done without any desire for recognition. Second, he was creating a compelling reason for them to return. Upon discovering their money returned, they would feel a moral obligation to come back and settle the matter, or they would be drawn by the honesty of this generous ruler. Joseph understood the psychology of his brothers and the values of their family, who were not thieves. The returned money would act as a powerful magnet pulling them back to Egypt with Benjamin.

This verse teaches the beauty of secret charity, where the left hand does not know what the right hand has given. It also illustrates strategic wisdom in reconciling relationships: sometimes small, unspoken acts of kindness can open hearts and create pathways for greater good. Joseph’s quiet arrangement was a masterpiece of foresight, all under the divine inspiration that guided his every move.

=63=

The brothers arrived home and immediately confronted the difficult conversation with Jacob. They began with the respectful address “O our father,” then presented the stark reality: “We have been denied further supplies.” They explained that the Egyptian minister had tied future grain allocations to the presence of Benjamin.

The phrase “send our brother with us” was a direct request that they knew would stab at Jacob’s wounded heart. They tried to soften it by stating the practical need: “so that we may receive our measure.” They promised, “we will definitely watch over him,” using words almost identical to the ones they had used years ago when they took Joseph. This repetition is painful in its irony. Jacob, the grieving father, heard these same assurances before, and his sons were now asking him to trust them again with his most beloved remaining son. The brothers may not have been plotting evil this time, but the shadow of their past betrayal hung heavily over every word. Their plea was a mixture of genuine desperation caused by the famine and a test of their father’s capacity to trust them again. This verse highlights the long-lasting consequences of broken trust; words that were once used to deceive lose their power and become triggers of sorrow. It also shows that when we seek to rebuild trust, we must be patient with those we have hurt, understanding that their hesitation is not unreasonable but earned by our past actions.

=64=

Jacob’s reply came from the depths of a father’s enduring pain. He did not lash out in anger, but his words cut to the heart of the matter: “Should I trust you with him as I once trusted you with his brother Joseph?” This rhetorical question is charged with grief. He reminded them of their past failure, not to humiliate them, but to express the profound difficulty of the position they were putting him in. Once bitten, twice shy; a father who lost his son through the very brothers now asking for his other son has every right to hesitate. Jacob’s memory of their betrayal was not a grudge but a shield.

Yet immediately after this, he turned his heart toward the true source of protection: “But only Allah is the best Protector, and He is the Most Merciful of the merciful.” This is the statement of a prophet. Jacob knew that no human promise, no matter how sincere, can guarantee safety; the ultimate protection belongs to Allah alone. He called Allah Khayr Hafizan, the Best Protector, acknowledging that even if the brothers were honest, only divine guardianship could truly preserve Benjamin. And by mentioning “the Most Merciful of the merciful,” Jacob expressed his hope that Allah’s mercy would protect his son where human guarantees fell short. His words model the believer’s balance between caution in worldly matters and complete reliance on Allah’s care. He did not blindly trust the brothers, but he entrusted Benjamin’s ultimate fate to the Most Merciful.

=65=

As the brothers unpacked their saddlebags, they stumbled upon Joseph’s secret gift: their payment for the grain had been surreptitiously returned. This discovery was a moment of astonished joy and became a powerful new argument to persuade Jacob. They exclaimed, “O our father! What more can we ask for? ” They presented the returned money as proof of the Egyptian minister’s incredible honesty and generosity. They reasoned that if a foreign ruler treated them so fairly, returning their wealth without any obligation, then sending Benjamin with them was not only safe but advantageous. They said, “Here is our money, fully returned to us,” emphasizing the completeness of the favour. They then laid out the material benefits: “Now we can buy more food for our family. We will watch over our brother, and obtain an extra camel-load of grain.” They added that this extra load “can be easily secured,” meaning it would not be a burden. The brothers argued from a purely practical standpoint, but behind their words was the gentle hand of Joseph, who had orchestrated this exact scenario to make their case compelling.

This verse shows how divine planning can use material blessings to open doors that emotional arguments cannot. The returned money served as a tangible sign of the minister’s goodwill, softening Jacob’s resistance. It is a reminder that when Allah wills a matter to move forward, He prepares the hearts through unexpected means, and sometimes a simple act of honesty can speak louder than a thousand promises.

=66=

Jacob, seeing the financial benefit but still deeply cautious, laid down his final, non-negotiable condition. He said, “I will not send him with you until you give me a solemn oath by Allah.” He required a binding covenant, not just a casual promise. The oath had to be by Allah, the highest possible witness, making any breach a matter of direct accountability to God. The specific wording of the oath was that they would “certainly bring him back to me, unless you are totally overpowered.” This exception is crucial and reveals Jacob’s realism. He knew that an oath is not broken if the person is prevented by an overwhelming force beyond their control. If they were all surrounded and overpowered by a superior enemy who took Benjamin, they would not be guilty of betrayal. This clause shows that Islamic oaths are not absurd absolutes; they account for genuine inability. After the brothers gave their solemn oaths, Jacob said, “Allah is a Witness to what we have said.” He elevated the matter from a human agreement to a divine covenant. With Allah as the Witness, the brothers could no longer treat this promise lightly.

This verse teaches the seriousness of oaths in Islam. An oath sworn by Allah is a sacred bond; violating it is a grave sin. Jacob’s insistence on this oath was his way of placing the brothers under the strongest possible moral and spiritual obligation, entrusting the ultimate outcome to the One who never breaks His trust.

=67=

Even after taking the solemn oath, Jacob took a further precaution, this time based on human wisdom and experience. He gave his sons a specific instruction: “O my sons! Do not enter the city all through one gate, but through separate gates.” He knew they were a striking group of eleven handsome young men, and entering a foreign city all at once might draw the envious eye of the populace, invite suspicion, or even put them at risk of the evil eye. Spreading them out would make them less conspicuous. This was sound, practical advice from a loving father who understood the ways of the world.

Yet immediately after giving this counsel, Jacob made a profound spiritual clarification: “I cannot help you against what is destined by Allah in the least. It is only Allah Who decides. ” He was teaching his sons that while taking means is perfectly permissible, and indeed wise, one must never believe that those means, in themselves, can avert the divine decree. If Allah has willed a matter, no strategy of separation or concealment can prevent it. Jacob then declared his ultimate reliance: “In Him I put my trust. And in Him let the faithful put their trust. ” This is the essence of tawakkul: using one’s intelligence and taking precautions, but anchoring the heart solely in Allah, knowing that He alone is the true protector. Jacob’s instruction perfectly balances the use of material means with unwavering faith, a lesson for every believer navigating the uncertainties of life.

=68=

The brothers followed Jacob’s advice and entered Egypt through separate gates. The verse then makes a crucial theological point: “this did not help them against the Will of Allah whatsoever.” The separation could not avert any decree that Allah had already ordained. If Joseph had planned to detain Benjamin, or if some other divine test was coming, the change of entry point would not alter the outcome.

The Quran clarifies that Jacob’s instruction was not a prophetic command from Allah but “just a desire in Jacob’s heart which he satisfied.” It was a fatherly instinct, a loving concern that he acted upon, and it was not wrong to do so.

In fact, the verse immediately praises Jacob: “He was truly blessed with great knowledge because of what We had taught him.” Jacob possessed immense knowledge of both spiritual and worldly matters, and his advice was sound as a human precaution. The statement “but most people have no knowledge” is a lament that the majority of humanity does not understand the proper relationship between means and divine decree. Many either neglect all means out of a misguided notion of reliance, or they rely entirely on means and forget the Decree. Jacob avoided both extremes.

This verse teaches that it is permissible to satisfy the natural inclinations of the heart for caution and care, as long as one does not attribute ultimate power to those measures. True knowledge is to act wisely while keeping the heart fully dependent on Allah.

=69=

The brothers, including Benjamin, finally stood before Joseph. Having secured Benjamin’s presence, Joseph set his carefully crafted plan into motion. The verse says, “he called his brother Benjamin aside, and confided to him.” This was a private, intimate moment. Joseph could now reveal his true identity to his full brother, the only other son of Rachel, who had been a child when Joseph disappeared. He whispered, “I am indeed your brother Joseph!” Imagine the shock and overwhelming relief that must have flooded Benjamin’s heart. For years he had lived in the shadow of a lost brother, surrounded by half-brothers who had been responsible for that loss. Now, in this foreign land, the powerful minister was embracing him as his long-lost sibling. Joseph immediately comforted him: “So do not feel distressed about what they have been doing.” He was referring to the coming plan, the false accusation of theft that Joseph was about to stage. He told Benjamin not to let the unfolding events trouble his heart, because everything was under control and part of a divine strategy to bring the entire family together. This private assurance was a profound mercy. Joseph did not want Benjamin to suffer the terror of being falsely accused without knowing the truth. The verse highlights the deep bond between the two brothers and the care with which Joseph managed the emotional weight of his plan. It also shows that even in difficult strategies aimed at a greater good, one should strive to minimize the pain of the innocent.

=70=

The plan moved into its next phase. Joseph had treated his brothers generously, giving them the full measure of grain they requested. But as they prepared to depart, he gave a discreet order. The verse says, “he slipped the royal cup into his brother’s bag. ” The word “slipped” indicates a secret placement, without the knowledge of any of the brothers, including Benjamin, though Benjamin knew from the earlier private conversation that some plan was afoot. The item used was the king’s measuring cup, a valuable vessel used for dispensing grain, and one that could be plausibly claimed as stolen state property. Once the brothers’ caravan set off, a herald, acting on Joseph’s instructions, cried out loudly, “O people of the caravan! You must be thieves! ” This public accusation was designed to stop them, to create a scene that would allow a search of their belongings. The accusation was, in a literal sense, false from Joseph’s knowledge, but it served a higher purpose permitted by divine law for the sake of reuniting a family and testing the brothers’ character. This action must be understood within its prophetic context: Joseph was a prophet acting under divine inspiration, as the subsequent verse will confirm, and his strategy was not a sinful lie but a divinely guided ruse to achieve a just outcome. It teaches that in extreme circumstances, permitted stratagems can be used to secure a right or to expose a truth, provided they do not violate core moral principles or harm the innocent.

=71=

The brothers, halted by the sudden accusation of theft, turned around with a mixture of surprise and indignation. Their question, “What have you lost?” was a natural response. They were not yet accused individually; the herald had addressed the whole caravan. By asking what was missing, they demonstrated their willingness to cooperate and their confidence in their own innocence. They had no idea that the missing item was already in one of their bags. This question, simple as it is, reflects a psychological moment of tension. The brothers, who had once been guilty of a terrible crime against Joseph, were now facing a false accusation of theft. The irony is deep: the real thieves of Joseph’s childhood are now being called thieves for something they did not do. This role reversal was part of Joseph’s test. Would they react with the same duplicity as before, or would they handle the situation with integrity? Their immediate response, asking for clarification, was a normal and innocent reaction. It sets the stage for the upcoming search and the eventual discovery of the cup in Benjamin’s bag, which would force them to confront their own past standards of justice and family loyalty.

The verse reminds us that in life, we are sometimes tested through being placed in situations that mirror our own past faults, compelling us to see our actions from the other side.

=72=

The herald, with the guards at his side, specified the missing item and made a public offer. He said, “We have lost the King’s measuring cup.

” The use of the word “lost” rather than “stolen” was perhaps a diplomatic choice, but the accusation of theft had already been made. The cup was a royal instrument, and its disappearance was a serious matter. The herald then announced a reward: “Whoever brings it will be awarded a camel-load of grain. I guarantee it.” This reward served a clever purpose. It encouraged voluntary disclosure: if someone had taken it accidentally or knew its whereabouts, they could return it and receive a generous compensation. It also set a standard of justice and fairness, showing that the authorities were not merely punitive but were willing to reward honesty. For the brothers, this was a test of their own honesty. If they had known where the cup was, would they have come forward? The guarantee of the camel-load of grain was a significant incentive during a famine, but the brothers, confident in their innocence, had nothing to offer. This offer also reflects the wisdom of Joseph: he gave every opportunity for the truth to surface without immediate punishment, embodying the principle that before blame is assigned, an avenue for redemption should be opened. It teaches those in authority to combine firmness with generosity.

=73=

The brothers, feeling the weight of the accusation and the implication that they could be spies or saboteurs, swore a solemn oath. They said, “By Allah! You know well that we did not come to cause trouble in the land, nor are we thieves.” They invoked Allah’s name, which for them, as the sons of Jacob, carried the deepest significance. They declared that their purpose in Egypt was entirely peaceful: they were there to buy food, not to foment corruption or steal.

The phrase “you know well” suggests they thought their previous honest dealings with the minister had established their good character. They had been given full measure and had even had their money returned; they reasoned that the Egyptians could see they were not criminals. This declaration of innocence was absolutely true. They were not thieves, and they had not come to cause mischief.

Yet the irony is that they had once committed a far greater crime—kidnapping their own brother—and had never been held accountable. Now they stood swearing by Allah about their innocence in a theft they did not commit, completely oblivious that the very brother they wronged was orchestrating this entire scenario. This moment is rich with moral complexity. It shows that a person can be truthful in one instance and yet carry unrepented sins. It also taught the brothers a direct lesson: to be accused of something one did not do is painful, exactly as Joseph had been falsely accused.

=74=

The Egyptian guards, acting under the instructions of Joseph, moved the confrontation to the next stage. Rather than accepting the brothers’ oaths at face value, they posed a legal question: “What should be the price for theft, if you are lying?

” The question was strategic. It invited the brothers to pronounce their own law regarding theft, to set the punishment themselves. This was a brilliant move by Joseph. He wanted to detain Benjamin, but he could not do so under standard Egyptian law, as that would be unjust and would not serve his purpose. By asking the brothers to state their own customary penalty for theft, Joseph was creating a trap based on their own tradition. The brothers, not knowing the true situation, fell into the trap readily, believing their innocence would be proven and wanting to demonstrate their strictness. This question also serves as a test of the brothers’ consistency: they had come from a family of prophets with a legal tradition; would they now apply that tradition fairly?

The verse teaches the importance of establishing laws and punishments clearly, and also that when people are asked to judge themselves, their own standards become the measure by which they are held accountable.

=75=

The brothers, confident and perhaps eager to prove their own integrity, declared the penalty without hesitation: “The price will be the enslavement of the one in whose bag the cup is found.” In the legal tradition of the family of Jacob, as derived from earlier prophetic law, the punishment for theft could be that the thief becomes the slave of the person from whom he stole, serving as restitution for the crime. They stated this penalty clearly, and then added, “That is how we punish the wrongdoers.” By stating this, they unknowingly sealed the fate of their own brother Benjamin. They had just prescribed a punishment that would legally bind Joseph to keep Benjamin as his slave. Little did they know that the cup was already in Benjamin’s bag. This self-imposed judgment is a perfect example of divine justice. The brothers, who had once sold Joseph into slavery for a few silver coins, were now setting the very law that would seemingly enslave their other brother. The poetic justice is breathtaking. It teaches that the measure you mete out for others can very well become the measure used against you. The brothers were not only setting a legal trap for themselves but also experiencing the very system of enslavement they had once inflicted, bringing their past sin full circle in a way that would force a deep moral reckoning.

=76=

Joseph now personally directed the search. He did not go directly to Benjamin’s bag, as that would raise suspicion; instead, “Joseph began searching their bags before that of his brother Benjamin, then brought it out of Benjamin’s bag.” The deliberate order made the discovery seem natural and the result of a thorough search. When the cup was found, the brothers were stunned.

The Quran then pulls back the curtain to reveal the divine orchestration behind the scene: “This is how We inspired Joseph to plan.” The entire ruse was a revelation from Allah, a clever but permissible stratagem to achieve a righteous end.

The verse clarifies that Joseph “could not have taken his brother under the King’s law.” Egyptian law would not prescribe enslavement for theft, nor would it allow the detention of a brother for a debt or a planted item. Joseph needed a legal basis that the brothers themselves would accept, and their own tribal law provided exactly that. Allah’s will overrode all other legal systems.

The final sentences draw a profound lesson: “We elevate in rank whoever We will. But above those ranking in knowledge is the One All-Knowing. ” Joseph was given a higher rank in knowledge and status by Allah, but even his knowledge is nothing compared to the infinite knowledge of Allah. This reminds every knowledgeable person to remain humble, for the ultimate source of all wisdom is Allah, the All-Knowing.

=77=

With the cup discovered, the brothers faced a dreadful situation. In a desperate attempt to distance themselves from the shame and perhaps to explain why a son of Jacob might stoop to theft, they made a cruel insinuation. They said, “If he has stolen, so did his full brother before.” The “full brother” they referred to was Joseph himself. This was a twofold slander: they falsely accused Benjamin of theft, and they revived the old lie about Joseph, insinuating that Joseph had also been a thief. This reveals that the brothers’ hearts still carried the stain of their old resentment. In their moment of crisis, they resorted to the same family slander, showing they had not fully repented for their past crimes. Joseph, hearing this, felt a surge of outrage. But the verse says, “Joseph suppressed his outrage—revealing nothing to them.

” This is a monumental demonstration of self-control. With a single word, he could have revealed his identity and exposed their lies, but he held back. Instead, he spoke to himself: “You are in such an evil position, and Allah knows best the truth of what you claim. ” He recognized that their spiritual state was wretched, and he entrusted the matter to Allah’s perfect knowledge. This teaches that the highest form of strength is to restrain anger when one has full power to unleash it, and to leave judgment to Allah when human emotions demand immediate revenge.

=78=

Having failed to exonerate themselves through slander, the brothers changed their approach and turned to a plea for mercy based on emotional and moral grounds. They addressed Joseph with his title of respect, “O Chief Minister!” acknowledging his authority. They then appealed to his compassion: “He has a very old father.” They spoke of Jacob’s advanced age, his frailty, and his immense love for Benjamin, the only remaining son of his beloved wife after Joseph’s loss. They knew that taking Benjamin would shatter their father completely.

Therefore, they offered a substitution: “so take one of us instead.” This was a remarkable suggestion. They were willing to sacrifice their own freedom to spare their father further grief. One of them would become a slave in Benjamin’s place. This offer stands in stark contrast to their earlier selfishness, when they had willingly thrown Joseph away for their own advantage. It indicates a significant moral development. They then said, “We surely see you as one of the good-doers,” appealing to Joseph’s reputation for righteousness and generosity, hoping he would extend his famous kindness to their family. This plea was a test of Joseph’s own mercy, and it also revealed that the brothers, despite their past sins, had a sense of duty towards their father. It shows that even in deeply flawed individuals, love and loyalty can exist, and that transformation is possible through life’s trials.

=79=

Joseph’s reply was firm and principled, even as his heart must have been moved by their plea. He said, “Allah forbid that we should take other than the one with whom we found our property.” He framed his refusal as a matter of divine law and justice. To him, it was unthinkable, something he sought refuge in Allah from, to punish an innocent person for the crime of another. The brothers had set the law themselves: the thief becomes enslaved. To take someone else in Benjamin’s place would be a perversion of justice, a clear act of oppression. He stated, “Otherwise, we would surely be unjust.” Joseph was teaching them a profound lesson about the consequences of their own legal rulings. They could not now demand an exception because the ruling affected them personally.

This is the very definition of injustice: to accept a rule when it benefits you but to seek to overturn it when it turns against you. Joseph’s commitment to justice, even when it meant causing his brothers distress and delaying the reunion, shows his integrity. He could not bend the law, even for a merciful purpose, because bending the law is itself a form of wrongdoing. This verse is a timeless reminder that true righteousness demands consistency in applying laws and principles, without favouritism, even when it is painful.

=80=

The brothers’ plea had been rejected. Despair settled in. They withdrew to confer in private, a familiar scene echoing their first secret council when they plotted against Joseph. Now, however, the conversation was of a different nature. The eldest brother, likely Reuben or Judah, stepped forward with words of painful remembrance and accountability. He said, “Do you not know that your father had taken a solemn oath by Allah from you?” He reminded them of the sacred covenant they had made with Jacob before taking Benjamin. They had sworn by Allah to bring him back. The oath was binding, and breaking it would bring a terrible spiritual cost. He then brought up their past failure: “nor how you failed him regarding Joseph before?

” This is an explicit acknowledgment of their collective sin. The eldest brother was connecting the present crisis to the unresolved guilt of the past. Their current dilemma was a direct consequence of their old crime. Then he made a bold decision: “So I am not leaving this land until my father allows me to, or Allah decides for me.” He chose to stay in Egypt, facing whatever fate awaited him, rather than return to Jacob with the news of another lost son. His trust was in Allah, whom he called “the Best of Judges. ” This moment marks a turning point in the brothers’ moral journey. The eldest had taken responsibility, refusing to repeat the sin of covering up a loss. This was a spark of genuine honour and repentance, born from decades of secret guilt and the intense pressure of the current situation.
