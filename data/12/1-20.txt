=1=

The surah opens with the disjointed letters Alif, Lãm, Ra, an opening that immediately captures attention and signals that what follows is of tremendous significance. These letters are among the mysteries of the Quran whose full meaning Allah has kept to Himself, yet they serve a profound purpose: they are composed of the very letters that make up the Arabic speech of the Quraysh, and when placed at the beginning of a surah they challenge the listener to produce anything like the revelation that follows using those same letters. It is as though the Creator is saying that this miraculous composition is formed from the same building blocks of your own language, yet you remain utterly incapable of bringing forth its equal. After this cryptic yet powerful beginning, Allah declares that these are the verses of the clear Book.

The word "clear" translates the Arabic mubin, which means something that makes the truth manifest, leaving no room for confusion or doubt. The Quran is described as a book of clarity because its stories, its laws, and its guidance are presented with such precision and wisdom that any sincere seeker will find in it a light that illuminates the path.

In the context of Sûrah Yûsuf, this description prepares the heart for a narrative that is not merely an entertaining tale but a divinely revealed account filled with lessons that are crystal-clear for those who reflect. By calling it a "clear Book" right at the outset, Allah assures the listener that every event in the story that follows is purposefully selected to guide, to comfort, and to instruct. The combination of the mysterious letters and the immediate assertion of clarity also teaches us that while some aspects of divine wisdom may remain hidden, the core message is always distinctly accessible, and the believer’s task is to engage with what has been made plain.

=2=

After establishing the clarity of the Book, Allah declares a fundamental reason for its revelation: He sent it down as an Arabic Quran so that its first audience might understand. The phrase "sent it down" underscores the divine origin of the message; it is not Muhammad’s own composition but a revelation from the Lord of the worlds. The description "Arabic Quran" carries a dual significance. First, it honours the Arabic language as the vessel chosen by Allah for His final revelation, a language rich in expression and depth. Second, it emphasizes that the message was delivered in the mother tongue of the Prophet and his people so that no one could claim that guidance came in a foreign tongue they could not grasp. The purpose "so that you may understand" addresses the Quraysh directly, affirming that Allah’s intent is for them to reflect upon the Quran’s meanings, not merely to recite its words without comprehension.

This verse reminds us that understanding is the gateway to faith and righteous action; a scripture that one does not understand cannot fully transform the heart. For the Prophet himself, at a time of great personal loss after the deaths of Khadijah and Abu Talib, this assurance that the Quran was made easy to understand carried immense comfort. It meant that the message he was entrusted with was perfectly suited to reach hearts and to bring solace. For all later generations, the verse encourages the learning of Arabic and the study of tafsir so that we may truly understand what our Lord has revealed, ensuring that the Quran remains a living guide rather than a closed book.

=3=

Here Allah directly addresses the Prophet, explaining the nature of what is to follow. He says, "We relate to you the best of stories," using the word ahsan al-qasas, which means the most beautiful, the most truthful, and the most instructive of narratives. The story of Joseph is considered the best of stories because it encompasses such a wide range of human experience: the pain of family jealousy, the trial of slavery, the temptation of lust, the loneliness of imprisonment, the patience of a prophet, the wisdom of a ruler, and ultimately the sweetness of forgiveness and reunion. It is a story where every situation models how a believer should respond. This relaying of the story is done "through Our revelation of this Quran," confirming that the Prophet did not learn these historical details from any human source.

The phrase "though before this you were totally unaware of them" highlights the Prophet’s previous state. He lived among the Arabs who did not possess these detailed accounts of the Hebrew prophets; he himself was unlettered and had no access to earlier scriptures. The fact that he now recites these events in precise and moving detail is a clear proof of his prophethood.

This verse also came as a profound consolation during the Meccan period. Just as Joseph’s story would culminate in relief and triumph after years of suffering, so too would the Prophet’s path. The "best of stories" was not given merely for entertainment but to strengthen the Messenger’s heart and to teach the believers that divine wisdom runs through every chapter of a true believer’s life.

=4=

The narrative begins with Joseph’s childhood vision, a dream that would define the arc of his entire life. The verse starts with the implied command to "remember," drawing the listener into the scene. Young Joseph, with the innocent trust a child has in a loving parent, confides in his father Jacob, whom he addresses with deep affection: "O my dear father." The dream itself is striking: eleven stars, the sun, and the moon all prostrating to him. The act of prostration here signifies profound respect and submission, not worship, and this distinction is crucial. In the symbolic language of dreams, the eleven stars were interpreted by scholars to represent his eleven brothers, while the sun and moon represented his father and mother. The dream was a prophecy of Joseph’s future status, when he would be raised to a position of authority and his entire family would come to him in need and honour him. For such a grand vision to come to a young boy, it signalled that he was chosen for a special destiny. The dream also establishes a pattern: Joseph’s life would be marked by visions and their true interpretation, a gift from Allah that would later save nations. This opening scene immediately situates the listener in the intimate family dynamics that will drive the plot, showing how divine election can stir human envy and how patience in the face of a great promised future becomes the central test of Joseph’s character.

=5=

Jacob, a prophet blessed with deep insight and knowledge of the human heart, immediately recognizes the danger embedded in his son’s glorious dream. He reciprocates Joseph’s tenderness by calling him "O my dear son," showing a household built on love. His warning is precise: "Do not relate your vision to your brothers." Jacob knew his other sons, and he sensed the jealousy that could be sparked if they learned that a dream had foretold Joseph’s superiority over them. His words "or they will devise a plot against you" were not empty fears but accurate foresight. The lesson here is profound: not every truth must be spoken in every context, and a blessing may need protection from envious eyes. Jacob then traces the root of such potential evil by reminding Joseph that "Surely Satan is a sworn enemy to humankind." This identifies the ultimate source of the kind of destructive envy that leads brothers to plot against their own flesh and blood. Satan works insidiously, exploiting any opening in the human heart, and family rivalry is one of his oldest traps. By linking the brothers’ potential actions to Satanic incitement, Jacob teaches his son to see the deeper spiritual battle behind personal animosities. This verse sets up the central conflict not as a mere sibling squabble but as a test where Satan will attempt to ruin what Allah has blessed, and where Joseph’s wisdom and patience will be the shield against that scheme.

=6=

After warning Joseph to guard his secret, Jacob now interprets the dream’s positive promise and expands upon it, showing that he fully understands the divine purpose behind the vision. He says, "And so will your Lord choose you," meaning that the dream is a sign that Allah has elected Joseph for prophethood and a special mission. This election would be accompanied by a specific gift: "and teach you the interpretation of dreams." The ability to interpret dreams is not merely a skill but a form of divinely granted knowledge that Joseph would later use to interpret the king’s vision, thereby saving Egypt and the surrounding lands from famine. Jacob then speaks of a broader blessing: Allah will "perfect His favour upon you and the descendants of Jacob." He ties this favour to the chain of prophethood running through their lineage, saying it would be just as He perfected it upon the forefathers Abraham and Isaac.

This connection places Joseph’s personal story within the grand narrative of the Abrahamic covenant; the same Lord who guided his great-grandfather and grandfather is now extending His grace to him. The verse ends with "Surely your Lord is All-Knowing, All-Wise. " This reassures Joseph that his election is not random but based on Allah’s perfect knowledge and wisdom.

For believers, it teaches that when Allah grants a gift or a trial, He does so with complete understanding of the individual and the situation, weaving a wisdom that may only become clear with time.

=7=

This verse acts as a divine pause, an interjection that tells us directly why this story is being recounted. It states emphatically that in the narrative of Joseph and his brothers there are numerous ayat, or signs and lessons, for those who ask.

The word translated as "lessons" is the same word used for the verses of the Quran itself, indicating that these historical events are as much signs of Allah’s wisdom and power as the verses of scripture. The phrase "for all who ask" is particularly inviting. It suggests that the benefits of this story are not automatic; they are unlocked by an inquisitive heart that approaches the Quran seeking guidance, understanding, and answers to life’s challenges. A person might ask about how to deal with envy, how to maintain chastity in the face of temptation, how to forgive those who have wronged you, or how to remain patient during long periods of hardship. The story of Joseph answers all these questions and more.

This verse also serves a thematic function: after establishing Joseph’s dream and his father’s recognition of his chosen status, it signals that the following account of the brothers’ conspiracy will not be a meaningless tragedy but a purposeful sequence of events filled with divine wisdom. It teaches the reader to adopt the posture of a seeker, to ask questions while engaging with the narrative, so that the full treasure of its guidance may be uncovered.

=8=

The story zooms into a private conversation among Joseph’s older half-brothers, revealing the toxic envy festering in their hearts. The verse begins with "Remember when they said," bringing us directly into that secret council. Their words betray a painful sense of grievance: "Surely Joseph and his brother Benjamin are more beloved to our father than we." The brothers perceived an unequal affection from Jacob, and they linked this not to any virtue in Joseph but to a blind favouritism that seemed to ignore their own numbers and strength.

The phrase "even though we are a group of so many" shows their logic: in tribal Arab culture, as in many cultures, sons were a source of pride and power, and having many strong sons should have, in their view, secured their father’s greatest love. Their complaint escalates to a damning judgement: "Indeed, our father is clearly mistaken." They accused a prophet of God of being in clear error, a grievous slander born entirely from their own insecure interpretation of events. What they failed to see was that Jacob’s love was not a contest of numbers but a recognition of the spiritual and moral qualities he discerned in Joseph and Benjamin. This verse powerfully diagnoses how envy distorts perception, making a person see favouritism where there might be none, and leading to the unjust criticism of those who are innocent.

=9=

The conversation among the brothers descends rapidly from complaint to a sinister plot. The jealousy that had been simmering now boils over into a proposal of murder or exile. "Kill Joseph or cast him out to some distant land" reveals a shocking hardness of heart; they are willing to extinguish their brother’s life or permanently sever him from his home and father solely to remove the object of what they perceived as unfair preference. Their stated goal is brutally honest: "so that our father’s attention will be only ours." This shows that their primary motive was not justice but a desperate desire to monopolize their father’s love. The ugliness of their intent is then wrapped in a thin veneer of future piety: "then after that you may repent and become righteous people." They planned to commit a heinous sin and then simply turn back to God, as if repentance were a casual eraser that could be pre-planned. This exposes a deep misunderstanding of sincere repentance, which requires remorse and a firm resolve not to return to the sin, not a calculated decision to sin now and seek forgiveness later. Their words illustrate how a person can deceive themselves into thinking that a future act of worship can justify a present crime, a dangerous rationalization that the Quran lays bare for us to recognize and avoid.

=10=

Amid the heated plotting, a voice of relative restraint emerges. One of the brothers, whom many commentators identify as the eldest or perhaps Judah, speaks up. He does not oppose the plan to remove Joseph, but he objects to the extreme of murder. "Do not kill Joseph" is a plea for a lesser evil, and he immediately offers an alternative: "But if you must do something, throw him into the bottom of a well." This suggestion was still monstrous; abandoning a child in the darkness of a desert well could easily lead to death.

Yet it left a sliver of a chance for survival, as indicated by his reasoning: "so perhaps he may be picked up by some travellers." The well lay on a caravan route, and it was possible that a passing caravan might draw water, discover the boy, and take him away. This brother’s intervention, however morally weak, served as the means by which Allah’s plan to save Joseph and eventually elevate him was set in motion. From the perspective of divine wisdom, this suggestion was the thread that led to Joseph’s journey to Egypt, his rise to power, and the ultimate salvation of the entire family from famine. The verse demonstrates how Allah can use even the flawed reasoning of humans, even a half-hearted effort to reduce sin, to bring about His grand design. It also subtly distinguishes degrees of wrongdoing, reminding us that even in the midst of a terrible consensus, a small pull towards mercy can become the instrument of deliverance.

=11=

Having settled on their scheme, the brothers now approach their father Jacob with carefully crafted words of deception. They begin by addressing him with respect, "O our father," but immediately follow it with a question designed to manipulate his emotions and weaken his natural protectiveness: "Why do you not trust us with Joseph?" This question is a clever psychological ploy. It attempts to make Jacob feel that his caution is an accusation against their honour, pressuring him to prove that he does trust them. They add, "although we truly wish him well," claiming pure intentions while their hearts harboured the darkest of plots. The gap between their outward show of concern and their inward reality illustrates the dangerous capacity of humans to cloak betrayal in the language of love and care. Jacob, a prophet with deep insight, surely sensed something, for a father’s intuition is sharp, yet the brothers’ smooth words put him in a difficult position.

This verse teaches us that evil intent rarely announces itself openly; it often comes wrapped in reassurances of goodwill and appeals to trust. Believers are reminded to be judicious in their trust and to look beyond charming words to the reality of a person’s character, while also understanding that even a prophet like Jacob had to navigate the painful deceit of his own children.

=12=

The brothers continue to paint a picture of innocent recreation to lower Jacob’s defences. They ask specifically that Joseph be sent out "with us tomorrow," a request for a single day’s outing that seems reasonable and hard to refuse. They describe the purpose: "so that he may enjoy himself and play," appealing to Jacob’s desire for his beloved son’s happiness. By suggesting that Joseph will be allowed to run freely, to eat and to have fun in the open countryside, they present themselves as caring older siblings who only want to see their younger brother smile. To seal their case, they make a strong verbal commitment: "And we will really watch over him.

" The emphasis in their promise, using a word that conveys vigilant guardianship, is meant to eliminate any fear Jacob might have about Joseph’s safety. This was a deliberate overpromise designed to conceal their true plan. Their words remind us of how easily words of protection can be used as a cover for harm, and how those who intend wrong often amplify their assurances of safety. From a spiritual perspective, the verse holds a mirror up to the listener: it calls us to ensure that our own promises of care and protection, especially towards the vulnerable, are never empty words but sincere commitments made with fear of Allah.

=13=

Jacob’s reply is tender and honest, revealing the deep emotional bond he shares with Joseph and his prophetic intuition. He says, "It would truly sadden me if you took him away with you," showing that even a brief separation from his young son would cause him profound grief.

This is the vulnerable heart of a father who has already formed a soul-deep attachment. He then voices a specific fear: "and I fear that a wolf may devour him while you are negligent of him. " Given that they are eleven strong men, a wolf attack might seem an improbable threat, but Jacob knows the wilderness and perhaps also knows the nature of his sons—that their attention could wander, leaving the young boy exposed. Unbeknownst to them, Jacob’s mention of a wolf would later become the very lie they would use to cover up their crime. This remarkable detail demonstrates a form of subtle divine irony: the false story the brothers would construct was already present in Jacob’s genuine fears.

The verse also illustrates that a prophet’s caution is not a sign of weak faith; Jacob trusted Allah completely, yet he did not ignore the material causes of danger. He balanced reliance on God with a rational assessment of risk, teaching us that tawakkul does not mean abandoning prudence.

=14=

The brothers seize upon Jacob’s mention of a wolf and twist it into a final, emphatic argument. They declare, "If a wolf were to devour him, despite our strong group, then we would certainly be losers!" Their statement is layered with irony that would soon become tragically evident. They appeal to their own physical strength and numbers, arguing that with so many able-bodied men present, no wild animal could possibly harm Joseph.

The phrase "we would certainly be losers" was meant to convey a sense of total incapacity and humiliation; they were saying that such a failure would prove them utterly worthless and incapable. In their minds, this argument would logically demolish Jacob’s fear. But what they failed to realize was that the real threat to Joseph was not a wolf of the wilderness but the wolf of envy that lurked within their own hearts.

The verse teaches us a profound lesson about the nature of danger: sometimes the greatest peril comes not from external enemies but from those who are closest to us and who swear they will protect us. The brothers’ confident boast only deepened their guilt, for they knew that if Joseph disappeared, it would be no wolf but their own treachery that had caused the loss, making them losers in the deepest sense—losers of their father’s trust, their own integrity, and, without repentance, their standing with Allah.

=15=

This verse brings us to the execution of the brothers’ plan and the simultaneous intervention of divine mercy. It says, "And so, when they took him away and decided to throw him into the bottom of the well," confirming that the plot was carried out.

The phrase "decided" indicates their collective, firm resolution to do this evil, and the "bottom of the well" paints a dark, frightening image of isolation for a child. Yet in that very moment of human cruelty, Allah’s care descended.

The verse continues, "We inspired him," using a word that can mean a form of direct divine communication suited to a prophet, even in his youth. The inspiration was a promise: "One day you will remind them of this deed of theirs while they are unaware of who you are." This was a lifeline of hope in the darkness. It assured the young Joseph that he would not perish in that well; he would survive, rise to a position of great authority, and one day confront his brothers when they would not recognize him. This divine promise turned a moment of terror into a seed of future triumph. It teaches us that when all earthly means of comfort vanish, Allah can send His solace directly to the heart. The promise that he would remind them of their deed also speaks to the certainty that every wrong will eventually be brought to light, either in this world or the next, and that the oppressed should hold on to the hope of divine justice.

=16=

After carrying out their heartless deed, the brothers now stage the final part of their deception. They came back to Jacob "in the evening," a time detail that adds to the emotional weight of the scene. The fading light of dusk matched the darkness of their lie. They approached their father "weeping," a display of false grief intended to convince him that a tragedy had befallen them while they were helpless. Their tears were crocodile tears, yet to a father who had already voiced his fear of losing Joseph, this sight would have been devastating. The verse captures the duplicity of sin: the very act of crying, which is supposed to be the most sincere expression of human sorrow, was here twisted into a tool of deception. The brothers’ performance was so convincing that it would have broken the heart of any parent. This moment reveals the depths to which envy had dragged them; they were not only killers in intent but now skilled liars who could weaponize emotion.

For the reader, this serves as a warning that outward displays of emotion are not always what they seem, and that one must pray for sincerity in all expressions of grief and concern. It also sets the stage for Jacob’s profound response, which would demonstrate that a prophet’s insight is not easily fooled by dramatic performances.

=17=

Continuing their act, the brothers present their fabricated tale. Their cry, "Our father!" is laden with a forced urgency. They concoct a story of innocent activity turned tragic: "We went racing and left Joseph with our belongings." They admit to leaving him behind, which they think makes the story more believable, as it explains how he could be alone. They then deliver the pre-planned lie: "and a wolf devoured him." This was the very creature Jacob had mentioned, and they used his own fear to lend credibility to their story.

Yet they could not resist adding a manipulative layer: "But you will not believe us, no matter how truthful we are." This is a masterstroke of emotional manipulation. By accusing Jacob of inherent distrust, they aimed to preempt his suspicion and paint themselves as victims of his unfairness. If he doubted them, he would seem to confirm their charge that he never believed them. This tactic of playing the victim while being the perpetrator is a common tool of the guilty. The verse lays bare the anatomy of a lie and how it often comes wrapped in self-pity and blame-shifting. It also shows that Jacob’s earlier caution about them had a real basis, and that his sons had sunk so low as to make their own father the target of guilt-tripping after they had already broken his heart.

=18=

As tangible "proof," the brothers presented Joseph’s shirt, but they made a telling mistake. The verse says the shirt was stained with "false blood," meaning they had slaughtered an animal and smeared its blood on the garment, yet in their haste they forgot to tear the shirt. A wolf would have rent the fabric with its teeth. Jacob, seeing the untouched shirt, immediately recognized the lie. His response is a masterpiece of prophetic composure. He said, "No! Your souls must have tempted you to do something evil." He did not curse or scream; he named the spiritual reality: their own lower selves had beautified a sin for them. Then he uttered words that define the believer’s response to unbearable calamity: "So I can only endure with beautiful patience." Sabrun jamil, beautiful patience, is the kind of patience that does not complain to creation, that maintains trust in Allah’s wisdom while the heart bleeds, and that refuses to sin in the midst of grief. He added, "It is Allah’s help that I seek to bear your claims," acknowledging that to withstand the pain of their falsehood, he needed divine assistance, not his own strength. This teaches us that the highest form of patience is not a stoic silence but a turning to Allah for help, recognizing that He alone can give the heart the capacity to bear what seems unbearable.

=19=

The narrative now shifts from the grieving father to the well where Joseph sits in darkness. A caravan of travellers approaches, and they dispatch their water-drawer to fetch water from the well. When he lets down his bucket, instead of water, he discovers a child clinging to it. His astonished cry, "Oh, what a great find! Here is a boy!" reveals that they immediately saw Joseph not as a lost child to be helped but as valuable merchandise to be exploited. The verse says they took him "secretly as merchandise," meaning they hid him among their goods, intending to sell him in a distant market as a slave. The act of hiding him shows they knew taking a free child and enslaving him was a crime, yet greed silenced their conscience.

However, the verse ends with a powerful reassurance: "but Allah is All-Knowing of what they did. " No secrecy could hide their deed from the One who sees all. While they thought they were making a covert profit, Allah was fully aware, and His plan was at work.

This statement comforts the believer that no act of injustice, however hidden from human eyes, escapes divine notice. For Joseph, being found by the caravan was the beginning of a journey that would lead him to Egypt, and despite the cruelty of being sold, it was the mechanism through which Allah’s promise of a great future would be fulfilled.

=20=

The final verse in this opening segment relates the anticlimactic transaction by which Joseph was sold into slavery. The caravan reached Egypt, and the travellers sold him "for a cheap price, just a few silver coins.

" The Arabic indicates a paltry sum, a mere handful of dirhams, counted out with indifference. The phrase "only wanting to get rid of him" exposes their true motive. They did not recognize the immense value of the child they had stumbled upon; they saw only a liability, a piece of goods to be offloaded quickly for any meagre profit. They had no idea that this boy was a prophet in the making, a future saviour of nations from famine, and a man of unparalleled beauty and character. This underestimation of Joseph by the world is a recurring theme: his brothers undervalued him, the travellers undervalued him, and later the Egyptian nobleman who bought him would get him for a bargain. But Allah’s valuation is what matters.

This verse teaches that a believer’s worth is not determined by the cheap price the world might place on them. People may overlook, undervalue, or discard you, but if Allah has chosen you, He will raise you in ways no one expects. For the Prophet Muhammad, who was also rejected and undervalued by his own people, this was a profound comfort: the one sold for a few silver coins would become the chief minister of Egypt, just as the orphan of Mecca would become the leader of Arabia.
