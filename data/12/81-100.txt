=81=

After the eldest brother’s firm decision to remain in Egypt, he gave the rest of the brothers clear instructions on what to tell their father when they returned. They were to say, “Your son committed theft.

” This statement was factually accurate from what they had witnessed: the king’s measuring cup was found in Benjamin’s bag, and under their own tribal law, that constituted theft. They were not lying to Jacob; they were reporting an event that had indeed occurred, even though they remained unaware of the hidden truth behind it. They added, “We testify only to what we know.

” This is a significant phrase. In the past, they had brought Jacob a shirt stained with false blood and constructed an elaborate lie about a wolf. Now, they were careful to limit their testimony to their direct observation, not adding false details or speculations. This restraint signals a moral growth, a shift from fabricators of deceit to cautious, truth-bound reporters. They then said, “We could not guard against the unforeseen.” This was an honest admission that despite their solemn oath to protect Benjamin, a situation beyond their control had arisen. The unforeseen decree of Allah had unfolded, and they could not prevent the discovery of the cup or its legal consequences. This phrase acknowledges the limits of human power against divine will. The entire message they were to deliver was a blend of painful truth and humble recognition of their inability to avert the decree, contrasting sharply with the arrogance and deception of their earlier years. It teaches that even when delivering unwelcome news, a believer must speak only what is true and acknowledge that ultimate control belongs to Allah.

=82=

To further establish their credibility and alleviate Jacob’s inevitable suspicion, the brothers were to invite their father to verify their account independently. They said, “Ask the people of the land where we were and the caravan we travelled with.” They referred to the inhabitants of the Egyptian city where the incident occurred, and the fellow travellers in the caravan that had journeyed with them. This was a remarkable move. Guilty men hide their crimes and avoid scrutiny; these men were asking for a public investigation. They wanted their father to send his own messengers or to inquire from others who might have witnessed the events, confident that the facts would confirm their story.

The phrase “We are certainly telling the truth” is a straightforward claim of veracity, not the manipulative emotional pleading they had used years ago. This open offer of verification demonstrates that their hearts, though still bearing the guilt of past sins, were now committed to honesty in this matter. It also reflects the Quranic principle that a believer should be transparent and willing to have his or her statements corroborated. Truth does not fear light. The brothers had learned a bitter lesson: a single lie can destroy trust for a lifetime, and rebuilding that trust requires an abundance of truth and an openness to scrutiny. By inviting Jacob to verify their story, they were doing everything in their power to prove that this time, their words matched reality, even if that reality was painful.

=83=

When the brothers returned and delivered the news, Jacob’s reaction was eerily similar to his response decades earlier when Joseph was lost. He cried out, “No! Your souls must have tempted you to do something evil.” He immediately discerned that, despite their truthful account of the theft accusation, there was more to the story—a deeper layer of human scheming involved. He did not believe that Benjamin was inherently a thief, and he sensed that their souls had once again beautified some action that led to a loss.

Yet this time, his grief was seasoned with a profound, expansive hope. He said, “So I am left with nothing but beautiful patience!” He was back at the same crossroads, and again he chose sabrun jamil, the patience that contains no complaint to creation and no despair of divine mercy. But then he added words that marked a new level of prophetic certainty: “I trust Allah will return them all to me.

” The word “all” is breathtaking. He included not only Benjamin and the eldest brother who stayed behind, but also Joseph, the son lost so many years ago. His hope now embraced the restoration of everything that had been taken from him. This was not blind optimism; it was knowledge rooted in divine inspiration. He anchored this hope in the names of his Lord: “Surely He alone is the All-Knowing, All-Wise.” The All-Knowing knows exactly where each son is and the state of Jacob’s heart; the All-Wise arranges every event toward a perfect purpose.

This verse teaches that beautiful patience, over time, can mature into a radiant hope that sees beyond the present darkness and trusts in the complete restoration that Allah can bring.

=84=

Despite his towering patience and his expressed hope, Jacob was still a human father with a heart that ached beyond measure. The verse reveals his private sorrow: “He turned away from them, lamenting, ‘Alas, poor Joseph!’” He turned away to conceal the full intensity of his grief from his sons, perhaps to avoid burdening them or showing a weakness that might be misconstrued. His lament was specifically for Joseph, because Joseph’s disappearance was the original, unhealed wound. Benjamin’s detention had scraped that wound raw again.

The mention of Joseph’s name after so many years shows that the memory had never faded; it was an ever-present companion. The verse then says, “And his eyes turned white out of the grief he suppressed.

” The Arabic indicates that his eyes became clouded and white, meaning he lost his sight or developed severe cataracts, from years of silent weeping and the enormous effort of holding his sorrow within his heart. He did not scream or tear his clothes; he suppressed the outward expression, but the inner pressure of grief manifested physically. This teaches us that even prophets are fully human in their emotional suffering, and that suppressed grief can exact a heavy toll. Jacob’s blindness was not a punishment but a mark of his deep love and the intensity of his trial. It serves as a reminder that when Allah tests His servants, He allows them to feel the full weight of the pain, even as He rewards their patience with ranks of honour that cannot be achieved without such profound sacrifice.

=85=

Observing their father’s deteriorating condition—his blindness, his constant sorrow, his undying memory of Joseph—the sons spoke to him with a mixture of concern and exasperation. They swore, “By Allah! You will not cease to remember Joseph until you lose your health or even your life.” Their words were a desperate attempt to shake him out of his grief, which they viewed as an unhealthy obsession that was literally killing him. They feared that his relentless sorrow would consume his vitality completely, leading to severe illness or death. They could not comprehend how a person could mourn so long and so deeply. What they failed to see was the prophetic dimension of Jacob’s remembrance. He was not merely a bereaved father clinging to the past; he was a prophet who possessed a secret knowledge that Joseph was alive and that a divine promise of reunion existed. His remembrance was not hopeless despair but a constant state of hopeful waiting, intertwined with the natural tears of separation. Their statement reflects the worldly perspective that intense grief must be silenced or outgrown. Jacob’s response, in the following verse, would correct their misunderstanding.

This verse also shows that even well-meaning family members may misunderstand the nature of a believer’s spiritual and emotional journey, and that one’s private relationship with Allah can sustain a sorrow that others find too heavy to witness.

=86=

Jacob answered his sons with words that define the highest etiquette of the afflicted believer. He said, “I complain of my anguish and sorrow only to Allah.

” This is a monumental lesson. Jacob did not deny his pain; he openly acknowledged his bathth, his crushing anguish, and his huzn, his deep sorrow. He was not a stoic who pretended to feel nothing. But he directed his complaint exclusively to his Creator, not to his children, not to the community, and not in a way that implied objection to Allah’s decree. Complaining to Allah is in itself an act of worship; it is the act of a slave who knows that only his Master can truly comprehend his suffering and provide relief. The Prophet Muhammad later taught that the pain Jacob expressed is a form of communicating with the Divine, not a failure of patience. Jacob then added, “and I know from Allah what you do not know.” This was a direct reference to the divine insight he had been granted. He knew, perhaps through inspiration or the lingering effect of the original dream interpretation, that Joseph was alive and that his status was elevated. He knew that the long night of separation would end in a dawn of reunion. This knowledge was his secret strength, allowing him to hold his grief and hope together in perfect balance. His words teach every believer to pour out the heart’s pain before Allah alone, while cherishing the unseen knowledge that His wisdom is always at work, even in the longest and darkest trials.

=87=

After lamenting and reaffirming his secret knowledge, Jacob turned from sorrow to decisive action and instruction. He commanded his sons, “O my sons! Go and search diligently for Joseph and his brother.” This was a remarkable directive. He did not tell them to sit and wait passively for a miracle; he sent them back into the field, to actively seek out the lost ones. The instruction to search for Joseph, who had been missing for decades, shows that Jacob’s hope was not a vague sentiment but a conviction that propelled real effort. He believed that some trace of both brothers could be found, and he mobilized his family to pursue it. He then gave them a spiritual charge that stands as one of the most beautiful verses of hope in the entire Quran: “And do not lose hope in the mercy of Allah, for no one loses hope in Allah’s mercy except those with no faith. ” Despair, ya’s, is directly linked to a lack of faith. A true believer can never believe that the door of divine mercy is permanently closed, no matter how vast the sin, how long the trial, or how impossible the situation appears. Allah’s mercy is wider than every human calamity. Jacob was training his sons to replace the despair they had shown over Benjamin with active, hopeful searching. This verse is a lifeline for every soul that feels trapped in a cycle of failure, loss, or sin. As long as a person has faith, they have a reason to hope, and that hope must translate into continuous, diligent effort to seek the mercy and relief that Allah has promised.

=88=

Complying with their father’s command, the brothers undertook the difficult journey back to Egypt for the third time, now seeking both grain and news of their lost brothers. When they finally stood before Joseph, they were in a state of extreme destitution and humility. The famine had ravaged their resources, and the shame of the previous incident weighed heavily upon them. They addressed him with utmost respect: “O Chief Minister! ” Then they laid bare their desperate situation: “We and our family have been touched with hardship.” They openly acknowledged their poverty and suffering. They added, “and we have brought only a few worthless coins,” referring to their meagre, low-quality currency that would normally be rejected. They were essentially admitting that they had no means to pay for the grain they desperately needed. So they made a direct appeal to his generosity: “but please give us our supplies in full and be charitable to us.” They asked for both the full measure and charitable treatment, an act of kindness that would overlook the deficiency of their payment. They reinforced their request with a spiritual truth: “Indeed, Allah rewards the charitable.” This was not merely flattery; it was a statement of their belief, and it showed they had begun to remember the values of their father’s household. This moment of utter need and humility was the precise point at which Joseph chose to reveal himself. Their proud, envious selves had been stripped away by years of hardship, leaving behind hearts that were finally ready to receive both physical and spiritual deliverance.

=89=

With the brothers now completely humbled and their need laid bare, Joseph could no longer contain the truth. But he did not reveal himself with an immediate flash of anger or a dramatic outburst. He asked a question designed to pierce their hearts and awaken their long-dormant consciences: “Do you remember what you did to Joseph and his brother in your ignorance?

” The question was calm, deliberate, and devastating. He referred specifically to their act against Joseph and his brother Benjamin, linking the two as the victims of their old cruelty.

The phrase “in your ignorance” is profoundly merciful. Joseph did not label them as evil, irredeemable criminals; he attributed their heinous actions to jahiliyyah, a state of ignorance that existed before they fully understood the gravity of what they were doing.

This choice of words opened the door for repentance rather than slamming it shut with condemnation. It taught them that their sin, while grave, could be framed as a product of a past state of ignorance that they could now leave behind. Hearing this question from the lips of the powerful Egyptian minister must have shattered their reality. Only Joseph, their long-lost brother, could know this secret.

The question bypassed all the years of hiding and pretense, forcing them to confront the very act that had defined their lives of guilt. It was a masterclass in how to confront wrongdoers: with a truth that offers both accountability and the possibility of reform by acknowledging that their past self was ignorant, and they need not remain defined by that ignorance.

=90=

The brothers stood stunned, their minds racing to reconcile the majestic figure before them with the young boy they had thrown into the well. The question burst forth from their lips in a mixture of astonishment and dawning recognition: “Are you really Joseph?

” The Arabic construction conveys an intense, almost breathless inquiry. Joseph’s response was direct and gentle, sweeping away all doubt: “I am Joseph, and here is my brother Benjamin! ” He included Benjamin, solidifying the family connection and immediately clarifying that the recent detention of Benjamin was part of a divine plan, not a random cruelty. Immediately after revealing his identity, Joseph directed all attention and praise to Allah: “Allah has truly been gracious to us.” In this moment of supreme personal triumph, when he could have boasted or sought retribution, he chose to speak of Allah’s grace. He attributed his entire journey—from the well, to the prison, to the throne—to divine favour, not his own merit. He then stated a universal spiritual law drawn from his own experience: “Surely whoever is mindful of Allah and patient, then certainly Allah never discounts the reward of the good-doers.

” This is the summary of Joseph’s entire life philosophy. Taqwa (mindfulness of Allah) and sabr (patience) were his twin shields in every trial, and Allah had not allowed a single ounce of his righteousness to be lost. The reward came in this world through elevation and reunion, and it would be perfected in the Hereafter. His first words of self-revelation were therefore a sermon of gratitude, hope, and a timeless lesson for all who struggle on the path of faith.

=91=

Faced with the undeniable reality of Joseph’s presence and his magnanimous words, the brothers could only respond with total, unqualified confession. They swore, “By Allah! Allah has truly preferred you over us.” This was the exact opposite of their old complaint that their father preferred Joseph over them unjustly. They now acknowledged that the preference was not an arbitrary human bias but a divine election. Allah Himself had chosen Joseph and endowed him with prophethood, knowledge, beauty, and now worldly power. Their envy had been a rebellion against the decree of the All-Wise. They continued, “and we have surely been sinful. ” This is a simple, direct admission of guilt, with no excuses, no shifting of blame.

The word khati’in denotes those who commit sin deliberately. They owned their transgression fully. This confession is the essential first step of sincere repentance. They did not try to minimize the crime by mentioning that they were young or that Satan tempted them; they simply declared their sinfulness. This moment of humble acknowledgment, spoken in the presence of the brother they had wronged, was the culmination of decades of concealed guilt. It shows that true repentance requires a person to state their wrong clearly before the one they have harmed, and above all before Allah. The brothers’ confession prepared the ground for the extraordinary forgiveness that Joseph was about to bestow, a forgiveness that could only be fully appreciated against the darkness of their admitted sin.

=92=

Joseph’s response to his brothers’ confession is one of the most sublime moments of forgiveness in human history and in scripture. He said, “There is no blame on you today.” With these few words, he completely absolved them of any worldly accountability.

The word “today” signals a definitive break from the past; the matter is closed, and no punishment, no reproach, no prolonged shaming will come from him. Despite having full authority as the chief minister of Egypt to exact any legal penalty—including the enslavement that their own law prescribed—Joseph chose mercy. He did not demand restitution, nor did he impose conditions. He gave a free and complete pardon. Then he went even further: “May Allah forgive you! ” He not only relinquished his personal right to retribution, but he also prayed for their spiritual forgiveness from the Almighty.

This is the perfection of character. He recognized that their sin was ultimately against Allah first, and against him second, and so he sought divine pardon for them. He anchored this prayer in the divine name: “He is the Most Merciful of the merciful. ” Joseph knew that his own mercy was merely a drop, but the mercy of Allah is an ocean vast enough to drown the gravest sins. This verse finds its echo in the life of the Prophet Muhammad, who, upon conquering Mecca, told his bitterest enemies, “Go, for you are free.” Joseph’s words are the Quranic template of forgiving those who have done grievous harm, and they teach that to be a true servant of the Most Merciful, one must embody mercy when one has the full power to punish.

=93=

Joseph’s mercy extended beyond words to a tangible act of healing. He gave his brothers his shirt and instructed them, “Go with this shirt of mine and cast it over my father’s face, and he will regain his sight.” This was a prophetic miracle granted by Allah. The shirt that touched Joseph’s body, a garment of a prophet, was made a means for Jacob’s physical and spiritual healing. Some narrations mention that this was the very shirt that had been torn from behind in the earlier incident, or a shirt of Paradise given to Abraham and passed down, but the Quran does not specify; what matters is that Allah placed within it the power to cure. The act of casting the shirt would become the sign that the long-awaited good news was true. Joseph then gave a final instruction: “Then come back to me with your whole family.” His vision was not only personal reunion; he wanted to bring the entire family, the children of Israel, out of the famine-stricken land of Canaan and into the safety and prosperity of Egypt, where he could provide for them. This was the culmination of his plan: the family, once fractured by jealousy and deception, would be reunited under his protection and care. The shirt was both a cure for the father’s blindness and a passport for the family’s migration. It symbolizes that divine favour can flow through physical objects when Allah wills, and that the restoration of the family was a matter of both physical and spiritual healing, all orchestrated by the One who never forgets His patient servants.

=94=

The scene shifts back to Canaan as the brothers’ caravan, carrying Joseph’s shirt, set out from Egypt. At that very moment, a great distance away, Jacob, blind and aged, suddenly spoke to those around him, whether they were his remaining family or neighbours. He said, “You may think I am senile, but I certainly sense the smell of Joseph.” He prefaced his statement by anticipating their likely mockery. They would attribute his claim to the delusions of an old man who had spent too many years obsessed with a lost son. But Jacob declared with certainty that he could detect the scent of Joseph. This was a miracle of spiritual perception. Allah had sent the fragrance of Joseph’s shirt on the wind, or placed a direct inspiration in Jacob’s heart, as a foretaste of the coming joy. This supernatural knowing was a reward for his decades of beautiful patience.

The word he uses for “smell” connects to the Arabic root meaning to find or discover, linking the physical sense to the deeper discovery of the truth. This verse highlights the intimate connection between the physical and the unseen. The approach of relief was not hidden from the man of God, even before any human messenger arrived. It serves as a powerful reminder that when Allah’s help is near, He may send signs to the hearts of the believers that fill them with inexplicable certainty, even when the people around them dismiss it as foolishness or wishful thinking.

=95=

The people around Jacob, hearing his claim of sensing Joseph’s scent from a caravan still far away, reacted with blunt disbelief and even disrespect. They said, “By Allah! You are definitely still in your old delusion.

” The word they used for delusion, dalal, is harsh; it implies that Jacob is lost in an old error, stubbornly clinging to a false hope that has clouded his mind. They swore by Allah to emphasize their conviction that he was completely mistaken. This was the response of those who judge by material senses and conventional wisdom. They could not perceive the spiritual realm, so they labeled the prophet’s inspiration as a chronic mental error.

This is a common trial for people of faith: when they speak of hopes and promises grounded in divine revelation, the world often mocks them as deluded dreamers. Jacob had endured this mockery for years, ever since he first refused to accept the lie of Joseph’s death.

Yet he never wavered. This verse illustrates the loneliness of prophets and the deeply faithful who carry a truth that others cannot see. It also sets up a dramatic contrast: the very “delusion” they mocked was about to become their tangible reality. The people’s words highlight the chasm between the surface-level judgment of the worldly and the prophetic insight that penetrates beyond the visible. Their mockery, recorded in the Quran, stands as a testament that one should never deride what a person of faith holds as divine truth.

=96=

The caravan finally reached home, and the one carrying Joseph’s shirt, identified as the bearer of good news, approached Jacob immediately. The verse says, “he cast the shirt over Jacob’s face, so he regained his sight.” As soon as the fabric touched his eyes, the miraculous healing occurred. The blindness that had been brought on by decades of suppressed grief was lifted in an instant, a beautiful symbol of how divine mercy transforms prolonged sorrow into sudden, radiant relief. Sight was restored, both physically and metaphorically; Jacob could now see the world again and, more importantly, see the truth of Joseph’s survival fulfilled before him. He turned to his sons and those who had doubted him, and said, “Did I not tell you that I truly know from Allah what you do not know?” There is no harshness or vindictive tone in his words, but rather a gentle reminder of his earlier statement. He was pointing them back to his prophetic knowledge, which they had dismissed as delusion. The lesson was now undeniable: the knowledge granted by Allah is the only certain truth, and it will always be confirmed eventually, no matter how long it is mocked. This verse marks the complete vindication of Jacob’s patience and faith, teaching us that trusting in Allah’s promises is never foolishness, and that those who hold on will one day see their faith become sight.

=97=

Witnessing the miracle of Jacob’s sight returning and hearing his gentle vindication, the brothers were overcome by a new wave of remorse. The physical return of Joseph’s shirt and the imminent reunion brought their old sin into the sharpest possible focus. They turned to their father and begged, “O our father! Pray for the forgiveness of our sins. ” This request shows that they now recognized two crucial things: first, that the sin they committed was not only against Joseph but also against their father Jacob, and required his intercession; second, that a prophet’s prayer carries weight with Allah. They were not merely asking Jacob to say “I forgive you” as Joseph had; they needed him to turn to Allah on their behalf. They openly confessed again: “We have certainly been sinful.” There is no pride left, no attempt to justify their actions. The fire of guilt, burning for so many years, had finally melted their egos. This plea for their father’s intercession is a powerful lesson in the importance of seeking forgiveness through all channels, especially through those whom we have wronged and through the righteous whose prayers we hope Allah will accept. It also shows the beauty of a family where the father is a spiritual leader who can carry his children’s repentance to God. Jacob, despite everything, remained that source of mercy for them.

=98=

Jacob’s response to his sons’ plea was immediate and full of paternal love. He said, “I will pray to my Lord for your forgiveness.” He did not refuse them, nor did he delay. He embraced their repentance and promised to take their case to the highest court—the court of the Almighty. Some scholars mention that Jacob delayed his prayer until the pre-dawn hours of the night, specifically during the time of suhoor, when supplication is most potent and likely to be accepted. Whether he did so or not, his promise itself was an act of healing. He directed their hope away from himself, saying that he would pray to his Lord, emphasizing that forgiveness ultimately comes from Allah alone. He then described his Lord with two magnificent names: “He alone is indeed the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. ” Al-Ghafur, the All-Forgiving, covers and erases sins completely. Ar-Rahim, the Most Merciful, showers continuous and specific mercy. By invoking these names, Jacob was teaching his children that no sin is too great for Allah’s forgiveness if the sinner is sincere. The very attributes of the Lord guarantee that the door is open. This moment completes the spiritual arc of the brothers’ story. From plotting murder and faking death, they arrived at the door of repentance, seeking forgiveness through their father’s prayer and Allah’s mercy. It serves as a timeless reminder that the worst of sins can be forgiven, and that parents should always be a source of spiritual intercession and hope for their children, not of permanent condemnation.

=99=

The entire family now undertook the journey from Canaan to Egypt. The verse describes the long-awaited moment of complete reunion: “When they entered Joseph’s presence, he received his parents graciously.

” The Arabic phrase indicates that Joseph embraced them with the utmost honour, affection, and warmth, drawing them close to himself. The parents mentioned are generally understood to be Jacob and his wife, or the maternal aunt who raised him after his mother’s death, as many traditions say his mother Rachel had passed away earlier. Regardless, the figure represented the parental love that Joseph had been separated from for so long. Joseph then spoke words of welcome and reassurance: “Enter Egypt, Allah willing, in security.” By saying “Allah willing,” Joseph displayed profound humility even at the peak of his power. He knew that any security Egypt offered was ultimately by Allah’s permission, and he tied the promise of safety to the divine will.

The word “security” encompassed freedom from hunger, fear, and any political or social danger. This was the fulfilment of the divine promise that the family of Jacob would settle in a land blessed by Allah. The migration of the Children of Israel to Egypt, which would eventually lead to their multiplication and then their exodus under Moses, began with this moment of safe, graceful entry, orchestrated by a son who had been sold as a slave. The verse perfectly captures the intertwining of human warmth and divine decree that characterizes the entire story.

=100=

The climax of the story arrives. Joseph seated his parents in a place of honour alongside him on the royal throne. Then, in a gesture of profound respect and gratitude, his parents and his eleven brothers all fell down in prostration before him. This prostration was not an act of worship but a customary salutation of deep honour permitted in their law, a physical act of bowing to a person of high rank, which was later abrogated in Islam. This scene was the precise, literal fulfilment of the dream Joseph had seen as a child: the eleven stars, the sun, and the moon prostrating to him. Joseph immediately turned to his father and exclaimed, “O my dear father!

This is the interpretation of my old dream.” The decades of suffering, the well, the slavery, the prison—all of it was the hidden pathway to this magnificent moment. He then poured out his gratitude to Allah, recounting specific favours: “He was truly kind to me when He freed me from prison, and brought you all from the desert after Satan had ignited rivalry between me and my siblings.” He did not blame his brothers alone but attributed the root cause to Satan’s incitement, showing complete forgiveness. The statement “Indeed my Lord is subtle in fulfilling what He wills” is a magnificent description of Allah’s Latif nature—His gentleness, subtlety, and precision in bringing about His plans through the most unexpected channels, without anyone perceiving the grand design until it is complete. He ended with “Surely He alone is the All-Knowing, All-Wise,” placing the final seal on a story that teaches humanity that every hidden detail of their lives is under the watchful, wise care of a Lord who never errs.
