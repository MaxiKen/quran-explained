=21=

The one who purchased Joseph was a high-ranking Egyptian official, often identified as the chief minister or the head of the treasury. The moment he brought Joseph home, he gave his wife a specific instruction: “Take good care of him, perhaps he may be useful to us or we may adopt him as a son.” These words reveal that despite Joseph’s status as a young slave, the official immediately recognized something special in him. He did not treat him as a mere servant to be worked but as someone to be nurtured and honoured, even entertaining the idea of adoption.

The phrase “perhaps he may be useful to us” hints at the man’s worldly foresight, but he could not have imagined the full extent of that future usefulness—Joseph would one day save the entire land from famine. The verse then shifts from the human scene to the divine perspective, declaring, “This is how We established Joseph in the land.

” The word “established” translates makkanna, meaning to grant a firm and secure position of influence. The purpose behind this establishment is explicitly stated: “so that We might teach him the interpretation of dreams. ” This was the specific prophetic gift Joseph would need to fulfill his destiny.

The final sentence, “Allah’s Will always prevails, but most people do not know,” is a profound reminder that behind every human transaction, whether a jealous plot or a slave sale, Allah’s overarching plan is working flawlessly. People see only fragments of events; they do not grasp that the Almighty is orchestrating every detail toward a wise end. The brothers thought they were removing Joseph from favor; the caravan thought they were making a quick profit; the Egyptian buyer thought he was getting a useful servant. None knew that Allah was using each of them to position Joseph exactly where he needed to be.

=22=

The verse fast-forwards through the years of Joseph’s youth in Egypt to the pivotal moment when he reached his full physical and mental maturity. It says, “And when he reached maturity, We gave him wisdom and knowledge.” The maturity spoken of is not merely biological adulthood but a state of complete soundness in judgment and character. At this stage, Allah granted him two distinct gifts: hukm, translated as wisdom, which encompasses sound judgment, the ability to decide rightly, and even prophethood itself; and ilm, knowledge, which refers to the deep understanding of matters, especially the interpretation of dreams that would later distinguish him. These were not acquired through formal schooling or human mentorship but were direct divine endowments. The statement “This is how We reward the good-doers” establishes a vital spiritual principle. Joseph’s moral excellence, particularly his patience in the face of being torn from his father and sold into a strange land, his refusal to become bitter, and his consistent turning to Allah, all made him a muhsin, a doer of good at the highest level. The reward was not postponed until the Hereafter alone; it began in this world with an increase in wisdom and knowledge. This teaches that righteous conduct attracts divine gifts. When a young person uses the blessings of youth in obedience to Allah, He honours them with insight and understanding beyond their years. Joseph had passed the test of adversity with beautiful patience, and now Allah was preparing him for even greater tests and a magnificent destiny by equipping him with the very tools he would need.

=23=

Joseph’s second great trial now begins, the trial of temptation. The wife of the minister, who had watched Joseph grow into a remarkably handsome young man, became infatuated with him. The verse describes her action with stark clarity: “she tried to seduce him.” This was a premeditated act, not a sudden impulse. To ensure no interruption, “she locked the doors firmly,” closing every means of escape and ensuring complete privacy. Then she issued the direct command, “Come to me!” In that suffocating moment, with all earthly avenues of retreat blocked, Joseph’s response was immediate and spiritually anchored. He said, “Allah is my refuge! ” This is the cry of a heart that knows that safety from sin is not found in one’s own strength but in seeking divine protection. He did not pause to negotiate or flirt with the temptation. He then grounded his refusal in a clear moral principle: “It is not right to betray my master, who has taken good care of me.” He recognized the sin not only as a violation against Allah but also as a betrayal of the man who had provided him a home. This dual awareness—the vertical accountability to God and the horizontal duty to fellow humans—is the fortress of a righteous person. He concluded, “Indeed, the wrongdoers never succeed,” affirming a divine law that ultimate success, in this world and the next, is never found in transgression. Joseph’s words are a timeless model for anyone facing temptation: turn to Allah instantly, remember the trust placed in you, and remind yourself of the empty outcome of sin.

=24=

This verse delves into the intensity of the moment, acknowledging that Joseph was a human being with natural inclinations. It says, “She advanced towards him, and he would have done likewise, had he not seen a sign from his Lord.” Scholars explain that Joseph’s potential inclination was not a settled intention to sin but a natural human response that was immediately checked by divine intervention. The “sign from his Lord” is variously interpreted as the appearance of his father Jacob biting his finger in warning, or a sudden clear manifestation of the ugliness of the sin, or a reminder of the divine prohibition. Whatever form it took, it was a powerful spiritual vision that restored his full consciousness and fortified his will.

The verse then clarifies the divine pedagogy behind this: “This is how We kept evil and indecency away from him, for he was truly one of Our chosen servants.” The Arabic terms su’ (evil) and fahsha’ (indecency) cover both the sinful act and its shameless nature. Allah protected Joseph because he was a mukhlis, a chosen and purified servant. This protection did not remove his free will; rather, Allah provided him with the spiritual insight and strength that rendered the temptation powerless. The lesson for believers is profound: when a person sincerely strives to remain pure, Allah will send signs—whether through conscience, a sudden insight, or a protective circumstance—that turn them away from the brink. Joseph’s purity was both his own striving and a divine gift.

=25=

Having rejected her advances, Joseph fled toward the door to escape the sealed room. The verse says “they raced for the door,” indicating that she pursued him, grabbing his shirt from behind in a desperate attempt to stop him. The force of her pull tore his shirt from the back, leaving a crucial piece of evidence. Just as the door opened, they were confronted with a shocking sight: her husband, the minister, was standing right there. The timing, divinely orchestrated, prevented any possibility of her story being accepted without question. In an instant, the guilty wife shifted from seductress to accuser. She cried out, “What is the penalty for someone who tried to violate your wife, except imprisonment or a painful punishment?” With calculated cunning, she framed Joseph as the aggressor and herself as the victim of an assault, appealing to her husband’s sense of honour and outrage. She demanded severe punishment, calling for either a long imprisonment or a painful physical chastisement. Her words perfectly illustrate how those caught in sin often project their own guilt onto the innocent, using emotional manipulation to escape blame. The presence of the husband at the very moment of escape shows that Allah does not abandon His righteous servants to the lies of the wicked; He brings forth circumstances that vindicate the truth, often in ways no one could have planned.

=26=

Confronted with her false accusation, Joseph did not retaliate with anger or slander. He spoke the simple truth: “It was she who tried to seduce me.” He did not elaborate unnecessarily; he stated the fact and entrusted the outcome to Allah. At this critical juncture, a witness from her own family stepped forward. The Quran does not name the witness, but many commentators say it was a wise infant, a cousin of hers, or an advisor inspired to speak. The witness proposed a logical test based on physical evidence: “If his shirt is torn from the front, then she has told the truth and he is a liar.” The reasoning was impeccable. If a man were truly attacking a woman, she would push him away, tearing his shirt from the front. The shirt being torn from the front would corroborate her story of an aggressive advance. This test turned the torn shirt from a simple piece of fabric into decisive legal proof. The proposal demonstrates Islam’s high regard for evidence and logical reasoning in matters of justice. It also shows how Allah can bring forth truth from unexpected quarters, even from within the household of the wrongdoer. For Joseph, this witness was a direct answer to his earlier cry for refuge; Allah did not leave his innocence hidden but made it evident through a clear and irrefutable sign that even her husband could not deny.

=27=

The witness completes the logical framework: “But if it is torn from the back, then she has lied and he is truthful.” This second clause sealed the case. A shirt torn from the back could only happen if Joseph was fleeing and she was pursuing him, grabbing his garment from behind as he ran toward the door. This scenario perfectly matched Joseph’s account of her attempt to seduce him and his subsequent escape. The clarity of this deduction left no room for ambiguity. The verse highlights how a simple piece of circumstantial evidence, when examined with a sound and unbiased mind, can completely unravel a complex lie. It also demonstrates that truth has a self-evident quality; a falsehood, however skillfully crafted, will contain inconsistencies that a keen observer can detect. The witness’s intervention teaches the importance of speaking up when one knows the truth, especially when an innocent person is being maligned.

Furthermore, this test of the shirt is a testament to the fact that Allah protects the honour of His righteous servants. Joseph had refused the temptation in private, so Allah ensured that his vindication would be public. The principles of forensic examination found in this verse are timeless, reminding judges, leaders, and ordinary individuals not to rush to conclusions based on emotional outcries but to examine the evidence carefully before passing judgment.

=28=

The husband examines the evidence and immediately recognizes the truth. The shirt torn from the back was undeniable proof of Joseph’s innocence and his wife’s guilt. His reaction, however, was not one of rage directed at Joseph but a mixture of embarrassment and worldly wisdom directed at his wife. He said, “This must be an example of the cunning of you women! Indeed, your cunning is so shrewd!” His words reflect a man who is trying to manage a scandal within his elite household without bringing public shame upon himself. By framing the incident as a typical example of female scheming, he deflects the gravity of the crime while implicitly acknowledging his wife’s fault. His statement, though it generalizes in a way that is not divinely endorsed, reveals the deep-seated patriarchal attitudes of the society and his personal desire to close the matter quickly. He did not punish his wife severely nor did he fully exonerate Joseph in a public manner; he chose a path of private reproach. This illustrates the complexity of human reactions to truth when personal reputation and societal standing are at stake. For Joseph, this was a form of vindication, but it also foreshadowed that the full clearing of his name would require a longer and more divine process. The episode teaches that even when the truth is evident, those in power may not always administer perfect justice, and the innocent may need to endure further trials before complete exoneration.

=29=

The husband now addresses the two parties separately. To Joseph, he says, “O Joseph! Forget about this.” He advises him to bury the incident in silence, to not speak of it to anyone, in order to preserve the household’s reputation. This was a request for Joseph to swallow the injustice of the false accusation and move on, a heavy burden for an innocent person.

Yet Joseph, in his prophetic character, would comply, demonstrating patience and forbearance. To his wife, he says, “And you O wife! Seek forgiveness for your sin. It certainly has been your fault.” Here the husband makes a direct attribution of blame. He does not simply say “seek forgiveness” generally; he explicitly declares that the fault was hers.

However, the command to seek forgiveness, while religiously sound, was given without any meaningful consequence for her actions. This leniency shows the limits of human justice when the perpetrator is a loved one. For the wife, this was a divine respite; she was not immediately punished but was given the chance to repent. For Joseph, the instruction to “forget about this” was a new test: to accept that his exoneration, though clear, would not be publicly proclaimed, and to trust that Allah’s ultimate justice was sufficient. The verse subtly contrasts the flawed, compromised judgment of the Egyptian elite with the perfect justice of the Almighty, and it teaches believers that sometimes part of our trial is to bear with the imperfect resolutions of worldly authorities while keeping our hearts attached to Allah.

=30=

Despite the husband’s attempt to suppress the scandal, news of the incident inevitably spread, as matters of elite private life often do. The women of the city, likely from noble and influential families, began to gossip about the chief minister’s wife. Their words, “The Chief Minister’s wife is trying to seduce her slave-boy,” were both a statement of the scandalous fact and a subtle form of social mockery. They expressed a mixture of astonishment and condemnation, noting that her infatuation had reached a pathetic level: “Love for him has plagued her heart. ” Their remark “Indeed, we see that she is clearly mistaken” shows that even by their own moral standards, her behavior was a grave error.

Yet it is important to note that their criticism was not necessarily rooted in piety or a love for justice. They were engaging in idle talk, relishing the downfall of a social peer. Allah allowed this gossip to spread so that the matter would not remain entirely hidden and to pave the way for Joseph’s eventual complete vindication. The verse highlights the reality that sins committed in private often have a way of surfacing, and that society’s judgment, though flawed, can serve as a tool in the divine plan. It also sets the stage for the coming scene where these very women will be confronted with the truth of their own words.

=31=

The minister’s wife, stung by the whispered mockery of the city women, devised a clever and dramatic plan to justify her infatuation. She invited them to a banquet, a setting of luxury and indulgence, and placed in their hands knives to use for the fruit or food. At the precise moment they were occupied with cutting, she summoned Joseph. The command “Come out before them” brought the young man into the view of the assembled women. The effect was immediate and overwhelming. The verse says they “were so stunned by his beauty that they cut their hands.” This was not a small nick; the Arabic implies they sliced deeply into their own hands without even feeling the pain, so utterly captivated were they by his appearance. Their exclamation, “Good God! This cannot be human; this must be a noble angel!” reveals that Joseph’s beauty was of a transcendent, almost otherworldly nature. They instinctively compared him to an angel, a being of light and purity, so striking was his form. This scene serves a dual purpose. First, it proves that Joseph’s beauty was indeed extraordinary, providing context for the wife’s overwhelming desire. Second, it exposes the hypocrisy of the women: they who had criticized her were themselves overwhelmed by the very same sight, to the point of self-harm. Their reaction becomes a testimony to the intensity of the trial Joseph faced daily.

=32=

Seizing the moment of the women’s stunned amazement, the minister’s wife made her case. She declared, “This is the one for whose love you criticized me!” With this, she directly confronted them, showing that her infatuation was not a sign of her depravity but a response to a beauty so powerful that it could make even them lose their senses. Then, she openly confessed a part of the truth: “I did try to seduce him but he firmly refused. ” This public admission of her own attempt and his steadfast refusal is remarkable. She did not lie about his innocence; rather, she acknowledged it in front of the city’s elite women, thereby becoming an unwitting witness to Joseph’s righteousness.

However, her confession was immediately followed by a brazen ultimatum. Turning her words toward Joseph, she said, “And if he does not do what I order him to, he will certainly be imprisoned and fully disgraced. ” The threat is naked: either he yields to her desire, or she will use her power to ruin his reputation and lock him away. This marks an escalation from private temptation to public coercion. Joseph was now faced not only with a sinful invitation but with a tyrannical command backed by the threat of severe punishment. The scene illustrates how unchecked desire, when partnered with power, can turn into oppression.

=33=

Faced with the threat of imprisonment and disgrace, and surrounded by a group of powerful women who had just witnessed his beauty and might now be part of the pressure campaign, Joseph turned to the only source of help that never fails. His prayer, “My Lord! I would rather be in jail than do what they invite me to,” is one of the most powerful declarations of moral preference in the Quran. Joseph expressed that a prison cell, with its darkness, confinement, and deprivation, was more beloved to him than the “freedom” to commit sin. This preference shows a soul that has utterly internalized the reality that spiritual ruin is a far greater prison than any physical one. He then humbly acknowledged his own weakness: “And if You do not turn their cunning away from me, I might yield to them and fall into ignorance.” Despite his prophethood and purity, Joseph admitted that without Allah’s protection, even he could slip.

This is the essence of true piety: not arrogance about one’s own righteousness, but a constant, desperate reliance on Allah for safeguarding. The word “cunning” underscores that temptation is not merely a direct invitation but a web of plots, social pressure, and threats. Joseph’s supplication teaches every believer that the only guaranteed shield against falling into sin is to ask Allah for protection, recognizing our own fragility.

=34=

This verse records the immediate divine answer to Joseph’s heartfelt supplication. “So his Lord responded to him” affirms that the prayer of a sincere servant in distress is never ignored. The response was specific: “turning their cunning away from him.” This did not mean the women stopped their plotting; in fact, they would proceed to have him imprisoned. Rather, it means that Allah fortified Joseph’s heart so that their schemes could not sway him, and He orchestrated events so that the ultimate harm of sin would not touch him. The divine response also manifested in the decision to imprison him, which, ironically, was the very means of separating him from the environment of temptation.

The verse ends with two majestic names: “Surely He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.” Al-Sami’, the All-Hearing, reminds us that Allah hears every whispered prayer, every cry for help, even when no human ear can perceive it. Al-‘Alim, the All-Knowing, assures us that He understands the full depth of the trial, the pain it causes, and the best way to deliver His servant. This combination offers immense comfort: when you call upon Him, He hears you perfectly, and He knows exactly what you need for your protection and deliverance, even if that deliverance comes in a form that initially appears to be a hardship, like prison.

=35=

After the scandal of the banquet, the matter had become public enough that the authorities, including the minister and his advisors, had to manage the situation. The verse says, “And so it occurred to those in charge, despite seeing all the proofs of his innocence, that he should be imprisoned for a while.” This reveals a profound moral failure on their part. They were not ignorant of Joseph’s innocence; the phrase “despite seeing all the proofs” confirms that the evidence—the torn shirt, the testimony of the witness, the wife’s own admission, the women’s reaction—was clear to them.

Yet, to protect the reputation of the elite household and to quiet the scandal, they chose to punish the innocent. Imprisonment was a political solution, a sacrifice of justice for the sake of social convenience.

The phrase “for a while” indicates they never intended it to be a permanent punishment; they just needed him out of sight until the gossip died down. From a human perspective, this was a gross injustice. From the divine perspective, it was the next step in Joseph’s journey. The prison would become a sanctuary from the relentless advances of the women and a school where he would refine his character, interpret dreams, and eventually meet the king’s cupbearer.

This verse teaches that what people intend for harm, Allah can repurpose for good, and that human courts may fail, but the divine plan never does.

=36=

Joseph’s imprisonment was not a solitary confinement in despair; it brought him into contact with two young men who worked in the royal service, one as a cupbearer and the other as a baker. They had fallen into disfavor and been thrown into prison. The verse introduces them as they approach Joseph with their dreams.

The first said, “I dreamt I was pressing wine,” a vision of his former occupation, squeezing grapes to make drink for the king. The second said, “I dreamt I was carrying some bread on my head, from which birds were eating,” a vision involving his own craft but with a disturbing twist of birds devouring the bread. These dreams were not random; they were divinely sent to open a door for Joseph’s eventual release. Both men prefaced their request by saying, “for we surely see you as one of the good-doers.” Even in the confines of a prison, Joseph’s character had shone through. The inmates had observed his conduct, his speech, his kindness, and his wisdom, and they recognized him as a man of exceptional righteousness. This reputation was the seed of their trust in his ability to interpret their dreams. It demonstrates that true goodness is recognizable and attracts people who are seeking truth, regardless of the surroundings. Joseph’s patience in prison was not passive; it was an active display of excellence that turned a jail into a field for dawah.

=37=

Rather than immediately interpreting their dreams, Joseph seized the opportunity to address their deeper spiritual need. He began with a demonstration of his gift: “I can even tell you what kind of meal you will be served before you receive it.” This was a sign of prophethood, a small supernatural insight into the immediate future, proving that his knowledge was not based on guesswork. He immediately attributed this ability to its source, saying, “This knowledge is from what my Lord has taught me. ” He took no personal credit; he directed their attention to Allah. This was his opening to call them to monotheism. He then said, “I have shunned the faith of a people who disbelieve in Allah and deny the Hereafter.” Joseph identified himself clearly as separate from the polytheistic and idolatrous beliefs of Egypt. His statement was not merely a declaration of personal identity; it was a subtle invitation for them to question their own faith. By presenting his rejection of disbelief and his adherence to the pure faith of his forefathers, he planted the seed of tawhid. This moment teaches a vital principle of prophetic calling: when someone comes to you with a worldly need, use that window to address their spiritual need. Joseph cared about their dreams, but he cared more about their souls, and he masterfully connected the two.

=38=

Joseph continues his call by linking himself to the noble lineage of prophethood. “I follow the faith of my fathers: Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.” He connects his monotheism to the most revered patriarchs, names that even non-Israelites in that region might respect. This rooting in a continuous chain of divine guidance shows that true religion is not a personal invention but a heritage of revelation from Allah. He then states the core of that faith: “It is not right for us to associate anything with Allah in worship.

” This is a simple yet profound negation of shirk, the one unforgivable sin if one dies without repenting. Joseph makes it clear that given the immense favours of Allah upon the line of Abraham, any form of idolatry would be a catastrophic betrayal. He calls it a divine “grace upon us and humanity” to be guided to pure monotheism, as it liberates humans from worshipping created things and directs them to the Creator.

Yet he laments, “but most people are not grateful.” Gratitude here is not just saying thanks; it is the proper response to the blessing of tawhid, which is to embrace it fully and live by it. Most people, then and now, take this blessing lightly or reject it outright. Joseph’s words in the prison were a powerful, succinct summary of the entire prophetic message, delivered with love and wisdom.

=39=

Joseph now poses a direct, rational question to his two cellmates, calling them “O my fellow-prisoners” with a tone of sincere companionship. He asks, “Which is far better: many different lords or Allah—the One, the Supreme?

” This is an appeal to the innate human intellect. He sets up a comparison between a fragmented system of multiple gods, each with their own will, who would inevitably clash and create chaos, and the one true God who is the Supreme, the Irresistible, who maintains perfect unity and order. The absurdity of multiple deities is obvious to any thinking mind: if there were many gods, the universe would be in constant conflict. Joseph reduces the argument to its simplest, most logical form. He does not begin by quoting scripture or demanding blind faith; he engages the natural reason that Allah has placed within every human being.

The name “the One, the Supreme” highlights two essential attributes: al-Wahid, the unique, indivisible One; and al-Qahhar, the One whose will cannot be thwarted. This dual description answers both the intellectual question of unity and the existential need for a sovereign protector. Joseph’s method teaches that presenting Islam can start with awakening the fitrah, the primordial nature, through clear and reasonable questions that lead to the inevitable conclusion of monotheism.

=40=

In this verse, Joseph delivers the decisive blow against idolatry, dismantling its very foundation. He begins, “Whatever idols you worship instead of Him are mere names which you and your forefathers have made up.

” This is a devastating critique: the gods of polytheism have no reality, no essence; they are just labels attached to wood, stone, or celestial bodies, invented by human tradition. They possess no power, no knowledge, and no authority.

The phrase “a practice Allah has never authorized” solidifies the point that a belief system is not validated by its age or the number of its followers; it is validated only if it comes from divine sanction. Joseph then establishes the exclusive sovereignty of Allah: “It is only Allah Who decides.” Judgment, legislation, and the right to command worship belong to Him alone.

This principle has profound implications: no human authority, tradition, or cultural norm can obligate worship of anything other than the Creator. He concludes with the clear divine command, “He has commanded that you worship none but Him,” and calls this “the upright faith,” the straight and stable religion that aligns with human nature and reason. “But most people do not know” is a sorrowful observation that ignorance veils this clear truth. Joseph’s prison sermon, encapsulated in these verses, is a timeless model of da‘wah: grounded in logic, rooted in revelation, and delivered with genuine compassion for those trapped in falsehood.
