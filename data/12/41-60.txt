=41=

Having delivered his clear call to monotheism, Joseph now addresses the immediate need that brought his two fellow-prisoners to him. He begins with the compassionate address “O my fellow-prisoners,” maintaining the bond of shared hardship even as he delivers news that will drastically separate their fates. He tells them plainly what their dreams signify. For the one who dreamt of pressing wine, the interpretation is a return to his former position: he will once again serve wine to his master, the king. For the other, who saw birds eating bread from his head, the interpretation is grim: he will be crucified, and the birds will eat from his head. Joseph does not soften the harsh truth or wrap it in comforting ambiguity. His prophetic integrity compels him to convey the message exactly as Allah has revealed it, regardless of how it will be received.

This is a profound lesson in honesty; when we are entrusted with knowledge, especially difficult knowledge, we must deliver it truthfully without distortion. Joseph then seals the interpretation with the statement, “The matter about which you inquired has been decided.” This means that the events are not mere possibilities but definitive decrees that will come to pass. The dreams were not random; they were a glimpse into the future as ordained by Allah. The finality of the statement also demonstrates Joseph’s absolute confidence in what his Lord has taught him. There is no hesitation, no “I think” or “perhaps,” because the interpretation is revelation. This moment also serves as a test for the two men: one would live and witness the truth of Joseph’s words, while the other would face his end with the knowledge that a prophet had spoken truth to him. It reminds every listener that the words of Allah’s messengers are always true, and that every soul will eventually see the reality of what it was promised.

=42=

After interpreting the dreams, Joseph saw a human opportunity for his release. He said to the one he knew would survive and return to royal service, “Mention me in the presence of your master.” This request was not a sign of weak faith or a lack of reliance on Allah; it was a perfect example of balancing tawakkul with permissible action. Joseph took the means available to him, asking a fellow human for a favour, while his heart ultimately depended on his Lord. This teaches us that true reliance on Allah does not mean abandoning the use of worldly channels; the prophets themselves utilized them.

However, the verse then reveals a twist in the unfolding divine plan: “But Satan made him forget to mention Joseph to his master.” The cupbearer, once freed and restored to his position, completely forgot about the man who had accurately interpreted his dream. The Quran attributes this lapse to Satan, who exploits human forgetfulness to serve a greater purpose in the cosmic drama. Because of this forgetfulness, Joseph remained in prison not for a short while, but for “several years.” Scholars mention a period ranging from three to seven years, and in the darkness of a jail cell, each year must have felt like an eternity. This prolonged stay was a profound test of patience. It teaches a critical lesson: sometimes, even when a door of relief appears to open, it may close again, not because Allah has abandoned His servant, but because the perfect timing has not yet arrived. The delay ensured that Joseph’s release would coincide with the king’s dream, making his rise to power absolutely undeniable and tying his freedom directly to a public demonstration of his prophetic gift. The years of forgotten imprisonment were not wasted; they were a crucible that refined Joseph’s character and prepared him for the immense responsibility of governing a nation.

=43=

The scene shifts from the prison to the palace, where the ruler of Egypt, referred to here as the King, experiences a deeply troubling dream. He tells his courtiers, “I dreamt of seven fat cows eaten up by seven skinny ones; and seven green ears of grain and seven others dry.” The imagery is vivid and unsettling. Seven healthy, plump cows, symbols of agricultural prosperity, are completely devoured by seven emaciated, starving cows. Similarly, seven lush green ears of grain are juxtaposed with seven brittle, dry ones. The king senses that this is no ordinary, chaotic dream; it is a message of great significance. He turns to his chiefs and advisors, the learned elite of his kingdom, and pleads, “Tell me the meaning of my dream if you can interpret dreams.” His conditional phrase reveals that he already suspects the dream’s symbolic nature requires a special skill that his court magicians and soothsayers might not possess. This dream was a divine alarm bell, a warning to the entire land of a coming catastrophe and its solution, planted by Allah in the mind of the king because He had already placed the interpreter in a prison cell. The king’s public appeal and the inability of the wise men of Egypt to answer would serve as the perfect prologue to Joseph’s moment of emergence.

This verse reminds us that Allah can use even the leaders of non-believing nations as instruments in His grand plan, and that a single truthful dream placed in a ruler’s mind can alter the destiny of millions.

=44=

The response of the king’s advisors is immediate and marked by a confession of complete impotence. They dismiss the king’s vivid dream as “confused visions,” a jumble of meaningless images that arise from a disturbed mind or random thoughts.

The Arabic term they used, adghath ahlam, literally refers to tangled, mixed-up dreams that have no coherent interpretation. This dismissal was not necessarily an honest diagnosis but a convenient way to mask their ignorance. They had no access to divine knowledge, and their worldly wisdom, sorcery, and intellectual systems were utterly bankrupt before this prophetic challenge. They added, “we do not know the interpretation of such dreams,” a frank admission that whatever skills they possessed were insufficient. This moment of collective failure is a powerful illustration of the limits of human knowledge when it is disconnected from revelation. The most brilliant minds in Egypt, with all their learning and their claimed connection to the unseen, stood helpless. It highlights that true knowledge of the unseen and the deep meanings of events comes only from Allah. The stage was now perfectly set. The king’s distress was at its peak, and the entire intellectual establishment had admitted defeat. In this vacuum of guidance, the forgotten prisoner, Joseph the truthful, would be remembered. This teaches us that Allah often allows human systems to fail completely so that His light may shine more brilliantly through His chosen servants, leaving no room for anyone else to claim credit.

=45=

The crisis in the palace triggers a sudden, long-dormant memory. The verse says, “Finally, the surviving ex-prisoner remembered Joseph after a long time.

” This is the cupbearer, the very man Joseph had asked to mention him to his master. For years, under the influence of Satan’s make-him-forget scheme, the cupbearer had been entirely oblivious to his promise and to the man who had correctly foretold his own release.

The phrase “after a long time” underscores the length of Joseph’s patient endurance and the complete nature of the forgetfulness. But now, at the exact moment when the king’s need was greatest, the memory returned. This was not a random recollection; it was a carefully timed divine intervention. The cupbearer, witnessing the helplessness of the court, immediately connected the king’s demand for interpretation with his own experience in prison. He stepped forward and said, “I will tell you its interpretation, so send me forth to Joseph.” He did not claim to know the interpretation himself but pointed to the true source of knowledge. His statement “send me forth” shows he wanted to go personally to Joseph, perhaps to make amends for his forgetfulness and to bring back the interpretation directly.

This verse teaches that no matter how long a promise is delayed or how completely a person seems forgotten, Allah’s timing is perfect. The remembrance came not a moment too soon or too late, and it transformed Joseph from a forgotten prisoner into the key advisor to the king.

=46=

The cupbearer makes his way to the prison and stands before Joseph, the man he had shamefully neglected. He addresses Joseph with the most honourable title: “O man of truth!

” The Arabic word siddiq denotes a person of consummate, verified truthfulness, someone whose words and deeds are in perfect harmony and whose integrity is beyond question. This salutation is a powerful public testimony to Joseph’s character, even within the confines of a jail. The cupbearer had witnessed Joseph’s truthfulness in the interpretation of his own dream years earlier, and that experience now compelled him to vouch for Joseph’s reliability. He then recounts the king’s dream, describing the seven fat cows being eaten by the seven skinny ones, and the seven green ears of grain alongside the seven dry ones.

The repetition of the dream verbatim confirms the accuracy of the cupbearer’s memory and the consistency of the divine message. He ends his request with a clear objective: “so that I may return to the people and let them know. ” This was not a private consultation; the entire kingdom awaited the answer. The cupbearer now acts as a messenger, bridging the gap between the forgotten prophet and the desperate ruler. This scene illustrates that when a person’s truthfulness is proven over time, it becomes an asset that eventually brings them opportunities for greater service. Joseph’s patience was now about to bear fruit, as the title “man of truth” opened the door to the palace.

=47=

Joseph, the man of truth, does not hesitate. He immediately begins to unfold the meaning of the dream with a precision that shows this is not guesswork but revelation. He says, “You will plant grain for seven consecutive years.” The seven fat cows and the seven green ears of grain represent a period of unprecedented agricultural abundance that will last for seven full years. This was a promise of prosperity, but Joseph immediately follows it with a critical, life-saving strategy: “leaving in the ear whatever you will harvest, except for the little you will eat.” The instruction to store the grain in its ears is a remarkably advanced piece of agricultural wisdom. Leaving the grain in the husk protects it from weevils, moisture, and spoilage, allowing it to be preserved for many years. Joseph was not merely interpreting a dream; he was laying down a comprehensive national economic policy. The exception, “except for the little you will eat,” teaches moderation even in times of plenty. The years of abundance were not to be a time of wasteful consumption and extravagant feasting, but a period of disciplined saving. The people were to eat only what was necessary and preserve the vast majority of the harvest for the crisis ahead.

This verse reveals that divine guidance is practical; it saves not only souls but also bodies and civilizations. Joseph’s plan would go on to save Egypt and the surrounding lands from a famine that would have killed millions.

=48=

Joseph continues his prophecy, moving from the years of plenty to the years of famine. He says, “Then after that will come seven years of great hardship.” The seven skinny cows and the seven dry ears of grain represent seven years of severe drought and famine that will follow the period of abundance. The description “great hardship” captures the totality of the suffering: the land will become barren, rivers will dry, and the people will face extreme hunger. The famine will be so consuming that it will “consume whatever you have saved,” meaning that even the massive grain reserves stored during the seven good years will be completely used up by the end of the seven hard years. Joseph then adds a vital detail: “except the little you will store for seed.” Even in the midst of consuming the reserves, they must set aside a portion of the grain to be used as seed for the future.

This is the foresight of a true leader. If, in the desperation of hunger, they consumed every last grain, there would be nothing to plant when the rains finally returned, and the famine would perpetuate itself. The preservation of seed grain was an act of faith, a belief that after the hardship would come relief, and one must prepare for that relief even in the darkest moment.

This verse teaches the importance of long-term thinking and the duty of safeguarding resources for future generations, principles that are foundational to any stable and righteous society.

=49=

Joseph completes the interpretation with a note of hope that extends beyond the crisis. He says, “Then after that will come a year in which people will receive abundant rain and they will press oil and wine.” The period of drought is not eternal; it has a divinely appointed end. The return of abundant rain signals the restoration of life to the parched earth. The specific mention of pressing oil and wine, products of olives and grapes, indicates that the land will once again produce its finest and most valuable crops. Oil and wine are not mere staples; they are symbols of prosperity, health, and joy. By including this detail, Joseph reassures the king that the future holds not just bare survival but a return to a state of full flourishing and divine blessing. The vision spans a complete cycle of fourteen years of agricultural history, followed by a year of recovery, demonstrating that Allah’s management of worldly affairs follows laws and rhythms that He reveals to His prophets. For the king and the people, this interpretation was a complete roadmap: save during the seven easy years, ration during the seven hard years, and preserve seed for the year of rain. The entire plan was a mercy from Allah, communicated through the mouth of a man who had been unjustly imprisoned. It shows that no matter how bleak a personal situation appears, one’s divinely given knowledge can still become the means of salvation for an entire nation, and that after every hardship, Allah sends ease.

=50=

Upon hearing the full interpretation and the brilliant plan, the king’s reaction is immediate and commanding: “Bring him to me.” Joseph was no longer just an interpreter; he was the solution to Egypt’s impending disaster.

However, when the royal messenger arrived at the prison to fetch him, Joseph did not rush out to his freedom. Instead, he gave the messenger a surprising response: “Go back to your master and ask him about the case of the women who cut their hands. ” This was a bold, principled move. Joseph refused to leave the prison while a cloud of suspicion still hung over his character. The years-old scandal involving the chief minister’s wife and the women of the city had never been formally resolved, and Joseph, a man of honour, insisted that his innocence be publicly established before he assumed any position of authority. He added, “Surely my Lord has full knowledge of their cunning,” affirming that Allah was his ultimate witness, but he still required a public clearing of his name as a matter of justice and proper procedure. This teaches a crucial lesson: a believer should not be careless about his or her reputation. While one’s worth is ultimately with Allah, seeking to clear one’s name from false accusations is not a sign of arrogance but of self-respect and a desire to prevent the slander from hindering future good work. Joseph’s patience was vast, but it was not passive; he chose the moment of his vindication to ensure it was complete.

=51=

The king, recognizing the justice of Joseph’s request, convened a court and summoned the very women who were involved in the old scandal. He put the question directly to them: “What did you get when you tried to seduce Joseph?” The wording of the king’s question reveals that the general outline of the story was known among the elite, but he wanted a formal declaration. The women, under oath and before the king, could no longer evade the truth. They responded with the exclamation, “Allah forbid! We know nothing indecent about him.” This was a full and unequivocal exoneration. The very women who had been captivated by his beauty and who had gossiped about him now testified that he was completely innocent of any wrongdoing. Then, the most crucial testimony came from the chief minister’s wife herself. In front of the king and the court, she confessed openly: “Now the truth has come to light. It was I who tried to seduce him, and he is surely truthful.” Her words are a complete vindication.

The phrase “now the truth has come to light” signals that the moment of forced honesty had arrived. She publicly owned her guilt and declared Joseph truthful. This was the fulfilment of the divine promise made to Joseph in the well: “One day you will remind them of this deed of theirs while they are unaware of who you are.” Even though they now knew who he was, the reminder and the confession brought the truth to light, clearing the path for his rise.

=52=

The speaker in this verse is most commonly understood to be the chief minister’s wife, continuing her confession. She says, “From this, Joseph should know that I did not speak dishonestly about him in his absence.” She is clarifying that after the initial incident, when the matter was hushed up, she did not continue to slander Joseph behind his back. Though she had sinned grievously and had once falsely accused him, she now claims that she did not persist in spreading lies about him later. Her admission of this fact is meant to fully clear the air and to let Joseph know that his reputation remained, at least from her tongue, free of further secret defamation. She then adds a profound theological truth: “for Allah certainly does not guide the scheming of the dishonest.

” This is her own recognition of a divine law. Those who engage in dishonesty and scheming may appear to succeed for a time, but Allah does not grant them true guidance or ultimate success in their plots. Her confession, therefore, carries a bitter lesson learned from her own experience. The entire affair had collapsed, and the truth had been laid bare despite all her efforts, proving that dishonest schemes are ultimately doomed. This verse sets the stage for her deeper reflection on the nature of the human soul, showing that even a person who committed a serious sin can arrive at a moment of painful self-awareness and acknowledgement of Allah’s justice.

=53=

In a moment of striking spiritual honesty, the wife of the chief minister continues her statement, saying, “And I do not seek to free myself from blame.” She refuses to offer excuses or to blame circumstances, society, or even Joseph’s exceptional beauty for her actions.

This is the posture of someone who has truly been humbled by the exposure of their sin. She acknowledges that the root cause was not external but internal: “for indeed the soul is ever inclined to evil. ” This is one of the most profound psychological insights in the Quran. The nafs, the inner self, has a natural tendency to command toward evil, to desire what is harmful or forbidden, unless it is disciplined and purified by divine guidance. She recognizes that this inclination is a universal human reality. The only exception, as she states, are “those shown mercy by my Lord. ” This is the recognition that purity and righteousness are not purely human achievements but gifts of divine mercy. When a person struggles against their lower self and wins, it is because Allah has shown them mercy and protected them. Her statement aligns with the Quranic teaching in Sûrah Yûnus that all human striving is ultimately encompassed by Allah’s grace. She concludes with the beautiful divine names: “Surely my Lord is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. ” Even in her confession, she holds onto the hope that the Lord she acknowledges is capable of forgiving her great sin and showing her mercy. This verse gives every sinner the roadmap to repentance: stop making excuses, recognize the soul’s natural flaws, attribute any good to Allah’s mercy, and turn to His forgiveness.

=54=

With Joseph’s name now thoroughly cleared and his innocence proclaimed by the very women who once testified against him, the king repeated his summons, this time with a clear statement of intent: “Bring him to me. I will employ him exclusively in my service.

” The phrase “exclusively in my service” indicates that Joseph would be appointed to a special, high-ranking position close to the king, not a general court appointment. When Joseph finally came and spoke with the king, the ruler was deeply impressed. After hearing Joseph’s wisdom, his analysis of the dream, and his detailed economic plan, the king declared, “Today you are highly esteemed and fully trusted by us.

” The word “today” emphasizes the dramatic transformation: the man who had been a slave bought for a few silver coins, then a prisoner falsely accused, was now, on this very day, a highly honoured and completely trusted advisor to the most powerful man in the land. The phrase “highly esteemed” speaks to his rank and dignity, while “fully trusted” indicates that the king placed complete confidence in his character and competence.

This is a powerful illustration of how Allah can reverse fortunes in an instant. The very qualities that Joseph had preserved in the darkness of prison—his truthfulness, his wisdom, his patience—became the direct cause of his elevation. The king’s trust was not blind; it was the result of witnessed virtue. This teaches us that if we remain faithful to Allah in our low moments, He will raise us in ways we cannot imagine, granting us both dignity and the trust of others.

=55=

In response to the king’s offer of a general high position, Joseph did not remain silent or wait to be assigned a vague role. He stepped forward with a specific proposal that matched his skills and the nation’s pressing need: “Put me in charge of the store-houses of the land.” This was a direct request to be appointed as the minister of finance, agriculture, and food reserves, effectively the chief administrator for the national survival plan. Joseph justified his request by citing two critical qualities: “for I am truly reliable and adept. ” The word hafiz, translated as reliable, means a diligent guardian and protector, someone who will preserve the resources with honesty and careful management.

The word alim, translated as adept, means knowledgeable and skilled, particularly in the interpretation of dreams and the agricultural and economic knowledge needed to execute the fourteen-year plan. Joseph’s action is a profound lesson in ethical leadership. He did not seek the position for power, prestige, or personal wealth; he sought it because he knew he possessed the divinely granted competence to save millions from starvation. When a person has unique skills that can serve the public good during a crisis, it is not only permissible but praiseworthy to offer oneself for that responsibility. Joseph combined the highest level of integrity with the highest level of expertise, demonstrating that in Islam, a leader must be both morally upright and professionally capable. The entire nation would soon depend on the work of this “reliable and adept” man, and his request shows that true humility does not mean hiding one’s God-given talents but deploying them in the service of humanity.

=56=

This verse shifts to the divine perspective, summarizing the grand outcome of Joseph’s long journey of trial and patience. Allah says, “This is how We established Joseph in the land to settle wherever he pleased.

” The word “established” translates makkanna, which implies granting full, secure authority and the freedom to act. After being thrown into a well, sold as a slave, falsely accused, and imprisoned for years, Joseph was now given complete mastery over Egypt.

The phrase “to settle wherever he pleased” illustrates the extent of his empowerment; he could travel, reside, and administer the land without restriction. This was not a worldly reward alone but a fulfilment of divine promise.

The verse continues, “We shower Our mercy on whoever We will,” reminding us that such favours are not earned mechanically but are bestowed by Allah’s sovereign choice and grace. Joseph’s rise was a mercy that also encompassed the people of Egypt and his own family.

The final clause, “and We never discount the reward of the good-doers,” connects the outcome directly to Joseph’s ihsan—his beautiful patience, his chastity, his honesty, and his unwavering devotion. Allah does not let a single act of righteousness go to waste. Even if the reward is delayed for years, as it was for Joseph, it will come in a form far greater than what was lost. This verse offers immense hope to every believer who is striving to do good in the face of hardship; the divine ledger never overlooks a single good deed, and the reckoning is always generous.

=57=

Lest anyone, including Joseph, be tempted to think that the pinnacle of success was the material power and comfort he had now attained, the Quran immediately reorients the heart. Allah declares, “And the reward of the Hereafter is far better.” Joseph, the chief minister of Egypt, with storehouses of grain and the obedience of a nation, possessed what would seem to be the ultimate worldly achievement.

Yet, compared to the eternal bliss of Paradise, this immense kingdom is nothing. The verse specifies that this far better reward is “for those who are faithful and are mindful of Allah.” Faithfulness and taqwa (mindfulness of Allah) are the true measures of a human being. Joseph had maintained both in the depths of the well and the prison; now he was required to maintain them in the palace. Power is often a greater spiritual test than hardship, and this reminder is a subtle protection for Joseph and all believers. It tells us that worldly success, even when granted by Allah as a reward, should never become the goal itself. The goal is the Hereafter, and the signs of worldly honour should increase one’s gratitude and mindfulness, not breed complacency. This verse serves as the perfect bridge in the story. Joseph’s rise to power is not the end of his tale; it is the platform for the next great chapter, the reunion with his family. But the spiritual lens through which we view this climax is calibrated by this reminder: the real happy ending is not the throne of Egypt but the eternal nearness to Allah.

=58=

Years passed, the seven years of abundance concluded, and the seven years of drought began to grip the land. The famine extended beyond Egypt, reaching into Canaan, where Jacob and his sons lived. In need of grain, Joseph’s ten half-brothers traveled to Egypt, as people from all surrounding lands did. The verse succinctly describes the dramatic encounter: “And Joseph’s brothers came and entered his presence. ” Here stands the very brothers who had thrown him into the well out of jealousy.

The next clause is filled with deep emotion: “He recognized them but they were unaware of who he really was.” Joseph, now a mature man dressed in Egyptian royal attire, speaking through an interpreter perhaps, was unrecognizable to them. They had last seen him as a terrified boy, and their minds could never have imagined that the powerful minister before whom they now stood in supplication was that same brother. He, on the other hand, recognized them instantly. Their faces, their voices, the marks of age, but still the same men who had plotted against him. This moment is a powerful testimony to the perfection of divine planning. The brothers who once sought to distance Joseph from his father now had to travel a great distance to bow before his authority, seeking the food that he controlled. Joseph’s recognition without their awareness gave him complete control of the situation, allowing him to test them, to observe their character after all these years, and to guide the reunion according to wisdom rather than raw emotion.

=59=

Joseph treated his brothers with generosity, giving them the grain they came for, but he also set a condition for their return. After equipping them with their supplies, he made a demand: “Bring me your brother on your father’s side.” He was asking for Benjamin, his only full brother, who had not come with them. Jacob had kept Benjamin close after losing Joseph, and the brothers had left him behind. Joseph’s request was deliberate and strategic. He wanted to see Benjamin, to have his beloved brother with him, and he used his authority to compel them to bring him. He then appealed to their reason by highlighting his own fair conduct: “Do you not see that I give full measure and I am the best of hosts?” He pointed out that he had been generous and hospitable, not short-changing them or treating them harshly. This was a subtle invitation for them to trust him as an honourable man who would do them no harm. It was also a test: would they return with Benjamin, showing they could be protective of their other brother, or would they abandon him too? The request was layered with psychological and moral complexity. He did not reveal his identity, but he created a situation that would force them to confront their past behaviour and demonstrate whether their hearts had changed. The requirement to bring Benjamin was the first step in a careful plan to reunite the entire family under divine guidance.

=60=

Joseph’s demand was not a polite suggestion; it was a firm ultimatum. He said, “But if you do not bring him to me next time, I will have no grain for you, nor will you ever come close to me again.” This was a calculated use of his authority as the distributor of the life-saving grain. The brothers had a large family depending on them, and the famine was relentless. The threat of no further grain was a matter of survival, not just inconvenience. The additional phrase “nor will you ever come close to me again” severed any possibility of negotiation or future mercy if they failed. This created a powerful incentive for them to convince their father to let Benjamin go with them on their next trip. Joseph’s strictness was necessary to achieve the greater good of bringing Benjamin to safety and initiating the process of family reconciliation. He did not know if the brothers would be honest, if they would harm Benjamin as they had harmed him, so he used the leverage he had to ensure they would bring their brother under the most secure terms they could manage. From a legal and strategic standpoint, Joseph’s manoeuvring was flawless.

This verse shows that firmness in a just cause, when accompanied by wisdom, is not cruelty but a tool for achieving righteous ends. The brothers would now have to return home and deal with their father’s deep-seated fears, setting the stage for the next emotional chapter in this greatest of stories.
