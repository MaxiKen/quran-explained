=101=

After witnessing the complete fulfilment of his childhood dream and reuniting with his entire family, Joseph turned to his Lord in a prayer that encapsulates the essence of his spiritual journey. He began by gratefully acknowledging the blessings he had received: “My Lord! You have surely granted me authority and taught me the interpretation of dreams.

” This is the recognition that both worldly power and sacred knowledge are gifts from Allah, not personal achievements. The authority he held as chief minister of Egypt was a trust, and the ability to interpret dreams was a prophetic endowment that had been the key to his rise. He mentioned these two specific blessings because they represented the tools through which Allah’s plan was executed. Then he called upon his Lord with the majestic title “Originator of the heavens and the earth.” By invoking Allah as the Fatir, the One who brings the universe into being from nothing, Joseph acknowledged the absolute creative power of his Lord, before whom all worldly kingdoms shrink into insignificance. He then declared, “You are my Guardian in this world and the Hereafter,” expressing complete dependence on Allah’s protection and friendship in all realms of existence. Having tasted the pinnacle of worldly success, Joseph’s heart remained fixed on the eternal, not on the fading kingdom of Egypt. This led to his profound final request: “Allow me to die as one who submits and join me with the righteous.

” This is the ultimate aspiration of a believer. He did not ask for a long life of comfort or continued power; he asked that his life conclude in a state of complete submission to Allah, and that his final abode be with the righteous, the prophets and the saints. Joseph’s prayer teaches that after every success, the heart must turn back to its Creator, and that the truest goal is not worldly achievement but a beautiful ending of faith and a gathering with the beloved servants of Allah in the Hereafter.

=102=

The narrative of Joseph’s life concludes, and the Quran now turns to directly address the Prophet Muhammad, drawing a powerful connection between the story and his own mission. Allah says, “That is from the stories of the unseen which We reveal to you O Prophet.

” The word “unseen” refers to events that were hidden from human knowledge, occurring in a distant past that no living person in Arabia could have witnessed or reported with such precise detail. The fact that the Prophet could recite these events in full, without having read the scriptures of the Jews or Christians, was a clear proof of his prophethood. The revelation of such a detailed and coherent narrative demonstrated that the source was Divine. Allah then specifies, “You were not present when they all made up their minds, and when they plotted against Joseph.” This points to the secret council of the brothers when they decided to throw Joseph into the well. The Prophet was not there to observe their whispered conspiracies, yet the Quran unveiled the hidden thoughts, dialogues, and motivations of those people. This serves to confirm to the Prophet and his followers that the Quran is not a human invention but a message from the One who knows all secrets. For the early Muslims in Mecca, who were themselves the targets of secret plots and conspiracies by their own relatives and tribesmen, this verse was profoundly comforting. It reminded them that Allah was fully aware of every scheme hatched against them, just as He was aware of the brothers’ plot against Joseph. And just as He delivered Joseph from the well and raised him to honour, He would deliver the believers from the persecution of the Quraysh.

=103=

Following the assurance that the story of Joseph is divinely revealed truth, Allah addresses a painful reality that the Prophet was facing: despite the clarity of the signs and the beauty of the narrative, the majority of the Meccan polytheists were not embracing faith. The verse says, “And most people will not believe—no matter how keen you are.

” The Arabic word for “keen” is haris, which conveys an intense, burning desire and eager concern. The Prophet’s heart was overflowing with love and anxiety for his people; he desperately wished for their guidance and salvation.

This verse reveals the Prophet’s deep compassion and his emotional investment in his mission. Yet Allah gently informs him that his keenness alone cannot override the free will of human beings or the divine decree. Most people, blinded by arrogance, worldly attachments, or tribal loyalties, would refuse to believe. This was not a failure on the Prophet’s part, nor was it a defect in the message. The story of Joseph itself illustrated this reality: Joseph was a prophet with clear signs, yet he was rejected by his own brothers and wrongly imprisoned by society for years before the truth prevailed. The verse serves to console the Prophet and to lift the burden of excessive grief from his shoulders. A caller’s duty is to convey the message with sincerity and wisdom; the outcome rests with Allah alone. This timeless lesson is vital for every believer who invites others to the truth. One must care deeply, but one must not be crushed when many reject the call, for the guidance of hearts is in Allah’s hands.

=104=

The verse continues the theme of the people’s rejection by highlighting an additional fact that should have removed any suspicion about the Prophet’s motives: he asked for no material reward whatsoever. “Even though you are not asking them for a reward for this Quran” points to the complete selflessness of the Prophet’s mission. In a society where poets, soothsayers, and leaders sought wealth, status, or influence, Muhammad came with a message that demanded nothing of their worldly possessions. This should have been a clear indicator that he was not an imposter seeking personal gain. The absence of any financial motive dismantles one of the common accusations against prophets—that they fabricate revelations to acquire wealth or power. The Prophet lived a life of poverty and simplicity, bearing the heavy costs of his mission himself.

The verse then declares the universal purpose of the message: “It is only a reminder to the whole world.” The Quran is a dhikr, a reminder that awakens the innate human knowledge of the Creator and the moral truths embedded in the soul. It is not restricted to a single tribe or nation but is sent as a mercy and a guide for all of humanity. This universality is a central feature of the Prophet’s message. The Meccan polytheists were not being asked to enrich a claimant to prophethood; they were simply being invited to a remembrance that would purify their souls and connect them to the Lord of all the worlds. Their rejection, therefore, was not against a man but against the universal truth meant for their own benefit.

=105=

Allah now turns the reader’s attention from the revealed signs in the story of Joseph to the cosmic signs that surround every human being. The verse asks, “How many signs in the heavens and the earth do they pass by with indifference!

” The word “signs” translates ayat, the same term used for verses of the Quran. The created universe is a book of divine signs that parallels the written revelation. The heavens, with their vastness, their precise celestial orbits, the sun, the moon, and the stars, all declare the power and wisdom of the Creator. The earth, with its mountains, oceans, rivers, and the cycle of life and death, provides endless testimony to a Sustaining Lord.

Yet the disbelievers walk past these signs every day “with indifference.” The Arabic term mu‘ridun implies a deliberate turning away, an active refusal to reflect. They look at the sky as a mere backdrop, the earth as mere ground, without pondering who brought them into existence and sustains them. This indifference is the root cause of their disbelief. If they would only pause and reflect on the intricate design in a single leaf, the water cycle that sustains life, or the vast constellations above them, they would be led inevitably to the knowledge of the One Creator. The verse is a powerful call to the believers and all humans to cultivate a state of mindful observation, to not be like those who live in a universe full of miracles but see none of them. The disbelievers of Mecca were not being asked to believe in a fairy tale; the evidence was woven into the fabric of their daily existence, yet they chose to look away.

=106=

This verse delivers a profound diagnosis of the spiritual condition of the Quraysh and, by extension, of much of humanity. It says, “And most of them do not believe in Allah without associating others with Him in worship.” The Meccan polytheists were not atheists; they affirmed the existence of Allah as the Supreme Creator and Sustainer. The Quran itself records in other places that if you asked them who created the heavens and the earth, they would say “Allah. ” However, their belief in Allah was polluted by shirk, the association of partners with Him. They worshipped idols, angels, and jinn as intermediaries, claiming these beings would bring them closer to Allah. Their belief was not a pure, exclusive monotheism but a muddled mixture of acknowledgment of the Creator and the worship of created things. This verse highlights that mere belief in the existence of God is not sufficient for salvation; what is required is tawhid, the uncompromising worship of Allah alone without any partners. The statement “and most of them do not believe” indicates that those who maintain pure monotheism are a minority across all times. The lesson is sobering: it is possible to profess belief in God and yet be a disbeliever in the true sense if that belief is tainted by shirk. This was the central message of every prophet, including Joseph in the prison when he called to the worship of the One, the Supreme. The verse serves as a warning to all believers to constantly examine their hearts for hidden forms of shirk, whether in obvious idol worship or in subtler attachments of the heart that rival the love and obedience due only to Allah.

=107=

Having described the polytheists’ state of indifference and their mixed belief, the Quran now delivers a sharp rhetorical question that shakes the complacent heart. “Do they feel secure that an overwhelming torment from Allah will not overtake them?” The security they feel is a false comfort, a deceptive peace rooted in their neglect of the Hereafter.

The word “overwhelming” translates ghashiyah, which means an all-enveloping punishment that covers and consumes everything, leaving no escape. This could refer to a sudden punishment in this world, like the destruction that came upon the peoples of the past, or the comprehensive torment of the Hereafter.

The verse then adds a second, even more urgent possibility: “or that the Hour will not take them by surprise when they least expect it?” The Hour, the Day of Judgment, is the ultimate reality toward which all existence is moving. Its exact timing is hidden, and one of its characteristics is that it will come suddenly, without prior warning for those who are heedless. People go about their daily lives—eating, trading, sleeping—and in an instant, the trumpet is blown, and the universe as they know it collapses. The polytheists who mock the Prophet’s warnings and feel secure in their worldly routines are living in a state of grave peril. This verse is a direct call to urgency. It teaches that one must never feel immune from divine punishment or delay preparation for the Hereafter. The only true security lies in repentance, faith, and constant mindfulness of Allah, not in the false sense of safety that a comfortable worldly life can provide.

=108=

After the warnings and the reflections on human indifference, Allah commands the Prophet to make a clear, confident declaration of his mission and methodology. “Say, O Prophet, ‘This is my way.’” The word “way” translates sabil, a path that is clear and distinct. The Prophet is to define his mission precisely, leaving no ambiguity. He says, “I invite to Allah with insight. ” The term “insight” renders basirah, which means clear evidence, profound understanding, and vision based on certain knowledge. The Prophet’s call is not blind or emotional; it is grounded in irrefutable signs, both in revelation and in creation, and it engages the intellect and the heart with wisdom. He then includes his followers in this noble declaration: “I and those who follow me.” This elevates the status of the believers, showing that every person who accepts the message and adheres to it becomes a participant in the great mission of inviting others to Allah. The way of the Prophet is not just for one individual but is the collective path of the entire Muslim community. The call is then sealed with two fundamental statements.

The first is “Glory be to Allah,” a declaration of tanzih, affirming that Allah is utterly free from any imperfection, partner, or flaw that the polytheists attribute to Him. The second is “and I am not one of the polytheists. ” This is a complete dissociation from shirk, a declaration of pure monotheism that leaves no room for compromise. This verse is a beautiful summary of the dawah methodology: a call based on knowledge, delivered with clarity, rooted in the glorification of Allah, and completely free from any association of partners with Him. It provides every believer with the template for how to present Islam to the world.

=109=

This verse continues the address to the Prophet and to the disbelievers by placing Muhammad’s mission within the long chain of prophethood. Allah says, “We only sent before you O Prophet men inspired by Us from among the people of each society.” This clarifies that all previous messengers were human beings, not angels or supernatural beings. They were men chosen from among their own communities, speaking the language of their people, sharing their experiences, and being living examples of the message they taught.

The word “inspired” confirms that revelation was the source of their knowledge. This refutes the Quraysh’s objection that a mere mortal could not be a messenger. If all previous messengers were human, why should the final messenger be any different?

The verse then poses a rhetorical question: “Have the deniers not travelled through the land to see what was the end of those destroyed before them? ” The ruins of past civilizations—‘Ad, Thamud, the people of Lot—lay along their trade routes. These physical remnants are historical proof of divine punishment falling upon those who rejected their messengers. Travel and observation should lead to reflection and lesson-drawing. Then Allah directs the gaze to the ultimate truth: “And surely the eternal Home of the Hereafter is far better for those mindful of Allah. ” The temporary pleasures and powers of this world are nothing compared to the everlasting bliss of Paradise reserved for those who have taqwa.

The final plea, “Will you not then understand?” is a call to the intellect. The evidence from history, the nature of prophethood, and the logic of the Hereafter all point to the truth. This verse masterfully combines reason, historical observation, and spiritual promise to invite the listener to a mature, informed faith.

=110=

This verse is a direct source of immense comfort for the Prophet and the struggling believers of Mecca. It describes the pattern of divine deliverance that occurs at the moment of greatest human helplessness. “And when the messengers despaired” refers to the point when the prophets, after centuries of persistent calling, faced such relentless rejection that humanly speaking, all hope of their people’s mass conversion was lost.

The word “despaired” indicates a state where, by all worldly calculations, the mission seemed to have failed. Then the verse adds, “and their people thought the messengers had been denied help.” The disbelievers, seeing the prophets’ small following and lack of worldly power, became convinced that the promised divine help was a lie and that the messengers were abandoned. At this precise moment of maximum darkness, when both the prophets and their opponents thought the situation was settled in favour of disbelief, the divine decree shifted: “Our help came to them at last.

” The word “at last” captures the dramatic, sudden reversal. Allah’s help arrived at the perfect time, shattering the illusion of the disbelievers and rescuing the prophets and their followers.

The verse continues, “We then saved whoever We willed,” meaning the believers who held on to faith were delivered, while “Our punishment is never averted from the wicked people.” The criminals, those who persisted in their denial and oppression, were inevitably seized. This verse was a prophecy and a promise for the Prophet Muhammad. The Quraysh were reaching the peak of their arrogance, and the Muslims were under severe persecution. The verse assured them that the pattern of Joseph—who was saved from the well and the prison at the perfect moment—would repeat itself on a larger scale. Victory comes with patience, and the punishment of the unrepentant wicked is a certainty that no force can turn back.

=111=

The surah concludes with a powerful summary of why these stories are narrated and a final affirmation of the Quran’s nature. “In their stories there is truly a lesson for people of reason.” The “stories” refer to the accounts of Joseph and his brothers, and by extension the narratives of all the prophets.

The word “lesson” translates ibrâ, which means an admonition that causes one to cross over from ignorance to understanding, from heedlessness to mindfulness. The phrase “for people of reason” specifies that the benefits of these stories are unlocked by those who possess albab, the core intellects that penetrate beyond surface events to the underlying truths. The stories are not entertainment; they are case studies in faith, patience, morality, and divine wisdom.

The verse then defends the Quran against the accusation of being a fabrication: “This message cannot be a fabrication.” The coherence of the narrative, the precision of the details, and the fact that it was produced by an unlettered Prophet who had no access to these historical records, all testify to its divine origin. It is instead “a confirmation of previous revelation,” aligning perfectly with the core truths sent to Moses, David, and Jesus. It is “a detailed explanation of all things,” meaning it provides clarity on every matter essential for human guidance in faith, ethics, law, and spirituality. It is “a guide” that shows the straight path, and “a mercy for people of faith,” for only those who embrace it with faith will taste the full sweetness of its guidance. This closing verse ties the entire surah back to its opening, where the Quran was introduced as the clear Book, and it invites every listener to approach these narratives with reason and an open heart, so that the stories of the past become a living light for the present and the future.
