=21=

After the call to travel and observe the evidence of creation’s origin and resurrection, this verse lays down the inevitable consequence of that return: the exercise of divine judgment. The twin statements “He punishes whoever He wills, and shows mercy to whoever He wills” might initially seem to suggest an arbitrary decision.

However, in the broader Quranic context, Allah’s will is always inseparably tied to His perfect wisdom and justice. The punishment falls on those who have earned it through persistent denial, oppression, and corruption, while mercy embraces those who respond to guidance, repent, and seek His forgiveness.

The pairing of punishment and mercy reflects the balance of divine attributes: He is severe in punishment but also infinitely merciful. No human being can dictate to Allah who should be punished or who should be shown mercy, because His knowledge alone encompasses the hidden realities of hearts and the full weight of every deed. The verse makes clear that the final allocation of these outcomes is entirely His prerogative. Then the verse closes with a powerful affirmation that pulls the listener back to personal accountability: “And you will all be returned to Him. ” The return is universal, embracing the believer and the disbeliever, the oppressor and the oppressed. This return is the meeting mentioned earlier in the surah, the appointed time that is sure to come.

The mention of return immediately after punishment and mercy reminds us that no one escapes this return; every single human being will stand before the One who decides their fate with absolute justice. This verse thus instills both fear and hope. Fear of His punishment for those who turn away, and hope in His mercy for those who strive. It also subtly corrects the earlier error of those who mistake persecution for divine punishment, by affirming that only Allah decides what is punishment and what is mercy, and He has the full picture.

=22=

Following the declaration that all will return to Allah, this verse eliminates any illusion of escape. The phrase “you cannot escape Him on earth or in heaven” uses the vastness of space to illustrate the absolute impossibility of fleeing from divine authority. In pre-Islamic Arabia, people might have thought that hiding in remote deserts or mountains could keep them safe from harm, but there is no geographical or even cosmic location beyond Allah’s reach. Even if one could ascend into the heavens, they would still be within His dominion. The verse strips away every false sense of security. It directly challenges the mindset of the Meccan polytheists who, despite their belief in Allah as the Creator of the heavens and earth as later verses will confirm, acted as if they could avoid accountability by ignoring the Prophet’s warning.

The second part, “Nor have you any protector or helper besides Allah,” closes off another avenue of false hope. The disbelievers used to rely on their idols, their wealth, their tribal alliances, and their social status as protectors. Here Allah declares that none of these can offer true protection or help against His will. The words “protector” and “helper” cover both defense from harm and active assistance in times of need. In the ultimate sense, only Allah can provide either. This verse therefore realigns the believer’s trust: reliance must be placed solely on Allah, not on any human or imagined power. For the persecuted believers in Mecca, this was a profound comfort. Their oppressors could not escape divine justice, nor could they find any helper to shield them from its execution.

The verse also prepares the heart for the story of Abraham, who relied on Allah alone when his people tried to kill him, and who found in Allah the only protector he needed.

=23=

The surah now states the condition that leads to the terrible loss described in the previous verses. The specific group identified are “those who disbelieve in Allah’s signs and the meeting with Him.” Disbelief in the signs includes the revealed verses of the Quran, the natural evidence of creation discussed just a few verses earlier, and the historical lessons of destroyed nations. To reject these signs is to shut the door on every avenue that could lead to faith. Disbelief in the meeting with Allah compounds this rejection by denying the very purpose of life and the reality of accountability. The consequence is devastating: “it is they who will have no hope in His mercy.” The wording indicates that they have cut themselves off from divine mercy entirely. Hope in Allah’s mercy is the lifeline of a believer; it is what drives repentance, encourages good deeds, and provides light in the darkest trials. To lose that hope is to be spiritually dead even before physical death.

The verse then seals their fate: “And it is they who will suffer a painful punishment.” The pain is described as continuous and deeply felt, not just physical but also the torment of regret and hopelessness.

This verse addresses a misconception that can arise from the earlier mention of Allah punishing and showing mercy to whom He wills. The will of Allah is not random; it is linked to the choices people make. Those who persistently reject His signs and deny the Hereafter have chosen the path that leads to the withdrawal of mercy. It also serves as a warning to the Meccans that their mockery of the resurrection and their demand to see the punishment would, if they died in that state, end in a situation where no hope remains. The verse connects directly to the preceding stories of destroyed nations, who also disbelieved and found no escape.

=24=

Returning to the narrative of Abraham that began earlier in the surah, this verse describes the climax of his confrontation with his people. After Abraham demolished their idols and presented clear arguments for monotheism, their reply was not intellectual.

The phrase “the only response” indicates that they had no logical counter-argument. Instead, they resorted to violence: “Kill him or burn him! ” Their call to burn him was a collective decision to make an example of him in the most public and painful way, hoping to terrify anyone else who might follow his path. According to the historical accounts, they gathered an enormous quantity of wood, lit a massive fire, and catapulted Abraham into it because the flames were too intense to approach.

Yet the verse immediately reveals the divine intervention: “But Allah saved him from the fire.” Allah commanded the fire, “O fire, be coolness and peace upon Abraham,” and it became a place of safety rather than harm. This miraculous deliverance is a central sign in Abraham’s story. The conclusion, “Surely in this are signs for people who believe,” draws out the lesson. The signs are manifold: the futility of human power when opposed to divine will, the protection that Allah grants His close servants, the fact that causes like fire only operate by Allah’s permission, and the ultimate victory of truth over falsehood. For the believers in Mecca, this was a source of immense reassurance. They too faced threats of violence and death; the story of Abraham reminded them that the same Lord who saved Abraham from the fire could protect them or, even if they were to be martyred, grant them a far greater salvation. The signs are only perceived by those who have faith, because only faith opens the heart to see beyond material causes.

=25=

After being saved, Abraham does not remain silent. He continues to advise his people, exposing the true nature of their idolatry. He says, “You have taken idols for worship instead of Allah, only to keep the bond of harmony among yourselves in this worldly life.

” This is a profound psychological analysis of why polytheism thrives. The idols served as a social glue, a shared tradition that united families and tribes around common rituals and interests. Their worship was not based on truth, but on convenience and social cohesion. Abraham points out that this harmony is merely temporary, confined to the worldly life.

The phrase “only to keep the bond of harmony” indicates that their true object of reverence was not the stone statues but the social order they maintained. Then he draws back the curtain on the ultimate outcome: “But on the Day of Judgment you will disown and curse one another.” The very relationships that held them together in this life will shatter. Leaders and followers, friends and allies, will deny each other and heap blame, each trying to escape responsibility. This truth is echoed elsewhere in the Quran where it says that close friends will become enemies on that Day, except the righteous. Abraham then delivers the final verdict: “Your home will be the Fire, and you will have no helper!” The false protectors, the idols and the social bonds, will be utterly useless. This verse serves as a timeless warning against placing social conformity above truth. It challenges the believer to maintain faith even when it strains relationships, because the worldly harmony built on falsehood is fleeting, while the consequences of compromising truth are eternal. For the early Meccan Muslims whose families were pressuring them to return to idol worship, this verse was a direct answer: your family bonds will not save anyone on Judgment Day if they are built on shirk.

=26=

In the midst of widespread rejection, the verse highlights a bright exception: “So Lot believed in him.” Lot, Abraham’s nephew, was the one person in that idolatrous society who responded to the call of truth. This mention reminds us that even when the majority refuses, there are often individuals whose hearts are open, and their faith is immensely valuable. The early Muslims, who were few in Mecca, could take comfort in the fact that Abraham, one of the greatest prophets, also had only a small number of followers at first. Immediately after this, Abraham makes a decisive announcement: “I am emigrating in obedience to my Lord.” Emigration, or hijrah, is a physical separation from a land of disbelief and persecution to a place where faith can be practiced freely. Abraham’s emigration was from Ur in Mesopotamia to the land of Canaan. He did not leave out of fear or despair, but specifically “in obedience to my Lord. ” This reframes emigration as an act of worship, not merely a survival strategy. He then declares the attributes of the One for whose sake he is migrating: “He alone is indeed the Almighty, All-Wise.

” The name “Almighty” assures that Allah has the power to protect and provide for the emigrant, replacing whatever they left behind with something better. The name “All-Wise” assures that the command to leave, even when it seems difficult, is rooted in perfect wisdom that the believer may not fully grasp at the moment. This verse served as a direct precursor to the eventual hijrah of the Prophet and his companions to Medina. It also laid the foundation for the later verse in this same surah, “O My believing servants! My earth is truly spacious, so worship Me alone,” which calls persecuted believers to migrate if necessary. Abraham’s emigration is a model of trust and complete submission, demonstrating that faith sometimes requires leaving behind homeland and family for the sake of Allah.

=27=

This verse catalogues the extraordinary blessings that followed Abraham’s sacrifice and emigration. After he left his homeland, Allah “blessed him with Isaac and later Jacob.” Isaac was the son granted to him in his old age, whose birth was announced by the angels as a miracle. Jacob was Isaac’s son, Abraham’s grandson.

The mention of Jacob specifically shows that the blessing extended to the next generation, fulfilling the prayer of Abraham for a righteous lineage. Even more magnificent is the spiritual legacy: “and reserved prophethood and revelation for his descendants.” This means that the great chain of prophets among the Children of Israel—including Joseph, Moses, David, Solomon, and many others, all the way to Jesus—came from Abraham’s line.

Additionally, from his son Ishmael came the final Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. Thus, almost all of prophetic history flows from this one man who left his homeland in obedience to Allah.

The verse also states, “We gave him his reward in this life.” This reward included a praiseworthy mention that endures across nations and generations; he is revered by Muslims, Jews, and Christians. It also encompassed prosperity in his new land and the joy of a faithful family. Then, looking beyond this world: “and in the Hereafter he will certainly be among the righteous.

” This is the ultimate success. The phrase “among the righteous” places him in the highest company, the company of the prophets and the most sincere worshippers.

The word “certainly” removes any doubt. The verse teaches that true sacrifice for Allah never leads to loss; it opens doors to blessings that the human mind could never have anticipated. For the believers facing loss of family and property due to their faith, Abraham’s story was a guarantee that Allah’s compensation is infinitely greater.

=28=

The narrative now moves from Abraham to his nephew Lot, whom Allah sent to the people of Sodom. The command “remember” draws the Prophet’s attention and, through him, the attention of the believers to this story as a warning and a lesson. Lot’s mission was to a society that had sunk into unprecedented depravity. His rebuke begins with strong emphasis: “You certainly commit a shameful deed. ” The word “shameful” translates fāḥishah, which in the Quran denotes an act so repulsive that it goes beyond all bounds of decency. Lot then specifies the historical uniqueness of their crime: “that no man has ever done before you.” This indicates that the sin of homosexual intercourse as a widespread, normalised practice had no precedent in human history until that time. It was not merely individual deviance but a societal norm that they openly practiced and defended. The verse here focuses on the sexual aspect of their corruption, which will be elaborated upon in the next verse. Lot’s role as a warner was to confront this evil head-on, despite being a stranger in that land, having migrated there with Abraham. His courage in speaking out against an entire culture’s pervasive sin is a model for speaking truth even when it isolates one from society.

The verse also serves a clear function within the surah’s overall message: just as Abraham’s people were destroyed for idolatry, Lot’s people were destroyed for sexual transgression. Both forms of corruption brought down divine punishment, and both serve as warnings to the Meccans and to all who would follow similar paths. The historical claim that this deed had never been done before is significant, as it refutes any attempt to normalise it by claiming it has always been part of human societies; the Quran draws a line at a specific moment when a boundary was first crossed.

=29=

Lot continues his rebuke by listing three specific, interconnected evils. The first and most prominent is, “Do you really lust after other men,” which explicitly names the homosexual acts that defined their society. The interrogative form “Do you really” expresses shock and moral outrage, as if to ask how they could have sunk so low.

The second crime is “abuse the travellers. ” The people of Sodom would waylay travellers who passed through their territory, robbing them and, according to many commentators, subjecting them to sexual assault. This added the sin of violating the sacred code of hospitality to their depravity, preying upon vulnerable strangers who had no local protection.

The third is “practice immorality openly in your gatherings.” Their obscene behaviour was not done in private shame; they boasted about it and performed lewd acts in public assemblies, turning their social spaces into arenas of shamelessness. This open flouting of moral boundaries showed that their corruption was not just in action but in their hearts, where the sense of right and wrong had been completely inverted. Their response to Lot’s sincere warning reveals the height of their arrogance: “His people’s only response was to say mockingly: ‘Bring Allah’s punishment upon us, if what you say is true.’” They did not engage his arguments. They did not deny their actions. Instead, they challenged him with sarcasm, demanding that the very punishment he warned about be brought down immediately.

This is the behaviour of a people who feel invincible and who treat divine threats as empty tales. Their mockery echoes the attitude of the Meccan pagans who also challenged the Prophet to hasten the punishment. The verse paints a vivid picture of a civilisation at the point of no return, where truth is met with ridicule and the call to righteousness is dismissed with a dare.

=30=

After his people answered his sincere warning with mockery and a challenge to bring down divine punishment, Lot turned entirely to Allah. His prayer is short but deeply loaded: “My Lord! Help me against the people of corruption.” The direct address “My Lord” shows his personal, intimate connection with Allah in a moment of complete vulnerability.

The word “help” translates a plea for victory, deliverance, and decisive support. He did not ask for a specific form of help; he entrusted the matter entirely to Allah’s wisdom. The description of his people as “the people of corruption” encapsulates their entire state. Their corruption was not a minor flaw but a comprehensive rot that had infected their beliefs, their sexual behaviour, their treatment of others, and their public discourse. By calling them a people of corruption, Lot was highlighting that the very fabric of their society had become a source of fasad (mischief and decay) on the earth. This prayer represents the point at which a prophet, after exhausting all efforts at invitation and warning, hands the matter over to Allah. It is not an act of impatience but of fulfilling one’s responsibility and then relying on divine judgment. For the Prophet Muhammad and the early believers, this prayer was a model. They too were facing mockery and demands for punishment. The lesson is that when a people refuse to listen and instead challenge Allah, the believer’s refuge is to turn to Allah in supplication, seeking His help while leaving the timing and manner of the response to Him. Lot’s prayer was answered, as the subsequent verses show, with the arrival of the angels who brought both destruction for the corrupt and salvation for the righteous. This verse thus marks the transition from warning to the execution of divine decree.

=31=

The scene shifts back to Abraham, tying the two prophetic missions together. The angels, described as “Our messenger-angels,” visited Abraham in human form before proceeding to Lot. They came with a dual purpose. First, they delivered “the good news of the birth of Isaac,” bringing joy to Abraham and his wife Sarah, who was beyond childbearing age. This good news confirmed the earlier promise of a righteous progeny. But then they revealed their other, graver mission: “We are going to destroy the people of this city of Sodom, for its people have persisted in wrongdoing.” The city is not named in the verse itself, but the context clearly identifies it as Sodom, Lot’s city. The angels explain the reason for the impending destruction: persistence in wrongdoing.

The word “persisted” indicates that their evil was not occasional or regretted, but continuous, entrenched, and unrepentant. They had crossed a line where divine punishment became inevitable. The juxtaposition of the good news of Isaac’s birth with the bad news of Sodom’s destruction is striking. It illustrates how closely divine mercy and divine justice are intertwined. In the same moment, Allah blessed Abraham with the glad tidings of a righteous son and decreed the annihilation of a corrupt civilisation. This teaches that the rise of goodness and the fall of evil are both under Allah’s control and often unfold simultaneously. For the believers in Mecca, this scene provided perspective: while they were being persecuted and the disbelievers appeared to thrive, the same divine power that was preserving the believing line through Isaac was preparing to wipe out the corrupt society of Sodom. The angels’ calm announcement also underscores that the destruction of a city is a simple, decisive act for Allah’s servants to carry out by His command.

=32=

Upon hearing the angels’ announcement about destroying the city, Abraham’s immediate concern was for his righteous nephew. His interjection, “But Lot is there!” reveals the merciful and compassionate heart of a prophet. He was not questioning divine justice but appealing to the fact that a righteous servant lived among those doomed people. Abraham’s concern parallels the instinct of every believer who worries that the innocent might be swept away with the guilty. The angels’ response is gentle yet firm: “We know best who is there.” They assure Abraham that their knowledge is perfect and that no injustice will occur. Their mission is precise; they do not operate on blanket destruction without discrimination. This phrase also carries an important theological point: Allah’s knowledge of individuals is complete, and His judgment never mistakenly punishes a righteous person for the sins of others. Then they give Abraham the specific assurance: “We will certainly save him and his family—except his wife, who is one of the doomed.” The exception of Lot’s wife is significant. Although she lived in the household of a prophet, her heart was with the corrupt people. She was not saved because salvation is not based on family connection but on personal faith and righteousness. In the Quran, she is described as betraying Lot, not in a sexual sense, but by sympathising with the evildoers and possibly alerting them to the arrival of guests. Her fate demonstrates that even the closest proximity to a prophet cannot protect a person who chooses disbelief. This was a powerful lesson for the Meccans, including relatives of the Prophet who rejected his message. Lineage and association do not determine one’s standing with Allah; individual faith and deeds do.

=33=

The angels now arrive at Sodom in the form of extraordinarily handsome young men. Lot, who was a stranger in the land and upheld a standard of righteousness, immediately felt “distressed and worried by their arrival.” His distress stemmed from knowing the wicked nature of his people. He feared that they would attack these guests and that he would be unable to protect them. His worry was not for his own safety but for the honour and security of those under his roof. This shows Lot’s deep sense of responsibility and his acute suffering living among such a corrupt people. The angels, perceiving his inner turmoil, reassured him with the command: “Do not fear, nor grieve.” The fear was about the immediate danger from his people; the grief was about the larger burden of witnessing their evil and his own helplessness. Both emotions were addressed by the angels’ promise: “We will surely deliver you and your family—except your wife, who is one of the doomed.

” This promise directly mirrors the one given to Abraham, confirming the consistency of the divine plan. The exception of his wife is repeated, underscoring that even within a prophet’s household, salvation is based on individual merit. The verse captures a moment of intense human anxiety transformed into reassurance by divine communication. For the early believers, this scene was deeply relatable. They too felt distress and worry living in a hostile society, and this verse reminded them that Allah’s help comes with precise timing, often when the pressure seems unbearable. The command to not fear or grieve is a timeless balm for every believer facing overwhelming odds.

=34=

The angels now reveal the specific nature of the coming destruction. The phrase “We are certainly bringing down” conveys absolute certainty and deliberateness. The punishment is described as “from heaven,” indicating that it would descend upon them from above, leaving them no means of escape or defense. In the fuller account of this story in other surahs, such as Surah Hūd, we learn that the punishment involved turning the city upside down and raining upon it stones of baked clay, marked by Allah. The cause is stated succinctly: “for their rebelliousness.

” The Arabic term used conveys the idea of fisq, a willful transgression that goes beyond mere error into a state of moral corruption and defiance. The people of Sodom had not merely slipped into sin; they had embraced and institutionalised it, making rebellion against divine order the hallmark of their society.

The word “rebelliousness” ties their sexual perversion, their mistreatment of travellers, and their mockery of Lot’s call together as manifestations of a single underlying disease: refusal to submit to Allah’s authority. This verse reinforces the surah’s central theme that punishment is not arbitrary; it is a direct response to persistent, unrepented wrongdoing.

The mention that the punishment is “from heaven” also refutes the idea that natural disasters are purely random events. In the Quranic worldview, such events, when they serve as divine punishment, are instruments of Allah’s justice. The certainty with which the angels speak leaves no room for negotiation or delay. The decree had been issued, and the execution was imminent. For the Meccans, this was a chilling warning. They too engaged in rebelliousness and challenged the Prophet to bring down punishment from heaven; the story of Sodom showed exactly what such a challenge, if answered, would mean.

=35=

After recounting the destruction, Allah points to a lasting effect: the ruins of Sodom and the surrounding cities were left as a visible sign. The phrase “We did leave some of its ruins” indicates that the remains were not entirely erased from the earth.

Historically, the area around the Dead Sea has been identified with the site of these destroyed cities. The ruins served and continue to serve as “a clear lesson. ” The Arabic word āyah, meaning sign or lesson, appears again, connecting this physical remnant to the broader pattern of signs in creation and history that the surah repeatedly mentions. The lesson is specifically for “people of understanding,” those who use their intellect to reflect on what they see rather than passing by heedlessly. The ruins speak silently of what happens when a society abandons all moral boundaries and defiantly rejects the warnings of a prophet sent to them. They are a tangible proof that the Quran’s warnings are not abstract myths but are grounded in historical realities that can be observed. For the Meccan traders who travelled north towards Syria, the route passed near this area. They could see the desolate landscape and hear the stories of the destroyed cities. This verse, then, was not asking them to believe in something unseen but to reflect upon evidence they could witness with their own eyes.

The phrase “people of understanding” is a recurring Quranic compliment; it distinguishes those who draw conclusions from those who merely see. It also ties into the surah’s emphasis on knowledge and reasoning. The destruction of Sodom was not just a past event; it was a living sermon embedded in the earth, inviting every generation to learn and avoid the same path.

=36=

The surah continues its historical survey with the story of Shu’aib, who was sent to the people of Midian, a community that lived in the northwestern part of Arabia and was known for its commercial activities. The phrase “their brother Shu’aib” highlights that he was one of them, from their own lineage and culture, so they had no excuse of a foreign messenger. His message begins with the foundational call of all prophets: “Worship Allah.

” This is the core of tawhid, directing all worship and devotion to the One God alone. Then he adds a specific instruction: “and hope for the reward of the Last Day.

” This is noteworthy because faith in the Hereafter motivates moral behaviour and honest dealing in this world. It provides a horizon of accountability that extends beyond immediate profit or loss. Shu’aib’s third command is “And do not go about spreading corruption in the land. ” The people of Midian were notorious for two major corrupt practices, as detailed in other surahs: cheating in weights and measures, and waylaying travellers on the roads. Their corruption was primarily economic and social. By cheating their customers, they spread a culture of dishonesty that eroded trust and justice. By terrorising travellers, they spread fear and insecurity. Shu’aib’s call thus addressed the specific diseases of his society, but the principles apply universally. Spreading corruption can take many forms: financial fraud, environmental destruction, spreading harmful ideologies, or undermining social peace.

The verse shows that true worship of Allah must be accompanied by hope in the Hereafter, which in turn must produce ethical conduct in society. Worship cannot be compartmentalised away from business and public life. For the Meccan traders, who also engaged in commerce and who sometimes dealt unjustly with orphans and the weak, Shu’aib’s message was a direct mirror.

=37=

The response of the people of Midian was, like many nations before them, rejection. The verse succinctly states, “But they rejected him.” They did not merely disbelieve privately; they actively denied Shu’aib’s message and presumably threatened him and his followers, as other passages indicate. Then the punishment came: “so an overwhelming earthquake struck them. ” The Arabic term for earthquake here is rajfah, which denotes a violent shaking or tremor that leaves nothing stable. It was specifically targeted at them, a precise act of divine retribution. The result was that “they fell lifeless in their homes.” The phrasing paints a grim picture: they were going about their daily lives, inside their dwellings, perhaps feeling secure and mocking the prophet’s warnings, when the ground beneath them convulsed and they collapsed, dead where they stood or sat. Their homes, which should have been places of safety, became their graves. This mirrors the fate of other nations mentioned in the surah and emphasises the suddenness and completeness of the punishment. There was no escape, no time to repent at the last moment. The verse stands as a stark warning against the illusion of security that comes from material structures and worldly routines. It also highlights a recurring Quranic theme: the punishment often matches the nature of the crime. The people of Midian shook the foundations of social justice by cheating and spreading corruption, so the very earth they walked and built upon shook them to death. For the early believers, this was a reminder that rejection of truth and economic oppression carry severe consequences, and that Allah’s justice may be delayed but is never abandoned.

=38=

The survey of destroyed nations continues with two more famous Arabian tribes: ʿĀd and Thamūd. The verse says they “met a similar fate,” linking their destruction to those already mentioned. Then it addresses the Meccans directly: “which must be clear to you from their ruins.” The ruins of ʿĀd and Thamūd were located in the Arabian Peninsula, along the trade routes that the Quraysh travelled regularly. The Meccans saw the remains of their sophisticated dwellings carved into mountains and built on the plains. They knew the stories of these powerful, advanced civilisations that were wiped out. The verse thus uses their own geographical knowledge as evidence. Then it reveals the spiritual mechanism behind their downfall: “Satan made their evil deeds appealing to them. ” This is a profound insight into the psychology of sin. Evil rarely presents itself as ugly; Satan beautifies it so that it appears desirable, reasonable, or even virtuous. The people of ʿĀd and Thamūd saw their arrogance, their luxurious building, their worship of false gods as marks of success and sophistication, not as crimes. This beautification “hindered them from the Right Way. ” The path of truth was visible, but their perception was so skewed that they could not see it or would not choose it.

The verse adds a crucial qualifier: “although they were capable of reasoning.” This negates any excuse of ignorance or lack of mental capacity. They had intelligence, they could think, but they allowed Satan’s beautification to override their reason.

This is a warning for every intelligent person: intellectual capacity alone does not guarantee guidance. One must also have the humility to submit reason to revelation and the moral clarity to resist the seductive packaging of sin. The verse is a direct lesson for the Meccans, whose own intelligence was being used to devise arguments against the Prophet rather than to recognise the truth.

=39=

The historical survey now moves to the well-known figures from the time of Moses. Korah (Qārūn) was an Israelite of immense wealth who became arrogant and oppressive, ultimately swallowed by the earth along with his treasures. Pharaoh was the tyrannical ruler of Egypt who claimed divinity and enslaved the Children of Israel. Hamān was his chief minister who aided in the oppression and in building a tower to reach the heavens in defiance of Allah. The verse groups these three because they represent different forms of transgression—wealth, political power, and administrative support—yet all shared the same arrogance.

The phrase “Moses had come to them with clear proofs” highlights that they were not left without guidance. Moses brought undeniable signs: the staff turning into a serpent, the shining hand, the splitting of the sea, and other miracles. But their response was not humility; “they behaved arrogantly in the land. ” Arrogance (istikbār) is the refusal to accept truth because of a sense of self-importance. Korah’s arrogance was rooted in his wealth, Pharaoh’s in his kingdom, and Hamān’s in his proximity to power. Despite all their might, the verse declares, “Yet they could not escape Us.” None of their resources, fortresses, armies, or treasures could protect them from divine seizure. Pharaoh drowned, Korah was swallowed by the earth, and Hamān perished with his master.

This verse addresses another facet of Meccan opposition. The leaders of Quraysh, like Pharaoh and his court, were wealthy and powerful, and they behaved arrogantly toward the Prophet, a man they considered socially inferior. The verse warns them that their wealth and status are not shields against Allah’s decree. It also reassures the believers that no matter how invincible the oppressors seem, they remain completely within the grip of divine power.

=40=

The surah now summarises the diverse modes of destruction that befell the various nations mentioned, weaving them into a single panoramic scene of divine justice. “So We seized each people for their sin” emphasises that every punishment was directly tied to specific transgressions, not arbitrary or collective.

The word “seized” suggests a sudden, firm grip from which there is no release. Then the verse lists the different forms: “against some of them We sent a storm of stones,” referring to the people of Lot upon whom a rain of baked clay stones descended. “Some were overtaken by a mighty blast,” which applies to ʿĀd and Thamūd and the people of Shuʿaib, who were struck by a deafening shout or an earthquake that left them motionless. “Some We caused the earth to swallow,” which refers to Korah and his dwelling, pulled down into the ground. “And some We drowned,” which applies to the people of Noah and to Pharaoh and his army. This catalogue demonstrates that Allah’s arsenal of punishment is not limited; He uses the elements of nature—wind, sound, earth, and water—as instruments of His will. Each group received a punishment suited to their context, and in many cases it was the very thing they felt most secure in that became their means of destruction.

The verse then concludes with a powerful theological statement: “Allah would not have wronged them, but it was they who wronged themselves.” This removes any doubt about divine injustice. Allah’s punishment is never an act of oppression; it is a consequence that people bring upon themselves through their own choices. They wronged themselves by denying the truth, by committing evil, and by mocking the warners. This final line is a key Quranic principle: every destruction is self-inflicted. For the Meccans and for all who hear this surah, the implication is clear: if they persist, their destruction will not be because Allah was unfair, but because they chose a path that leads inevitably to ruin.
