=1=

The surah opens with three disconnected Arabic letters: Alif, Lām, Mīm. These are among the mysterious openings that appear at the beginning of twenty-nine chapters of the Quran. The most authoritative position, held by the vast majority of the companions and early scholars, is that their full meaning rests with Allah alone.

Yet they immediately served a powerful function for the first listeners in Mecca. The Quran was revealed in the very language of the Quraysh, made up of these same letters they used every day. By starting with letters like Alif, Lām, Mīm and then following them with a challenge that they cannot produce anything like it, the Quran silently declares that its miraculous nature is woven from ordinary elements they know perfectly well but cannot imitate. This introduction prepares the reader for a surah that will test claims and separate truth from falsehood. The abrupt appearance of these letters also jolts the listener into paying close attention, much as a speaker might begin with a dramatic pause. They remind us that revelation is not a product of human intellect but comes from a source beyond our full comprehension.

In the context of this surah, which repeatedly speaks about trials, sincere faith versus hypocrisy, and the true nature of reliance upon Allah, these letters set a tone of deep seriousness. They tell the believer that what follows is not ordinary speech but a message that will demand everything from you. This opening humbles the mind and prepares the heart to receive the test described in the very next verse, where the claim of faith is immediately questioned.

Thus the disconnected letters work as a silent but profound gateway, confirming that the Quran’s origin is divine and that its guidance must be approached with humility and readiness to be tested.

=2=

This verse asks a rhetorical question that strikes at the heart of every believer’s assumption. When someone declares “We believe,” the phrase “Do people think” shows that there is a common human tendency to imagine that a verbal profession is enough. The expression “that they will be left” indicates abandonment or being left alone without any further demands. The early Muslim community in Mecca was facing increasing hostility from the Quraysh, and some new converts were experiencing severe persecution, making them wonder why suffering follows faith. This verse directly addresses that mindset. It explains that merely saying “We believe” does not earn one a life free of difficulty; rather, it is the very reason trials come.

The question form challenges the listener to examine their own assumption. The word “put to the test” translates the Arabic concept of fitnah, which involves being tried, tested, and purified like gold in fire. Faith is not a static label but a reality that must be proven under pressure. Throughout the Quran, this principle is reaffirmed: in Surah Al-Baqarah, Allah says He will certainly test believers with fear, hunger, and loss of wealth and lives. The hadith literature adds depth: the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, said that the people most severely tested are the prophets, then the next best, then the next best. A person is tested according to his religion. This verse, then, corrects a misunderstanding that can arise in every generation: that claiming belief is a safe harbour from hardship. It prepares believers for the stories of Noah, Abraham, Lot, and other prophets that follow later in the surah, showing that the path of truth has always been paved with trials. It also provides comfort: the test is not a sign of Allah’s displeasure but a means to distinguish the sincere from the insincere.

=3=

After questioning the assumption that faith brings a life without tests, Allah gives historical certainty. The phrase “We certainly tested those before them” connects the experience of the Meccan believers to the long chain of previous communities of faith. The testing is presented as a deliberate, established divine practice, not an accident or a sudden decision. The prophets and their followers before them—like the followers of Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus—all endured severe trials. This comforts believers because they realise they are not singled out for punishment; they are walking the same road as the righteous. Then the verse explains the divine purpose: “And in this way Allah will clearly distinguish between those who are truthful and those who are liars.

” The phrase “clearly distinguish” points to a separation that becomes evident to all. The original Arabic implies making something plainly manifest, leaving no ambiguity. The truthful are those whose inner reality matches their outward declaration when pressure comes. The liars are those whose claim of faith evaporates when it costs them comfort, security, or relationships.

This distinction is not just for Allah’s knowledge—He already knows what is in hearts—but it serves as a proof for the people themselves and for the community, and it establishes justice on the Day of Judgment. The companions of the Prophet experienced this distinction vividly: some stood firm during the boycott in Mecca and the torture, while others fell away. This verse warns that a mere claim will not benefit anyone if it is not verified by patience and perseverance. It also teaches believers not to be surprised when those they considered strong in faith turn away under hardship, because the trial reveals what was always hidden.

Ultimately, the verse anchors the concept of trial within divine wisdom: it is a refining process that sifts genuine belief from empty words, ensuring that the reward of the truthful is based on proven commitment.

=4=

This verse shifts the focus from the tested believers to the oppressors. The term “evildoers” refers to those who reject faith and actively persecute the believers, like the Meccan polytheists.

The question “Or do they think that they will escape Us?” directly challenges the arrogance of power. The word “escape” implies outrunning or getting beyond Allah’s reach. In Mecca, the disbelievers enjoyed economic strength, social dominance, and apparent safety while they mocked the Prophet and harmed his followers. A superficial observer might think that they were winning and that their denial of the truth carried no consequences. This verse shatters that illusion. The phrasing “How wrong is their judgment!” expresses strong condemnation of their reasoning. It tells us that their entire evaluation of reality is flawed because they measure outcomes only by this worldly life and assume that if punishment does not come immediately, it will never come.

This is a persistent human error. Many who commit injustice mistake the delay of consequences for the absence of accountability. The Quran elsewhere reminds that Allah grants respite to the wrongdoers, but His seizure, when it comes, is severe. The verse thus serves as a warning to oppressors in every era, not just the Quraysh. It also indirectly comforts the oppressed believers: the apparent victory of falsehood is temporary and based on a false calculation.

The earlier verses spoke about the believers being tested; this verse assures them that the test is not because Allah is unaware of the oppressors. On the contrary, the evildoers are under a complete divine watch, and their escape is impossible. The judgment they make—that they can harm the believers and deny the truth without facing any reckoning—is described as evil, demonstrating that intellectual error in matters of faith is not neutral but morally culpable. This sets the stage for the following verses that call believers to remain steadfast by looking forward to the meeting with Allah, knowing that ultimate justice is certain.

=5=

After warning the evildoers about their false sense of security, the Quran now turns to the believer’s source of strength. The expression “Whoever hopes for the meeting with Allah” describes a person whose entire orientation is toward the final encounter with their Lord. This hope is not wishful thinking but an active, eager expectation that shapes how one lives.

The phrase “Allah’s appointed time is sure to come” grounds that hope in certainty. The appointed time refers primarily to the Day of Judgment, but it also encompasses the moment of each person’s death, which is a personal meeting with Allah. The verse declares that this meeting is not a possibility; it is an inevitable reality fixed by divine decree.

Therefore, one who truly hopes for it must prepare accordingly. The verse ends with two divine names: “the All-Hearing, All-Knowing. ” He hears every supplication, every cry of the oppressed, every claim of faith; and He fully knows the sincerity behind those words. This provides immense comfort to the early Muslims who were enduring persecution. They could not always see immediate justice, but they knew that the One who hears their prayers and knows their pain has set a term for all things and will bring about a meeting where every matter is settled.

The mention of these attributes also implies a warning: if one’s hope is false and their deeds contradict their words, Allah hears their declarations and knows their hidden reality. The appointed time will expose everything. This verse links the test from the previous verses with the ultimate goal. The believer endures not because suffering is good in itself, but because they are journeying toward a certain meeting with a Lord who is perfectly aware and just. It also connects to the next verse, which speaks about striving; the certainty of the meeting is the fuel that drives all sincere effort.

=6=

This verse explains the real beneficiary of human effort. The word “strives” translates jāhada, which carries the meaning of exerting one’s utmost in any struggle, including the inner struggle against one’s desires and the outer struggle to uphold truth. The Quran immediately clarifies that this striving is not a favour done to Allah.

The phrase “only does so for their own good” is a profound corrective to any hidden sense of self-importance a believer might feel. Sometimes a person can imagine that their worship, sacrifice, or activism somehow benefits God or fulfills a need He has. Allah declares Himself absolutely independent: “Surely Allah is not in need of any of His creation.” He is Al-Ghani, the Self-Sufficient. Every act of obedience, every prayer, every act of patience under persecution, every migration for the sake of faith, all of it returns its benefit solely to the one who performs it. This liberates the believer from acting out of a transactional mindset and also consoles those who strive and see little worldly result. Their effort is never wasted because it shapes their own soul, elevates their rank, and secures their eternal reward. It also teaches humility in success; a person should not become arrogant about their religious achievements because they are the primary beneficiary, not Allah.

In the context of the Meccan period, this verse was a powerful motivation. The believers were sacrificing social ties, wealth, and physical safety. It would have been easy to become despondent or to feel that their suffering served no purpose. This verse assures them that the struggle itself is an investment for their own eternal future. It also serves as a universal principle: any good done for the sake of Allah is never a loss. The self-sufficiency of Allah is a central Quranic theme, grounding all worship in the worshipper’s need rather than any supposed divine deficiency.

=7=

After establishing that striving is for one’s own good, the Quran now describes the specific reward for those who combine faith with righteous action. The phrase “those who believe and do good” joins inner conviction with outward practice, a pairing that appears throughout the Quran. Belief alone, if genuine, must manifest in deeds. Here the promise is twofold. First, “We will certainly absolve them of their sins,” which addresses the human fear of past mistakes.

The Arabic expression conveys complete covering and removal of sins so that no trace remains to cause shame or punishment. This is a tremendous mercy because even sincere believers stumble and fall short.

The second part is even more remarkable: “and reward them according to the best of what they used to do. ” Allah will not judge them by an average of their deeds, nor by their worst moments. He will look at the best they offered—perhaps a single act of profound sincerity, an exceptional patience, a moment of supreme sacrifice—and make that the standard for their reward. This shows the immense generosity of Allah. It encourages the believer to keep striving for excellence, knowing that even if their entire record is mixed, the highest points will be the measure of their recompense. In a community facing persecution, this promise provided hope. Many new Muslims had lives full of sin before Islam; others committed errors under pressure. This verse assured them that sincere faith and continued good deeds would erase what was past and elevate them. It also reframes how we look at our own lives. Instead of being paralysed by past sins, we are called to move forward, to produce beautiful acts of worship, kindness, and patience, trusting that the Most Generous will reward us based on our finest efforts, not our lowest falls.

=8=

This verse introduces a crucial balance between two great obligations. The opening declaration, “We have commanded people to honour their parents,” establishes kindness, respect, and care for parents as a universal divine command, ranking just after the worship of Allah Himself in many Quranic passages.

The Arabic word used for honour implies the highest excellence in treatment. Yet this command has one exception, introduced by “But if they urge you to associate with Me what you have no knowledge of. ” The phrase “what you have no knowledge of” indicates that shirk, associating partners with Allah, is based on ignorance and falsehood with no real evidence. When parents, whom you love and revere, press you to commit shirk, then you must not obey them. Notice that the verse does not say to be harsh or cut off ties; it only says “do not obey them” in that specific matter while still maintaining the duty of honour in all other affairs. This was a real and painful dilemma for some early converts in Mecca whose parents were pagans fiercely opposed to Islam. The verse validates their struggle and gives them clear guidance. The conclusion, “To Me you will all return, and then I will inform you of what you used to do,” places the final judgment entirely with Allah. This comforts the believer who faces family conflict: they are answerable to Allah, not to human pressure. The parent and the child will both stand before Allah, and the truth of their actions will be made clear. The verse thus safeguards the believer from two extremes: abandoning parental duties in the name of religion, and compromising monotheism in the name of family loyalty. It teaches that the vertical relationship with the Creator must remain supreme, but the horizontal relationship with parents remains sacred and must be honoured to the fullest extent that does not violate faith.

=9=

This verse offers a specific and heartwarming promise connected to the previous discussion. After being told to resist parental pressure toward shirk while maintaining honour, the believer might wonder about their spiritual standing and ultimate destination. Allah answers: “Those who believe and do good will surely be admitted by Us into the company of the righteous.

” The emphasis is on divine personal involvement: “by Us. ” The admission into the company of the righteous is not just a spatial entry into Paradise but a social and spiritual elevation. The righteous are the ṣāliḥīn—the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the sincerely pious. To be gathered with them is the greatest honour. This verse assured early converts who were isolated from their pagan families that they would gain a far better family in the Hereafter. It addresses the loneliness that often accompanies conversion when one’s own parents and relatives reject the truth. The believer may lose worldly companionship, but the eternal companionship awaiting them is with the best of humanity.

This promise also serves as motivation to strive for righteousness; the standard is not merely avoiding major sins but actively doing good. The linking of “believe” with “do good” shows that both are required to be counted among this blessed company.

The verse also has a subtle connection to the story of Abraham that appears later in the surah. Abraham had to oppose his own father’s idol worship, yet he is described as a model of righteousness. Those who follow his example in upholding tawhid while dealing justly with parents will join him and others like him. This verse therefore offers hope, direction, and a clear picture of the ultimate reward, all within a few powerful words.

=10=

This verse begins to expose the reality of a group whose faith is weak and conditional. It identifies “some who say, ‘We believe in Allah,’” making a verbal claim like others, but their inner state is different. The test comes “when they suffer in the cause of Allah”—meaning when they face persecution, mockery, economic loss, or physical harm because of their faith. The critical failure is that “they mistake this persecution at the hands of people for the punishment of Allah. ” This is a profound psychological and spiritual error. Instead of seeing their trial as a test from Allah that purifies and elevates, they interpret it as divine displeasure or punishment. They think that if Allah truly loved them, He would not let them suffer, and so they waver. This reveals a transactional mindset that cannot comprehend the wisdom of trials. When the situation changes and “victory comes from your Lord,” these very same people rush forward and say to the believers, “We have always been with you.” Their claim is opportunistic. During hardship they distanced themselves or crumbled internally, but when the tide turns in favour of the believers, they want to share in the success and its worldly benefits.

The verse ends with a rhetorical question: “Does Allah not know best what is in the hearts of all beings? ” He is fully aware of their hypocrisy, their secret doubts, and their real motives. This description closely matches the behaviour of the hypocrites in Medina later, but its principles were already evident in Mecca among those who could not withstand the persecution. The verse is a warning against interpreting suffering as a sign of Allah’s abandonment and against linking one’s commitment to outward victory. It teaches that faithfulness is measured in the storm, not in the calm after.

=11=

Building directly on the previous portrayal, this verse states the inevitable outcome. The Arabic emphasis “certainly” leaves no doubt that a separation is coming. The two groups placed side by side are “those who have sure faith” and “the hypocrites.” Sure faith is not a superficial declaration but a deep-rooted conviction that withstands persecution without misinterpreting it. The hypocrites are those described earlier, who claim belief but collapse under pressure and then seek to attach themselves to the believers in times of ease. Allah “will certainly distinguish” between them, making the difference manifest.

This distinction happens in multiple ways. In this world, trials gradually reveal who is sincere and who is not; the community becomes aware, and the pretenders often expose themselves. On the Day of Judgment, the separation will be absolute and eternal, with the faithful being led to Paradise and the hypocrites to the lowest depths of Hell. The verse serves both as a comfort and as a caution. For the sincere believers who were suffering in Mecca, it was reassuring to know that Allah sees their struggle and will make their sincerity evident, ultimately rewarding them.

For those who might be tempted to hide weak faith behind public claims, it is a sobering reminder that no amount of pretending can escape divine scrutiny. The juxtaposition of “sure faith” and hypocrisy also shows that there is no middle ground in the face of trials: one either holds fast with genuine trust or falls into the duplicity that the surah exposes. This clear line aligns with the earlier verse about the test that distinguishes the truthful from the liars, and it lays the foundation for understanding the following verses about the false promises of the disbelievers.

=12=

This verse captures one of the psychological tactics used by the Quraysh against the early Muslims. The disbelievers presented what seemed like an easy way out of persecution: “Just follow our way,” meaning abandon Islam, return to the religion of the forefathers and the worship of idols, and we guarantee that “we will bear the burden of your sins.” This offer aimed at the believers’ fear of divine punishment. The pagans pretended to take responsibility, as if to say, “If there is any punishment for apostasy, we will carry it for you. ” It was a clever manipulation playing on the human desire to avoid both worldly hardship and eternal risk. Allah immediately exposes the falsehood of this claim: “But they would never want to bear any of the believers’ sins.” On the Day of Judgment, no soul will be willing to carry even an atom of another’s burden. The offer is hollow because it is based on a lie about how divine justice works. The disbelievers cannot transfer sins from one person to another, and even if they could, they would not do so.

The verse concludes, “They are simply lying,” a blunt and clear verdict. The lie is twofold: they lie about the nature of sin and accountability, and they lie about their willingness to help. This verse reinforces a core Quranic principle: no bearer of burdens shall bear the burden of another. Every person is individually responsible for their own choices. The disbelievers’ promise was a satanic deception, similar to Satan’s promise to bear responsibility for leading people astray, which he will disown on Judgment Day. Understanding this protects believers from the pressure to compromise their faith based on false assurances of impunity from those who can neither help nor harm in the Hereafter.

=13=

This verse follows directly from the previous exposure of the disbelievers’ lie. The false promise that they would bear the sins of those they mislead is refuted with the reality of divine justice. “They will certainly be made to carry their own burdens” refers to the sins they personally committed—their disbelief, their oppression, their worship of idols. But then the verse adds a heavier dimension: “as well as other burdens along with their own. ” These additional burdens are the sins of those whom they led astray, not because they take them away from the misled, but because they bear the sin of misleading them. The Prophet, peace be upon him, explained this principle in a well-known hadith: whoever calls to misguidance will have upon him the sin of those who follow him, without that diminishing the followers’ own sins in the slightest. So the disbeliever who urged a believer to apostasy carries the weight of his own disbelief and the weight of having caused another to stumble.

The second part of the verse says, “And they will surely be questioned on Judgment Day about what they used to fabricate. ” The fabrication includes the lies they invented about Allah—such as claiming that idols are intercessors—and the false promise they made to the believers about taking their sins. This questioning is not to gain information but to expose them and establish the justice of their punishment. This verse serves as a severe warning for anyone who misleads others in matters of faith, whether through direct pressure, false teaching, or a bad example. It also comforts those who resisted the pressure; the burden of the misleader’s crime will not be overlooked.

=14=

The surah now begins a series of historical examples to strengthen the hearts of the believers and warn the disbelievers. Noah is the first. The verse emphasises the extraordinary length of his mission: “a thousand years, less fifty,” meaning nine hundred and fifty years. This incredible span of patient preaching, day and night, public and private, with very few responding positively, establishes a towering model of perseverance. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, whose mission in Mecca had lasted around a decade and faced intense rejection, the story of Noah served as both consolation and instruction. If Noah endured nearly a millennium of ridicule and hostility without giving up, then the followers of the final messenger must also remain steadfast.

The phrase “Then the Flood overtook them” marks the decisive end. Noah’s warning was ignored for centuries, yet the punishment, when it finally came, was swift and total.

The word “overtook” suggests being seized suddenly and completely. The verse explains why: “while they persisted in wrongdoing.” They were not destroyed for occasional sins but for entrenched, continuous injustice and rejection of truth despite the lengthy opportunity to repent. This teaches that the delay of punishment is not a sign of weakness in divine justice but an extension of mercy, giving people time to return.

However, when that mercy is persistently refused, the consequence becomes inevitable. This story directly relates to the earlier verses that mocked the idea that evildoers would escape. Noah’s people thought the same, and the flood proved them wrong. The Meccans are being reminded that their rejection of the Prophet does not endanger him but themselves.

=15=

After the swift description of destruction, the verse turns to deliverance. “We delivered him” emphasises that Noah’s salvation was a direct act of Allah’s mercy and power, not a coincidence.

The phrase “and those in the Ark” includes the small band of believers and the pairs of creatures carried by Allah’s command. The ark itself becomes the central symbol of this verse: “making it a sign for all people. ” The Arabic word for sign, āyah, points to something that serves as a lesson, a reminder, and evidence of a higher truth. The ark and the entire event of the flood are preserved as a sign across generations. It teaches that faith and obedience lead to salvation even when the whole world seems to be drowning in corruption. It also demonstrates that Allah’s help comes to the righteous in ways they could not have imagined; Noah built a ship on dry land, mocked by his people, yet that very vessel became his rescue. For the believers in Mecca, this was a powerful message: they were a small, mocked minority, but if they held fast, Allah’s deliverance would come, just as it came to Noah. The sign is also a warning to the oppressors: they may feel secure in their power, but the flood shows that Allah can overturn the order of nature itself to punish the rebellious. The ark floating on the waters serves as a timeless image of the small community of faith being preserved amidst the flood of divine wrath, a lesson for “all people” until the end of time. This conclusion naturally links to the next passage about Abraham, another prophet who stood alone against his people and was delivered.

=16=

The surah now transitions from Noah to Abraham, another great model of monotheistic conviction. The command “remember” invites the Prophet and the believers to recall this scene as a source of strength and guidance. Abraham’s call to his people begins with two fundamental commands: “Worship Allah, and fear Him.” Worship encompasses all forms of devotion directed solely to the One God. Fear of Allah means a reverent awe that stops a person from disobedience. Together they represent the core of the relationship between the Creator and the creation. Abraham then adds a gentle but profound appeal: “This is better for you, if only you knew.” He does not present faith as a burden or a favour to himself, but as something that is inherently better for his people. This method of calling to truth combines clarity with compassion. Abraham is not seeking any personal gain; he wants his people to see that their idolatry is harming only themselves and that turning to Allah brings benefit in this life and the next.

The phrase “if only you knew” implies that their rejection is rooted in ignorance, and that if they truly understood the reality of their situation, they would run toward monotheism. This mirrors the earlier verse about striving being for one’s own good. Abraham’s words also contain an implicit warning: the contrast to “better for you” is that persisting in idolatry is worse for you, leading to ruin. The verse introduces the Meccans to their own ancestor’s argument against the very polytheism they practiced. It places them in an awkward position: they claim to follow the way of Abraham, yet Abraham’s core message was exactly what the Prophet Muhammad was calling them to. This powerful connection will be developed in the coming verses.

=17=

Abraham continues his argument by directly naming the falsehood of his people’s practice. He declares: “You worship besides Allah nothing but idols.

” The phrase “nothing but idols” reduces their objects of worship to mere statues, stripped of any divine pretense. He then exposes the intellectual root of their idolatry: “simply creating lies about them. ” The polytheists fabricated stories and attributes for these idols—calling them intercessors, daughters of God, or sources of blessing—without any truth or revelation. This fabrication is not a small error; it is a lie against Allah. Abraham then appeals to a basic human experience: provision. “Those you worship besides Allah certainly cannot give you any provision. ” He points to the most tangible necessity—sustenance, food, wealth, the means of life. Idols have no power to provide even the smallest grain. Only Allah provides.

Therefore, the logical conclusion follows: “So seek provision from Allah alone, worship Him, and be grateful to Him. ” Seeking provision from Allah means turning to Him in need, trusting Him as the source of all that sustains you, while worship and gratitude are the proper responses to being sustained. Abraham ties the material and spiritual together: if Allah is the only Provider, then He alone deserves worship and thanks.

The verse ends with a reminder of the final return: “To Him you will all be returned.” This links provision in this world to accountability in the next. The One who provides for you now is the One before whom you will stand for judgment, which makes ignoring Him utterly irrational. This line of reasoning was directly relevant to the Meccans, who were traders relying on the provision and security Allah gave them, yet they turned to idols. Abraham’s argument dismantles the foundations of shirk by exposing its intellectual emptiness and its practical absurdity.

=18=

This verse marks a shift in the surah. After recalling Abraham’s call to his people, Allah now addresses the Meccan pagans directly, though this speech is relayed through the Prophet.

The opening condition, “If you persist in denial,” acknowledges their current rejection and presents it as a choice with historical precedent. The statement “so did many communities before you” is both a warning and a means of diminishing their sense of uniqueness or power. The Meccans were not the first to reject a messenger; nations far stronger and more established than them, such as the people of Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, and the people of Lot, did the same. The implication is clear: look at what happened to them.

The verse then clarifies the role of the Prophet: “The Messenger’s duty is only to deliver the message clearly.” The word “only” limits his responsibility. He is not tasked with forcing them to believe, nor with bringing the punishment on demand, nor with guaranteeing their guidance. His job is to convey the message in a manner that is plain, complete, and removes all excuses.

This statement served to comfort the Prophet, peace be upon him, who was deeply grieved by his people’s rejection. It reassures him that he has fulfilled his task as long as he delivers the message with clarity; the outcome is in Allah’s hands. This also places responsibility squarely on the disbelievers. The message has been made unmistakably clear, so their persistence in denial is entirely their own fault. The verse sets the stage for the subsequent call to reflect on the signs of creation and resurrection, moving from warning to intellectual invitation.

=19=

After stating the messenger’s duty, the Quran turns the disbelievers’ attention to the observable world. The question “Have they not seen” is a rhetorical appeal to their intellect and their everyday experience.

The phrase “how Allah originates the creation” points to the beginning of life—the emergence of plants from dead earth, the birth of animals and humans from seemingly nothing, the intricate design of the heavens and the earth. This origination is happening continuously around them, evident to anyone who looks. Then the verse moves from what they can see to what they deny: “then resurrects it.” The same power that brought creation into existence without any prior model can certainly bring it back after death. The argument is simple and powerful: the one who begins creation with such perfection can repeat it with ease.

The phrase “That is certainly easy for Allah” drives home the point. Human beings may find repetition difficult, but for Allah, origination and resurrection are equally effortless. His power is not dependent on precedent or effort.

The use of the word “easy” challenges the idea that resurrection is too great a thing for Allah to accomplish. The Meccans’ denial of resurrection was a central tenet of their disbelief; they thought that once bones were decayed, a return to life was impossible. This verse uses the logic they could witness to dismantle that assumption. It also ties back to the earlier verse about Allah’s appointed time being sure to come; resurrection is the means by which that meeting happens. The argument prepares for the next verse, which calls people to travel and observe the earth for further evidence.

=20=

The previous verse asked if they had not seen the pattern of origination and resurrection; this verse issues a direct command to the Prophet to tell the disbelievers to travel and observe. “Travel throughout the land” is an invitation to explore the earth with attentive eyes. The purpose is specified: “and see how He originated the creation.” In the ruins of past civilisations, in the diverse forms of life, in the geological layers and the cycle of seasons, there are visible traces of the original act of bringing things into existence. The observation of origination is meant to lead to a certain conclusion: “then Allah will bring it into being one more time.” The logic flows naturally. If you see the first creation stamped everywhere across the earth, then the second creation—the resurrection—follows as a necessary consequence of the same divine power.

The phrase “one more time” indicates that the resurrection is a second act of bringing into existence, similar in its dependence on Allah’s will and power to the first. The verse concludes with a comprehensive statement of divine capability: “Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything.

” This is not limited to creation and resurrection; it encompasses all things. This verse was especially relevant to the Meccans who travelled for trade, passing by the ruins of the people of ʿĀd, Thamūd, and others. They could see the remnants of destroyed nations and ponder how those people were once created, then wiped out, and how easily Allah could bring them back to life for judgment. The command to travel turns the earth into a living textbook of faith. It also contains a contemporary lesson: the more we discover about the complexity and vastness of the original creation through science and exploration, the more the Quranic argument for the ease of resurrection gains force. This verse rounds off the section on warning, linking history, nature, and the logical necessity of the Hereafter.
