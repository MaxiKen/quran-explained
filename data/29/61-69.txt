=61=

This verse exposes a glaring contradiction in the worldview of the Meccan polytheists. The Prophet is instructed to pose a simple question, though the verse already knows the answer: “If you ask them who created the heavens and the earth and subjected the sun and the moon for your benefit, they will certainly say, ‘Allah!’” The polytheists never denied that the supreme Creator and Controller of the cosmos was Allah. Their belief, corrupted as it was, still acknowledged Him as the ultimate originator of the universe and the One who set the celestial bodies in motion for human benefit.

The phrase “subjected the sun and the moon” points to the precise, ordered movements that make life possible—day and night, seasons, tides—a cosmic order they attributed to Allah. But then the verse delivers a devastating blow: “How can they then be deluded from the truth?” How can someone who admits that Allah alone created everything and runs the universe then turn around and worship stones, idols, angels, or jinn alongside Him?

The word “deluded” indicates that they have been turned away and deceived, not by lack of evidence, but by a deep spiritual blindness. The question implies that their intellect has been hijacked by tradition, pride, or satanic suggestion, because the logical conclusion of monotheism is staring them in the face. This verse ties back to the parable of the spider’s web: they acknowledge the solid structure of the Creator’s power yet cling to the flimsiest of shelters. The Meccans’ own testimony becomes a proof against them.

The verse also teaches that mere intellectual acknowledgment of Allah’s existence is insufficient; it must translate into exclusive worship and submission. The question “How can they then be deluded?” is timeless, challenging every person who believes in a Supreme Being yet dilutes that belief with other ultimate loyalties.

=62=

After exposing the Meccans’ contradictory belief, the surah turns to the issue of provision, a matter intimately tied to worship and reliance. The statement “Allah gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills of His servants” establishes that all sustenance—wealth, food, children, opportunities—comes directly from Allah’s will. The alternation between abundance and limitation is not random; it is based on divine wisdom that the verse’s ending clarifies: “Surely Allah has full knowledge of everything.” He knows the true state of every servant, what is best for them, and how prosperity or poverty will affect their faith. Some people are corrupted by wealth, becoming arrogant and forgetful, so limitation is a mercy that protects their afterlife. Others are ennobled by wealth, using it for good, so abundance is a means of reward and testing. The polytheists believed their idols could bring provision or that their own cleverness in trade secured their fortunes. This verse refutes that by placing the entire distribution of sustenance in Allah’s hands alone. It also links to the previous verse about the creatures who cannot secure their own provision; the same Allah who feeds the bird provides for the human, and He adjusts that provision according to His knowledge.

For the believer facing economic hardship because of their faith, this verse is a powerful reassurance. If Allah has limited their provision, it is not because He has forgotten them or because the idols could have done better; it is a deliberate, knowledgeable decree.

The verse also serves as a warning to those who have abundance: it is a test, not a sign of divine approval, and it comes from the One who fully knows what they do with it.

=63=

The argument continues with another undeniable sign that the polytheists acknowledged. The verse describes a process they witnessed regularly in the Arabian desert: “who sends down rain from the sky, giving life to the earth after its death.” The barren, cracked land, lifeless under the scorching sun, is miraculously transformed by rainfall into a lush, green carpet teeming with vegetation. This cycle of death and revival was a visible proof of divine power that the Meccans witnessed year after year. The verse asserts that if asked who is responsible for this, “they will surely say, ‘Allah!’” Again, their innate knowledge and transmitted tradition confirmed that only the Supreme God could bring about such a transformation. Allah instructs the Prophet: “Say, ‘Praise be to Allah!’” On the surface, this is an expression of gratitude that they at least acknowledge this truth. But it is also a subtle rebuke. If they admit that Allah alone sends rain and revives the earth, why do they not direct all worship and gratitude exclusively to Him? Their praise is inconsistent and incomplete.

The verse then gives the diagnosis: “In fact, most of them do not understand.” Their problem is not a lack of information but a failure of comprehension. They see the rain, they see the revival, but they do not connect this observable resurrection of the earth to the promised resurrection of human beings, nor do they draw the necessary conclusion that the Giver of rain must be the sole object of worship. Their understanding remains shallow, stuck at the level of acknowledging a fact without letting that fact reshape their entire worldview. This verse thus links the natural sign of rain directly to the twin doctrines of tawhid and resurrection, both of which the Meccans rejected despite the evidence they themselves recognized.

=64=

After repeatedly exposing the Meccans’ intellectual contradictions, the surah now delivers a concise, profound verdict on the nature of existence. The statement “This worldly life is no more than play and amusement” is not a denigration of the world as intrinsically evil, but a declaration of its true proportion relative to eternity. Play and amusement are activities that occupy time, engage attention, and can feel very serious in the moment, yet they are temporary and leave no lasting substance. The world’s concerns—its wealth, power, rivalries, pleasures, and even its sufferings—are like a game that ends. The believer participates in life seriously as a test, but never loses sight of its transient nature.

The verse then contrasts: “But the Hereafter is indeed the real life.” The word “real” means the life that is substantial, permanent, and worthy of the name. All the qualities we associate with true life—peace, fulfillment, justice, unending joy—belong to the Hereafter. The worldly life is a shadow; the next life is the substance.

The phrase “if only they knew” is a lament over the disbelievers who have inverted this reality. They live as if this world is all there is, and they treat the mention of the Hereafter as a myth. If they truly knew, their priorities would be reversed. This verse serves as the keystone for the entire surah’s argument. It explains why tests are bearable, why migration for faith is worth it, why the spider’s web of false reliance is a disaster. The believer who internalizes this verse finds their attachment to the world loosening and their hope for the meeting with Allah intensifying. The verse is both a comfort for those who suffer in this life and a warning to those who are enjoying it at the expense of their eternal future.

=65=

This verse paints a vivid psychological portrait of the polytheists’ relationship with Allah. The scenario involves a ship caught in a storm at sea. In the pre-modern world, a sea voyage was an undertaking fraught with peril. When the winds howled and the waves rose like mountains and death seemed certain, every sailor and passenger would instinctively abandon all false deities. The verse says, “they cry out to Allah alone in sincere devotion.” At that moment of extreme fear, the human fitrah, the primordial nature, surfaces with perfect clarity. All the layers of idolatry and false philosophy are stripped away, and the soul calls upon the only One who can actually save. Their devotion in that moment is described as “sincere,” making their religion pure for Allah alone.

Yet the verse exposes the tragedy: “But as soon as He delivers them safely to shore, they associate others with Him once again.” The moment safety returns and their feet touch solid ground, they forget the terror, and the idols reclaim their places. This pattern, well known to the Meccans who either travelled by sea or heard the tales of merchants, is a damning indictment of human ingratitude and inconsistency. The emergency monotheist reverts to routine polytheism. This verse is a direct challenge to anyone who treats faith as a last resort rather than a constant commitment. It also reveals that deep in the human psyche, the truth of tawhid is undeniable; polytheism is merely a surface coating that cracks under extreme pressure. The verse warns that such situational sincerity is not sufficient; the test of life is to maintain that same sincere devotion when the storm is over and the sun is shining.

=66=

After describing their emergency monotheism and subsequent relapse into shirk, the verse issues a terrifying divine ultimatum. The phrase “So let them be ungrateful for all We have given them” uses the imperative form as a threat, not as permission. It is as if Allah is saying: if they choose ingratitude—denying the very blessings of safety, provision, and life that they acknowledged at sea—then let them continue in that state. The ingratitude here is specifically associating partners with Allah after He saved them.

The next phrase, “and let them enjoy themselves for now,” reinforces this grim allowance. Their enjoyment is temporary, confined to the brief span of this worldly life.

The final clause, “For they will soon know,” is a short sentence loaded with dread. The word “soon” indicates the certainty and nearness of the appointed time, whether it is death, a worldly punishment, or the Day of Judgment. What they will know is the consequence of their ingratitude and their false enjoyment. This verse parallels earlier warnings in the surah where the Meccans were told that the punishment would take them by surprise. It is a stylistic shift from argument and parable to direct, ominous warning.

For the believer, this verse inspires a healthy fear of ever becoming complacent or ungrateful after receiving Allah’s favours. It highlights that Allah’s patience, while vast, should never be mistaken for indifference. The apparent freedom of the disbelievers to enjoy life is actually a respite that is hurtling toward a fixed endpoint, after which the reality of their situation will become agonizingly clear.

=67=

The surah now brings the argument directly home to Mecca itself. “Have they not seen how We have made Mecca a safe haven” refers to the inviolable sanctuary, the Haram, where bloodshed and fighting were forbidden. While the rest of Arabia was plagued by tribal raids, kidnappings, and constant insecurity, the Quraysh lived in remarkable safety and prosperity. Their caravans travelled with a respect no other tribe enjoyed, because they were the custodians of the House of Allah built by Abraham. The contrast is stark: “whereas people all around them are snatched away.

” The word “snatched” conveys sudden violence—people being killed, captured, or robbed in the territories surrounding Mecca. This safety was not a product of Quraysh ingenuity; it was a direct favor from Allah, granted in response to Abraham’s prayer when he asked that Mecca be made a city of peace.

Yet, despite this clear blessing, the verse asks incredulously: “How can they then believe in falsehood and deny Allah’s favours? ” Their belief in falsehood—idolatry—and their denial of Allah’s favors are presented as a single, monstrous ingratitude. They enjoy the fruit of the sanctuary’s sanctity, which stems from the legacy of Abraham’s pure monotheism, yet they fill that sanctuary with idols and oppose the prophet who calls them back to that very monotheism. The verse makes their hypocrisy undeniable. It also provides a lesson for all people who live in peace and security: such blessings are easily taken for granted, but they come from Allah and require gratitude in the form of exclusive worship and righteous living. The safety of Mecca was a sign so visible that no Meccan could honestly deny it, making their continued devotion to falsehood an act of the most profound willful blindness.

=68=

The surah approaches its conclusion by posing a rhetorical question that defines the peak of wrongdoing. “And who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or reject the truth after it has reached them? ” The verse presents two categories of the worst offenders.

The first are those who invent falsehoods about Allah—such as claiming He has partners, daughters, or sons, or that He has forbidden what He has permitted and vice versa. The second are those who, when the pure truth delivered by a prophet reaches them in clear form, choose to reject it outright. The Meccans fell into both categories: they fabricated lies by attributing divinity to idols and by making up rituals, and they rejected the Quran when it came to them through Muhammad, peace be upon him.

The question implies that no injustice can be greater than this, because it corrupts the very source of guidance and leads countless others astray. Then the verse delivers the logical conclusion: “Is Hell not a fitting home for the disbelievers? ” The word “fitting” conveys a sense of appropriate recompense. Hell is not an arbitrary punishment; it is the necessary consequence and the just abode for those whose entire orientation in life was toward falsehood and denial. This verse serves as a final warning before the surah’s closing note of hope for the believers. It leaves no ambiguity about the severity of shirk and willful rejection. At the same time, it appeals to the reader’s sense of justice: if someone spends a lifetime lying about God and suppressing His truth, what destination other than Hell would be compatible with divine justice?

The question invites reflection, not only on the fate of the Meccans but on the reader’s own honesty toward the truth.

=69=

The surah, which began with the test of faith and wove through stories of prophets, parables, and warnings, ends on a deeply encouraging and empowering note. “As for those who struggle in Our cause” uses the word jāhadū, from the root jihād, meaning to exert utmost effort and strive against all obstacles. This struggle encompasses every dimension: the inner battle against one’s ego and desires, the intellectual battle against falsehood, the physical struggle to defend the community and practice the faith, and the spiritual struggle of migration and perseverance under persecution.

The phrase “in Our cause” specifies that this striving is not for personal glory, wealth, or tribal honor, but purely for Allah’s sake. The reward is breathtaking: “We will surely guide them along Our Way.” The promise is that those who genuinely strive for Allah will receive an increase in guidance. The guidance here is not the initial guidance to Islam, but a continuous, deepening guidance that illuminates the path, removes obstacles, grants clarity in confusion, and leads ultimately to the straight way that ends in Paradise. This means that struggle is not just a duty but a means of drawing closer to Allah and gaining spiritual insight. The verse closes with the ultimate reassurance: “And Allah is certainly with the good-doers.” The good-doers, the muḥsinīn, are those who perform their actions with excellence and sincerity, going beyond the minimum. The “with-ness” of Allah (maʿiyyah) implies His special support, protection, love, and victory. This concluding verse ties together the entire surah. The tests of the beginning, the models of Noah, Abraham, Lot, and Shuʿaib who struggled mightily, the call to migration, the parable of the spider’s web—all lead to this final truth: success lies in struggling for Allah, and that struggle itself brings divine guidance and companionship. It is a timeless promise that turns the hardships of faith into a journey of increasing light and divine closeness.
