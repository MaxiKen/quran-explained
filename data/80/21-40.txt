=21=

After describing the ease with which Allah brings the human being into life and facilitates his path, verse twenty-one brings the first journey to a quiet close: “then causes them to die and be buried.” The transition marked by “then” moves from the active, facilitated life to its inevitable end.

The word “causes them to die” translates amātah, reminding us that death is not an accident or a mere biological failure; it is a deliberate act of the Creator who first gave life. Every soul experiences death by His decree, at a time and in a manner that no one can escape.

The second part, “and be buried,” refers to the honour Allah has bestowed upon human beings even after their souls depart. Unlike animals whose carcasses remain exposed, the human body is typically interred into the earth, covered and protected.

The Arabic fa-aqbarah implies not just the act of burial but the provision of a grave, a hidden resting place. This is a sign of divine regard, a final act of dignity for the human body that was once a humble sperm-drop.

The verse continues to dismantle arrogance; the same self-sufficient person who turns away from the reminder must now reckon with the reality that all his power and status will end in a silent grave. The mention of burial also evokes the physical return to the earth from which humanity was originally created, bringing the life cycle full circle.

This is not a pessimistic statement but a reminder of the transition to the next phase. It connects seamlessly to the earlier mention of creation from a drop and ordination, showing that the One who proportioned life’s beginning has also proportioned its end.

For the believer, it cultivates detachment from worldly vanity; for the disbeliever, it is a warning that the life he clings to will inevitably be cut short. The verse sets the stage for what follows: resurrection. It also indirectly refutes the pagan Arab notion that death is final annihilation; by saying “then causes them to die and be buried,” the Qur’ān implies a careful process that will later lead to being brought forth again.

=22=

Verse twenty-two provides the glorious culmination of the human journey: “Then when He wills, He will resurrect them.” The phrase “when He wills” places the timing entirely within Allah’s knowledge and decree.

This is not a continuous process but a single, decisive event that will occur at the exact moment appointed in the divine plan. The human being has no control over when this resurrection will happen; it is concealed wisdom.

The word “resurrect” translates anshara, which literally means to raise up, to bring out from the grave, to spread forth after being compacted. This is the ultimate undoing of burial. The One who caused death and the hidden grave will reverse the process and call every soul back to life. The verse serves as a direct answer to the Meccan disbelievers who mockingly asked, “Who will bring us back after we have become dust and bones? ” It asserts that just as the origin was a simple, forgotten drop, and just as the path of life was made easy, the return to life for judgment is equally simple for the Almighty.

The phrase “when He wills” also indicates that this resurrection is not a natural emergence but a deliberate act of sovereign will. It does not depend on natural laws but on the command “Be!” and it is. The connection to the preceding verses is profound: the One who created from a drop, ordained development, made the way easy, then caused death and burial, will certainly resurrect when He wills.

This is the logical completion of the argument against ingratitude. If the entire trajectory of human existence is in Allah’s hands, then denying the final resurrection is a selective blindness.

Moreover, this verse brings hope and warning. For the believer who lived in awe, resurrection is the door to eternal joy; for the ungrateful one condemned earlier, it is the beginning of inevitable accountability. It anchors the moral weight of the surah, reminding all that the end of the story is not the grave.

=23=

Immediately after establishing the certainty of resurrection, verse twenty-three delivers a sharp verdict: “But no! They have failed to comply with what He ordered.” The strong opening “But no!” (kallā) is again a forceful negation, rejecting any assumption that the disbeliever has fulfilled his purpose.

The phrase “they have failed” translates lammā yaqḍi, which implies not yet having carried out, fulfilled, or completed a command. The command referred to here is the full range of divine obligations and the very purpose of creation: to recognise and worship Allah, to be grateful, to heed the reminder. Despite the clear signs of creation from a humble drop, the facilitation of life, the inevitability of death, and the promise of resurrection, the disbelieving human still neglects what he has been ordered. The verse does not specify a single command, making it inclusive of the entire religious and moral duty. Some scholars have also connected this to the specific obligation of reflecting on the signs leading to faith, while others mention the primordial covenant taken from the children of Adam. Regardless, the core meaning is that the human being has not risen to the trust he was given.

This statement echoes the earlier condemnation of human ingratitude and intensifies it. The one who was given so many means of ease has not even made the effort to comply with the most basic orders of his Creator.

The verse also carries a tone of regret and warning: despite all the stages of care—creation, guidance, death with dignity, and the promise of resurrection—people still fall short. It reminds the believer of the constant need for self-examination. Have we truly complied with what Allah has ordered, or are we among those who are described as not having fulfilled their duty? The verse thus bridges the argument from creation to the coming judgment, showing that the failure to comply now will have consequences when resurrection occurs. It reinforces the theme that knowledge and opportunity demand action.

=24=

In verse twenty-four, the surah introduces a new but deeply connected line of reflection: “Let people then consider their food.” The word “then” links the command to the preceding critique. Since the disbelieving human has failed to comply with what was ordered and remains ungrateful despite the signs in his own creation, he is now invited to reflect on a daily, tangible miracle: his food. The verb “consider” translates yanẓur, which means to look carefully, to examine, to contemplate with insight.

This is not a casual glance but a deliberate and thoughtful observation. Food is something every human being interacts with multiple times a day, yet few pause to trace its journey from the heavens to the plate. The verse universalises the invitation: “let people” includes every human being, believer and disbeliever alike. The food we eat is so familiar that its miraculous nature often escapes us. This verse employs a profound pedagogical technique, moving from the creation of the self to the provision of sustenance, leaving no excuse for heedlessness. The argument is that the same Lord who fashioned you from a drop and caused you to die is the One who provides the food by which you stay alive. If you cannot deny His hand in your own existence, how can you overlook His hand in the nourishment that reaches you every single day?

The verse also indirectly addresses the Meccan pagans who were largely an agricultural society surrounded by barren deserts and occasional fertile valleys. They knew the harsh reality of drought and the life-giving mercy of rain. By turning their attention to food, the Qur’ān is grounding their spiritual reflection in their immediate, practical experience. For the modern reader, the command remains equally urgent: to see in every meal a sign of the Creator’s meticulous care, and to respond with gratitude rather than the condemned ingratitude that marked the disbelieving human.

=25=

Verse twenty-five begins the detailed answer to the call to consider food, starting from the very first cause: “how We pour down rain in abundance.” The pronoun “We” is the majestic plural, drawing attention to the majesty and power of Allah. The act of pouring rain is described with the word ṣabb, which conveys gushing, copious, and forceful pouring. Rain is not a mere drizzle; it is sent in abundance to saturate the earth and create the conditions for life. This language emphasises divine generosity. Rain is also purely a mercy from Allah; no human can cause the skies to release water, and the ancient Arabs knew their utter dependence on rainfall for survival. The verse positions rain as the first link in the food chain, proving that human effort in agriculture is secondary and dependent. The farmer may plough and sow, but if the rain is withheld, all his labour is in vain. By highlighting rain, the Qur’ān directs the human gaze upward to the heavens, to the source of provision.

This is a powerful argument against polytheism; the pagans acknowledged that Allah was the ultimate provider of rain, yet they worshipped other deities. The verse implicitly asks: if Allah alone pours down the rain that grows your food, why do you turn away from Him?

The phrase “in abundance” also points to the delicate balance of the earth’s ecosystem. Too little rain causes famine; too much causes destruction. The abundance is measured perfectly to meet the needs of creatures. This care and precision are signs for those who reflect. The modern reader, with all the technology of irrigation and desalination, is still ultimately dependent on the water cycle set in motion by the Creator. The verse strips away human arrogance by reminding us that the very first step of our daily sustenance is entirely in divine hands, an act of pure mercy that should fill the heart with awe and gratitude.

=26=

Following the pouring of rain, verse twenty-six describes the next miracle: “and meticulously split the earth open for sprouts.” The Arabic verb shaqaqnā means We split, cleave, or crack open. It is paired with the word “meticulously” to reflect the precise, purposeful, and intentional nature of this action. After the rain saturates the soil, a profound transformation occurs. The seemingly solid, lifeless earth becomes soft and receptive, and then it literally splits apart as delicate green sprouts push their way through.

This is a powerful image of life emerging from death. The hard, dry ground that appeared barren and unyielding suddenly surrenders to the gentle force of a tiny seedling. It is one of the most common yet most easily overlooked miracles of daily existence. The verse uses this visual to prepare the mind for the concept of resurrection. The same Allah who splits the earth to bring out a living plant from a dead seed will split the graves to bring out human beings for judgment. The “sprouts” here refer to all forms of vegetation, the beginning of the plant life that will eventually provide grain, fruits, and fodder. By drawing attention to the splitting of the earth, the Qur’ān encourages human beings to observe the natural world not as a mechanical process but as a living sign of divine power. The meticulousness indicates that nothing is random; the type of plant that emerges is determined by the seed and the care of the One who created it. The crack in the soil is not a chaotic rupture but a perfectly designed emergence. The verse thus prepares the heart for the detailed listing that follows, creating a sense of wonder and anticipation. It also humbles those who see themselves as self-sufficient, reminding them that even the food they boast of comes from a process they did not initiate and cannot control.

=27=

The next verse narrows the focus from the general splitting of the earth to a specific, life-sustaining result: “causing grain to grow in it.” The grain mentioned here, ḥabb, includes all seed-bearing crops such as wheat, barley, rice, and other staples that form the foundation of human sustenance. The act of “causing to grow” translates anbatnā, from the root n-b-t, which indicates the sprouting and thriving of plants. It is attributed directly to Allah. The farmer casts seeds into the earth, but the actual growth—the swelling of the seed, the breaking of its shell, the upward reach of the stalk, the formation of the heavy grain head—is entirely beyond human power.

The verse reminds us that every grain is a sign of the Creator’s artistry. A single seed can produce a stalk bearing dozens of new grains, all containing the same latent potential for life. This multiplying miracle feeds civilisations.

The mention of grain also echoes the theme of resurrection, as the seed must “die” in its original form, buried in the soil, split open, before a new plant can emerge. This is precisely the process the disbelievers find so difficult to accept about their own resurrection, yet they witness it every harvest season.

The verse also connects to the practical reality of the Meccan audience who relied on grain caravans and local farming. By mentioning grain, Allah is pointing to something familiar and indispensable. The lesson is that the One who places this staple within the split earth is worthy of gratitude and worship. For the modern believer, this verse is an invitation to see every piece of bread as a verse of the Qur’ān manifest in nature. It should inspire not just a passing “Alhamdulillah” but a deep reflection on the chain of mercy that started with rain, moved through the splitting of the earth, and now results in the grain that sustains life.

=28=

Verse twenty-eight expands the list of provisions with a combination of delight and nourishment: “as well as grapes and greens.” The shift from grain to grapes introduces the element of pleasure and variety in Allah’s provision. Grain is the staple, the necessary sustenance, but grapes represent flavour, sweetness, and enjoyment.

The mention of grapes is particularly significant for the Arabs of the Hijaz, who valued vineyards and the fruit they produced, whether eaten fresh or dried as raisins. Grapes also symbolise a higher level of blessing, as they are not essential for bare survival but are gifts that beautify life.

The word “greens” translates wa-qadbā, which is commonly understood as fresh green fodder, alfalfa, or moist vegetables and herbs. This extends the provision to a wide range of fresh, green plant life that delights the senses and nourishes both humans and animals.

The pairing of grapes and greens highlights the diversity of the plant kingdom, a diversity designed to meet varied tastes and needs. The verse underscores that Allah did not merely provide monotonous subsistence but a colourful, flavourful, and textured provision that brings joy. This abundance is a clear proof against the ingratitude of the disbeliever. How can one enjoy the cool sweetness of a grape or the freshness of green herbs and not feel awe for the Creator who designed their seeds, their growth, and their taste?

Furthermore, the mention of greens that are eaten directly without processing, alongside grapes that can be eaten fresh or transformed, shows the range of divine favour. For the contemporary reader, walking through a market and seeing the vivid colours of fruits and vegetables should trigger the same reflection: all these are grown from the same earth and watered by the same rain, yet each has a unique taste, shape, and nutritional profile, all by the meticulous design of the Almighty.

=29=

Verse twenty-nine continues to paint the landscape of divine provision: “and olives and palm trees.” These two trees hold a very special place in the Qur’ān and in the lives of the people of the Arabian Peninsula and the Levant. The olive tree is described elsewhere in the Qur’ān as blessed, and its oil is a source of light and nourishment. It is a tree of endurance, thriving in harsh, rocky environments and producing fruit that yields one of the purest oils. The olive’s mention here calls to mind both its immediate edible fruit and the extracted oil, which was a staple for cooking, lighting, and medicinal purposes. The date palm, nakhl, is the tree of life in the desert, offering not only its sweet fruit in various stages of ripeness but also trunks, leaves, and fibres for construction, shade, and weaving. By naming olive trees and palm trees together, the verse brings to attention two of the most generous and beneficial creations. Their produce is rich, sustaining, and storable, representing the long-term provision that secures communities against scarcity.

The pairing also connects the humid, fertile lands of the Mediterranean olive groves with the arid date-palm oases of Arabia, reminding the Meccans, who travelled north to Syria and south to Yemen for trade, of the varied landscapes Allah has created. The underlying argument is that if people reflect on these specific trees, they will see the careful handiwork of the Creator in their unique structures. A date seed, so hard and seemingly lifeless, splits open under the soil with the right conditions and pushes forth a shoot that eventually becomes a massive palm tree laden with clusters of sweet fruit.

This is a sign of resurrection as vivid as any. The verse thus urges the disbelieving human to look at the olive and the palm and see the trace of the One who fashioned him from a drop, then brought forth a complete being.

=30=

Continuing the rich visual catalogue, verse thirty adds “and dense orchards.” The Arabic word used is ḥadā’iq ghulban, a beautifully evocative phrase. Ḥadā’iq are walled gardens or lush orchards, with the root implying a rich, dark green colour, almost black in its intensity due to the density of the vegetation. Ghulban means thick, tangled, massive, and towering.

The image is of orchards so heavily planted with trees that their branches intertwine, creating a canopy of abundant shade and fruit. This description goes beyond single trees to an entire landscape of concentrated blessing. It conjures the oasis gardens that were the pride of Arabia, where multiple varieties of fruit trees grew together, providing not only food but also a cool, fragrant shelter from the harsh desert sun.

The term implies a deliberate planting and order, yet the word also acknowledges a certain majestic wildness and profusion. This verse shows that Allah’s provision is not merely functional but also breathtakingly beautiful. A dense orchard delights all the senses: the sight of layered green, the scent of fruit and earth, the sound of rustling leaves, the taste of fresh produce, and the touch of a cool breeze. All of this is presented as a direct result of the rain poured down and the earth split open, demonstrating that the same process that yields a single grain can also yield an entire orchard of interwoven abundance. For the ungrateful human, this lavish generosity is a shaming reminder. The Creator did not hold back His gifts but spread them in dense profusion.

The verse also encourages the believers to cultivate and appreciate the earth, seeing in a garden a reflection of the Paradise that awaits those who are grateful and mindful. The dense orchard is a foretaste of the gardens of eternity, a sign pointing from this world to the next.

=31=

The list reaches its comprehensive conclusion with verse thirty-one: “and fruit and fodder.” By mentioning “fruit” (fākihah) and “fodder” (abban), the verse covers the whole spectrum of plant-based provision intended for both humans and animals. Fruit is typically the sweet, enjoyable produce of trees and bushes, consumed directly for pleasure and nutrition. Fodder, here a broad term, refers to grasses, herbs, and pasture that serve as primary feed for livestock.

The pairing is highly instructive. Humans are not the only creatures in Allah’s design; animals are also under His care, and their sustenance is provided through the same process of rain and growth. This subtle inclusion reminds humanity of the interconnectedness of creation. The cattle, camels, sheep, and goats upon which the Arabs depended for milk, meat, wool, and transport are fed by the same mercy that feeds the human being.

Thus, the provision of fodder is indirectly a provision for human beings as well, since the well-fed animal becomes a source of immense good. The word “fodder” has also been interpreted by some scholars to include all kinds of herbage and vegetation not directly eaten by humans but essential to the ecosystem. This verse beautifully rounds off the reflection on food by showing that nothing is created in vain; even the most humble grass has a purpose in the divine economy. The sheer variety—grains, grapes, greens, olives, dates, orchards, fruits, fodder—demonstrates a generosity that defies the ingratitude condemned earlier. The human being is surrounded by a cascade of provisions, each a sign pointing back to the Giver.

The verse also subtly answers the potential arrogance of someone who might say, “I produced this food by my own labour.” The raw materials—rain, soil, seeds, growth—are all provided by Allah. The human role is merely that of a caretaker and harvester, not an originator.

=32=

The detailed catalogue of plant life concludes with verse thirty-two, which explicitly states the purpose: “all as a means of sustenance for you and your animals.” The word “sustenance” translates matā‘, which conveys the sense of provision, enjoyment, and benefit for a designated period. It emphasises that the bounties of the earth are a temporary gift, not an eternal possession. The explicit mention of “you and your animals” ties together the whole community of living beings under human care. The animals, referred to as an‘ām (livestock), are a blessing in themselves, and their sustenance is part of the blessing granted to human beings. This verse frames the entire discussion on food as a reminder that both human and animal life is upheld by a single, merciful Provider. It connects back to the condemnation of ingratitude: when Allah provides so comprehensively for every need, even for the beasts that serve humans, what excuse remains for arrogance and denial?

The verse also implicitly calls for responsible stewardship. If all this is a provision from the Creator, then it must be consumed with gratitude and without waste. The arrangement of the verses is deeply logical: from the fundamental act of rain, through the splitting of the earth, to the variety of crops, and finally to the declaration of their purpose. It is a complete journey from cause to final benefit.

For the disbeliever who questions the resurrection, the argument is clear: just as the dead earth is revived with vegetation to provide sustenance, the dead human bodies will be revived for judgment and eternal recompense. The sustenance of this world is a sign of the sustenance of the next. The verse leaves the human being standing amidst these blessings, called to respond not with the heedlessness of the indifferent one but with the eager mindfulness of the blind man who came seeking purity.

=33=

Having established the signs of creation, provision, and resurrection, the surah moves dramatically to the event of the Hour in verse thirty-three: “Then, when the Deafening Blast comes to pass.” The Arabic word for “Deafening Blast” is al-ṣākhkhah, one of the many names for the Day of Resurrection. Its root meaning conveys a violent, piercing noise that strikes the ears so intensely that it almost deafens. It is a single, unimaginable sound, the blowing of the Trumpet by the angel Isrāfīl, that will signal the end of the present order and the beginning of the resurrection.

The word “comes to pass” translates jā’at, indicating its absolute certainty and arrival at its decreed time. The use of such a terrifying auditory image is profoundly effective; no one can escape a sound that overwhelms the entire cosmos. It will silence every noise of this world, rendering all worldly concerns insignificant. This verse serves as a sharp turn in the surah. After the gentle, detailed description of the growth of food, the scene abruptly shifts to the apocalyptic event that renders all that provision irrelevant except as a source of accounting. The logical connection is clear: the One who demonstrated His power through the quiet miracle of a seed splitting the earth will also demonstrate His power through this overwhelming blast. The disbeliever who felt self-sufficient and indifferent is now being warned that a day will come when all his self-sufficiency will dissolve in an instant. The deafening nature of the blast symbolises the end of heedlessness; the arrogant will no longer be able to ignore the call of their Creator.

For the believer, this verse is a reminder to live with the awareness that the ṣākhkhah may arrive at any moment, turning the familiar world into a memory and ushering in the ultimate reality.

=34=

The surah now paints the human reaction to the Deafening Blast, beginning in verse thirty-four: “on that Day every person will flee from their own siblings.” The phrase “on that Day” anchors the following verses in the reality of the ṣākhkhah. The reaction is one of complete, terrified flight. The verb yafirru means to flee in panic, to run away with urgency and desperation. The astonishing detail is the object of flight: “their own siblings,” those brothers and sisters who were the closest companions in the worldly life. The bonds of siblinghood, forged through shared upbringing, blood, and affection, will be severed by the sheer terror of that moment. The verse indicates a complete breakdown of all social ties and natural affections. The person will not pause to inquire about their sibling’s safety; they will not seek to help or comfort them. Instead, they will run from them. This reversal of nature is a proof of the overwhelming horror of the Day. It is not a matter of hatred but of a terror so consuming that every soul will be entirely preoccupied with its own fate. The verse subtly answers the earlier arrogance of the disbelieving human who relied on his family, clan, and status. All these supports will vanish. Even the most intimate human relationship will be shattered by fear. The scene is a direct challenge to those who, in this world, prioritise family loyalties over their duty to Allah. The blind man who came seeking purification understood that his true need was not his tribe but his Creator. On that Day, the wisdom of his choice will become blindingly clear. This verse serves as an urgent wake-up call, reminding believers that the only bond that will avail is the bond of faith and righteous deeds.

=35=

The escalation continues in verse thirty-five: “and even their mother and father.” After siblings, the verse moves to the parents, who are the very source of one’s existence and typically the most beloved of all people.

The word “even” captures the shock of the statement. The mother’s womb that carried and nurtured, the father’s protection and provision—all these intimate ties are dissolved in the terror of the Day. In normal human life, a person would instinctively sacrifice everything for their parents, but on that Day, the natural instinct of filial devotion will be overtaken by a greater, more immediate survival instinct of the soul. This does not imply a loss of love but a complete paralysis of the usual caring response due to the magnitude of the event. Every person will be in a state of ultimate self-concern. The verse highlights the loneliness of the Day of Judgment; each soul stands alone before its Creator, stripped of all the relationships that provided comfort and identity in the world. The wisdom of mentioning the parents here is profound. In Meccan society, loyalty to ancestors and elders was a cornerstone of their social and religious life. The pagans often rejected the Prophet’s message by claiming they were merely following the way of their fathers. On the Day of the Deafening Blast, those same fathers will be fled from. All the arguments based on ancestral tradition will evaporate. The verse thus dismantles a core justification for disbelief and reinforces the absolute importance of individual accountability. The message for every believer is to love their parents with the love that draws them together toward Allah, not with a blind loyalty that competes with the truth. On that Day, only a heart that has lived in awe of its Lord will find peace.

=36=

The flight from human ties reaches its climax in verse thirty-six: “and even their spouse and children.” The spouse is the life partner, the source of intimacy, comfort, and shared secrets. The children are the delight of one’s eyes, the hoped-for continuation of one’s legacy, and the object of the strongest parental love.

Yet on that Day, a person will flee even from them. This is the final stage of the severance of worldly attachments. The Qur’ān is not saying that love will turn into hate, but that the overwhelming terror will make everyone intensely focused on their own salvation. The person will have no mental or emotional space to think of anyone else. This verse, along with the previous ones, forms a powerful parallel to Sūrah ‘Abasa’s earlier theme of priorities. The Prophet was gently corrected for prioritising the powerful pagan over the eager blind man. Here, the surah shows that on the Day of Judgment, all worldly priorities will be upended. The family ties that people often place above Allah will be of no benefit. The lesson is clear: while family bonds are sacred and must be maintained with kindness, they must never become a reason to turn away from the truth. The person who fled from the reminder in this world will, on that Day, flee from the very people he used as an excuse.

For the believer, the verse is a source of both solemn reflection and reassurance. It is a reminder to order one’s loves correctly, placing love of Allah and His Messenger at the pinnacle, and to raise one’s family in faith so that they are not fled from in terror but are reunited in the gardens of Paradise. The verse beautifully sets the stage for the next statement, which explains the reason for this universal flight.

=37=

After depicting the comprehensive flight from family, verse thirty-seven provides the rationale: “For then everyone will have enough concern of their own.” The Arabic phrase “sha’nun yughnīh” is deeply expressive. Sha’n means affair, condition, state, or concern. Yughnīh means it will occupy him fully, it will suffice him, leaving him in need of nothing else because he is entirely engrossed. The verse states that on that Day, every single person will be absorbed in their own personal state to such an extent that they will have no spare attention for anyone else, no matter how beloved. The cause of this total absorption is the overwhelming reality of standing before the Almighty for judgment. The self will be faced with its own record of deeds, its own fears, its own hopes. The normal human psychology of mutual concern will be suspended.

This is not a punishment but a necessary condition of that moment of truth. The individuality of the soul, which was so often buried under social relationships and tribal identities in this world, will stand forth naked and unassisted. The verse reinforces the Qur’ānic theme that no bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another. The believers are consoled by this because it means that the oppressors and the worldly powers cannot harm them on that Day; everyone is busy with their own account. Conversely, it warns those who assume that their family’s status or their ancestors’ piety will help them; all such interwoven chains of support are cut. This profound isolation is the ultimate manifestation of divine justice. The lesson for the living is to prioritise the state of one’s own soul before that day arrives. It calls for a healthy spiritual selfishness in seeking salvation, not at the expense of others but in recognising that one’s primary responsibility is one’s own standing before Allah. The blind man ‘Abdullāh understood this; his urgent concern was his own purification, and he sought it actively.

=38=

With the Day’s terror fully established, the surah now divides humanity into two categories based on their outcome. Verse thirty-eight describes the first group: “On that Day some faces will be bright.

” The word “bright” translates musfirah, from the root safar, which means to shine, to become radiant, to be lit up with light. These faces are luminous with joy, relief, and the light of faith. The brightness is both a physical radiance, as mentioned in other verses where the believers’ light runs before them, and a visible expression of inner bliss. The faces of the saved will reflect the contentment of souls that have received their record in their right hands and know they are destined for eternal delight. The contrast with the previous flight from family is striking. While the disbelievers flee in panic, these bright faces are serene and hopeful. Their worldly relationships may also be a source of joy if those loved ones are also among the blessed, but the primary radiance comes from the individual’s own relationship with Allah. This verse rewards the mindfulness and awe that the blind man ‘Abdullāh carried in his heart. Those who were purified and benefitted from the reminder will have their inner purification manifested as outward radiance. The light on these faces is not a reflection of the sun but a gift from Allah, a direct result of their faith and righteous deeds. It is the visible sign of salvation. The verse provides immense hope to the believers who may suffer in this world, reminding them that a Day is coming when their faces will shine while the faces of their oppressors will be covered with dust. It also serves as an incentive to seek that brightness through following the teachings of the Qur’ān.

=39=

The state of the bright-faced ones is further elaborated in verse thirty-nine: “laughing and rejoicing.” The laughter mentioned here is not the mocking, arrogant laughter of the disbelievers in this world but the pure, joyful laughter of delight and relief.

The word ḍāḥikah conveys an open, genuine happiness. This is the spontaneous expression of a soul that has escaped the terror of Hell and has been granted the gardens of bliss.

The phrase “and rejoicing” translates mustabshirah, which implies a face aglow with the expectation of good news, beaming with pleasure. They are rejoicing at the immense reward, the mercy of their Lord, and the beautiful companionship of Paradise. This verse provides a dramatic emotional release after the intense description of the Deafening Blast and the flight from family. It tells us that the Day of Judgment, for all its terror, culminates in a joy that is beyond human imagination for the righteous. The laughter and rejoicing are not restrained; they are the full, overflowing happiness of a heart that has achieved its purpose.

In the context of the surah, this is the fruit of being eager to learn and having awe of Allah. The one who came to the Prophet with urgency and fear is, on that Day, the one laughing and rejoicing. The worldly roles are reversed. Those who were considered lowly or inconvenient in this world become the radiant, joyous ones. This verse should inspire every believer to seek that laughter, a laughter that is never again tainted by sorrow. It reminds us that the trials and the moments when faith required sacrifice were all worth it, because they lead to this face beaming with joy. The Prophet, who lovingly accepted the divine rebuke, will be the foremost of those laughing and rejoicing, along with the companions who followed his corrected example.

=40=

The stark contrast is presented in verse forty: “while other faces will be dusty.” The conjunction “while” sets up the division of humanity on that Day. The “other faces” belong to the disbelievers and the wicked.

The word “dusty” translates ‘alayhā ghabarah, meaning a coating of dust, like a layer of dirt that covers the skin. This dust is the physical manifestation of their humiliation, gloom, and exhaustion. In stark opposition to the bright, shining faces of the believers, these faces are darkened, soiled, and weary. The dust suggests both the aftermath of a catastrophic collapse—as if they have been crushed and coated with the debris of the world—and the internal darkness of their hearts made visible. The pristine honour they clung to in the world is gone. This verse directly connects back to the earlier condemnation of the ungrateful human being. The one who was indifferent and self-sufficient, who turned away from the reminder, will stand on that Day with a face covered in dust. The imagery is deeply humbling. Wealth, beauty, status, and tribal pride will be replaced by a coat of misery. The dust also symbolises the sterility of their deeds. Just as dust yields no fruit, their worldly actions, which were not done for Allah, are utterly worthless. The verse serves as a powerful warning. It encourages a person to look in the mirror of the soul and ask which face they are preparing for that Day. The surah that began with a gentle frown now concludes with this powerful visual separation of ultimate fates. The dust-covered face is the direct outcome of failing to comply with what Allah ordered, of refusing to consider one’s food and its Provider. It is the visible sign of a lost soul, a warning that should stir the heart to seek the purification and mindfulness that lead to brightness instead.
