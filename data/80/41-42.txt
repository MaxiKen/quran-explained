The description of the condemned faces reaches its final depth in verse forty-one: “cast in gloom.” This short but weighty phrase adds a new dimension to the dusty faces mentioned just before.

The word “gloom” translates qatarah or tarhaquhā qatarah, which conveys a covering of darkness, blackness, and abasement that overcomes the face. If the dust represented the exhaustion and worthlessness of their deeds, the gloom represents the inner despair, humiliation, and sorrow made visible. The face is not just soiled with external dirt; it is darkened by a deep, spiritual blackness. This darkness is the opposite of the radiant light on the faces of the believers. In many Qur’ānic descriptions of the Last Day, the believers’ light will shine before them and on their right, while the hypocrites and disbelievers will be plunged into darkness. Here, the gloom is directly upon their faces, as if they are already being enveloped by the shadows of Hell.

The word “cast” implies that this gloom is a covering thrown over them, not a natural complexion. It is a punishment, a visible mark of their disgrace. The verse forces the listener to visualise the stark contrast: one group is laughing and rejoicing with bright faces, while the other is covered in dust and darkness. This gloom is not merely physical; it is a reflection of the state of their hearts in the world. Their hearts were blind to the truth, and on that Day, the blindness manifests as a visible darkness on their faces. The surah thus completes the physical and emotional portrait of the losers. It is a terrifying warning that urges every soul to seek the purification and mindfulness mentioned in the earlier verses, so that they may not be among those whose faces are overtaken by this gloom. The brevity of the verse heightens its impact, leaving the mind to contemplate the horror of that eternal shadow.

The surah concludes by identifying the people of the dusty, gloomy faces with absolute clarity in verse forty-two: “those are the disbelievers, the wicked sinners.” After the vivid imagery of the Day of Judgment, the revelation now explicitly names the two defining characteristics that led to such a terrible end: disbelief (kufr) and wicked, sinful behaviour (fujūr).

The term “disbelievers” (al-kafarah) refers to those who actively covered up the truth, who rejected the reminder despite its clarity, and who persisted in the indifference and self-sufficiency condemned earlier in the surah. The second term, “the wicked sinners” (al-fajarah), comes from the root fajara, meaning to split open or to plunge into sin. It describes those whose moral restraints are shattered, who indulge in immorality, transgression, and defiance of Allah’s commands without remorse.

The pairing of these two descriptors is critical; it shows that disbelief and sinful conduct are inseparable. The one who rejects faith inevitably leads a life of wickedness, because without the light of revelation, there is no moral compass. Conversely, those who persist in wickedness often end up burying their innate sense of truth, becoming disbelievers. The surah thus ends with a clear division of humanity based on their response to the reminder. On one side are the believers, whose faces are bright and rejoicing; on the other are the disbelievers and wicked, whose faces are dusty and gloom-covered. The entire surah can be seen as a journey from the gentle divine correction of the Prophet regarding a sincere seeker, through the signs of creation and sustenance, to this final, stark outcome. It reminds us that the indifference of the elite and the arrogance of the self-sufficient will result in this ultimate disgrace, while the eagerness and awe of a blind believer will result in radiant joy. The surah’s closing verse leaves no ambiguity; there is no middle ground on that Day. It is a direct call to examine one’s own state: are we among those who honour the reminder, or among those who turn away in self-sufficiency?
