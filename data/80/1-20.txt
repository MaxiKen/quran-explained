=1=

The opening verse of Sūrah ‘Abasa takes us directly into a moment of profound divine correction. It says of the Prophet (ﷺ) that “He frowned and turned his attention away.

” The word “frowned” translates the Arabic ‘abasa, a facial expression showing mild displeasure or constriction, while the turning away indicates a shift of focus. The background, reported in a well-known ḥadīth collected by At-Tirmidhī and others, is that the Prophet (ﷺ) was earnestly engaged in conversation with a group of influential Meccan leaders, hoping to bring them to Islam. During this delicate moment, ‘Abdullāh ibn Umm Maktūm, a blind Muslim eager for guidance, approached and repeatedly interrupted, unaware of the ongoing discussion. The Prophet’s natural human reaction was to show a flicker of discomfort and to continue addressing the elite pagans, as he deeply desired their acceptance of the truth for the benefit of the whole community. This verse captures that subtle, unspoken response. What makes it so remarkable is that the Qur’ān records it not as a sin but as a lesson for all time. The Lord of the Worlds did not overlook even this minor neglect of a sincere seeker. This teaches us that no matter how noble one’s intentions, spiritual priorities must remain clear. Those who come with humility and need should never be sidelined for those who are self-sufficient and indifferent.

The verse also establishes the absolute honesty of the divine revelation; it does not hide the human moments of the Messenger but uses them to elevate his character and instruct the believers. Thus, a simple frown becomes the opening of a sūrah that realigns the priorities of da‘wah, reminding us that the worth of a person is not measured by social standing but by the eagerness of the heart to be purified. It is a gentle yet firm opening that immediately draws the listener’s attention to the timeless value of mercy and accessibility in teaching the faith.

=2=

The second verse reveals the specific reason behind the Prophet’s reaction: “simply because the blind man came to him interrupting.” The Arabic construction uses an interrogative particle to ask, “What would make you know?” but the meaning is that the only cause for frowning was the blind man’s interruption. ‘Abdullāh ibn Umm Maktūm, being blind, could not see the Prophet’s preoccupation with the Quraysh nobles, nor could he perceive the subtle cues of the situation. His repeated calls, “Teach me from what Allah has taught you,” came from a burning desire for guidance. The verse implies that the Prophet turned away solely because the man’s timing seemed inconvenient, not because of any disdain for the man himself.

Yet Allah, the Most Merciful, wanted to etch in the consciousness of the Ummah that physical blindness and social inconvenience are never barriers to the mercy of the message. The man who was blind in his eyes possessed a heart full of insight, and his interruption, however untimely it appeared, was in reality a test of prophetic attentiveness. This rebuke is exceptionally beautiful because it shows that the divine standard prioritises spiritual need over worldly calculations. The one who came seeking to be purified was implicitly more valuable in that moment than those who were indifferent.

The verse also underscores that the sincerity of the seeker transforms a seeming interruption into a blessed opportunity. It is as if the revelation is saying that no moment spent with a genuine student of faith is ever wasted, even if it means momentarily turning away from the powerful. For all callers to truth, this remains a foundational instruction: never underestimate the person who comes to you “interrupting” because their urgency may be a sign of the very eagerness that Allah loves.

=3=

With gentle words, the third verse tells the Prophet, “You never know, O Prophet, perhaps he may be purified.” This is a profound teaching about the limits of human judgment.

The phrase “you never know” is not merely a statement of ignorance but a redirection of the Prophet’s perception. Even the most discerning of human beings cannot peer into the ultimate destiny of a soul. The blind man, ‘Abdullāh ibn Umm Maktūm, might have seemed, from a purely worldly perspective, a simple man with little influence, but his inner readiness was known only to Allah.

The word “purified” here translates yazzakkā, which conveys spiritual cleansing, growth, and the shedding of sins. The verse suggests that the very act of seeking knowledge is a means of purification, and the one who approaches the teacher with a sincere heart is on the brink of being cleansed. The Prophet’s eagerness to invite the powerful chiefs was rooted in a noble hope that their conversion would bring many others, but the divine wisdom reminds him that the purification of a single eager soul is a treasure in itself.

This verse also dismantles any notion that outward status can indicate who deserves the message. In contemporary application, it compels every teacher, every caller to faith, and indeed every believer, to look beyond appearances and social labels. No one can tell whose heart is prepared to receive the light. The “perhaps” leaves the outcome with Allah, meaning the teacher’s duty is to offer the reminder without prejudging who is worthy. The beauty of this instruction is that it liberates us from the tyranny of results; we are not responsible for who becomes purified, but we are responsible for not turning away those who come seeking. The uncertainty in “perhaps” also guards against arrogance, keeping the teacher humble, always aware that the ultimate transformation of hearts is entirely in the hands of the Almighty.

=4=

Building directly on the previous hope, the fourth verse completes the picture: “or he may be mindful, benefitting from the reminder.” The Arabic word yadhdhakkaru means to remember, to take heed, to actively recall what one inherently knows about the Creator. The blind man was not simply asking for dry information; he was seeking a reminder that would awaken his soul.

The term “reminder,” here dhikrā, refers to the revelation itself, which serves as a powerful admonition for those with living hearts. The verse presents two parallel possibilities: that he may be purified, and that he may be mindful and thus draw benefit from the reminder. Together, they illustrate the complete process of spiritual learning: internal purification and the active engagement of the mind and heart with the truth. What is striking is that the benefit of the reminder is not automatic; it depends on the mindfulness of the recipient. The Prophet, by turning away, was in effect delaying this potential benefit for a man who was fully primed to receive it. This verse thus reinforces the error being corrected: the one who was indifferent, the pagan chief, was not in a state of mindfulness, so the reminder would not yet benefit him, whereas the eager blind man was entirely ready. The lesson for all believers is profound. When we present the message of Islam, we must gauge spiritual readiness rather than worldly prominence. A poor, disabled, uneducated person whose heart trembles with awe of Allah is infinitely more receptive than a wealthy, influential person whose heart is encased in self-sufficiency. This divine calibration of priorities teaches that the words of the Qur’ān are a living reminder that yields its fruits only in the soil of a humble, mindful heart. The Prophet later embodied this lesson so completely that he would honour Ibn Umm Maktūm and appoint him as his deputy in Madinah, proving that the one who benefits from the reminder becomes a leader of the mindful.

=5=

After establishing the high rank of the eager seeker, the sūrah now turns to the other side of the incident, beginning with verse five: “As for the one who was indifferent.” This describes the attitude of the wealthy pagan leaders whom the Prophet (ﷺ) had been addressing.

The Arabic term used is istaghnā, which literally means to consider oneself self-sufficient or beyond need. It captures the spiritual disease of arrogance and pride, where a person feels no compelling need for guidance or purification. Such an individual relies on his wealth, status, or intellect and therefore becomes indifferent to the call of truth. The verse is carefully crafted; it does not mention the man’s name or tribe because his identity is irrelevant in the divine scale. What matters is his condition of heart. The Quraysh chiefs to whom the Prophet was speaking were steeped in their lineage and commercial success, and their indifference was a wall that prevented the reminder from penetrating. By drawing this contrast, the Qur’ān teaches that the call to Islam is not about chasing the powerful in the hope that they will bring others by their influence. Rather, the call is for those whose hearts are not yet encased in the delusion of self-sufficiency. The historical context shows the Prophet’s deep, merciful desire that these leaders embrace faith, a desire that was entirely in line with his compassionate nature.

Yet the Lord wanted to refine this prophetic zeal, ensuring that the message is never withheld from the humble while being excessively offered to the indifferent. This is a timeless principle: when someone exudes an attitude of “I have no need of your reminder,” the teacher must not invest all attention there to the neglect of the one who says “I am in desperate need.” The indifference of the elite is portrayed here not as a reason to abandon them entirely but as a reason to not make them the priority over the eager.

=6=

Verse six continues the description of the Prophet’s interaction with the indifferent pagan leader: “you gave him your undivided attention.” The phrase indicates a deliberate and complete turning toward the chief, a form of active engagement and hopeful persuasion. The Prophet was, after all, the most merciful and dedicated of teachers, and his eagerness to guide this man was driven by a profound concern for his eternal wellbeing.

The Arabic phrase taṣaddā conveys the sense of going out of one’s way, leaning in with focus. It shows the intensity of the prophetic effort in da‘wah. The verse is not a condemnation of engaging with disbelievers or people of influence; rather, it is a gentle correction of the allocation of that attention. The Prophet’s action was rooted in his perfect character, and the Qur’ān acknowledges this reality before subtly realigning it. There is a deep psychological and spiritual wisdom here. Often, well-meaning callers to truth invest tremendous energy trying to convince those who are hardened and indifferent, while neglecting sincere souls who are thirsting for even a drop of guidance. The divine instruction is not to ignore the indifferent completely but to never let their worldly stature cause the sincere to be pushed aside. The verb “gave you your undivided attention” also highlights the human limitation of the Prophet: he could not give equal simultaneous attention to everyone. Since he had to choose where to focus in that moment, the revelation indicates that his choice, though understandable, was not the most spiritually optimal one. It is an eternal reminder to weigh our priorities not by the noise or power of the audience but by the purity of their seeking. The one who trembles and asks should hear our voice before the one who turns away in scorn.

=7=

Verse seven brings a beautiful note of relief and clarity: “even though you are not to blame if he would not be purified.” This statement is inserted to protect the Prophet (ﷺ) from any misunderstanding of the previous correction. It makes clear that the Messenger’s duty is only to convey the message with clarity; he is not accountable for the ultimate purification of the listener. The indifferent chief, stuck in his self-sufficiency, may never be purified, and that outcome is not a failure on the part of the Prophet. The verse liberates the teacher from the crushing weight of results.

In the context of the incident, it is as if Allah is saying, “You turned your full attention to him, but you are under no obligation to ensure his purification.” The implication is profound: since his purification is not your responsibility, why then give him priority over the one whose heart is already inclined and whose purification is likely? The logic is gentle yet piercing. It reframes the entire purpose of da‘wah. The call to truth is an offering, not a negotiation. We are not salespeople trying to close a deal with the most prestigious client; we are gardeners watering the seeds that are already showing life.

The phrase also acknowledges a deeper theological reality: guidance is entirely in Allah’s hands. The Prophet (ﷺ) could not purify anyone; he could only provide the reminder that serves as a means. This verse thus reinforces the concept of tawakkul, reliance on Allah. The teacher does his duty, offers the reminder with wisdom, but leaves the outcome with the Almighty. This prevents burnout, despair, and misdirected efforts. It also corrects a common misconception that the Prophets had the ability to compel belief. They were human messengers, and this verse defends the Prophet’s honour by stating that even his intense attention would not be blamed had the chief remained impure, so his attention was better directed elsewhere.

=8=

In verse eight, the focus returns to ‘Abdullāh ibn Umm Maktūm with the words: “But as for the one who came to you, eager to learn.” The Arabic verb yas‘ā means he came striving, walking quickly, making an effort.

This is not a casual visit; it is an intentional journey of the seeker. The blind man’s physical movement toward the Prophet, despite his disability, mirrors the movement of his heart toward guidance.

The word “eager” captures his inner state: he did not come reluctantly or half-heartedly but with a palpable yearning to receive knowledge and purification. This description stands in sharp contrast to the “indifferent” one who was self-sufficient and felt no urgency. The Qur’ān is painting a picture of two archetypes: the arrogant one who stays away or listens with an air of superiority, and the humble one who rushes forward despite obstacles. This eager coming is, in itself, a sign of faith, because no one strives to reach a teacher of truth unless Allah has already placed light in their heart. The verse therefore exalts the very act of seeking sacred knowledge. It teaches that the student’s striving is honoured in the sight of Allah and should be honoured by the teacher. The Prophet, before this revelation, might have subconsciously valued the potential of the powerful man over the obvious need of the blind man, but the divine words recalibrate this completely. Now the standard is revealed: the one who comes to you, striving, regardless of his social station, is the one deserving of your welcoming attention. This verse is a charter of Islamic education, insisting that access to knowledge is a right for every eager heart, not a privilege for the elite. It also highlights the beauty of the Prophet’s later conduct, when he would spread his cloak for this very man, showing that the lesson had been absorbed fully into his noble character.

=9=

The ninth verse adds a critical inner dimension to the blind man’s eagerness: “being in awe of Allah.” The Arabic term is khashyah, a profound form of fear mixed with reverence, love, and deep knowledge of Allah’s majesty.

This is not the terror of a tyrant but the trembling of a heart that recognises the greatness of its Creator. ‘Abdullāh ibn Umm Maktūm did not come merely to satisfy curiosity or gain social standing; he came because his heart was saturated with awe of the Almighty. This quality is the very essence of beneficial knowledge. The verse indicates that the reason he was so eager to learn was precisely this awe; he sensed his own ignorance and the magnitude of what he did not know, and he feared standing before Allah without the guidance he desperately sought. This inner state makes a person profoundly receptive to the reminder. The Prophet’s frown, however slight, was therefore not a response to an ordinary interruption but to the approach of a heart filled with khashyah. The divine correction is all the more poignant because the Qur’ān is teaching that a person with khashyah is never an inconvenience; he is a blessing.

The verse also subtly defines the most important characteristic of a true student of the Qur’ān. Before the ears can truly benefit, the heart must be in a state of humble awe. This connects beautifully with other Qur’ānic verses that say only those who fear Allah will receive the reminder. By describing the blind man in this way, Allah is validating the man’s sincerity and showing that his interruption was spiritually motivated.

For every believer today, this verse serves as a mirror: when we approach the Qur’ān or sacred knowledge, are we coming with the casual attitude of the indifferent one or with the urgent awe of this noble blind companion, whose heart could see what many with eyes could not?

=10=

Verse ten delivers the direct point of correction with the words: “you were inattentive to him.” The Arabic talahhā means to be distracted or to turn away from one thing while occupied with another. The verse acknowledges that the Prophet’s inattention was not out of malice but out of his absorption with the chieftain.

Yet, in the economy of divine justice, even a moment’s neglect of a person with such awe and eagerness required a gentle but clear recalibration. The tone is not one of harsh punishment but of loving education. The Prophet (ﷺ) was being trained to become the most perfect guide for all humanity, and this training involved reorienting his natural human reflexes to align seamlessly with divine priorities.

The verse reveals that the one who possesses khashyah should never experience inattention from the carriers of the reminder. It underscores that the value of a human being is entirely tied to their piety and yearning for truth, not their sight, wealth, or influence. After this revelation, the Prophet completely internalised the lesson. When he later met ‘Abdullāh ibn Umm Maktūm, he would greet him with extraordinary warmth, saying, “Welcome to the one for whom my Lord rebuked me.” He would spread his own cloak for him to sit upon. This transformation shows that the Prophet’s character was a living embodiment of the Qur’ān; he did not resist the correction but embraced it as a gift from his Lord. For all Muslims in positions of teaching and leadership, this verse stands as an eternal reminder to examine who we might be unintentionally neglecting. Is there a sincere soul whom we have turned away from because we were busy with those who seem more important? The verse calls us to actively seek out and attend to the God-conscious, the humble, and the sincere, even if they come at an inconvenient moment. Inattention to them is a loss not to them alone but to the teacher who misses the blessing of their company.

=11=

With verse eleven, the sūrah transitions from the specific historical incident to a broader declaration about the nature of the revelation. It begins with the strong interjection “But no!

” The Arabic kallā is a forceful negation, rejecting any idea that the preceding values might be neglected. It serves as a pivot, as if to say that the truth is far greater than the momentary human interactions just described.

The verse then states: “This revelation is truly a reminder.” Here, the specific message of the sūrah and the entire Qur’ān are identified as a tadhkirah, a powerful reminder that awakens the primordial covenant written in every soul.

The use of “truly” emphasises certainty and importance. The reminder is not a new, alien teaching but a reawakening of what human nature already recognises. By calling it a reminder, the verse connects the etiquette of teaching just outlined to the very essence of the revelation. The Qur’ān is not a book of mere information to be distributed to the highest bidder; it is a soul-stirring reminder that should be offered first to those whose hearts are trembling with awe. The incident with the blind man was a practical demonstration of how the reminder should be delivered, and now the sūrah elevates the reminder itself. It declares that this message is sufficient for all who wish to be mindful. The rebuke to the Prophet (ﷺ) is now placed in the context of the grandeur of the revelation. The Messenger is being taught that the weight and honour of the message he carries demand that he give it to those who reach for it, not chase those who run away.

This verse also acts as a bridge, reminding the reader that the ultimate purpose of these verses is not just to narrate a story but to serve as a living reminder for all generations, calling them to the same mindfulness and fear of Allah that the blind man possessed.

=12=

The logical consequence of the reminder’s nature is given in verse twelve: “So let whoever wills be mindful of it.” The word “so” links the availability of the reminder with the human response. The Qur’ān has been described as an honoured, clear reminder, and now the door is thrown open.

The phrase “whoever wills” (man shā’a) affirms human free will within the framework of divine decree. Allah has provided the reminder in its most perfect and accessible form, and He has endowed human beings with the ability to choose to be mindful. This verse balances the previous emphasis on divine guidance; while guidance ultimately comes from Allah, the individual must turn their will towards the reminder. The expression “be mindful of it” translates yadhdhakkarahu, meaning to actively remember, to take it to heart, to make it a continuous part of one’s consciousness. It is an ongoing, deliberate act, not a one-time acknowledgement. The verse thus places a profound responsibility on each person. No one can claim that guidance was kept from them; the reminder is clear and available. The blind man, despite his physical blindness, willed to be mindful, and he came striving. The pagan leaders, with all their faculties, willed to remain indifferent. The verse dismantles any excuse based on circumstance.

In the context of the sūrah, it is also a direct comfort to the Prophet (ﷺ). He does not need to exhaust himself chasing those who refuse to will; his task is to present the reminder with excellence and then allow each soul to make its choice. This verse liberates the caller from the burden of coercion. The invitation is made, the message is clear, and whoever truly wills to remember can do so. It is an empowering statement that places the accountability for disbelief squarely on the disbeliever’s own rejection of the reminder, while honouring the seeker who, like ‘Abdullāh, made the conscious choice to come forward.

=13=

The sūrah now draws our attention to the sublime nature of the reminder itself, beginning with verse thirteen: “It is written on pages held in honour.” This verse describes the physical or celestial record of the Qur’ān. The pages, or suhuf, refer to the scrolls upon which the revelation was recorded, and by extension, the Preserved Tablet (al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ) from which it was sent down.

The phrase “held in honour” translates mukarramah, meaning honoured, exalted, and dignified. These are not ordinary sheets of parchment; they are pages elevated in status because of the majestic words they bear. The honour of the pages stems entirely from the honour of the One who revealed them and the truth they contain.

This description serves multiple purposes. First, it emphasises the integrity and sanctity of the Qur’ānic text, countering any Meccan accusations that it was poetry or magic. Second, it reminds the Prophet (ﷺ) and the believers that the message they carry is so noble that it deserves to be treated with the utmost respect.

The earlier incident, where the Prophet was corrected for turning away from the blind seeker, is now placed in the shadow of these honoured pages. The teacher of this book must embody its honour by honouring every sincere student, regardless of worldly status. The imagery also inspires awe in the listeners: the book they hear recited is a transcript of heavenly pages.

Thus, the recipient should approach it with the same eagerness and awe that Ibn Umm Maktūm showed. The verse also connects to the Islamic tradition of showing deep respect for the muṣḥaf, the written copy of the Qur’ān, by handling it in a state of purity and with reverence. It is not a mundane object but a physical manifestation of honoured pages that angels themselves attend to.

=14=

Continuing the description of the sacred pages, verse fourteen says they are “highly esteemed, purified.” The word “highly esteemed” (marfū‘ah) conveys the idea of being raised high, both literally in the heavens and in status. This exaltation indicates that the Qur’ān is far above any falsehood or distortion; it occupies a rank that no human speech can attain.

The word “purified” (muṭahharah) adds a further dimension of absolute cleanliness and sanctity. No impurity, whether physical or spiritual, can touch its core essence. The pages are free from any satanic influence, error, or deviation. This purification also points to the fact that the true meanings of the Qur’ān are accessible only to those who are purified, as alluded to in Sūrah al-Wāqi‘ah. Together, these two qualities reinforce the transcendence of the revelation. They assure the believers that what they are receiving is of the highest heavenly standard, untainted by the disagreements and impurities of the earthly realm. For the Prophet (ﷺ), who had just been gently corrected, this description serves as an uplifting reminder that the message he is delivering is far above the petty dynamics of the Meccan chiefs. The status of the book he carries is so lofty that his own honour is inextricably linked to it, and his actions, in delivering it, must reflect its lofty purity.

This verse also carries a profound lesson for every reciter and student: the Qur’ān must be approached with a sense of its exaltation and with a heart purified from arrogance and distraction. The blind man came with a heart full of awe and a desire for purification, which made him the perfect recipient of a message described as purified and esteemed.

Thus, the qualities of the book mirror the required qualities of its true seeker.

=15=

Verse fifteen reveals the celestial agents through whom these honoured pages were transmitted: “by the hands of angel-scribes.” The term safarah, translated as “angel-scribes,” can also mean ambassadors or messengers. These are the angels who serve as intermediaries between Allah and His prophets, carrying the revelation from the Preserved Tablet to the earthly realm. The chief among them is Jibrīl (peace be upon him), who is described elsewhere as trustworthy and mighty.

The phrase “by the hands” personalises the transmission, indicating direct contact and meticulous care. The angels write and convey the message with absolute fidelity. This depiction counters the false claims of the Meccans that the Prophet (ﷺ) was taught by a mere human or that he received the revelation from devils. The true scribes are noble heavenly beings, not fallen spirits or poets. Their involvement elevates the status of the Qur’ān even further, connecting it to the realm of pure obedience and devotion.

The verse also indirectly teaches the virtue of the act of writing sacred knowledge. The angels themselves are scribes, recording the divine decree and the revelation.

For believers, this is an encouragement to write down, preserve, and honour the Qur’ān through the written word. The reference to hands does not imply human-like limbs for the angels; rather, it uses language understood by people to convey the care, skill, and direct involvement of these beings in the process of revelation. This entire beautiful imagery of honoured pages, high and pure, held by the hands of angelic ambassadors, instils a profound reverence for the book. It also serves as a contrast to the human interactions described earlier in the sūrah. The Prophet’s momentary inattention to the blind man is set against this cosmic backdrop of angelic precision and honour, highlighting that the message the Prophet delivers is not his own but a trust of unimaginable grandeur, and thus its distribution must be handled with the highest ethical standards.

=16=

The description of the angel-scribes culminates in verse sixteen with the words “honourable and virtuous.” The Arabic terms are kirāmin bararah. Kirām means noble, generous, and inherently good in character. Bararah refers to those who are devoutly obedient, righteous, and pure in their worship of Allah.

Thus, the angels who carried the revelation are not only of high rank but also of impeccable moral and spiritual integrity. There is no chance of betrayal, forgetfulness, or alteration. Their nature perfectly mirrors the qualities of the message they carry.

This verse also provides a profound lesson about the relationship between honour and virtue. In the Islamic worldview, true honour cannot be separated from righteousness and obedience. The angelic scribes are not just powerful; they are also deeply righteous. This serves as a model for anyone who handles the Qur’ān, whether through memorisation, teaching, or scholarship. One must strive to embody nobility of character and devotion to Allah, becoming a vessel worthy of the message.

The mention of these angelic qualities immediately after the incident with the blind man subtly draws a parallel: the Prophet (ﷺ), the human carrier of the Qur’ān, is being trained to exemplify the same virtues in his conduct. To be honourable is to welcome the sincere seeker; to be virtuous is to prioritise the fearful heart over the indifferent elite. By describing the angel-scribes in this way, the sūrah reassures the Prophet that he is in the company of the most noble beings in creation, even as he is lovingly guided. It also demolishes the pagan notion that the revelation could be the work of mischievous spirits. The bringers of the Qur’ān are models of piety, so the message can only lead to the highest human excellence. This verse etches into the believer’s heart the dignity and moral demand of interacting with the Word of God.

=17=

With verse seventeen, the sūrah shifts powerfully from the description of the glorious revelation to a sharp rebuke of the disbelieving human being. It declares: “Condemned are disbelieving humans! How ungrateful they are to Allah!

” The Arabic opening “qutila al-insān” is a severe expression literally meaning “may he be killed” or “he is destroyed,” used here as a curse or a declaration of utter loss. It targets al-insān, the generic human being who persists in disbelief and ingratitude despite the overwhelming evidence of Allah’s mercy and power. The rhetorical question “How ungrateful they are!” (mā akfarah) expresses astonishment at the intensity of their rejection. What makes a human being, who has been granted life, intellect, and the honoured reminder carried by noble angels, respond with such profound ingratitude? The context of the sūrah provides a stark contrast: on one side stands the blind but grateful believer, ‘Abdullāh ibn Umm Maktūm, and on the other side stands the disbelieving human, steeped in denial. The ingratitude here is kufr, which originally means to cover up or to bury the truth. The human being covers the blessings of Allah with arrogance and refusal. This verse echoes the earlier description of the “indifferent” person but now universalises it to all who share that attitude. It is a powerful warning that the blessings of creation, sustenance, and revelation are so immense that rejecting them is an unimaginable betrayal.

The use of such strong language is meant to shake the listener awake. It challenges every person to examine their own response to the countless favours of their Lord, reminding them that ingratitude is not a small failing but the very root of spiritual destruction.

=18=

Following the condemnation of human ingratitude, the sūrah immediately begins to dismantle the arrogance that causes it by asking in verse eighteen: “From what substance did He create them?” This rhetorical question is the first step in a powerful argument that draws the human being’s attention to his utterly humble origin.

The question forces the ungrateful person to reflect on the very beginning of their existence. It is not asked because Allah does not know, but to compel the human being to acknowledge a truth that he often forgets. The substance referred to is not mentioned explicitly in this verse, leaving the listener in suspense and preparing the mind for the answer in the next verse. The purpose of the question is to induce humility. The one who struts around in arrogance and turns away from the reminder must pause and consider his own material beginnings. The creation of a human being from an insignificant, lowly substance stands as an undeniable proof of the Creator’s power and the creature’s inherent dependence. If the human being reflects honestly, he must admit that he did not create himself, nor did he come from something grand and self-sufficient. The verse thus connects back to the theme of the sūrah: those who are indifferent and feel self-sufficient (istaghnā) need to be reminded of their origin in a drop of sperm, something that would normally cause a person to feel humble and shy.

The question also serves as a transition from the high heavenly realm of the honoured pages and angel-scribes to the earthly realm of biological creation, showing that the same Lord who sends down revelation is the One who originates human life from the most humble beginnings. The contrast deflates human arrogance.

=19=

Verse nineteen answers the previous question with devastating precision: “He created them from a sperm-drop, and ordained their development.” The “sperm-drop” translates nuṭfah, which refers to a tiny, insignificant fluid that is the origin of human life.

This is a recurring Qur’ānic theme meant to humble human beings. The lowly nature of the starting material exposes the absurdity of human arrogance; the same person who now feels self-sufficient and turns away from truth was once a mere drop that could have been washed away and forgotten.

The second part of the verse, “and ordained their development,” translates faqaddarah, meaning He then proportioned, measured, and destined the stages of growth. From that single drop, Allah created a complex human being with limbs, senses, intellect, and a spiritual capacity. This ordination includes the genetic blueprint, the precise developmental stages in the womb, and the unique characteristics of each individual. The wording implies an intricate, purposeful design, not a random, chaotic emergence.

The phrase also carries the meaning of decreeing one’s life span, provisions, and deeds. So, not only does Allah create the physical form from a drop, but He also decrees the course of the person’s life. This double action—creation from a lowly origin and meticulous ordination—proves the power and wisdom of the Creator. For the ungrateful human, this verse is a mirror. If Allah brought you from a drop to a full human being, how can you be indifferent to His reminder? The contrast with the earlier verses about the honoured revelation is striking: the same Lord who sends down the most exalted pages from the heavens also carefully fashions a human being from the humblest of substances. Both actions testify to His mercy and deserve profound gratitude, not the arrogance of self-sufficiency and the rejection of His signs.

=20=

Verse twenty continues the description of Allah’s abundant favours upon the human being, stating: “Then He makes the way easy for them.” After creation and the ordination of development, the human being is not left to struggle alone. The “way” refers to the entire course of life that follows birth. The verb yassara means to make smooth, to facilitate, to ease. It applies to the physiological process of birth itself, which, despite its pains, is a facilitated passage out of the womb. It also extends to the journey of life: Allah has made the paths of sustenance, growth, and understanding easy. The infant finds the mother’s milk ready; the child’s intellect develops in stages that allow for learning; the natural world is subjugated for human benefit. Even the path to guidance is made easy for the one who sincerely seeks it, as demonstrated by the blind man ‘Abdullāh who was guided to the Prophet and received the reminder. The verse thus completes a triad of divine actions: creation from a humble drop, precise proportioning and decree, and the facilitation of life’s path.

This is the ultimate argument against ingratitude. The human being who is condemned in verse seventeen is the one who receives all these generosities and yet denies the Giver. The way being made easy also carries a moral and spiritual dimension. Allah has sent messengers, revealed books, and granted intellect so that the human being can easily recognise truth from falsehood. The indifference described earlier in the sūrah is therefore entirely unjustifiable. There is no insurmountable obstacle blocking the path to faith; the obstacles are created by the heart’s own arrogance. By mentioning this ease, the verse subtly invites the disbeliever to reconsider. If every step of your existence was made easy, will you not make easy your own heart’s turning toward the One who facilitated it all? It is a tender and logical call that completes the foundation for the next verses, which speak of death and resurrection.
