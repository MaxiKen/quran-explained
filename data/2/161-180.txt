=161=

"Indeed, those who disbelieve and die while they are disbelievers — upon them is the curse of Allah and of the angels and of the people all together."

The door of repentance in the previous verse was wide; this verse shows what happens when a person walks past it until death locks the gate. Disbelief here is not a passing doubt; it is a state held until the last breath. Upon such people is la'nah — curse, expulsion from mercy — from Allah, from the angels, and from people all together. The wording is heavy on purpose. It is not one voice of condemnation but a unanimity of creation against a soul that refused its Lord until refusal became permanent.

Dying upon disbelief is the point. Many verses leave room while life remains; this one speaks of the end of that room. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that a man may do the deeds of the people of Paradise until only an arm's length remains, then the decree overtakes him and he does the deeds of the people of the Fire and enters it — and the reverse is also true. Endings matter. That is why he taught frequent prayer for a good ending and why the companions feared their final state more than their résumés.

"The people all together" includes even those who shared the disbelief in the world; on the Day, alliances of falsehood dissolve into mutual cursing (29:25). For the living reader the verse is not an invitation to despair over others but a warning about delay. As long as the heart beats, tawbah is nearer than the jugular. Once it stops upon rejection, the curse described here is not rhetoric. It is the verdict.

=162=

"Abiding eternally therein. The punishment will not be lightened for them, nor will they be reprieved."

The curse of the previous verse is given its location and its duration. Khalidin fiha — abiding therein forever. No parole built into the sentence for those who died upon clear rejection after proof. "The punishment will not be lightened for them" closes the hope that time itself might dull the torment; "nor will they be reprieved" closes the hope of a postponement once the Hour has arrived.

The Quran uses this severity as mercy for people still able to choose. A warning that softens the truth about eternity is not compassion; it is betrayal of the listener. The Prophet, peace be upon him, wept until his beard was wet when some companions laughed lightly, and he said if you knew what I know you would laugh little and weep much. He also described the Fire in ways that make this verse concrete — heat, regret, skins replaced so pain does not expire (4:56) — not to entertain fear but to make neglect impossible for a sane heart.

Eternity is the weight that makes a decade of stubborn pride look insane in retrospect. The verse asks a simple question while breath remains: is anything you are clinging to worth an unlightened, unreprieved forever? If the answer is no, act on the no before the gate of 161 closes.

=163=

"And your God is one God. There is no deity except Him, the Entirely Merciful, the Especially Merciful."

After the terror of dying upon disbelief, the surah sets down the truth that saves: your God is One. Ilahukum ilahun wahid. Not one among many, not a high god with junior partners, not a force that needs children or intercessors as equals. La ilaha illa Huwa — nothing worthy of worship except Him. The sentence is short enough for a child and deep enough for a lifetime of scholarship.

Then two names that prevent tawhid from being heard as cold: al-Rahman, al-Rahim. The One who alone deserves worship is the One whose mercy fills the horizon and whose mercy reaches the particular servant. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that Allah's mercy outstrips His wrath, and that He divided mercy into a hundred parts, sent one down by which creation shows kindness, and kept ninety-nine for the Day people will need it most. Oneness without mercy could crush; mercy without oneness becomes vague sentiment. Together they are Islam's centre.

This verse is a hinge. Behind it lies the fate of those who refused. Ahead of it lie the signs in creation (164) that point back to this One. Whenever the world feels crowded with rival lords — money, audience, desire, fear of people — return to this line. One God. No other. Merciful twice named, so that turning to Him is never turning to a tyrant without care.

=164=

"Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth, and the alternation of the night and the day, and the ships that sail through the sea with that which benefits people, and what Allah has sent down from the heaven of water, giving life thereby to the earth after its lifelessness, and dispersing therein every kind of creature, and the directing of the winds, and the clouds controlled between the heaven and the earth are signs for a people who use reason."

Tawhid is not left as a claim without evidence. The verse opens a gallery of ayat — signs — hanging in plain sight. Heavens and earth in their scale and order. Night and day in their precise swap, neither swallowing the other permanently (36:40). Ships that float by laws He wrote, carrying food and people. Rain that hits dead ground and pulls green life out of dust. Creatures spread in every niche. Winds driven where they are sent. Clouds suspended and steered between sky and soil. Each is ordinary enough to ignore and extraordinary enough, if you stop, to undo atheism for a thinking mind.

"For a people who use reason" — ya'qilun. The Quran does not praise empty gazing. It praises the intellect that moves from sign to Signer. The Prophet, peace be upon him, would look at the sky and recite verses of reflection, and he said there are signs in the selves and on the horizons (41:53). Modern knowledge has only lengthened the gallery: cellular machinery, orbital mechanics, water's odd properties that make life possible. The signs did not shrink because microscopes arrived. They multiplied.

The practical habit this verse wants is interruption. Once a day, let a cloud or a ship or a glass of water complete its journey to the heart: this is not random, and I am being addressed. Reason that stops at the mechanism and refuses the Maker is not the reason named here. The verse is an invitation to think all the way home.

=165=

"And yet among the people are those who take other than Allah as equals; they love them as they should love Allah. But those who believe are stronger in love for Allah. And if only those who did wrong could see, when they see the punishment, that all power belongs to Allah and that Allah is severe in punishment."

The tragedy after the signs is not ignorance of the sky; it is misdirected love. Some people take andad — rivals, equals, partners — and love them the way only Allah should be loved. The rivals may be idols of stone, or living personalities, or the self, or wealth, or a nation raised to absolute loyalty. Love is the clue to worship. Where the heart fears total loss, seeks ultimate refuge, and obeys without limit, there its god is.

"But those who believe are stronger in love for Allah." Ashaddu hubban lillah. Faith is not colder than idolatry; it is hotter in the right direction. The believer's love for Allah outranks every other attachment without erasing legitimate loves of family and beauty; it orders them. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that none of you truly believes until he loves him more than child, father, and all people, and that the sweetness of faith is tasting that Allah and His messenger are more beloved than anything else.

"If only the wrongdoers could see when they see the punishment…" On that Day the mirage of the rival's power collapses. All strength belongs to Allah. The severity of punishment matches the crime of giving a creature the love of the Creator. The verse asks you to run that film now, while love can still be redirected. Who, if removed from your life, would make obedience to Allah feel optional? That answer is where andad hide.

=166=

"When those who were followed disassociate themselves from those who followed them, and they see the punishment, and all relationships are cut through."

Picture the Day as the verse does. Leaders, icons, scholars of misguidance, celebrities of desire — those who were followed — suddenly pull away from their own followers. Tabarra'a — they disown, wash their hands. The bonds that felt like family in the world, the group chats of shared mockery, the parties of shared sin, the movements of shared ideology, are cut as if with a blade. Asbab — ties, means, relationships — fall severed.

This is the end of every cult of personality that competed with Allah. The followed cannot save the follower; they cannot even bear to stand beside them. The Quran repeats the scene in many forms: partners abandoned (6:94), friends turned enemies (43:67), the weak arguing with the arrogant (14:21). The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned most severely those who mislead others, and he said whoever starts a bad sunnah carries its burden and the burden of those who act on it without theirs diminishing.

For anyone choosing whom to follow — a preacher, a trend, a friend group — the verse is due diligence. Follow only those you would not be ashamed to stand with when all relationships are cut and the only relationship that remains is with Allah. If your leader would disown you on that Day to save his own face, he is not a leader worth your life now.

=167=

"And those who followed will say, 'If only we had another turn so we could disassociate from them as they have disassociated from us.' Thus will Allah show them their deeds as regrets upon them, and they are never to emerge from the Fire."

The followers answer the leaders' betrayal with a wish that arrives too late: one more chance in the world so we could disown them first. The wish is granted only as a mirror — they see what loyalty to falsehood bought — not as a return ticket. "Allah will show them their deeds as regrets upon them." Hasarat — griefs that sit on the chest. Every laugh at the truth, every share of the lie, every step behind a guide who was walking into the Fire becomes a separate weight of regret.

"And they are never to emerge from the Fire." The door that was open in life does not reopen by nostalgia. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that the least-punished person in the Fire will think he is the most punished, so severe is even the lightest share — a warning against gambling on "I will not be the worst."

The cure is upstream of the regret. Disassociate now from paths that require disobeying Allah, while disassociation still helps. Choose companions who remind you of Him (18:28). Leave a following that feeds your ego and starves your akhirah. The worst sentence in this verse is not the Fire alone; it is knowing you could have switched sides when switching was still possible.

=168=

"O mankind, eat from whatever is on earth that is lawful and good, and do not follow the footsteps of Satan. Indeed, he is to you a clear enemy."

The address widens to all people — ya ayyuha al-nas — and lands in the most daily of acts: eating. Eat of what is on the earth, but with two filters: halal, lawful by Allah's decree, and tayyib, good, pure, wholesome. Islam does not invent monastic hunger. It sanctifies appetite by fencing it. The earth is a table laid with provision; Satan's project is to either forbid what Allah allowed through false piety or allow what Allah forbade through appetite without limit.

"Do not follow the footsteps of Satan." Footsteps — khutuwat — are plural and gradual. He rarely drags a person into the worst sin in one pull. He leaves tracks: a doubtful bite, a "small" haram, a habit of not checking. Each step makes the next easier. "He is to you a clear enemy." Not a frenemy, not a useful trickster. An enemy whose enmity has been announced since Adam (2:168 is of a piece with 7:22 and 20:117).

The Prophet, peace be upon him, said the halal is clear and the haram is clear and between them are doubtful matters; whoever avoids the doubtful protects his religion. He also taught that a body nourished on haram is less available to answered du'a. Eating is worship when it is grateful and lawful, and it is a leash in Satan's hand when it is not. Check the plate. Check the income that bought the plate. The enemy is clear; the only question is whether the footsteps are being followed or refused.

=169=

"He only orders you to evil and immorality and to say about Allah what you do not know."

Satan's entire programme is summarised in three moves. He commands al-su' — evil that harms; al-fahsha' — indecency, the sins that soil honour and public morals; and saying about Allah what you do not know — theology by guesswork, fatwas of desire, claiming "God is fine with this" without proof. The third is the most sophisticated. Many who would never touch open indecency will still speak over revelation with confidence they did not earn.

"He only orders you" — the restriction is important. He does not command good. When a suggestion comes that clearly leads to sin, shame, or inventing rulings, the believer has identified the source enough to refuse it. The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught isti'adhah — seeking refuge with Allah from the rejected Satan — at the start of Quran recitation and in moments of anger, because anger is one of the doors through which those orders enter.

Watch your speech about Allah especially. "I feel like Islam would allow…" is not evidence. "My culture always did…" is not revelation. The enemy loves religious-sounding fiction. Answer him with what is known from Book and Sunnah, or with honest silence until you learn. Evil, indecency, and invented claims about God: if a path needs all three or any one of them, it is not the path of the Rahman.

=170=

"And when it is said to them, 'Follow what Allah has revealed,' they say, 'Rather, we will follow that which we found our fathers upon.' Even though their fathers understood nothing, nor were they guided?"

The appeal to ancestors is among the oldest refusals of revelation. Follow the Book. Reply: we follow what we found our fathers on. The Quran does not honour that as heritage; it interrogates it. Even if the fathers understood nothing and were not guided? Blind tradition is not a sanad. A chain of transmission only has value if the first link is connected to truth.

This was the argument of the Makkans around idols, and it appears whenever a community elevates custom over the clear command of Allah — in marriage abuses, inheritance theft, nationalism that outranks shariah, or family pressure to abandon prayer. The Prophet, peace be upon him, came to a people steeped in ancestral pride and rewired their loyalty: no obedience to a creature in disobedience to the Creator.

Honouring parents is a great duty in Islam (17:23), but it never includes following them into misguidance. The verse gives permission to love a father and still refuse his error. Understanding and guidance are the criteria — 'aql and huda — not age and repetition. If your fathers had both, following them is following truth. If they had neither, repeating their path is choosing darkness with company rather than light alone.

=171=

"The example of those who disbelieve is like that of one who shouts at what hears nothing but calls and cries — deaf, dumb, and blind, so they do not understand."

A shepherd calls to sheep; the sheep hear sound without grasping meaning. That is the image laid over those who refuse the message. Words reach the ear as noise — sermons, verses, advice — and exit without becoming understanding. Summun bukmun 'umyun — deaf, dumb, blind — the same triad used earlier for the hypocrites' spiritual senses (2:18). Here it is the disbeliever who has let the warning bounce off so long that the senses of guidance no longer work.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that hearts rust as iron rusts, and that the polish is seeking forgiveness and remembering Allah. He also likened the heart that receives reminder to good land that holds water and grows vegetation, and the heart that does not to hard ground or a bare plain (the hadith of the valley and rain). Exposure alone does not equal reception.

If you teach or call, this verse steadies you: some lack of response is not your poor phrasing; it is a blockage on the other side. Convey clearly, then leave the opening of ears to Allah. If you listen, the verse is a self-scan. When reminder comes, do you hear only tone and personality, or do you hear the call? Understanding — ya'qilun — is the difference between being livestock in the image and being a human addressed by his Lord.

=172=

"O you who believe, eat from the good things which We have provided for you and be grateful to Allah if it is Him that you worship."

The believers are addressed with a near echo of the call to mankind, but now gratitude is tied openly to worship. Eat of the tayyibat — the good provisions We provided. Provision is named as gift, not self-creation. Then: be grateful to Allah if you truly worship Him. Shukr becomes a proof of sincerity. A person who claims to worship while treating food, health, and income as pure entitlement has a crack in the claim.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, would eat with praise, lick his fingers, and teach that Allah is pleased with the servant who eats a meal and praises Him for it. He said looking at a lawful bite with gratitude is a form of worship the heedless miss. Gratitude is also protection: the Quran ties increase to shukr (14:7) and ties loss of blessing to ingratitude in the stories of past towns.

This verse also gently polices false asceticism. Believers are not told to renounce good food; they are told to eat it and thank. Monastic refusal of the tayyib can be its own kind of arrogance. The middle path is a full plate that is halal, and a full heart that knows Who filled it. If He alone is worshipped, the tongue and the limbs will show thanks — not only in alhamdulillah after meals, but in obedience bought with the strength those meals gave.

=173=

"He has only forbidden to you dead animals, blood, the flesh of swine, and that which has been dedicated to other than Allah. But whoever is forced by necessity, neither desiring it nor transgressing — there is no sin upon him. Indeed, Allah is Forgiving and Merciful."

The wide permission of good food is given a short, clear list of prohibitions. Carrion — animals that died without proper slaughter. Blood poured forth. Swine flesh. And anything slaughtered in a name other than Allah's, because food is tied to tawhid; dedicating a sacrifice to a false god is worship with meat. "Only" these — the religion does not bury people under endless food taboos invented as culture.

Then the safety valve of necessity: idtirar. Whoever is compelled — famine, true lack of alternative — without craving the haram for its own sake and without eating beyond the need that keeps life, no sin upon him. The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught that necessity lifts liability within its limit, and the scholars built from this the rule that hardship brings ease. Forgiveness and mercy close the verse so the desperate do not add despair of Allah to their hunger.

Modern application is both strict and humane. Do not normalise the forbidden list under the name of taste or integration. Do not invent new eternal prohibitions without proof. And do not let a real emergency become an open door to appetite once the emergency ends. The same Lord who forbade is the One who forgives the one who had no lawful way and took only what kept him alive.

Ethics in conflict are part of faith, not decoration after victory. The same Book that allows force fences it. If your anger cannot accept a fence, it is not yet fighting in the way of Allah.

=174=

"Indeed, those who conceal what Allah has sent down of the Book and exchange it for a small price — they consume into their bellies nothing but fire. Allah will not speak to them on the Day of Resurrection, nor will He purify them, and for them is a painful punishment."

Concealment again, now priced. They hide what Allah sent down and take a small price in exchange — a fee, a position, a seat at a table, social peace. What enters their bellies from that trade is described as fire. The image is brutal on purpose: the bribe, the salary for silence, the gift for a favourable twisted ruling, feels like food now and becomes fire later.

Three deprivation's on the Day: Allah will not speak to them — no honourable address; will not purify them — no cleansing of their stain; and painful punishment. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said there are three to whom Allah will not speak, nor look at, nor purify, and they have a painful punishment — including the one who withholds excess water from the traveller and forms of commercial deceit. Silence sold for gain sits in that moral family.

"A small price" is all any worldly gain is when set beside what was sold. A career built on buried verses is still small. If you hold knowledge people need — a witness statement, a fatwa that prevents wrong, a verse that corrects your community's favourite sin — and you hide it for comfort, this verse is talking about the meal you think you earned.

=175=

"Those are the ones who have exchanged guidance for error and forgiveness for punishment. How patient they are for the Fire."

The trade is itemised. Guidance given away for misguidance; forgiveness given away for punishment. They had access to the first of each pair and walked to the counter with the second. "How patient they are toward the Fire" — ma asbarahum 'ala al-nar — is biting irony. They show, in their persistence upon the path to the Fire, a stubborn endurance that would have been noble if aimed at Paradise. The energy is not missing; the direction is inverted.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, said paradise is surrounded by disliked things and the Fire by desires. Endurance is required on both roads; one endurance ends in coolness and the other in the irony of this verse. Watching people work overtime for what harms them — scams, addictions, ideologies that hollow the chest — is watching asbarahum 'ala al-nar in real time.

The mercy in the sarcasm is a chance to redirect stamina. If you can be patient upon a sin's costs, you can be patient upon quitting it. If you can tolerate the Fire's path, you can tolerate the slightly harder morning of the path of forgiveness. Switch the trade while the market of this life is still open.

=176=

"That is because Allah has sent down the Book in truth. And indeed, those who differ over the Book are in extreme dissension."

Why such a fate for concealers and traders of revelation? Because the Book was sent down in truth — bil-haqq — with a weight that makes tampering a cosmic crime, not a clerical error. Truth as the mode of revelation means it matches reality: who Allah is, what souls owe, what the Hour will open. To hide it or split it for sectarian sport is to fight reality itself.

"Those who differ over the Book are in deep schism" — shiqaq ba'id, a far-reaching rift. Difference here is not the legitimate variety of scholarly ijtihad among people who all submit to the text. It is the opposition that pulls the Book apart to protect desires and parties. The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned that the nations before you divided and that his ummah would split, and he pointed to the jama'ah and to his sunnah as rope when the splitting starts.

Hold the Book as truth, not as a buffet. Where clear verses speak, step out of shiqaq. Where scholars differ in good faith, differ with adab without turning the Book into flags for far-flung camps. Extreme dissension is not a sign of intellectual vitality here; it is a warning light that the Book has stopped being the judge and has started being the weapon.

Ethics in conflict are part of faith, not decoration after victory. The same Book that allows force fences it. If your anger cannot accept a fence, it is not yet fighting in the way of Allah.

=177=

"Righteousness is not that you turn your faces toward the east or the west, but righteousness is that one believes in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Book, and the prophets; and gives wealth, in spite of love for it, to relatives, orphans, the needy, the traveller, those who ask, and for freeing slaves; and establishes prayer and gives zakah; and those who fulfil their promise when they promise; and the patient in poverty and hardship and during battle. Those are the ones who have been true, and it is those who are the mindful."

One of the Quran's great definition verses. After the qibla debates, Allah cuts the obsession with the outward angle of the face: birr is not east or west as a performance. Birr is a structure with layers. First creed: Allah, Last Day, angels, Book, prophets — the unseen frame that makes good deeds meaningful. Then wealth given while still loved — not leftovers after the heart has cooled on money, but giving that pinches. The list of recipients starts near and widens: relatives, orphans, needy, traveller, petitioners, ransoming slaves. Then the pillars of prayer and zakah. Then integrity of promises. Then sabr in three climates: poverty, illness and pain, and the heat of battle.

"Those are the ones who have been true" — sadaqu — their claim of faith matches their life. "And it is those who are the mindful" — al-muttaqun. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said the most beloved people to Allah are those most beneficial to people, and he tied faith to wanting for others what you want for yourself. He also called the one who breaks promises a hypocrite in sign.

Memorise the shape of this verse as a mirror. Direction of prayer matters — the surah already established that — but without this interior and social substance, facing any qibla is a body without birr. Belief, giving while love of money still bites, prayer, zakah, kept promises, patience under three fires: that is the uprightness the east and west were never enough to replace.

=178=

"O you who believe, prescribed for you is legal retribution in the matter of the murdered — the free for the free, the slave for the slave, the female for the female. But whoever overlooks from his brother anything, then there should be a suitable follow-up and payment to him with good conduct. This is a lightening from your Lord and a mercy. But whoever transgresses after that will have a painful punishment."

Life is sacred; murder tears the social fabric. Qisas — just retribution — is prescribed so that blood is not answered by endless tribal overkill or by cheap indifference. The equality named — free for free, slave for slave, female for female — answered Jahiliyyah abuses in which a powerful clan would refuse justice for a victim they looked down on, or kill many for one. Islam put the scales in the middle.

Then the door of mercy inside justice: if the victim's heir pardons, the path opens to diyah — blood money — on fair terms, paid with ihsan, good conduct, not humiliation. "A lightening and a mercy from your Lord." The default of law includes space for pardon. The Prophet, peace be upon him, never was given a choice between two matters but that he chose the easier when it was not sin, and he encouraged pardon in qisas while affirming the heir's right. Transgression after settlement — taking blood money then killing anyway, or demanding more than right — brings painful punishment.

Modern readers sometimes hear only severity. Hear also the mercy clause and the aim stated in the next verse: life. A community that neither ignores murder nor drowns in vendetta is a community where people can walk outside. Justice with a road to forgiveness is how that balance is kept.

=179=

"And there is for you in legal retribution life, O people of understanding, that you may become mindful."

The paradox is the point. In qisas there is life. Not death-worship, but life protected. When the murderer knows that the law may take his life in just measure, many murders are deterred before they happen. When families know justice will be done by a court rather than by their own excess, cycles of revenge cool. The dead victim is honoured; the living are safer.

"O people of understanding" — uli al-albab. The rule is for people who can see past the surface of a single execution to the lives preserved by the rule's existence. "That you may become mindful" — la'allakum tattaqun. Taqwa here includes the restraint of the powerful hand that could kill and the restraint of the grieving hand that could massacre a household for one crime.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, judged in blood cases with both firmness and the offer of pardon, and he said the destruction of the world is less to Allah than the unjust killing of a single believer. Retribution, rightly applied, serves that sanctity. Without it, life is cheap. With it, measured and open to mercy, life is expensive again — expensive enough that people think twice. That is how "in retribution there is life" stops being a riddle and becomes policy written on the heart.

=180=

"Prescribed for you when death approaches one of you if he leaves wealth is that he make a bequest for the parents and near relatives according to what is acceptable — a duty upon the righteous."

Before the fuller inheritance laws of Surah al-Nisa fixed shares, and still in the spirit of care that those laws later organised, this verse trains the dying believer not to leave chaos behind. If wealth is left, a wasiyyah — bequest — should be made with ma'ruf, fairness recognised by decent custom and later by shariah limits, toward parents and near relatives. It is written upon the muttaqin as a duty of taqwa, not a tip for the unusually kind.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, said a Muslim should not pass two nights with something to will without having his will written with him. He also limited bequests to non-heirs and to a third of the estate so that fixed heirs are not robbed by deathbed favouritism — a balance between the dying person's voice and the family's right. Fairness is the soul of the verse. A will used to punish a daughter or to starve an orphaned relative is not this wasiyyah.

Death approaches every day one day closer. Writing what is just while the mind is clear is part of readiness for the return to Allah — less a legal technicality than a final act of birr toward people who will live with the consequences of how you left the table.
