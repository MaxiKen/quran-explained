=121=

"Those to whom We have given the Book recite it as it should be recited — it is they who believe in it. And whoever disbelieves in it — it is they who are the losers."

After the hard talk about envy, exclusive claims to Paradise, and the chase for people's approval, the Quran points to a living standard: not who holds a Book, but who holds it properly.

"Recite it as it should be recited" — yatlunahu haqqa tilawatihi — is not about melody alone. Tilawah in Arabic means to follow as well as to recite. Haqqa tilawah therefore gathers three things into one: pronouncing it carefully, understanding what it says, and living what it calls for. A man can recite every letter beautifully and remain distant from it if nothing in his practice moves. Another may stammer but weep and change his life upon hearing it; that second person is closer to reciting as it should be. Ibn Mas'ud explained it as making lawful what it makes lawful, unlawful what it makes unlawful, believing its clear verses, and not twisting its ambiguous ones into excuses. The Quran describes such readers elsewhere: "When the verses of the Most Merciful are recited to them, they fall in prostration and weeping" (19:58).

The Prophet, peace be upon him, said the best of you are those who learn the Quran and teach it, and he warned of the person who recites so that people say "what a reciter" while the heart never bowed. Recitation without following is a kind of theatre; following without recitation loses the words that feed the following. Both together are the haqq.

"It is they who believe in it." True belief shows in this kind of reading. And "whoever disbelieves in it — it is they who are the losers" returns the merchant's word khasirun. The Book was capital given to be traded for eternity. To reject it after receiving it is not neutrality; it is active loss of the one investment that could not be replaced.

The verse praises a righteous remnant among the People of the Book — those like Abdullah ibn Salam who recognised the truth when it stood in front of them — and it sets the bar for this ummah. Do we recite as it should be recited, or do we recite as we were taught to rush it between notifications? The measure is not fluency. It is whether the Book is in front of us as a guide, or behind us as a neglected inheritance.

=122=

"O Children of Israel, remember My favour which I bestowed upon you and that I preferred you over the worlds."

The call returns almost word for word from 2:47. Repetition in the Quran is never filler. When a favour is named again after a long stretch of rebuke, claim, and warning, it is a fresh chance to hear it with a softer heart — and a hinge into what comes next.

The favour — al-ni'mah — gathers everything already counted: rescue from Pharaoh, the sea split, manna and shade, water from stone, the Torah, prophets raised among them generation after generation. No nation of their time was given what they were given. "And that I preferred you over the worlds" was real for their era. Preference was an appointment, not a blood guarantee. When Ibrahim was made an imam and asked about his offspring, the answer came: "My covenant does not include the wrongdoers" (2:124). Honour is kept by obedience, not by insisting on exclusivity.

Why here? Because the surah is about to open the story of Ibrahim and the building of the Ka'bah — the father claimed by more than one community, the House that would become the qibla of a different ummah. The reminder says: you were honoured, but the honour points forward to submission, not backward to pride. Remember the gifts so that you recognise the Giver when He sends what the gifts prepared you for.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, used to stand in prayer so long that his feet swelled, and when asked why, he said, "Should I not be a grateful servant?" Favour remembered becomes fuel for obedience; favour forgotten becomes fuel for entitlement. For every Muslim reader the same caution lands with equal force. This ummah too is called "the best nation brought forth for mankind" (3:110), but that verse continues: you enjoin what is right, forbid what is wrong, and believe in Allah. Remove those qualities and the description does not stick. Chosen-ness has always been for a purpose. He told his own family that lineage would not avail them against Allah. Remembering favour without remembering responsibility is how earlier communities lost both.

=123=

"And fear a Day when no soul will avail another at all, nor will compensation be accepted from it, nor will intercession benefit it, nor will they be helped."

The warning echoes verse 48 almost line for line, and after the long stretch of claims about who alone enters Paradise it hits with new force. The Day is drawn as a place where every backup plan people trust is shut — four doors closed one after another.

"No soul will avail another." No lineage, no tribe, no alliance. A man will flee from brother, mother, father, wife and children (80:34-36). Ibrahim cannot carry his father's account, and Nuh cannot pull his son from the water by blood alone. "Nor will compensation be accepted from it." The wealth hoarded to buy exits will not transact. "If those who did wrong had all that is in the earth and the like of it with it, they would ransom themselves with it" (13:18) — and it will not be taken. "Nor will intercession benefit it" on its own, without Allah's permission and pleasure, because intercession that Day is not influence peddling but mercy granted: "Who is it that can intercede with Him except by His permission?" (2:255). "Nor will they be helped" — no faction arriving late with a rescue.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, said people will be resurrected barefoot, naked, and uncircumcised, and when Aishah asked about looking at one another he replied that the matter would be too severe for that. Severity is the point of the verse's mercy. Two nearly identical warnings frame the long address to the People of the Book as if to say: Jew, Christian, Muslim by name, or pagan — none of those labels negotiates the account. What goes with you is what you did when you remembered, or when you forgot, that you would stand alone before your Lord.

Live now as someone who will have no proxy. That single thought cleans speech, contracts, and private habits faster than a hundred slogans.

=124=

"And when Ibrahim was tried by his Lord with words and he fulfilled them, He said, 'I will make you an imam for the people.' He said, 'And of my offspring?' He said, 'My covenant does not include the wrongdoers.'"

The surah now turns to Ibrahim, peace be upon him — the one Jews, Christians, and Muslims all claimed. If the long conversation with the People of the Book was going to be settled, it had to be settled at the root, and the root is him.

"When Ibrahim was tried by his Lord with words." The kalimat were commands. Scholars listed among them the rites of purification and hajj, the call to leave Hajar in a barren valley, the fire of Nimrud, and the command to sacrifice his son. Whatever the full list, the key is he fulfilled them — atammahunna — completely, without half-obedience. When told to submit, he submitted. When told to leave what he loved, he left. When told to argue, he argued with adab. When told to be patient, he was patient. Leadership in this religion is not inherited in a moment of comfort; it is proven under testing.

Because he fulfilled, he was made an imam — not a tribal chief but a model for mankind until the end of time. Then, thinking as a father, he asks, "And of my offspring?" Wanting good for one's children is the instinct of every prophet; it is not blamed. The answer redraws every later claim to prestige: "My covenant does not include the wrongdoers." It does not pass automatically by blood to those who abandon its requirements. A disbelieving son of a prophet is not nearer than a sincere believer from the far ends of the earth.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, stood as the most complete heir of that imamate, and still told Fatimah that he could not avail her against Allah by kinship alone. This one line dismantles Paradise-by-race and imamate-by-surname. The door is open. Wrongdoing closes it — no matter whose child you are. For every parent who prays for their children, the verse is both hope and boundary: ask for them, guide them, and know that their standing will finally be their own submission, not your name.

=125=

"And when We made the House a place of return for the people and a sanctuary, and take the standing-place of Ibrahim as a place of prayer. And We covenanted with Ibrahim and Isma'il: 'Purify My House for those who circumambulate, those who seclude themselves for worship, and those who bow and prostrate.'"

Now the House itself enters the story. The Ka'bah is called mathabah — a place people return to again and again, not once. Even after they leave, something draws the heart back. Those who have tasted tawaf know that pull years later. And amn — security. From the moment Ibrahim prayed for its safety, Allah made it a sanctuary. Even the pagan Arabs who fought everywhere else respected its precinct. Animals are safe there, plants left untouched; a person fleeing finds a kind of asylum. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said Allah made this city sacred the day He created the heavens and the earth, and it will remain sacred by Allah's sanctity until the Day of Resurrection.

"Take the standing-place of Ibrahim as a place of prayer." Maqam Ibrahim — the stone he stood on while building, footprints still marked — is not worshipped. It is prayed behind, so devotion remembers the builder. Faith ties place to person in order to teach gratitude, not idolatry.

Then the trust: "We covenanted with Ibrahim and Isma'il to purify My House." The custodians are two prophets, father and son, and their job is not revenue or prestige but taharah — purity. Keep it clean of idols, of indecency, of turning worship into a market, for those who do tawaf, those who sit in i'tikaf, and those who bow and prostrate. Three forms of worship are named — movement around the House, stillness in it, and prayer — so the purpose of the building is never in doubt.

If two great prophets were ordered to purify the House, every later caretaker and every visitor inherits a share of that duty: keep the heart as fit for worship as the building must be, and do not let the world's noise rebuild inside what Ibrahim and Isma'il were told to clear out.

=126=

"And when Ibrahim said, 'My Lord, make this a secure city and provide its people with fruits — whoever of them believes in Allah and the Last Day.' He said, 'And whoever disbelieves — I will grant him enjoyment for a little, then I will force him to the punishment of the Fire, and wretched is the destination.'"

Ibrahim, peace be upon him, now a builder, prays as a neighbour. He does not only ask that stones stand; he asks that the city around them be secure and provided for. Security first, then fruits. Without amn no one worships well, trades fairly, or raises a family in peace. The order of the du'a is itself a lesson in what a righteous urban planner of the soul asks for.

His wording at first limits provision to believers: "whoever of them believes." It is an understandable thought from a sincere man — why ask supply for those who will reject the Giver? The reply gently corrects and widens. The disbeliever too is given enjoyment for a while. Rizq in this world is not a certificate of Allah's pleasure; it is part of the test and the time-limit. Believer and disbeliever eat from the same field and trade in the same market until the term ends. Then reckoning divides what this world mixed. "The enjoyment of this world is little, and the Hereafter is better for whoever is mindful of Allah" (4:77).

Ibrahim's du'a was answered in ways he could not have pictured. Makkah, with no river and no fertile valley, became a city to which fruits of every kind are carried — by caravan once, by flight now — and its security has held a unique place in the world's imagination across centuries of turmoil around it. The Prophet, peace be upon him, when he entered Makkah as a victor, still spoke of its sanctity and forbade violence in it except what the necessity of that day required.

For anyone watching the wicked prosper, this verse steadies the eye. Temporary enjoyment is not endorsement. And for anyone building a home, a masjid, or a community, Ibrahim's sequence remains the template: ask for safety, ask for provision, and leave the sorting of hearts to the One who gives both — and who will not let the little enjoyment of this world be confused with success.

=127=

"And when Ibrahim was raising the foundations of the House with Isma'il, saying, 'Our Lord, accept from us. Indeed, You are the Hearing, the Knowing.'"

Picture it. An ageing father and his son, under a hard sun, set stone on stone where there was nothing. No crowd. No ceremony. No one to applaud. The House of Allah rises because two people keep lifting, and while they lift they do not boast — they beg.

"Our Lord, accept from us." Rabbana taqabbal minna. That sentence, preserved word for word, becomes the model for every act of worship after it. A deed can look enormous in human eyes and still be rejected; a deed can look small and be accepted and weigh heavy on the scales. The difference is taqabbul — acceptance. Ibrahim could have said, we built it. Instead he says, accept it. That is the heart of sincerity: knowing that even your best needs to be received, because your strength to do it was from Him anyway.

He calls on two names that fit the moment exactly. Al-Sami' — You hear our words, the scrape of stone, the whispered hope. Al-'Alim — You know why we do this, for whom, and what sits in our chests while the walls rise. Hearing and knowledge together mean nothing about the act is missing from His appraisal — not the public shape, not the private motive.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught that Allah does not look at your forms and wealth but at your hearts and deeds, and he would ask for acceptance after good work the way a needy man asks for a gift. Building is itself worship when it is done this way: raising walls, teaching a class, setting a table, writing what helps people, carrying a burden no one sees. Any lawful work done for Allah, with the tongue still saying taqabbal minna, joins that foundation. The hands lifted the stones. The hearts stayed low. Without that, even building the Ka'bah would have been only masonry.

=128=

"Our Lord, make us muslims to You, and from our descendants a nation muslim to You, and show us our rites, and accept our repentance. Indeed, You are the Acceptor of repentance, the Merciful."

The second du'a comes while the walls are still rising. Notice how Ibrahim and Isma'il, already obedient at the highest level, do not speak as people who have arrived. They ask to be made muslimayni laka — two who submit to You — as if submission is not a stamp received once but a state that must be renewed. The closer a servant comes, the more he sees how much submitting remains.

Then their concern runs past their own lives: "and from our descendants a nation submissive to You." They do not ask first for an empire or for riches. They ask for an ummah that will submit. It is the du'a that opens onto this community, and it is why the Prophet, peace be upon him, is described as the answer to the prayer of Ibrahim. Every person who says "I am Muslim" stands inside that answered request.

"And show us our rites" — manasikana. Even Ibrahim needed to be shown how to worship. Worship is not invented from taste; it is taught by Allah. That includes the stations of hajj and, more broadly, the shapes of prayer, charity, and remembrance. The servant does not dictate the manner in which the Lord is to be served.

"And turn to us" — tub 'alayna — even though no particular sin is listed. A heart near Allah feels a steady need for tawbah, not only after a fall but because of the gap between what it longs to offer and what it actually brings. They close with two names that have never been more fitting: al-Tawwab, the One who turns again and again toward His servants so they may turn; and al-Rahim, whose mercy makes that turning welcome rather than terrifying. A father and son's prayer stretches across thousands of years and includes the reader who still says Rabbana from a city they never walked.

=129=

"Our Lord, and raise among them a messenger from themselves who will recite to them Your verses and teach them the Book and wisdom and purify them. Indeed, You are the Almighty, the Wise."

This is the third du'a of the two builders, and it seals the others. First they asked acceptance, then submission and rites; now they ask for a teacher who will keep that submission alive after they are gone.

"Raise among them a messenger from themselves." Not an angel far from hunger and grief, but one of them, who speaks their tongue, whose character they can watch at close range. A teacher whose life is the walking commentary on his lesson. Four tasks are named, and the order is precise: he will recite Your verses — tilawah, bringing Allah's words unchanged to their ears; teach them the Book and the wisdom — ta'lim, opening meanings, rulings, purposes, and al-hikmah, the Prophetic way that puts knowledge in its right place; and purify them — tazkiyah, cleaning the heart of shirk, envy, and arrogance so that knowledge actually settles. Recitation first, purification woven through, because there is hearing that never enters, and a zeal without knowledge that has no direction.

This is exactly how Muhammad, peace be upon him, is described: "It is He who sent among the unlettered a messenger from themselves, reciting to them His verses and purifying them and teaching them the Book and wisdom" (62:2). The prayer made at the rising Ka'bah was answered from the same valleys, after generations, in a man who still circled that House.

"Indeed, You are the Almighty, the Wise." Power to change hearts; wisdom to change them rightly. When Allah answers, He times the answer better than those who prayed could have planned. For parents, teachers, and anyone building community, the verse is a curriculum: do not only hand people information. Recite, teach, and leave room for the purification that makes teaching stick — and ask the Almighty, the Wise, to raise what you cannot raise alone.

=130=

"And who would turn away from the religion of Ibrahim except one who fools himself? We certainly chose him in this world, and in the Hereafter he will surely be among the righteous."

After Ibrahim's du'as, the Quran steps back and asks a question that strips the opposing argument of dignity. Who would turn away from the millah of Ibrahim? That way is not a tangled philosophy. It is pure tawhid, submission, generosity, courage against falsehood, and mercy to creation. Who leaves that except a person who has made a fool of himself — safiha nafsahu — degraded his own soul?

Ibrahim is then framed in words that make the alternative look even thinner. "We chose him in this world" — istafaahu — selected, brought close. He was given leadership, sons who were prophets, and a name still spoken in every adhan and prayer on earth. Turning from his way is not a bold intellectual move; it is walking away from a path Allah already marked as chosen. "And in the Hereafter he will be among the righteous." Al-salihin — the company every soul wants at the end — and Ibrahim is assured a place among them.

The implication for the disputants in Madinah is plain. You claim to love Ibrahim and to descend from him. His religion was not the later labels of Judaism or Christianity as formed after Musa and Isa. It was islam — submission — hanif, without partners. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said, "I am the answer to the prayer of my father Ibrahim," and he restored that millah in its clarity. On the Day of Resurrection people will be called to follow what they used to follow; those who followed Ibrahim's way follow a man who never compromised tawhid for comfort.

Whoever turns from it has not wounded Ibrahim. He has only fooled himself, trading a chosen way for something that will not stand beside it when claims of lineage fall quiet. The practical test is simple: when a command comes that costs status, money, or habit, do you turn toward Ibrahim's answer — aslamtu — or away from it with a clever excuse? That turn is where foolishness or wisdom is decided.

=131=

"When his Lord said to him, 'Submit,' he said, 'I have submitted to the Lord of the worlds.'"

This is the whole religion of Ibrahim in one exchange. No long debate. One command: aslim — submit. One reply that holds a lifetime: aslamtu li-Rabbi al-'alamin — I have submitted to the Lord of the worlds.

Notice the form. Aslamtu is past tense, as if to say it is already done, settled, not pending negotiation. And li-Rabbi al-'alamin — not to a tribe, not to custom, not to a king, but to the Lord who nurtures and owns all worlds, seen and unseen. Ibrahim's submission was never abstract. Told to face Nimrud, he submitted. Thrown toward the fire, he submitted — and the fire was commanded to be coolness and peace for him (21:69). Ordered to leave Hajar and Isma'il in a valley without crops, he submitted, and Zamzam sprang where the infant's heels struck. Commanded to sacrifice the son he had waited for across decades, he submitted, and the knife was stayed with a ransom from heaven. Each test larger than the last; the answer the same size every time.

This is why the word Islam carries his imprint. It is not stubborn ritual and not empty slogan. It is the active handing over of the self after recognising who Allah is. Salam — peace — grows from the same root, because the heart only rests when it stops bargaining with its Lord. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that none of you truly believes until his desires follow what he brought — the same posture Ibrahim took when the command came without a committee. He also said, "Say, I have believed in Allah, then remain upright," echoing this settled aslamtu with a life that stays on it.

The verse corrects anyone who wants Ibrahim's prestige without his religion. Lineage is not submission. To claim him while refusing to say aslamtu when your own command arrives — about money, about a relationship, about a public stance — is to miss him completely. The word is short. The life it demands is the work of years, renewed every time a new instruction reaches you.

=132=

"And Ibrahim enjoined this upon his sons, and so did Ya'qub: 'O my sons, indeed Allah has chosen for you the religion, so do not die except while you are muslims.'"

The scene moves from personal submission to transmission. Ibrahim does not leave his children wealth as the first bequest; he leaves them the din. And Ya'qub — Jacob, Israel himself — does the same centuries later with his twelve sons. One father near the root of the line, another at its branching into tribes, both speaking the same charge. The Quran wants that double witness so no one can say the will was only Ibrahim's private intensity.

"Indeed Allah has chosen for you the religion." Chosen for you — not invented by you from a menu of options. The faith was selected by the One who knows what fits the human being, what mends its breaks, what saves it. To pick something lesser after that choice is to refuse a gift. "So do not die except while you are muslims." No one controls the hour of death, so the scholars read this as: live in submission at every hour, so that whenever death finds you, it finds you there. Hold it in the morning market and the night prayer, in ease and in anger, because one of those moments will be the last.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught people to ask for a good ending — husn al-khatimah — and to renew faith, because hearts turn and the wise father fears the turn that comes too late. He said actions are judged by their endings, and he would often swear by Allah that he sought forgiveness more than seventy times in a day — a man already guaranteed Paradise still treating submission as something to be kept alive until the final breath. Ibrahim and Ya'qub embodied a love that looks past careers and marriages to the face a child will present to Allah. That is what makes them imams for later parents. Leave your children, if you can leave them anything, a relationship with submission strong enough that death cannot catch them empty of it.

=133=

"Or were you witnesses when death attended Ya'qub, when he said to his sons, 'What will you worship after me?' They said, 'We will worship your God and the God of your fathers, Ibrahim and Isma'il and Ishaq — one God — and to Him we submit.'"

The Quran takes us into the death room. Ya'qub, after a long life of travel, loss, and reunion, feels death near. The question he asks is not about land or inheritance maps. It is ma ta'buduna min ba'di — what will you worship after me?

For a man with twelve sons who would become twelve tribes, that was the only question that finally mattered. If worship is secure, much else can be lost and recovered. If worship is lost, everything kept is husk. His sons answer with a creed scholars still quote as a clear definition of the faith across ages: your God, and the God of your fathers Ibrahim, Isma'il, and Ishaq — one God. Not "the god of our tribe." Ilahan wahida — one God. And wa nahnu lahu muslimun — to Him we submit.

Several lessons sit inside the scene. Legacy at the highest level is creed, not real estate. Isma'il is named among the "fathers" though he was Ya'qub's uncle, because the uncle is like the father — and because the Quran deliberately ties the line of Isma'il and the line of Ishaq under one faith, refusing the rivalry later built between their descendants. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said, "The uncle is like the father," and he himself stood as the most complete fruit of Isma'il's branch without ever dishonouring Ishaq's. He also taught, when a man's child dies, that the child intercedes by the permission of Allah — a reminder that what we plant in children of tawhid outlives us in both worlds.

Death clarifies priorities. Pretence falls. What remains on Ya'qub's tongue is what actually lived in his heart. A person should ask now, while the room is still quiet: if my children gathered at my bedside, what would I think worth making sure they say? If the answer is only worldly advice, this verse has not yet done its work.

=134=

"That was a nation that has passed away. For them is what they earned, and for you is what you earn. And you will not be asked about what they used to do."

After the intimacy of Ya'qub's deathbed, the verse steps back and puts history in its place. Tilka ummatun qad khalat — that was a community that has gone ahead. The stories were not told so that listeners could decorate themselves with borrowed glory. They were told so that a pattern of submission could be recognised and chosen again.

"For them is what they earned, and for you is what you earn." No merged accounts. Ibrahim will not be questioned about your prayers, and you will not answer for his son's near-sacrifice except insofar as you follow its spirit. Lineage is cut at the reckoning. The Quran will repeat this sentence again at 2:141, bracketing the claims that follow, because after stories of prophets the temptation is to treat them as a family discount. "You will not be asked about what they used to do." Allah will not ask you why Nuh's son drowned or why Ibrahim walked toward a fire. He will ask about your own hearing, sight, and heart (17:36). Did you submit when your smaller test arrived?

The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that whoever is slowed by his deeds will not be hastened by his lineage. On another occasion he warned his closest kin that he could not avail them against Allah if they did not work. That is both sobering and liberating. Sobering, because no ancestor's piety lifts you automatically. Liberating, because no ancestor's failure damns you automatically — and no community's past sins are your mandatory inheritance. Each soul is sent guidance and given a span. The grand story of Ibrahim and his children shows the continuity of truth; continuity still requires a personal yes. The door of that household is open, but each person walks through it on his own feet, with his own aslamtu.

=135=

"And they say, 'Be Jews or Christians and you will be guided.' Say, 'Rather, the religion of Ibrahim, the hanif — and he was not of the polytheists.'"

Both groups in Madinah were inviting the Muslims into their fold as the path to guidance. Each said, in effect: enter our name and you will be rightly guided. The Quran records the invitation to show how little such invitations have changed across centuries — join us, wear our label, and safety will follow.

The answer is not a split-the-difference compromise. It is a redirect to a root both claimed. "Rather, the millah of Ibrahim, hanifan." Millah is the whole way — belief and practice together. Hanif is a word with light in it: one who turns away from crookedness toward straightness, who never made peace with the idols around him. Ibrahim watched his people bow to stars and said he did not love things that set (6:76). He watched idols that could not protect themselves and asked whether they heard when called (26:72). He turned, alone at first, toward purity — and kept turning until every turn was toward Allah.

"And he was not of the polytheists." Not only that he avoided shirk, but that he was never counted among its people even when he was surrounded by it. His own father carved idols; comfort was available; he separated anyway. The Prophet, peace be upon him, was sent to restore that millah, and Allah told him, "Then We revealed to you: follow the religion of Ibrahim, hanifan, and he was not of the polytheists" (16:123). He cleared the Ka'bah Ibrahim had built, and he refused every offer to dilute tawhid for political peace.

Guidance is not found by picking the contemporary faction whose brand feels safest. It is found by returning to the fountain of submission all true prophets drank from: tawhid without additions, worship without partners, readiness to obey when commanded. That way is still intact, still open, and still named hanif — the upright turning. When someone says "be one of us to be guided," answer with the same redirect: rather, the way of the one who submitted when no crowd was clapping.

=136=

"Say, 'We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us, and what was revealed to Ibrahim, Isma'il, Ishaq, Ya'qub, and the tribes, and what was given to Musa and Isa, and what was given to the prophets from their Lord. We make no distinction between any of them, and to Him we submit.'"

If the previous verse gave the principle — follow Ibrahim's way — this one gives the detailed charter of that way.

The believer is taught to say amanna — we believe. Not in one prophet and one book alone, but in a chain. "What was revealed to us" — the Quran. "What was revealed to Ibrahim, Isma'il, Ishaq, Ya'qub and the asbat" — the tribes descended from Ya'qub who carried prophethood among them. "What was given to Musa and Isa and what was given to the prophets from their Lord." Every link counted, none orphaned. The list deliberately braids the lines of Isma'il and Ishaq so that no later rivalry can split what revelation joined.

"We make no distinction between any of them." La nufarriqu bayna ahadin minhum. This is among the Muslim's clearest traits: wholeness. Others accepted some messengers and rejected others — denying Isa, or Muhammad, or both. The believer accepts all, because the criterion is not ethnicity but message, and the message from first to last was one: worship Allah alone. "To Him we submit" — wa nahnu lahu muslimun — closes the creed with action. Faith is not a museum of prophets; it ends in surrender to what they jointly brought, not to what we wish they had brought.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught his companions words of this creed in the morning and evening remembrances, and he said the prophets are half-brothers; their mothers differ but their religion is one. When a man tried to elevate him above Yunus ibn Matta in a way that diminished the other prophet, he forbade it. The verse trains a heart large enough to honour every prophet and precise enough to refuse distortions added after them. No racism of revelation. No picking favourites among those Allah sent. One Lord, one chain, one submission — said with the tongue, then proven when the next command arrives.

=137=

"So if they believe in the same as you believe in, then they are rightly guided. But if they turn away, they are only in schism, and Allah will suffice you against them. And He is the Hearing, the Knowing."

The outcome of the invitation is now stated without drama. The standard is not one group's taste against another's; it is what the believers have just affirmed — faith in Allah and all His prophets without distinction, sealed with submission. "If they believe in the like of what you believe in, they are guided." The road is open and clearly marked. No secret password, no ethnic gate. "But if they turn away, they are only in schism" — fi shiqaq — a rift, a tearing away, opposition that splits off after clarity has already arrived. Not a mild confusion; a wilful parting.

Then comes comfort for the Prophet, peace be upon him, and for anyone who holds this inclusive faith while being painted as misguided: "Allah will suffice you against them." Fa-sayakfikahumu Allah. The promise wrote itself into history. Plots, letters to hostile tribes, coalitions gathered for Badr and beyond — and Allah sufficed him. The promise still stands for the caller who stays sincere without becoming cruel. "He is the Hearing, the Knowing." He hears the arguments, the private meetings, the public claims that Paradise is a members-only lounge. He knows the motives — envy, pride, fear of lost status. Nothing escapes, so the believer need not chase every accusation down every alley.

The companions learned from this not to panic when numbers turned away. Guidance is not a headcount; it is adherence to what Allah revealed. The Prophet continued to pray for people who hurt him, and he also knew when to leave a heart to its choice. If people remain hostile after the message is clear, their schism is not something you created by refusing to dissolve the truth. You held a line. He hears. He knows. He suffices.

=138=

"The colouring of Allah — and who is better than Allah in colouring? And we are worshippers of Him."

Sibghat Allah — Allah's dye, His colouring. The Arabs knew sibghah as the dye into which cloth is lowered until the colour reaches every fibre. Some Christians around the Prophet's time spoke of a baptismal dyeing that claimed to cleanse. The Quran answers at the level of reality, not rivalry of rituals: if you want a transformation that holds, take Allah's sibghah.

What is His colouring? His religion. The fitrah He created people upon. The tawhid of Ibrahim lived until it soaks the private and the public, joy and grief, trade and prayer. It does not sit on the surface like a splash of trend. It penetrates. People eventually see on a face where that soul has been steeping. "Who is better than Allah in colouring?" Human dyes fade. Ideologies peel. Fashions date themselves. Sincerity, mercy, justice, clean worship — these stay fresh in the grave and on the Day of meeting.

"And we are worshippers of Him" — wa nahnu lahu 'abidun. Not admirers of a colour from far off, but wearers of it in acts of 'ibadah. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that every child is born on the fitrah and then parents and culture steer it — a reminder that Allah's original colouring is already there, and the work of faith is to stop staining it with partners and habits that do not belong. He also said that Allah has ninety-nine names; whoever lives by them enters Paradise — another way of saying: take on the colour of what you worship by reflecting His mercy, justice, and patience as a servant can.

Sibghah does not happen in one quick dip. Cloth is left in the vat. The heart is left in prayer, Quran, truthful company, repentance after slips. It soaks. After a time, without a speech, people can tell where you have been leaving your soul.

=139=

"Say, 'Do you argue with us about Allah while He is our Lord and your Lord? For us are our deeds, and for you are your deeds. And we are sincere to Him.'"

The debate in Madinah had grown sharp. "Do you argue with us about Allah?" A-tuhajjunana — back-and-forth aimed at overcoming the other. They were disputing about Allah Himself, as if the Lord of the worlds could be made a tribal property.

The answer grounds the whole noise: "While He is our Lord and your Lord." Rabbuna wa rabbukum. He is not a private deity of one camp. He feeds, gives life, and decrees for you as for us. To argue as though one party holds exclusive title to Him is already to forget what Lordship means. "For us are our deeds, and for you are your deeds." Accountability is personal. We will not carry your record, nor you ours. Each stands with what he earned — the same principle verse 134 stamped on history. Conflict is pulled out of mob identity and placed back on the shoulders of individual responsibility.

"And we are sincere to Him" — wa nahnu lahu mukhlisun. Mukhlis: unmixed, purified of partners in intention. Not sincere to heritage, to winning the room, to cultural survival as an end in itself. The Muslim does not worship in order to defeat someone in debate. He worships for Allah alone. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that Allah accepts only what is done seeking His face, and he taught that a man may fast, pray, and give while fighting for a reputation — and be told on the Day to take his reward from those he wanted to impress.

The verse is a small manual of adab al-khilaf — manners in disagreement. It does not insult. It does not dig up history only to wound. It states: He is our Lord and yours; our deeds are ours and yours are yours; we are trying to be sincere to Him. When argument reaches that clarity, more volume rarely adds more light.

=140=

"Or do you say that Ibrahim, Isma'il, Ishaq, Ya'qub and the tribes were Jews or Christians? Say, 'Is it you who know better, or Allah?' And who is more unjust than one who conceals a testimony he has from Allah? And Allah is not unaware of what you do."

Now the claim is faced head-on. Were those great prophets Jews or Christians? Judaism as a law-centred communal identity and Christianity as a distinct community took shape after them. How could Ibrahim, who lived long before Musa, be a "Jew" in the later legal sense? How could he be a "Christian" before Isa? The question exposes the anachronism without needing a long lecture.

"Is it you who know better, or Allah?" A-antum a'lamu ami Allah? The question ends the debate. Allah states that Ibrahim was neither Jew nor Christian but a hanif, a muslim — one who submitted — (3:67). To insist otherwise is to claim better knowledge than the One who sent him and knew his inner and outer life.

Then the severest edge: "Who is more unjust than one who conceals a testimony he has from Allah?" Kitman al-shahadah — hiding testimony. They possessed, in what remained with them, descriptions of the final prophet and the truth that righteousness is not a racial inheritance. Hiding that testimony meant seekers who trusted them were misled. The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned that whoever is asked about knowledge and conceals it will be bridled with a bridle of fire — a warning that lands on every scholar, teacher, and parent who buries an unpopular truth to protect status.

"And Allah is not unaware of what you do." He watches speech and silence alike — the footnote omitted because it would change the conclusion, the verse not quoted because it would cost a following. Concealment that saves face while wasting souls is counted among the great wrongs. The counsel to the sincere in every age is simple and costly: you hold an amanah from Allah's Book. Speak it. Let the consequences belong to the One who is never unaware.
