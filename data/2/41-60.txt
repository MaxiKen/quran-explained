=41=

"And believe in what I have sent down confirming what is with you, and be not the first to disbelieve in it, and do not exchange My verses for a small price, and fear Me."

Here is the specific request behind the general appeal — and notice how it is framed. The Quran does not ask them to abandon their scripture. It presents itself as "confirming what is with you." Same God, same core message, same moral law, the last instalment of something they already held. A person who genuinely loved the Torah should have recognised the family resemblance immediately, which is why the Quran says elsewhere that they know it "as they know their own sons" (2:146).

"Do not be the first to disbelieve in it." This is a strange thing to say to a community that was not first chronologically — the Makkans had rejected it years earlier. The meaning is heavier than sequence: you, of all people, should not be found in that column. When those who know the most reject something, everyone else feels licensed to follow. Rejection by the learned carries the sin of everyone it takes down with it.

Then the real obstacle is named: "do not exchange My verses for a small price." Their scholars held genuine authority — respected, consulted, supported by their communities. Accepting a prophet from the line of Isma'il would have cost all of it. So the trade is exactly the one described earlier with the hypocrites: something eternal handed over for something that expires.

Note the word "small." Not worthless — a real position, real income, real respect. But small measured against what was on the other side of the scale. Everything this world offers is small in that comparison, which is why the Prophet, peace be upon him, said that the place of a whip in Paradise is better than the world and everything in it.

And this is not a problem confined to one community. Any person who has knowledge and a livelihood attached to it faces the same test — the scholar who softens a ruling to keep his patrons, the professional who stays silent because speaking costs, the person who knows what is true and calculates what it would cost to say so. The verse ends with the only thing strong enough to counter that pull: "and fear Me." One fear cancels the other.

=42=

"And do not mix the truth with falsehood, nor conceal the truth while you know it."

Two ways to bury a truth, and the Quran names them both because they are different techniques.

The first is mixing. Not deleting the truth but diluting it — adding human opinion into revelation until nobody can tell where one ends and the other begins, keeping the sacred vocabulary while changing what it carries, quoting the text accurately and framing it so it means the opposite. This is more dangerous than open denial precisely because it keeps the appearance of religion. The falsehood borrows the authority of the truth it is mixed into, and the ordinary person, who trusts the scholar, has no way to separate them.

The second is concealment — simply not mentioning what is inconvenient. No lie is told. The description of the awaited prophet was in their hands, and it was enough to withhold it. Ibn Abbas and others report that some of their scholars knew and kept quiet, and the Quran repeats the charge: "a group of them used to hear the words of Allah and then distort it after they had understood it, while they knew" (2:75).

"While you know" is the phrase that removes every excuse. This is not error, not misunderstanding, not a different reading in good faith. It is knowing and choosing.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, made concealment a serious matter for his own community too: "Whoever is asked about knowledge and conceals it will be bridled with a bridle of fire on the Day of Resurrection." Knowledge is not private property; it arrives as a trust, and it has to be passed on intact.

And the modern shapes of both are easy to recognise. Selective quotation. A verse without its context. A ruling explained with the uncomfortable half removed. Religious language attached to whatever the audience already wanted to hear. The test is simple and hard: does the person I am speaking to walk away closer to what Allah actually said, or closer to what suits me?

=43=

"And establish the prayer, give the zakah, and bow with those who bow."

After belief and honesty comes practice, and it is worth noticing that these three commands were not new to the people being addressed. Prayer and charity were prescribed to every community; the Quran reports Isa saying, "He has enjoined upon me prayer and zakah as long as I remain alive" (19:31), and Isma'il commanding his family with the same two (19:55). Nobody is being asked to adopt a foreign religion — only to return to what their own prophets brought and to follow it to its conclusion.

"Establish the prayer" again uses the word for setting something up properly, not merely performing it. And placing prayer and zakah side by side, as the Quran does dozens of times, defines what a religious life looks like: a duty owed upward and a duty owed sideways. One connects you to your Lord, the other connects you to people who need you. Take either one out and what remains is deformed — private devotion with no social conscience, or charity with no relationship to the One who gave you what you are giving.

Then the third command, which is the pointed one: "and bow with those who bow." Bowing was not part of their prayer, and it was distinctive of the Muslim congregation forming down the road. Nor is the wording "bow" but "bow with" — join the row.

That is a much harder request than the first two, because a person can pray at home and give charity privately and remain in charge of his own religious life. Standing shoulder to shoulder in a congregation means accepting a shared leadership, a common form, a place in a line where the scholar and the servant stand in the same row with no distinction. It asks for the one thing pride resists: belonging on terms you did not set.

The lesson survives the original context. Faith in Islam is not designed to be practised alone. The Prophet, peace be upon him, put congregational prayer at twenty-seven times the reward of praying by oneself, and described the believers as one body — when one part suffers, the rest responds with fever and sleeplessness. A person can be sincere in isolation and still be missing something the religion considers essential.

=44=

"Do you order people to righteousness and forget yourselves, while you recite the Scripture? Then will you not use reason?"

This is one of the most uncomfortable verses in the Quran, and it is aimed squarely at religious teachers.

The charge is not that they taught the wrong thing. They were telling people the truth — be just, be charitable, follow the Book, believe in the prophet who is coming. The problem is the gap between the instruction and the instructor.

"And forget yourselves" is precise. Not "you disbelieve in it" but you forget yourselves — you were so busy applying the standard outward that you never turned it around. It is remarkably easy to do. A person can spend years studying the rulings on backbiting and be a backbiter, can teach the virtues of the night prayer and never pray it, can advise everyone to be patient and lose his temper at home the same evening.

"While you recite the Scripture" is the aggravating clause. Ignorance would have been an excuse; there is none here. And then the closing question is not "have you no shame?" but "will you not use reason?" — because the contradiction is not merely ugly, it is stupid. A man handing out medicine while refusing to swallow it is not wicked so much as irrational.

The Quran says elsewhere how much Allah hates this: "Why do you say what you do not do? Great is hatred in the sight of Allah that you say what you do not do" (61:2-3). And the Prophet, peace be upon him, gave an image that has never lost its force: a man will be brought on the Day of Resurrection and thrown into the Fire, and his intestines will spill out and he will circle them like a donkey at a mill; the people of the Fire will gather and ask, "What is wrong with you? Did you not command good and forbid evil?" and he will say, "Yes, I used to order you to do good and not do it, and forbid you from evil and do it myself."

One clarification, because people misuse this verse to silence advice: it is not saying stop teaching until you are perfect. Nobody would ever teach. The sin is the forgetting, not the speaking. The correct response is to keep calling to good and to start applying it to yourself first — which is why the sincere teacher is usually the one who sounds like he is talking to himself.

=45=

"And seek help through patience and prayer. Indeed, it is difficult except for the humbly submissive."

After a long section of hard truths, the tone changes completely. Having been told what is wrong, they are now told what to do about it — and the prescription is two things, given to every believer facing anything difficult.

Sabr is usually translated as patience, but the word is stronger than waiting. Its root means to restrain, to hold something back. It covers three situations: holding steady under hardship, holding yourself back from what is forbidden, and holding yourself to obedience when you would rather not. So sabr is not passivity. It is the effort of staying put when everything in you wants to move.

Salah is the other half, and it is what makes sabr possible. Patience by itself is willpower, and willpower runs out. Prayer plugs the exhausted person into a source outside himself. This was the Prophet's own practice — when anything troubled him, he hurried to prayer — and he described it as his comfort: "The coolness of my eyes has been placed in prayer." Notice too that the two are joined in the Quran's opening chapter of counsel to the believers, and joined here for a community facing exactly the pressure that makes people give up.

"Indeed, it is difficult." The Quran does not pretend otherwise. Getting up for Fajr is hard. Standing still when your mind is full is hard. Holding your tongue when you have the winning reply is hard. Anyone who has been told that religious practice is easy has been misinformed, and this verse is more honest than most encouragement.

"Except for the humbly submissive" — the khashi'in, those whose hearts have softened before Allah. This is the key to the whole verse. The difficulty is not in the act; it is in the heart doing it. The same prayer that feels like an interruption to one person is the best part of the day for another. Nothing changed except the person. So if worship feels heavy, the answer is not to try harder at the mechanics but to work on the heart that is performing them — and the next verse says exactly how.

=46=

"Those who are certain that they will meet their Lord and that they will return to Him."

This defines the humble ones from the previous verse, and it does so with a single quality: they are certain of the meeting.

The Arabic uses a word that ordinarily means to think or suppose, but here — as the commentators note and as the context requires — it carries the sense of settled conviction. The distinction is worth making because most people would say they believe in the Hereafter. Very few live as though they are certain of it. That gap between assent and certainty is where most of our failures live.

Two things follow from real certainty. First, hardship shrinks. A person who knows this life is the short part can absorb losses that would otherwise break him, because the accounting is not closed here. The Prophet, peace be upon him, put the proportion starkly: what this world is compared to the next is like what your finger brings back when you dip it in the ocean.

Second, sin loses its appeal. Not because it stops being tempting, but because the price becomes visible. The person who can picture actually standing there — with no lawyer, no crowd to hide in, nothing between him and his Lord, as the Prophet said, peace be upon him, that each of you will speak to his Lord with no interpreter between them — finds it much harder to reach for something he will have to explain.

So the verse quietly answers the problem raised in the one before it. If prayer feels heavy and patience feels impossible, the deficiency is not in your discipline. It is that the meeting is an idea rather than a fact in your heart. And that can be worked on: by visiting graves, which the Prophet recommended precisely because it reminds you of the Hereafter; by reading the passages that describe that Day; by sitting with people who live as though it is real.

"And that they will return to Him." The word for return implies going back to where you came from. That is the frame the surah has been building since Adam — we came from Him, we are here briefly, and we are going back. The person who holds that clearly is the one for whom none of this is a burden.

=47=

"O Children of Israel, remember My favour which I bestowed upon you and that I preferred you over the worlds."

The address begins again, word for word as it did in verse 40, and the repetition is deliberate. After a series of hard corrections, the tone resets to kindness. Allah does not simply list their failings; He keeps returning to what He gave them. That is a method worth learning from — correction sandwiched in reminders of favour is heard very differently from correction alone.

The favours being recalled were extraordinary, and the surah is about to walk through them one by one: rescue from Pharaoh, the sea split open, food from the sky, water from a rock, the Torah, and a line of prophets unmatched in any other nation.

Then the striking phrase: "I preferred you over the worlds." This was a real and specific distinction. In their era, no community had been given what they were given. The Quran states it elsewhere: "We gave the Children of Israel the Scripture, judgement and prophethood, provided them with good things, and preferred them over the worlds" (45:16).

But the nature of that preference has to be understood correctly, and the whole passage is built to explain it. It was not a racial privilege or a guarantee. It was an appointment — and appointments carry conditions. The Quran makes the terms explicit later in this very surah, when Ibrahim is told he will be a leader for mankind and asks about his descendants: "My covenant does not include the wrongdoers" (2:124). Lineage does not transmit standing before Allah.

This is precisely why the passage matters for Muslims reading it today, and it would be a serious misreading to treat it as being about somebody else. This community has also been told, "You are the best nation produced for mankind" (3:110) — and that verse, read to the end, attaches the status to conduct: enjoining right, forbidding wrong, believing in Allah. The Prophet, peace be upon him, closed the door on any other reading when he said, "Whoever is slowed by his deeds will not be hastened by his lineage."

Being chosen means being given more to answer for. That is the point of the entire section that follows.

=48=

"And fear a Day when no soul will avail another at all, no intercession will be accepted from it, no compensation taken from it, and they will not be helped."

Having recalled the favours, the verse moves immediately to the Day when favours are no longer inherited. It closes four doors, one after another, and each one is an escape route people actually rely on.

The first: "no soul will avail another." Nobody's merit transfers. This is aimed directly at the assumption that descent from prophets provides cover — and by extension at every version of that assumption. The Quran drives it home elsewhere: "no bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another" (35:18), and on that Day a man will flee from his own brother, mother, father, wife and children (80:34-36).

The second: "no intercession will be accepted from it." Islam does affirm intercession — the Prophet, peace be upon him, is granted the great intercession, and others will intercede too. But the Quran is precise about the conditions: "Who is it that can intercede with Him except by His permission?" (2:255), and "they cannot intercede except for one with whom He is pleased" (21:28). Intercession is not influence exercised over Allah; it is a mercy He permits, for people He has already accepted. Nobody can be lobbied into Paradise.

The third: "no compensation taken from it." No ransom, no payment. This world runs largely on the ability to buy your way out of trouble — a fine, a settlement, a bribe, a good lawyer. There, that entire system does not exist. "If those who did wrong had all that is on the earth and the like of it with it, they would offer it to ransom themselves" (39:47).

The fourth: "nor will they be helped." No faction, no connections, no rescue.

Read together, these four remove every intermediary a person might place between himself and his own record. What is left is the relationship you actually built. And that is exactly why the Prophet, peace be upon him, called his own daughter by name and told her he could not avail her anything against Allah — the closest possible relation to the greatest human being, and even that does not transfer. The only thing worth investing in is your own file.

=49=

"And when We saved you from the people of Pharaoh, who were afflicting you with the worst torment — slaughtering your sons and keeping your women alive. And in that was a great trial from your Lord."

The list of favours begins at the lowest point, because that is where the story starts. Before the sea and the Torah, there was slavery.

The Quran does not soften what was happening. Systematic humiliation, forced labour, and a policy of infanticide — the boys killed at birth, the girls spared for service. It is worth pausing on why a tyrant does that: killing the sons destroys a people's future and its capacity to resist, while keeping the daughters preserves a workforce. It is calculated cruelty, and Pharaoh's stated reasoning is given elsewhere: "He divided its people into factions, oppressing one group among them" (28:4). Dividing people into castes and then crushing the weakest is the oldest political technique there is, and the Quran names it as the behaviour of the archetypal tyrant.

"And in that was a great trial from your Lord." The word bala' covers both a severe test and, in some readings, the favour of deliverance. Both senses fit. The oppression itself was a trial, and surviving it was a gift — and what a nation does after being rescued is the deeper test of the two.

That last point is where this verse aims. A community that has known slavery has a choice about what it becomes: a people who never forget what oppression feels like and refuse to inflict it, or a people who take the first opportunity to do to others what was done to them. The Quran holds up this rescue as an obligation, not merely a memory.

And there is a note here for anyone in the middle of something crushing. Musa was born under a death sentence and raised in the tyrant's own house. The rescue came from inside the palace. Allah's promise in that story is one of the most encouraging lines in the Quran: "We wanted to bestow favour upon those oppressed in the land and make them leaders and make them the inheritors" (28:5). No situation is closed.

=50=

"And when We parted the sea for you and saved you, and drowned the people of Pharaoh while you were looking on."

The escape from Egypt reached a point where it should have ended in massacre. The sea in front, Pharaoh's army closing from behind, no route left. The Quran preserves the two reactions at that moment: the people cried, "We are surely to be overtaken," and Musa answered, "No! Indeed, my Lord is with me; He will guide me" (26:61-62). Same facts, two completely different readings of them. Then the command came to strike the sea, and it opened.

Notice the final clause: "while you were looking on." They did not hear about this. They watched the water stand up on both sides, walked through on dry ground, turned around and saw the same water close over the army that had enslaved them for generations. Few communities in history have been given evidence like it.

Which sets up the tragedy that follows, because the surah is about to report that within days these same eyewitnesses were asking for an idol. That is the real lesson of this verse, and it is not about them. Miracles do not create faith. A spectacular experience produces a spectacular feeling, and feelings fade. What lasts is built slowly — habits, prayer, company, discipline. Anyone waiting for a dramatic sign to finally fix their religion should read this passage and stop waiting.

There is also a pattern here worth holding onto. Relief came at the last possible moment, not before. The road opened only after there was visibly no road. That is how the Quran repeatedly describes divine help: "until the messengers despaired and thought they had been denied, there came to them Our victory" (12:110), and "Unquestionably, the help of Allah is near" (2:214) — said immediately after describing believers shaken to the point of crying out.

And Pharaoh's end is the counterweight. The most powerful man of his age, who called himself the highest lord, was finished by water — the most ordinary substance on earth, and the one he had used to drown their sons.

=51=

"And when We made an appointment with Musa for forty nights, then you took the calf after him, while you were wrongdoers."

The forty nights were an honour: Musa summoned to the mountain to receive the Torah, fasting and in seclusion, preparing to bring back the law that would organise his people's entire life. He left them in the care of his brother Harun with clear instructions.

And then, in the sentence immediately after, they are worshipping a metal calf.

The speed is the point. The Quran places the appointment and the betrayal in a single line, with no interval, because that is how it happened. Not generations later, not after the memory dimmed — within weeks of the sea splitting open, in the lifetime of the people who walked through it, while their prophet was on the mountain collecting the book for them.

How? The details are given elsewhere: their jewellery melted down, a figure of a calf produced that made a lowing sound, and a man telling them, "This is your god and the god of Musa, but he forgot" (20:88). Harun warned them and was nearly overwhelmed; they told him they would not stop until Musa returned (20:91).

Two things are worth taking from this. The first is about the pull of the visible. They had been slaves in Egypt for generations, surrounded by gods you could see, carry and touch. Worshipping an unseen God demands a maturity that has to be built, and under pressure people revert to what they were formed by. Environment shapes a person more deeply than any single experience corrects.

The second is about absence. The moment leadership stepped away for forty days, the structure collapsed. That is a comment on how thin the faith was underneath — and a warning about religion that depends on a personality rather than a conviction. When the Prophet, peace be upon him, died, the community wavered similarly until Abu Bakr stood and said, "Whoever worshipped Muhammad, Muhammad has died; whoever worshipped Allah, Allah is alive and does not die," and recited the verse about the Messenger being no more than a messenger before whom messengers had passed (3:144).

Every believer has his own forty nights — the stretch when the reminders stop, the good company is away, Ramadan is over. What a person does in that gap is a truer measure of him than what he does in the presence of others.

=52=

"Then We pardoned you after that, so that perhaps you would be grateful."

One short verse, and it comes immediately after the worst offence in the entire narrative.

They had committed the sin the whole religion exists to prevent — shirk, and they committed it while the evidence of Allah's power was still fresh on their clothes. There is no defence available. And the next thing the Quran says is: We pardoned you.

That deserves to be sat with rather than read past. The pardon was not cheap; the following verses show it involved a hard and painful reckoning within the community. But it was granted. If that sin, at that moment, by those people, could be forgiven, then the question of whether one's own record is too far gone has been answered. "Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of Allah. Indeed, Allah forgives all sins" (39:53). The Prophet, peace be upon him, reported his Lord saying: "O son of Adam, if you came to Me with sins nearly filling the earth and then met Me without associating anything with Me, I would meet you with forgiveness nearly the like of it."

Then the purpose clause: "so that perhaps you would be grateful." Notice what forgiveness is for. It is not an erasure that leaves a person where he was; it is meant to produce something — a changed relationship with the One who let it go.

This is the part most people miss about repentance. The response to being forgiven is not merely relief but gratitude, and gratitude is expressed in action. The Prophet, peace be upon him, whose past and future faults were already forgiven, prayed at night until his feet swelled, and when Aisha asked why, he answered, "Should I not be a grateful servant?" Forgiveness that produces nothing has not been properly received.

And there is a quiet mercy in the word "perhaps." Allah pardoned them without knowing certainty of gratitude in advance — He gave first, and left the response to them. Which is exactly what happens with every one of us who wakes up on a morning after a sin, still breathing, with the day still available.

=53=

"And when We gave Musa the Scripture and the criterion, so that perhaps you would be guided."

The Torah is described with two words, and the second one explains why revelation is necessary at all.

Al-Kitab — the Book, the written, preserved text. Al-Furqan — the criterion, from a root meaning to separate. It is the instrument that divides truth from falsehood, right from wrong, the permitted from the forbidden. The same title is given to the Quran (25:1), and it names the central function of revelation.

Why does anyone need such a thing? Because unaided human judgement cannot reliably draw those lines, and the previous verses have just demonstrated it. These were people who had witnessed the sea open, and within weeks they had decided that a metal calf was worth worshipping. They were not stupid; they were human, and human beings rationalise whatever they want badly enough. Every society in history has been fully convinced of the rightness of things later generations found monstrous — and every one of them had reasons. Without a fixed external standard, morality is just the current consensus wearing formal clothes.

That is what a furqan provides: a line that does not move when your interests move. The Quran connects the criterion to the state of the heart, too — "if you fear Allah, He will grant you a criterion" (8:29) — so taqwa sharpens a person's ability to tell things apart, while sin blurs it.

And the purpose is stated as always: "so that perhaps you would be guided." Not so that you would possess a book. The scripture was given to be used. A community can preserve a text with enormous care, memorise it, decorate it, argue about it, and remain unguided by it — which is precisely the charge this whole section is building towards.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned his own community about exactly this outcome: that a time would come when the Quran is recited but does not pass beyond their throats. The Book on the shelf is not the point. The Book as a criterion — actually used to decide what you do on Tuesday — is.

=54=

"And when Musa said to his people, 'O my people, you have wronged yourselves by taking the calf, so repent to your Maker and kill yourselves. That is best for you with your Maker.' Then He accepted your repentance. Indeed, He is the Accepter of repentance, the Merciful."

This is one of the severest commands recorded in the Quran, and it needs to be read carefully rather than quickly.

First, notice how Musa opens: "O my people" — gently, still claiming them — and "you have wronged yourselves." Not "you have harmed Allah." Sin never reaches Him; it damages the one who commits it. That framing runs through the whole Quran and it is the correct way to understand every prohibition.

He calls Allah "your Maker," bari'ikum, rather than a more general name. The choice is pointed: they had worshipped something they manufactured, so they are reminded of the One who manufactured them.

Then the command. The classical commentators explain it in two main ways: that those who had not worshipped the calf were to carry out the sentence on those who had, or that the community as a whole was ordered to a reckoning in which the guilty were executed. Either way this was a specific legal penalty for a specific act of mass idolatry, delivered by a prophet under direct revelation to one nation. It is not a general rule, and nothing in Islamic law derives from it — the Shari'ah forbids suicide absolutely and lays down its own carefully limited penalties with strict conditions of proof.

What the verse establishes is proportion. A nation that had been rescued by miracles and then abandoned Allah within weeks was not going to be restored by an apology. Some wrongs are grave enough that the repentance has to cost something, and Musa tells them plainly: "That is best for you with your Maker." Better to lose life than to carry that. It is the same logic as the Prophet's words about the woman who came repeatedly insisting on her own punishment: she repented with a repentance that, if divided among seventy of the people of Madinah, would have sufficed them.

And then the ending, which is where the verse actually lands: "He accepted your repentance. Indeed, He is the Accepter of repentance, the Merciful." The severity is real, and mercy is what it opens onto. The same two names that closed the story of Adam close this one.

=55=

"And when you said, 'O Musa, we will never believe you until we see Allah plainly.' So the thunderbolt seized you while you were looking on."

The demand comes from the seventy men Musa had chosen to accompany him to the mountain — the best of them, according to the account in the Quran elsewhere (7:155). That detail matters. This was not the mob at the bottom of the mountain; it was the delegation.

And look at what they asked for. Not evidence — they had witnessed more evidence than almost any people in history. They wanted Allah produced for inspection, on their terms, as a condition of belief. It is the same posture the Makkans took: "Bring us the angels, or let us see our Lord" (25:21). The Quran calls that arrogance rather than curiosity, because the person is not seeking a Lord; he is setting terms to a Lord.

There is also a theological error inside the request. Musa himself had asked to see Allah — "My Lord, show me Yourself that I may look at You" — and was told, "You will not see Me, but look at the mountain; if it remains in place you will see Me." The mountain crumbled and Musa fell unconscious (7:143). If a prophet speaking directly with Allah could not bear it, and if a mountain could not, what did seventy men imagine would happen to them? Human beings in this life are not built for that. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said, "Know that none of you will see his Lord until he dies" — and the vision is promised in the next life, to the believers, as the highest of all rewards.

"While you were looking on" is repeated from the drowning of Pharaoh. They watched their own punishment arrive.

The practical lesson is about the shape of faith itself. Belief that will only accept what the senses can verify is not faith but sight, and it earns nothing — everyone will have sight at the end. This is why the very first quality of the believers, back in verse 3, was belief in the unseen. And it is why the Prophet, peace be upon him, said of those who would come after him and believe without ever having seen him: "Blessed are those who have seen me and believed, and blessed — seven times — are those who have not seen me and believed."

=56=

"Then We raised you up after your death, so that perhaps you would be grateful."

They were struck dead. And then they were not.

There is no drama in the wording — one clause for the resurrection, and then straight to the purpose. But consider what these people experienced: they died, and then they woke up, back in the same bodies, in the same desert, with the memory of what had happened. That is a rehearsal for the Day of Judgement performed in advance, granted to people who had just declared they would not believe without seeing.

And what does the Quran say it was for? "So that perhaps you would be grateful." Not so that you would be impressed. Not so that you would have a story. Gratitude — expressed, as always, in what you do next.

The pattern in this passage has now repeated three times: they sinned with the calf and were pardoned so that they might be grateful (2:52); they were given the Book so that they might be guided (2:53); they were killed and revived so that they might be grateful. Allah keeps giving second chances and keeps naming the same expected response. That is worth noticing as a description of how He deals with people generally, and it is a great deal more patient than most of us are with each other.

The theological point is also worth taking plainly. This is a resurrection that happened in this world, in front of witnesses — one of several the Quran records, including the man who was made to die for a hundred years and then raised (2:259) and the story that comes shortly in this very surah of the slain man restored to speak (2:73). Each one answers the objection the Makkans kept raising about bones and dust. The One who has already done it once, in public, will do it again.

And there is a smaller version of this that happens to every person every night. The Quran calls sleep a death — "Allah takes the souls at the time of their death, and those that have not died during their sleep" (39:42) — and the Prophet, peace be upon him, taught us to say on waking, "Praise be to Allah who gave us life after having caused us to die, and to Him is the resurrection." A daily return to life, with the same purpose attached to it.

=57=

"And We shaded you with clouds and sent down to you manna and quails. Eat from the good things We have provided you. And they did not wrong Us, but they were wronging themselves."

Picture the situation. A whole nation in the Sinai desert — no crops, no herds worth speaking of, no shelter, no trade routes, no way to feed itself. And it was fed anyway.

The cloud moved with them, holding off a sun that kills. The manna appeared each morning to be gathered, and the quails came within reach. Two things worth noticing: the food arrived daily rather than in a storehouse, which meant every single day was an exercise in depending on Allah; and it was described as "good things," tayyibat — not survival rations but wholesome provision.

Then the verse ends somewhere unexpected. Instead of concluding the favour, it jumps to their ingratitude — the complaints recorded a few verses later, when they asked to trade this food for onions, lentils and garlic. And it makes a statement that is one of the most important in the Quran about the nature of sin:

"They did not wrong Us, but they were wronging themselves."

Allah gains nothing from our obedience and loses nothing by our disobedience. He tells us so directly in the hadith qudsi: "O My servants, if the first of you and the last of you, the human of you and the jinn of you, were as pious as the most pious heart of any one of you, that would not increase My kingdom in anything; and if they were as wicked as the most wicked heart, that would not decrease My kingdom in anything."

So every command in the religion is for our benefit, not His. The person who skips prayer has not deprived Allah of anything; he has deprived himself of the one thing that steadies him. The person who cheats has not damaged Allah's kingdom; he has damaged his own record and his own character. Understanding this changes how the whole law feels — less like rules imposed by an authority that benefits from our compliance, more like instructions from someone with no stake except our welfare.

And it makes ingratitude look like what it is: not an insult that lands, but a wound that is self-inflicted.

=58=

"And when We said, 'Enter this town and eat freely wherever you wish, and enter the gate bowing humbly and say, "Relieve us of our burdens," and We will forgive your sins and increase the doers of good.'"

After the wandering, a town and its provisions. Notice again the structure the Quran keeps using: eat freely, wherever you like — abundance first — with one condition attached to how you come in.

The condition is about posture, and it has a physical part and a verbal part. Enter the gate sujjadan — bowing, lowered, in an attitude of submission rather than striding in as conquerors. And say hittah, a single word meaning: put down our load, relieve us of our sins.

Why insist on this at the moment of arrival? Because victory is the more dangerous test. Hardship makes people pray; success makes them forget who gave it. The Prophet, peace be upon him, entered Makkah at the height of his triumph — the city that had driven him out, now his — and he entered with his head bowed so low on his camel that his beard almost touched the saddle, reciting Surah al-Fath. Twenty years of persecution ended in that moment, and there was no gloating in it at all. That is what this command was asking for, and it is the standard.

The reward offered has two levels: "We will forgive your sins" for everyone who complies, "and increase the doers of good" for those who go beyond compliance. Forgiveness clears the debt; the increase is a bonus for those who bring excellence — the muhsinin, who do things beautifully rather than adequately.

There is a general principle here that applies to every promotion, graduation, house purchase and answered prayer. The way to receive a blessing is with your head down and your sins on your mind, not with a sense of having earned it. Every provision that comes is arriving in the hands of someone who has not been perfect, which makes asking for forgiveness the most fitting response to good news.

The next verse records what they actually did with these instructions.

=59=

"But those who wronged changed the word to other than that which had been said to them, so We sent down upon those who wronged a punishment from the sky because they were defiantly disobeying."

They were told to say one word. They said a different one.

The reports describe them entering not bowed but dragging themselves in on their backsides, and replacing hittah — "relieve us of our burdens" — with a similar-sounding nonsense phrase about grain, turning a plea for forgiveness into a joke. It was not a slip of the tongue or a failure of memory. It was a deliberate parody of a divine instruction, performed at the exact moment they were being given a town they had not earned.

Notice how carefully the verse assigns blame: "those who wronged" — not the whole nation. Twice in one sentence. The Quran is precise about collective punishment; the ones who mocked were the ones seized. Elsewhere it insists on the same principle: "They are not all the same; among the People of the Book is an upright community" (3:113).

What makes changing a word such a serious offence? Because it is the mechanism by which revelation gets hollowed out. You do not need to burn a scripture to neutralise it. You need only substitute the words — soften a command until it is a suggestion, rename a prohibition until it is a preference, keep the ritual while emptying the meaning out of it. The Quran accuses them elsewhere of exactly this, "distorting words from their places" (5:13), and the Prophet, peace be upon him, warned that a time would come when people would rename wine and consume it. The label changes; the thing does not.

Then the aggravating factor, which is really the heart of it: mockery. Sin committed out of weakness is one thing — the Quran is full of mercy for that. Sin committed as a joke, with the command itself as the punchline, is something else. It means reverence is gone, and reverence is the last thread holding a person to his religion. "Say, was it Allah, His verses and His Messenger that you were mocking? Make no excuse; you have disbelieved after your belief" (9:65-66).

The word rendered "defiantly disobeying," fisq, means stepping outside the line knowingly. It is not stumbling. It is walking out.

"And when Musa prayed for water for his people, We said, 'Strike the rock with your staff.' And twelve springs burst forth from it, and every tribe knew its drinking place. Eat and drink from the provision of Allah, and do not commit abuse on the earth, spreading corruption."

Water in a desert, from a stone. But the detail the Quran chooses to record is not the miracle — it is the plumbing.

Twelve springs, one for each tribe, "and every tribe knew its drinking place." Allah could have produced one enormous spring. Instead the provision arrived organised, so that thousands of thirsty people from twelve clans with a long history of rivalry would not be pushing each other away from a single source. Anyone who has watched a crowd around one water point knows exactly why that matters.

There is a whole way of thinking in that detail. Divine generosity is not only abundant, it is arranged — with an eye on dignity, on avoiding conflict, on people not having to fight for what they need. And it suggests something about how we should distribute what we are given, too: a relief effort, a family inheritance, a queue at a food distribution. Getting the resource there is half the work; getting it there without humiliating people or setting them against each other is the other half.

Then, attached to the gift, the limit: "Eat and drink from the provision of Allah, and do not commit abuse on the earth, spreading corruption."

Two verbs of enjoyment, and one prohibition. Take the water, take the food, no guilt about it — but do not turn provision into an occasion for damage. The word for abuse here, 'athaw, carries the sense of going too far arrogantly; joined with "spreading corruption," it covers hoarding, waste, injustice in distribution, and ruining what others depend on.

The pairing is deliberate and it recurs throughout the Quran. Every blessing arrives with a boundary. Wealth comes with zakah and a prohibition on interest and fraud; food comes with a prohibition on waste; water comes with a prohibition on abusing it — the Prophet, peace be upon him, objected to a man using too much even while performing ablution at a river. The gift and the responsibility are issued together, and receiving one without the other is how a favour turns into a case against you.
