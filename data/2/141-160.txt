=141=

"Those are a people who have passed away. For them is what they earned, and for you is what you earn. And you will not be asked about what they used to do."

The same sentence as verse 134 returns, word for word, to close the Ibrahim passage. Repetition here is a stamp. After the long debate about whether the patriarchs were Jews or Christians, the surah says again: that was a nation that went ahead. Do not wave their names as flags in an argument while avoiding the religion they actually lived. Their religion was submission. Their tests were real. Their record is sealed. Yours is being written now.

"For them is what they earned, and for you is what you earn." Kasb — earning — implies intentional work. Nothing was automatic for them, and nothing will be automatic for you. The same taqwa, the same honesty, the same prayer sit on your shoulders that sat on theirs. "And you will not be asked about what they used to do." Nostalgia for golden ancestors is cheap; imitation in sincere faith is costly and therefore valued.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, cut the same cord when he told his closest kin that lineage would not hasten anyone slowed by his deeds. By closing the long appeal twice with this line — once at 134 and again here — the Quran teaches a method: tell the stories fully, draw the lessons, then detach the heart from dependency on ancestry. Every person meets Allah alone. What helped those before was not the sound of their names but the weight of their deeds. The same will help whoever comes after.

=142=

"The foolish among the people will say, 'What has turned them away from their qibla which they used to face?' Say, 'To Allah belong the east and the west. He guides whom He wills to a straight path.'"

The surah now opens one of its great Madinan themes: the change of qibla. For about sixteen months after the migration, the Prophet, peace be upon him, and the Muslims prayed facing Bayt al-Maqdis — Jerusalem — while his heart longed for the Ka'bah of Ibrahim. Then the command came to turn to al-Masjid al-Haram, and the city filled with questions.

"The foolish among the people will say…" Sufaha' are not merely uninformed; they are light in judgement, unable to see purpose beyond the surface. Some People of the Book asked why leave our direction if your religion confirms ours. Pagans said: if the first direction was right, why abandon it; if wrong, why pray wrong for so long. Hypocrites stirred the pot: does Muhammad not know where to face?

The answer refuses their maze: "To Allah belong the east and the west. He guides whom He wills to a straight path." No direction owns Him, and no people own direction. Guidance is to submit to what He directs now, not to cling to what He directed before as if the old command had trapped the Commander. The verse also quietly announces belonging. Jews faced Jerusalem, many Christians faced the east, pagans circled idols. Muslims were now given their own qibla and, with it, a clearer independent identity among the nations.

Whenever a command of Allah shifts a habit — leaving a doubtful income, correcting a relationship, waking for a prayer long neglected — the same foolish whisper returns: why change what used to be acceptable? Because the One who owns every direction has commanded this one now. The straight path is not our preference frozen in time. It is His guidance as it arrives.

=143=

"And thus We have made you a middle nation that you may be witnesses over the people and that the Messenger may be a witness over you. And We did not make the qibla which you used to face except that We might know who follows the Messenger from who turns back on his heels. And indeed it was difficult except for those whom Allah has guided. And never would Allah let your faith go to waste. Indeed Allah is, to the people, Kind and Merciful."

The qibla change is not only about geography. It announces an identity: ummatan wasatan — a middle nation, balanced and just, standing between excess and neglect. Middle between those who drowned religion in dry legalism and those who dissolved it into sentiment without law; middle between rejecting the world and worshipping it. Wasat also carries the sense of the best — the middle of the valley where water gathers and life thrives.

"That you may be witnesses over the people." On the Day when nations deny that messengers came, this ummah will testify from what the Quran taught them that the messengers did come. And the Messenger will be a witness over you — confirming your testimony and your actual deeds. Witness is not a slogan; it is a life that can be pointed to.

"And We did not make the qibla you used to face except that We might know who follows the Messenger from who turns on his heels." The old direction became a filter. Following when the command matches your longing is easy. Following when it turns the body away from a cherished habit is the real following. Some whose attachment was to comfort more than to revelation turned back. "It was difficult except for those whom Allah guided." Physically a turn of the body; spiritually a test of who owns the heart.

"And never would Allah let your faith go to waste" — the prayers already offered toward Jerusalem remain fully rewarded. The Prophet, peace be upon him, conveyed that reassurance when companions feared for the worship of those who had died before the change. "Kind and Merciful" closes a ruling that could have felt abrupt. Behind the command is care for people who were learning, in real time, what it means to belong to a messenger.

=144=

"We have certainly seen the turning of your face toward the heaven, so We will surely turn you to a qibla that will please you. So turn your face toward al-Masjid al-Haram. And wherever you are, turn your faces toward it. Indeed, those who have been given the Book know that it is the truth from their Lord. And Allah is not unaware of what they do."

One of the tenderest lines in the Quran: "We have seen the turning of your face toward the heaven." Not once — again and again. The Prophet, peace be upon him, out of adab, did not demand with his tongue. He looked upward, hoping. And Allah says: We saw. Before any spoken request is recorded, the silent longing of His beloved is answered.

"We will surely turn you to a qibla that will please you." Yurdika — that will please you — shows how much Allah cares about what delights the heart of His messenger. He does not only give a correct direction; He gives the one that reconnects him to Ibrahim and to the House Ibrahim raised. The command came while prayer was in progress — in what became known as Masjid al-Qiblatayn, the mosque of the two qiblas — and the rows turned with him mid-salah.

"Wherever you are, turn your faces toward it." Not only in Madinah. A global ummah until the Hour — Nigeria, Indonesia, Toronto, a ship at sea — faces the same stones. "Those who were given the Book know that it is the truth from their Lord." Their own scriptures had spoken of a final messenger and of the house of Ibrahim; concealment did not erase knowledge. "And Allah is not unaware of what they do."

For anyone whose du'a is mostly a face turned upward without polished words, this verse is companionship. Allah sees the turning of your face too. The request that never quite became a sentence is still heard. If He answered the silent hope of His beloved, He is not closed to yours.

=145=

"And even if you brought to those who were given the Book every sign, they would not follow your qibla, nor will you be a follower of their qibla, nor are some of them followers of the qibla of others. And if you were to follow their desires after the knowledge that has come to you, indeed you would then be among the wrongdoers."

After tenderness comes boundary. Every ayat — every sign — could be laid before them, and stubbornness after knowledge has its own inertia. They would not follow your qibla because the issue was never missing evidence. One camp held Jerusalem as final; another held another direction; they agreed mainly in not following Muhammad, peace be upon him. "Nor are some of them followers of the qibla of others" exposes the point: their opposition was not a united principle but scattered desires wearing religious clothes.

"Nor will you be a follower of their qibla." Not out of spite, but out of fidelity to revelation that has now settled the matter. Muslims are not to drift from direction to direction hunting approval. And the warning lands on the Prophet as a lesson for the whole ummah: "If you were to follow their desires after knowledge has come to you, you would be among the wrongdoers." Knowledge raises the stakes. Once the qibla is known, once the Book is clear, turning back toward people's wishes is not diplomacy. It is zulm — putting something in the wrong place, putting desire where revelation belongs.

The verse still speaks wherever a believer is pressured to reshuffle clear guidance for the comfort of a room. Bring every proof and some will not move. Your job is not to dissolve the qibla until they smile. Your job is to stay with what knowledge brought, kindly and firmly, and refuse to trade it for a desire that has already refused the truth.

=146=

"Those to whom We gave the Book recognise him as they recognise their own sons. But indeed, a party of them conceal the truth while they know it."

Recognition is not the missing piece. "They recognise him as they recognise their own sons." A father does not squint in doubt at his child in a crowd; the features are too familiar. In the same way, the descriptions in their scripture — the place of the final prophet, his qualities, the unlettered messenger (7:157) — were familiar enough that honest scholars among them, like Abdullah ibn Salam, said they knew him more certainly than their own children, because of a child one might secretly wonder, but of this prophet the signs did not leave that crack.

"But a party of them conceal the truth while they know." The crime is kitman again — hiding what would change the conclusion. Knowledge becomes a burden the moment it threatens status, income, or the story a community has told about itself. The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned that whoever is asked about knowledge and conceals it will be bridled with fire — because concealment is not silence in private study; it is blocking a road others need to walk.

The verse divides the People of the Book with justice: not all, but a party. Some believed. Some knew and hid. For readers today it also asks an uncomfortable question: what truth have you recognised as clearly as a face you love, and then softened, delayed, or buried because speaking it would cost you? Recognition without disclosure is a kind of betrayal of the very knowledge that was meant to be a mercy for people.

=147=

"The truth is from your Lord, so never be among the doubters."

A short verse, placed like a nail after the long discussion of qibla, recognition, and concealment. "The truth is from your Lord" — al-haqq min rabbik. Not from the consensus of Madinah, not from which camp shouts louder, not from the anxiety in your chest when people mock the turn. From your Lord. That origin settles the epistemology: once something is established as coming from Him, the believer's job is not to keep it on probation until every objector is satisfied.

"So never be among the doubters." The address can touch the Prophet, peace be upon him, as honour and strengthening, and it lands on everyone who carries the message after him. Doubt here is not the passing whisper that crosses a sincere heart and is refused; it is taking up residence with the sceptics, letting their confidence in falsehood shake your confidence in truth. The Quran elsewhere teaches the believers to say, when a matter of faith comes, "We hear and we obey" (24:51), not "we hear and we renegotiate."

In practice the verse is a discipline of attention. When proof has already been given — a clear ayah, a known sunnah, a qibla already commanded — returning again and again to the mockers' framing is how doubt is rehearsed into strength. Turn the face to the House. Turn the heart to the Lord who assigned it. Leave the theatre of endless objection to those who profit from it. The truth does not become less true because someone is still unconvinced.

=148=

"And for each is a direction toward which he turns, so race with one another to good deeds. Wherever you may be, Allah will bring you forth all together. Indeed, Allah is over all things competent."

After fixing the qibla, the Quran refuses to let the community drown in argument about other people's directions. "For each is a faceah" — a direction they turn toward. Communities will keep facing what they face. That is not your race. "So race with one another to the good." Khayrat — plural goods: prayer, charity, honesty, courage, reconciliation, knowledge lived. The energy that could be wasted proving the other camp wrong is redirected into outrunning one another in what actually weighs on the scales.

"Wherever you may be, Allah will bring you forth all together." The scattering of humanity across east and west, different qiblas and different roads, ends in one gathering. Competence over all things means no soul is misplaced in the final roll call, and no deed raced toward is lost in the crowd. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said the strong believer is better and more beloved to Allah than the weak believer, while there is good in both — and he urged aiming for what benefits, seeking help from Allah, and not helplessly drifting. That is the spirit of "race."

The verse is also a cure for sectarian paralysis. When religious conversation becomes only about who faces where and who is invalid, the khayrat wait undone. Face your qibla correctly, then run. Visit the sick. Pay the debt. Learn the portion of the Book you still ignore. Allah will gather everyone in any case. The only question is what you will be carrying when He does.

=149=

"And from wherever you go out, turn your face toward al-Masjid al-Haram. And indeed, it is the truth from your Lord. And Allah is not unaware of what you do."

The command is repeated with a traveller's wording: "from wherever you go out." Life is motion — departure for trade, for battle, for migration, for a day's work — and the qibla must travel with the body. Facing the Sacred Mosque is not a Madinan luxury for people who stay near one mihrab. It is a portable identity.

"And indeed it is the truth from your Lord." Emphasised again so that repetition itself becomes reassurance against the noise outside. Truth does not wear out by being restated; weak hearts wear out by needing novelty more than stability. "And Allah is not unaware of what you do." He sees the prayer in the desert when no one is watching the alignment, the effort to find the direction on a strange road, and also the careless prayer thrown toward any wall because checking felt inconvenient.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught methods for seeking the qibla and, when honest effort still errs, affirmed that the whole earth had been made a place of prostration. Effort matters; theatre does not. For a global ummah the verse is glue. You leave your house, your city, your country — and still turn to the same House Ibrahim and Isma'il raised. That shared turn is how scattered people remain one body in worship even when they cannot see one another.

=150=

"And from wherever you go out, turn your face toward al-Masjid al-Haram. And wherever you are, turn your faces toward it so that people will have no argument against you, except for those of them who wrong. So do not fear them, but fear Me. And that I may complete My favour upon you and that you may be guided."

Third emphasis, now with both audiences clear: the Prophet singular — "turn your face" — and the ummah plural — "turn your faces." The direction is personal devotion and collective identity at once.

"So that people will have no argument against you, except those who wrong." Before the change, some People of the Book argued: if he follows earlier prophets, why a different qibla? Once the face returns to Ibrahim's foundation, that script collapses. Only the dhalimun — those committed to argument rather than truth — keep talking. "So do not fear them, but fear Me." A liberating swap of fears. Fear of mockery, of being called backward for facing a house among desert hills, of social isolation — Allah says that fear does not deserve your obedience. One rightly placed fear cancels a dozen misplaced ones.

"And that I may complete My favour upon you and that you may be guided." Favour is completed not only by provision but by clear direction, independent law, and a messenger from among yourselves. The Prophet, peace be upon him, lived this verse when pressure from every side tried to renegotiate revelation; he feared Allah enough to disappoint people. Whenever you align with a command and voices rise, remember: perfected favour lies on the far side of godly fear, not on the near side of human approval.

=151=

"Just as We have sent among you a messenger from yourselves reciting to you Our verses and purifying you and teaching you the Book and wisdom and teaching you what you did not know."

The qibla sequence opens into its companion gift. "Just as" — kama — ties the favour of direction to the favour of a messenger who makes that direction meaningful. Four tasks, answering Ibrahim's old prayer at the House (2:129), now stated as present reality: he recites Our verses — not his opinions, but Allah's words; purifies you — from shirk, rancour, greed, the diseases this surah has already named; teaches you the Book and the wisdom — the Quran and the Sunnah that places it in lived form; and teaches you what you did not know — realities no human curriculum could have reached on its own: who the Lord truly is, what follows death, what prayer does to a soul.

"From yourselves" — minkum — a brother, a neighbour, speaking your tongue, walking your markets. Not an angel you cannot imitate. A man whose purification you can watch and whose teaching you can apply the next morning in a contract. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that he was sent only as a teacher — and that understates nothing, because this teaching includes the cleaning of the heart, not only the filling of the mind.

Qibla gives the body a centre. The Messenger gives the centre an interior life. Favour is one fabric: a House to face, and a teacher who shows how facing becomes sincerity. Gratitude for him is not nostalgia; it is using what could never have been learned alone.

=152=

"So remember Me; I will remember you. And be grateful to Me and do not be ungrateful."

After law — prayer, qibla, purification — comes a verse that feels like the heart under the ribs of all of it.

"Remember Me; I will remember you." Fadhkuruni adhkurkum. The pairing is staggering. Your remembrance is small, broken, often invaded by distraction. His remembrance of you is complete — from the Lord of the worlds, in a company better than yours, with nearness and reward. You remember Him on earth; He remembers you above the heavens. The Prophet, peace be upon him, reported that Allah says: "I am as My servant thinks of Me, and I am with him when he remembers Me. If he remembers Me in himself, I remember him in Myself; if he remembers Me in a gathering, I remember him in a gathering better than it."

"Be grateful to Me and do not disbelieve" — shukr not as a word only, but as using the gift the way it was meant. Given a qibla, face it. Given a Book, follow it. Given a Messenger, obey him. Remembrance feeds gratitude; forgetfulness feeds the illusion of self-made success. The simplest practice in Islam is hidden here as one of the greatest: dhikr. No special wealth required, no fixed building — and it draws the remembrance of the One to whom you will return. If the meaning of that second remembrance were unveiled — your name held in Allah's mention — you would not want the exchange to stop.

=153=

"O you who believe, seek help through patience and prayer. Indeed, Allah is with the patient."

Having asked to be remembered and thanked, Allah hands over the tools for remaining on that path. The road ahead — a new qibla under mockery, trials named in the next verses, struggle approaching — will be heavy. So: seek help. Isti'anah is not mere advice-seeking; it is asking for power to stand.

Through what? Patience and prayer. Sabr is holding firm — against the urge to sin, in the tiredness of worship, and in the shock of affliction without collapse. Salah is the direct connection you were just given a direction for. The order matters. Patience holds the line; prayer fills the heart that is holding. Without prayer, patience hardens into bitterness. Without patience, prayer becomes a burst that fades when the mood changes.

"Indeed Allah is with the patient." Ma'iyyah — with-ness of aid and company, not only of knowledge. He is with all by knowledge; He is with the patient by support. You may feel alone in a trial; if you are patient, you are not alone. The Prophet, peace be upon him, when something distressed him, would hurry to prayer, and he would tell Bilal, "Give us rest through it." That is lived tafsir. When the world presses, do not first open a feed of other people's panic. Open prayer. The help you seek through sabr is often sent while you are still inside the salah.

=154=

"And do not say of those who are killed in the way of Allah, 'They are dead.' Rather, they are alive, but you do not perceive."

A correction of speech that is really a correction of worldview. On the tongue of grief people say "dead" as if the story ended under the soil. Allah forbids that wording for those killed in His way — not to deny the funeral or the tears, but to deny the conclusion that they are finished. "Rather, they are alive, but you do not perceive." A life with their Lord, provided for, as made explicit in 3:169-171: rejoicing in what Allah has given them, glad for those still behind who have not yet joined them.

The verse prepared a community that would soon bury loved ones at Badr, Uhud, and beyond. It did not ask them not to feel. The Prophet, peace be upon him, wept over his son Ibrahim and said the eye tears and the heart grieves, and we say only what pleases our Lord. Feeling is human; measuring existence only by breath in the chest is not the believer's measure. Martyrs are not the only people who live after death — everyone is raised — but they are singled out here so that sacrifice in the way of Allah is never tallied as pure loss.

For anyone who has buried a person of faith and righteousness, the verse stretches the horizon of the graveyard. You do not perceive their life. Your not perceiving it does not cancel it. Speak of them with the dignity of those still alive with their Lord, and let that certainty steady the living who are still walking the same road.

=155=

"And We will surely test you with something of fear and hunger and loss of wealth and lives and fruits, but give good tidings to the patient."

Not "We might." "We will surely test you." The promise of trial is as certain as the promise of help. The list is concrete: fear — insecurity, threat, dread of what may come; hunger — need in the body; loss of wealth — money, property, trade; lives — deaths of people you love, or danger to your own; fruits — crops, produce, the yield of your work and hope. "Something of" — bi-shay'in — a portion, not necessarily every form at once, but enough to show what the heart worships when ease is peeled back.

Why tell us in advance? So that when the hit comes, the first thought is not "where is Allah?" but "this is what He said would come." Expectation is half of sabr. And the verse does not end on the blow; it ends on bushra — good tidings — for the patient. The trial is real; the congratulations are for a specific response.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that if Allah wants good for someone He afflicts him, and that no fatigue, illness, worry, sorrow, harm, or grief befalls a believer, even a thorn that pricks him, except that Allah expiates some of his sins by it. He also said the greatest reward comes with the greatest trial. This ayah is the map: tests will vary across a life; the constant demanded is patience; the headline is not the fear or the hunger but the good news waiting on the other side of holding firm.

=156=

"Who, when disaster strikes them, say, 'Indeed we belong to Allah, and indeed to Him we will return.'"

Here are the patient of the previous verse, caught in the moment the disaster lands. Their first speech is not a curse on fate and not a performance for onlookers. It is inna lillahi wa inna ilayhi raji'un — we belong to Allah, and to Him we are returning.

"We belong to Allah" rewrites ownership. The wealth that burned, the person who died, the health that broke — none of it was yours in absolute title. You were a trustee. When the Owner recalls a trust, the trustee may cry, but he does not talk like a plundered absolute king. "And to Him we will return" rewrites the timeline. This disaster is not the final scene. You will stand before Him, and so will every loss and every response to loss. That double truth — ownership and return — is why the phrase became the Muslim's reflex in grief.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught that Allah says when a servant's loved one dies and he bears it patiently, seeking reward, "Build for My servant a house in Paradise and name it the House of Praise." He also taught this very statement as what the believer says when hit by calamity, with the prayer for reward and replacement that follows it. The words are short enough to say when the knees are weak. Their meaning is large enough to carry a funeral, a bankruptcy, a diagnosis. Belonging and return: say them until the heart catches up with the tongue.

=157=

"Those are the ones upon whom are blessings from their Lord and mercy. And it is those who are the rightly guided."

The patient who said the words of belonging and return are not only promised a vague future comfort. They are described as already marked: upon them are salawat from their Lord — praises, blessings, honour mentioned by Allah Himself — and rahmah, mercy that reaches the wound. And "it is those who are guided." Guidance here is not only finding the right road in law; it is finding the right posture in pain. Many know the qibla of the body who still lose the qibla of the heart when calamity hits. These people stayed pointed toward Him.

Notice the order in the three verses: test promised (155), speech of the patient (156), honour named (157). The Quran does not ask for stoicism. It asks for a sentence of tawhid in the middle of the storm, then it pours dignity over those who manage it. The Prophet, peace be upon him, was the most patient of people and the most open in tears; the combination is the sunnah. Blessings and mercy do not mean the pain was imaginary. They mean the pain was not wasted and the person was not abandoned.

When you see someone hold to inna lillahi through a year that would have emptied another person of faith, you are looking at this ayah walking. And when you are the one inside the year, hold the sentence. The salawat and the rahmah are not poetic padding. They are what the Lord of the worlds places upon those who refuse to let disaster become disbelief.

=158=

"Indeed, Safa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah. So whoever performs hajj to the House or umrah — there is no blame upon him for walking between them. And whoever volunteers good — then indeed, Allah is Appreciative and Knowing."

After martyrdom and calamity, the surah touches a rite that looked, to some new Muslims, uncomfortably like old Makkan practice. Safa and Marwah are two small hills in Makkah. Running between them is sa'y, part of hajj and umrah. Some believers hesitated: the idols Isaf and Na'ilah had once been associated with that area in Jahiliyyah; was walking there still tainted? The verse clears the air. These hills are sha'a'ir Allah — His symbols, marks He designated for worship. The pagan crust does not own what Allah has claimed.

"There is no blame" removed the hesitation. The Prophet, peace be upon him, performed sa'y, and the story behind it is Hajar, peace be upon her, running between those hills in search of water for Isma'il until Zamzam was given — a mother's struggle written permanently into the worship of millions. Every believer who walks that path re-enacts trust under thirst.

"And whoever volunteers good — then Allah is Appreciative and Knowing." Beyond the required sa'y, extra good is seen and valued. Shakir — Appreciative — is a stunning name here: the Lord of the worlds thanks His servants, multiplies what they offer, never misses a voluntary step taken for Him. Knowing — no anonymous charity of effort is anonymous to Him. Hesitation about a rite is cured by revelation; laziness about extra good is cured by knowing Who appreciates it.

=159=

"Indeed, those who conceal what We sent down of clear proofs and guidance after We made it clear for the people in the Book — those are cursed by Allah and cursed by those who curse."

The surah returns to concealment with sharper heat. Clear proofs and guidance were not sent to be locked in private libraries. They were "made clear for the people in the Book." Once clarity has been given for the public good, hiding it is a crime against the people as well as against the Sender. The threat is la'nah — curse, expulsion from mercy — from Allah and from those who curse, understood by many commentators as angels and believers whose very nature recoils from buried truth, or the curse that spreads through creation against injustice.

What was being hidden in the Madinan context included descriptions of the Prophet, peace be upon him, and rulings that threatened social advantage. The pattern is older and younger than Madinah. Every age has scholars, influencers, and ordinary people who know a verse or a hadith that would correct a profitable wrong — and who stay quiet. The Prophet warned that the one who hides knowledge is bridled with fire, and he said convey from him even if a single verse. Conveying is not always a lecture; sometimes it is refusing to let a lie sit unchallenged in a family chat.

Concealment can wear polite names: neutrality, timing, not wanting fitnah. This verse names it from the other side: after clarity, silence that protects falsehood is not neutrality. It is participation in the dark.

=160=

"Except for those who repent and correct themselves and make clear. Those — I will accept their repentance, and I am the Accepting of repentance, the Merciful."

The door swings open immediately after the curse. Exception is not vague regret. Three conditions are named: repent — turn back to Allah in remorse; correct themselves — islah, fix what their concealment damaged as far as they can; and make clear — the very truth they hid must now be shown. Private tears without public correction where the harm was public do not complete the repair. The one who buried a proof must dig it up and set it in the light.

"Those — I will accept their repentance." First person: I. The same Lord who announced the curse announces the acceptance, and He names Himself al-Tawwab, al-Rahim — the One who turns again and again to the servant, the One whose mercy makes that turning possible. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that Allah is more delighted with the repentance of His servant than a man who finds his lost camel in the desert with his food and drink on it. Delight — not reluctant paperwork.

For anyone who has withheld truth, this verse is both a mirror and a rope. The curse is real. The exception is real. Speak what you buried. Repair what you can. Then trust the Tawwab who already announced He will take you back.
