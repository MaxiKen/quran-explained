=81=

"Yes — whoever earns evil and his sin has encompassed him, those are the companions of the Fire; they will abide in it eternally."

The verse opens by knocking down the wishful claim of the one before it. No numbered days, no ancestral discount. Here is the actual rule.

But notice how carefully it is phrased, because it is not saying that anyone who sins goes to the Fire forever. Two conditions are stacked. The person "earns evil" — the word implies acquiring it deliberately, the way you earn a wage, not stumbling into it. And then his sin "has encompassed him," surrounded him on every side, with no opening left.

That image is the heart of the verse. A sin becomes encompassing when it stops being something a person does and becomes what he is — when there is no corner of the heart left that objects, no intention of ever stopping, no faith remaining underneath it. Ibn Abbas and others understood the encompassing sin to be disbelief itself, since nothing else swallows a person whole; a believer's sin, however serious, always has faith beside it, and faith is the opening.

That distinction matters enormously for anyone reading this with a heavy record. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that whoever dies not associating anything with Allah will enter Paradise, and that a man who says "there is no god but Allah" sincerely from his heart is forbidden to the Fire. Sinners from among the believers may be punished, but they are not the "companions" of it — that word, ashab, describes belonging.

So the verse cuts both ways, and it is meant to. It refuses false security: no lineage, no label, no membership card holds when a person has given himself completely to what Allah forbade. And it refuses despair: as long as the sin has not closed over you entirely — as long as it still bothers you, as long as you still turn to Him — you are not the person being described.

The practical work is to make sure nothing is allowed to become encompassing. Sins grow that way quietly, one accommodation at a time. The safeguard is the one the Prophet gave: "Follow a bad deed with a good deed and it will wipe it out." Keep something on the other side of the ledger, always.

=82=

"And those who believe and do righteous deeds — those are the companions of Paradise; they will abide in it eternally."

The mirror of the previous verse, and the Quran almost never gives one without the other. Warning and hope arrive as a pair, because a heart running on only one of them goes wrong in a predictable direction: fear alone hardens into despair, hope alone softens into carelessness.

The two conditions here are the same two the Quran keeps joining: belief and action. Not belief that stays in the chest, and not action with nothing behind it. Faith without deeds is a claim nobody tested; deeds without faith are a building with no foundation, and the Quran says of such work that it will be found to be scattered dust (25:23).

Notice the balance in the phrasing, though. The condition for the Fire in the previous verse was demanding — a sin that has encompassed the whole person. The condition here is comparatively gentle: believe, and do good. There is no requirement of perfection, no threshold of flawlessness. That asymmetry is deliberate and it appears throughout the Quran and Sunnah. Allah says in the hadith qudsi that whoever intends a good deed and does not do it has one good deed recorded, and if he does it, it is recorded as ten to seven hundred times; whoever intends a bad deed and does not do it has a good deed recorded, and if he does it, it is recorded as one. The scales are not level, and they are tilted in our favour on purpose.

And the same word appears as in the verse before: companions. But here it means belonging to the Garden — being at home in it, not visiting. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that a place in Paradise the size of a whip is better than the world and all it contains.

"They will abide in it eternally" is what makes it worth everything. Every pleasure in this life is on a timer; that is the thing that quietly spoils all of it. Remove the ending and the whole nature of the reward changes. Which is why the Prophet described the announcement made to its people: you will live and never die, be healthy and never fall ill, be young and never grow old.

=83=

"And when We took the covenant from the Children of Israel: worship none but Allah; and to parents, good treatment; and to relatives, orphans and the needy; and speak to people good words; and establish prayer and give zakah."

This is the whole religion in one sentence, and it is worth noticing that the covenant given to them is almost word for word what was later given to us.

It begins where everything begins: worship Allah alone. Every other instruction in the list depends on that one, because kindness without tawhid has no anchor and turns into whatever the culture rewards.

Then, immediately after Allah's right, comes the parents. Not after prayer, not after charity — straight after. The Quran does this repeatedly (17:23, 31:14), and the placement is the argument. When a man asked which deed Allah loves most, the Prophet, peace be upon him, said the prayer in its time, then kindness to parents, then striving in Allah's path. And when another asked who deserved his good company most, he said "your mother" three times before saying "your father."

The circle then widens outward in exactly the order a person's obligations actually run: relatives, orphans, the poor. Not a vague love of humanity, but concrete duties starting with the people nearest to you. "Begin with those you are responsible for."

Then something easy to skip past: "and speak to people good words." Not "to believers" — to people, all of them. This is the cheapest good deed available and one of the most neglected. The Prophet, peace be upon him, called a good word charity, said that a kind word and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury, and warned that a man may speak a word he thinks nothing of that drops him seventy years into the Fire. A community can be scrupulous about prayer and vicious in how it talks, and this verse rules that out.

And then the two pillars: prayer and zakah — the vertical duty and the horizontal one.

Look at the shape of the list as a whole. It moves from belief, to family, to society, to speech, to worship, to wealth. Nothing is left in a private compartment. This is why the verse ends with the note that they turned away except a few — because it is easy to accept a covenant like this in principle and very hard to live in it. The same list is sitting in front of every Muslim reading it.

=84=

"And when We took your covenant: do not shed each other's blood or evict one another from your homes. Then you acknowledged it, while you were witnesses."

A second covenant, narrower than the first and aimed at one thing: how a community treats its own.

Two prohibitions. Do not kill each other, and do not drive each other out. And notice the wording — "each other's blood," "one another." The Arabic phrases it as though the victim were the same person as the killer, which is precisely the point. Harming a member of your own community is self-harm; a body does not benefit from attacking itself. The Prophet, peace be upon him, used exactly this image: "The believers in their mutual kindness, compassion and sympathy are like one body — when one limb suffers, the whole body responds with wakefulness and fever."

The two crimes named are also not chosen at random. Killing takes a life; expulsion takes everything that makes a life liveable — home, land, neighbours, safety, history. The Quran treats forced displacement as belonging in the same category as killing, and elsewhere says that "persecution is worse than killing" (2:191). Anyone who has seen what happens to a displaced family understands why the two sit in one sentence.

"Then you acknowledged it, while you were witnesses." They agreed. Publicly, with full understanding, and they stood as witnesses to their own agreement — which means that when they later violated it, no one else needed to testify against them.

That clause is the hinge for the verses that follow, because the surah is about to describe them doing exactly these two things to each other in Madinah, in the Prophet's own lifetime, while quoting their scripture to justify it.

And there is nothing dated about the warning. Muslim history has more than enough of it too, and the Prophet's farewell sermon addressed a hundred thousand people with the same instruction: "Your blood and your property are sacred to one another, like the sanctity of this day, in this month, in this city." He also said, "Do not return after me to disbelief, striking the necks of one another." A community can hold its scripture in one hand and do this with the other. That is the possibility this verse exists to name.

=85=

"Then you are those killing one another and evicting a party of you from their homes, cooperating against them in sin and aggression. And if they came to you as captives, you ransomed them — although their eviction was forbidden to you."

Here is the covenant of the previous verse being broken in real time, and the details make it one of the most pointed passages in the surah.

In Madinah before Islam, the Jewish tribes were allied with the two Arab clans, Aws and Khazraj, who were locked in a long feud. When the Arabs fought, their Jewish allies fought alongside them — which meant Jews killing Jews and driving Jewish families out of their homes, in violation of a covenant they all held.

Then came the part that exposes everything. Their law required them to ransom Jewish captives. So after the fighting, they would pay to free the very people they had just helped to capture. And they did it as an act of religious obedience.

The Quran's question lands with full force: "Do you believe in part of the Scripture and disbelieve in part?" You kept the law about ransom and discarded the law against the killing. Why? Because ransoming cost money, and refusing to fight would have cost an alliance. The scripture was consulted where it was affordable.

This is the definition of selective religion, and it is not a historical curiosity. Everyone does it in some form: keeping the rulings that suit one's temperament, culture or income, and quietly setting aside the ones that do not. The man scrupulous about halal meat whose business runs on interest. The family that will not miss a prayer and will not speak to a relative for years. The person passionate about one Islamic obligation and unmoved by every duty that carries a cost.

The verdict is stated in full: "disgrace in the life of this world, and on the Day of Resurrection they will be sent back to the severest of punishment. And Allah is not unaware of what you do."

Islam does not come in modules. "Enter into submission completely" (2:208) — completely, because a religion accepted piece by piece is a religion in which the person, not Allah, is still choosing.

=86=

"Those are the ones who have bought the life of this world in exchange for the Hereafter, so the punishment will not be lightened for them, nor will they be aided."

The trade metaphor returns, and by now the surah has used it enough times that the reader should recognise it as its central way of describing every human choice. Verse 16 said the hypocrites purchased misguidance with guidance. Verse 41 warned against selling Allah's verses for a small price. Here the whole transaction is named plainly: this world bought, the next one paid.

What is striking is how bad the exchange rate is, and the Quran does not let us guess at it. "The enjoyment of this world is little" (4:77). "What is with Allah is better and more lasting" (28:60). The Prophet, peace be upon him, put a number on it in a way nobody forgets: he passed a dead goat with clipped ears and asked who would want it for a dirham, and they said they would not want it for anything — and he said, "By Allah, this world is more insignificant to Allah than this is to you." And: "What is this world compared to the Hereafter except that one of you dips his finger in the sea — let him see what he brings back."

Then two doors close. "The punishment will not be lightened" — no reduction, which was exactly the false hope of a few numbered days from verse 80. "Nor will they be aided" — no rescue from any quarter, which answers the assumption that connections, ancestry or intercessors would cover the difference.

But the verse is not really about them. It is a description of a transaction every person is making continuously, usually in small instalments and rarely with the price displayed. Sleep bought with Fajr. A reputation bought with a lie. An income bought with something forbidden. A relationship bought with obedience to Allah.

The believer is not asked to hate this world; he is asked to price it correctly and use it. Ali described it as travelling away from us while the Hereafter travels towards us, and told his listeners to be children of the Hereafter rather than children of this world, because today is action with no reckoning and tomorrow is reckoning with no action.

=87=

"And We gave Musa the Scripture and followed him up with messengers, and We gave Isa, son of Maryam, clear proofs and supported him with the Pure Spirit. Then is it that every time a messenger came to you with what your souls did not desire, you were arrogant — some you denied and some you killed?"

The verse begins by laying out the scale of what they were given. A Book through Musa, then messenger after messenger in unbroken succession — a privilege no other nation in history received. Then Isa, with miracles that were undeniable: healing the blind and the leper, raising the dead, all by Allah's permission, and supported by Jibril, "the Pure Spirit."

And then the question, and it is one of the sharpest in the Quran.

Look at what it identifies as the trigger. Not "every time a messenger came with something confusing" or "with insufficient evidence," but "with what your souls did not desire." That is the whole diagnosis. The problem was never the message's clarity. It was that the message wanted something from them.

This is how rejection usually works, and it is worth being honest about it because it is not a foreign disease. People rarely reject a religious truth because they examined the evidence and found it wanting. They reject it because accepting it would require changing something they have no intention of changing. The argument gets constructed afterwards, and it can be a very good argument. Desire comes first; the reasoning is hired later.

Then the two responses are named in ascending order of horror: "some you denied and some you killed." Prophets — men who came offering nothing but guidance, who asked for no payment — murdered by the people they were sent to save. The Quran mentions it repeatedly (3:112, 3:181), and it stands as the extreme end of a road that begins with the mild irritation of being told something you do not want to hear.

The test that follows from this verse is simple and uncomfortable. When a piece of religious knowledge reaches you and your first reaction is resistance, ask which is really objecting: your mind, or your appetite. The Prophet, peace be upon him, described the fully surrendered heart with a standard almost nobody meets: "None of you truly believes until his desire follows what I have brought."

=88=

"And they said, 'Our hearts are wrapped.' Rather, Allah has cursed them for their disbelief, so little is it that they believe."

Their excuse was that their hearts were ghulf — covered, sealed, wrapped in a casing. Two ways of reading it were current among the commentators, and both are revealing. Either: our hearts are already full of knowledge, containers packed with the Torah, with no room for anything further. Or: our hearts are covered, so nothing you say can get in — do not bother.

Both versions are excuses dressed as facts. Both say the same thing: the situation is fixed, so no response is required of me.

The Quran refuses the framing outright with a single word — "rather." The coverings are real, but they are not an unfortunate condition someone was born with. They are the consequence of the disbelief the person chose, exactly as described back in verse 7. Nobody is issued a sealed heart at birth. It is manufactured slowly, by repeated refusals, and then produced as an alibi.

The first reading deserves its own attention, because it is the more sophisticated failure. A heart so full of what it already knows that nothing new can enter — that is a description of the learned, not the ignorant. It is entirely possible to be so certain of one's tradition, one's school, one's teacher, one's settled position, that revelation itself could arrive and find no space. The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned that arrogance is "rejecting the truth and looking down on people," and rejecting truth is much easier when you are convinced your container is already full.

"So little is it that they believe" is the closing note, and it is a precise one. It does not say they believe in nothing. They did believe — in Allah, in Musa, in the Torah, in the Hereafter. What they had was a partial faith, edited to exclude whatever cost too much, which is the same charge as verse 85: believing in part of the Book and disbelieving in part.

The practical question is whether one's own heart still has room in it. A useful test: when was the last time you changed your mind about something because of evidence? A person who cannot remember may want to check whether the covering he is describing is a condition or a choice.

=89=

"And when there came to them a Book from Allah confirming what was with them — although before they used to pray for victory over those who disbelieved — but when there came to them what they recognised, they disbelieved in it. So the curse of Allah is upon the disbelievers."

The irony recorded here is almost unbearable, and it was public knowledge in Madinah.

Before the Prophet's arrival, the Jewish tribes of Yathrib lived alongside the pagan Arabs of Aws and Khazraj, and when disputes turned violent they would tell their opponents that a prophet was about to be sent, and that when he came they would follow him and finish the pagans as the ancient nations had been finished. They were the source of the expectation. The Arabs heard about the coming prophet from them.

Then he came. And it was the pagans who believed and the ones who had been announcing him who refused.

The verse gives the reason for the refusal in three words: "what they recognised." This was not a case of failing to identify him. They knew — the surah says shortly that they recognise him as they recognise their own sons (2:146). The problem was that he came from the wrong lineage, from the descendants of Isma'il rather than their own, and that accepting him meant handing over religious leadership to somebody else's community. When Abdullah ibn Salam accepted Islam, he asked the Prophet to question his people about him before announcing it — they called him their best and the son of their best, and the moment he declared his faith they called him the worst of them, on the spot.

That is what recognition costs when it is inconvenient, and it is why the Quran keeps insisting that most rejection is not intellectual.

There is a warning here for anyone who is waiting for something and imagines they will certainly welcome it when it comes. People pray for guidance and then find the guidance came through a person they dislike, a community they look down on, or a teacher younger than they are. They pray for relief and reject the form the relief arrives in. Wanting the truth in the abstract is easy. Wanting it in whatever shape Allah chooses to send it is the actual test — and that is a test with no lineage requirements and no comfortable answer.

=90=

"How wretched is that for which they sold themselves — that they would disbelieve in what Allah has revealed, out of resentment that Allah should send down His grace upon whom He wills among His servants."

The Quran names the motive directly, and it is one word: baghyan — envy, resentment, the refusal to accept that someone else was chosen.

That is the whole obstacle. Not the evidence, not the doctrine, not any defect in the message. They could not accept that prophethood had gone to the line of Isma'il, to an unlettered man from Makkah, to people they had regarded as beneath them religiously. The Quran identifies the same disease elsewhere: "Or do they envy people for what Allah has given them of His bounty?" (4:54).

Envy has a particular history in this Book. It was the first sin ever committed — Iblis could not accept that Adam had been honoured above him, and his objection was precisely a comparison: "I am better than him" (7:12). It was the second sin too, when one son of Adam could not bear that his brother's offering was accepted. The pattern is identical each time: not "I want good for myself" but "I cannot bear that it went to him."

And notice what envy does to a person's reasoning. These were people of scripture, waiting for a prophet, and their resentment led them to reject the very thing they had spent generations praying for. Envy does not merely make you unhappy; it makes you argue against your own interests. The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned: "Beware of envy, for envy consumes good deeds as fire consumes wood."

The remedy is in the verse itself: Allah "sends down His grace upon whom He wills among His servants." The distribution is His. Nobody earns a gift, which means nobody is being cheated when a gift goes elsewhere. Resenting another person's blessing is, at bottom, a complaint against the One who gave it — "Do they envy?" is answered by "We have apportioned among them their livelihood" (43:32).

And the everyday version needs no explanation. The colleague promoted, the relative's marriage, the friend whose memorisation is faster, the neighbour's house. The Prophet gave the antidote in a single instruction: love for your brother what you love for yourself. Not tolerate it. Love it.

=91=

"And when it is said to them, 'Believe in what Allah has revealed,' they say, 'We believe in what was revealed to us,' and they disbelieve in what came after it, while it is the truth confirming what is with them."

The answer sounds principled. We are loyal to our own scripture; we do not accept innovations. Stated that way, it has the ring of religious integrity.

The Quran dismantles it in the second half of the sentence. What they are rejecting "is the truth confirming what is with them." The Quran does not contradict the revelation they hold; it verifies its origin, affirms its prophets, and continues its message. So rejecting it is not loyalty to the Torah — it is a refusal that has nothing to do with the Torah at all. A person who genuinely believed the first message would recognise the family resemblance in the second.

Then the verse turns the argument inside out with a question: "Say, 'Then why did you kill the prophets of Allah before, if you are believers?'"

That is devastating, and it is precise. You say you believe in what was revealed to you — but what was revealed to you was brought by prophets your own forefathers killed. Zakariyya, Yahya, and others. If loyalty to your scripture were the real principle, that history could not have happened. So the claim is not a principle; it is a preference, and the preference is for the version of religion that makes no demands.

The general lesson is one worth applying inward. "We follow our tradition" is one of the most respectable-sounding reasons for refusing a truth, and it is often doing exactly the work described here — using inherited religion as a shield against present obligation. The Quran addresses the same reflex among the pagans: "We found our fathers upon a way, and we are guided by their footsteps" (43:22).

Real fidelity to a tradition means being willing to be corrected by its own sources. A person who quotes his madhhab, his family, or his community to avoid a clear proof from the Quran and Sunnah is doing what this verse describes, in a different accent. The test of whether "we believe in what was revealed to us" is sincere is simple: does the revelation ever get to overrule you?

=92=

"And Musa had certainly brought you clear proofs. Then you took the calf after that, while you were wrongdoers."

The reply to their claim of loyalty continues, and now it moves from the prophets they killed to the prophet they say they follow.

"Clear proofs" — bayyinat. Not arguments open to interpretation: a staff that became a serpent, a hand that shone, plagues that came and went on command, a sea that opened and closed. And these were not stories they inherited. This was their own nation, watching.

"Then you took the calf after that." The Quran uses the shortest possible construction. No transition, no interval, no explanation offered on their behalf. Proofs, then a calf.

The timing is what makes it damning. Not generations later, when memory had faded — while Musa was still on the mountain, within weeks, in the same lifetime, using the gold they had brought out of Egypt with them.

And the point of raising it here is to answer their present claim. They had just said, "We believe in what was revealed to us." Very well: the man to whom it was revealed was standing in front of your forefathers with undeniable miracles, and they abandoned him at the first opportunity. So the loyalty being claimed now is not a historical fact about this community; it is a position adopted for the current argument.

There is something in this for anyone who imagines that stronger evidence would fix his own religious life. It would not. The Quran is establishing across this whole passage that faith is not a function of evidence beyond a certain point — these people had the maximum available and it did not hold them for forty days. What holds a person is what he does daily: the prayer that keeps interrupting him, the company he keeps, the small obediences that build a habit of submission.

"While you were wrongdoers" closes the verse, and the word zalimun means people who put things where they do not belong. That is the exact description of idolatry: taking worship, which belongs to the Creator, and giving it to something you made yourself out of jewellery.

=93=

"And when We took your covenant and raised over you the mount: 'Take what We have given you with strength and listen.' They said, 'We hear and we disobey.' And their hearts absorbed the calf because of their disbelief."

The mountain scene returns, and this time the Quran records the response.

"We hear and we disobey." Whether they said the words aloud or their conduct said it for them, the commentators note that either way this is what the covenant amounted to in practice: full acknowledgment, zero compliance.

Set that against the sentence the Quran teaches this community to say: "We hear and we obey" (2:285). One word different, and it is the entire difference between faith and its absence. Hearing was never the issue. Nobody in that generation could claim they had not been informed.

Then a phrase with a startling image: "their hearts absorbed the calf" — they were made to drink it, the way water soaks into cloth. The love of that idol had got into them, and the verse gives the reason in the same breath: "because of their disbelief." Note the order, because it is counterintuitive. It does not say they disbelieved because they loved the calf. It says the calf soaked into them because of the disbelief that was already there. Whatever a heart is emptied of Allah, something else fills.

That is a principle worth keeping. Nobody worships nothing. A heart cleared of devotion to Allah does not stay neutral; it gets absorbed by money, status, a person, an image of itself, a cause. The Prophet, peace be upon him, spoke of the "slave of the dinar and the dirham" — the word he chose was slave, because the relationship is the same as worship, just with a different object.

The verse ends by turning the argument back on them: "Say, 'How wretched is that which your faith enjoins upon you, if you should be believers.'" You claim to be believers — but if your faith is what produced calf-worship and prophet-killing and a covenant answered with defiance, then look at what your faith is instructing you to do.

That question can be asked of any of us. A religion is known by what it makes its people do, not by what they say it is.

=94=

"Say: 'If the home of the Hereafter with Allah is exclusively for you, to the exclusion of other people, then wish for death, if you are truthful.'"

This is a challenge of astonishing confidence, and its logic is airtight.

The claim being tested was that Paradise belonged to them alone — that they were "the children of Allah and His beloved" (5:18) and that the Fire would touch them only for a few numbered days. Very well, says the Quran: then death is not a threat to you. It is the door to what you say is guaranteed. A person certain of an inheritance does not dread the journey to collect it. So wish for it.

Nobody could take the offer. And that is the argument — the refusal itself is the verdict. What a person truly believes shows up in what he is willing to bet, and no amount of confident speech survives contact with a real test.

It is worth being careful about what this verse does and does not establish. Islam does not ask believers to want to die; the Prophet, peace be upon him, forbade wishing for death because of a hardship, and told anyone who felt compelled to say, "O Allah, keep me alive as long as life is better for me, and take me when death is better for me." Life is an opportunity to add to the record, and the best of people is the one who lives long and does well.

What the verse targets is the gap between claimed certainty and actual conviction. And that gap is not confined to the people addressed here. Most of us say we believe Paradise is better than this world, and then measure our lives by exactly the standards of this world. The test does not have to be death, either. The way a person handles losing money, losing status, or losing an argument tells you what he actually thinks is real.

The Prophet described the believer's end differently: the one who loves to meet Allah, Allah loves to meet him. And when Bilal was dying and his wife cried out in grief, he said, "What joy! Tomorrow I meet the beloved ones — Muhammad and his companions." That is what conviction sounds like when it is genuine.

=95=

"But they will never wish for it, ever, because of what their hands have put forth. And Allah is Knowing of the wrongdoers."

The prediction is absolute — the Arabic doubles the negation and adds "ever" — and it was made publicly, in a city where the people concerned could have refuted it in a sentence.

That is worth pausing on as a matter of evidence. All any of them had to do was say the words: we wish for death. The Prophet's argument would have collapsed in front of the whole community, and his opponents had every motive to do it. Nobody did. Ibn Abbas commented that had they wished for it, no one among them would have remained alive.

The reason is given plainly: "because of what their hands have put forth." Their own record. Whatever a person says out loud, he knows what is in his file, and that private knowledge cannot be argued away. It is the same principle the Quran states elsewhere: "Rather, man will be a witness against himself, even if he presents his excuses" (75:14-15).

That is the mechanism at work in almost everyone's relationship with death. Fear of it is not usually a fear of pain — it is the unease of a person who suspects he is not ready. The Prophet, peace be upon him, described the two ends of it: the righteous soul is told, "Come out, contented and pleased," while the other is told to come out to the anger of its Lord — and the one longs for the Hour and the other dreads it.

Which turns this verse into a diagnostic anyone can use privately. How does the thought of dying tonight sit with you? Not as a morbid exercise but as an honest reading of your own file. Whatever discomfort surfaces is pointing at something specific — a right unfulfilled, a debt, a relationship severed, a habit — and the discomfort is a mercy, because everything it points to is still fixable today.

"And Allah is Knowing of the wrongdoers" closes it. He is not persuaded by public statements. He knows exactly what is in the hands that were put forth, which is why the Prophet advised remembering often "the destroyer of pleasures" — not to spoil life, but to keep the file in a state you would not mind being opened.

=96=

"And you will surely find them the greediest of people for life — even more than those who associate others with Allah. One of them wishes that he could be granted life a thousand years, but it would not remove him from the punishment that he should be granted life."

The comparison is deliberately shocking. More attached to this life than the idolaters — and the idolaters had a reason, because a man who expects nothing after death has only this. What excuse does someone have who believes in the Hereafter?

The answer is the point of the whole passage. It is not disbelief in the next life that produces this clinging. It is knowing what is waiting there. Someone who genuinely expects reward looks forward; someone who suspects a reckoning holds on to whatever time remains. Fear of death is often not doubt about the Hereafter at all — it is belief in it, combined with an honest reading of one's own record.

Then the image of a thousand years, and it is chosen precisely. A man might want that much time, and it would change nothing. Not because a thousand years is short — it is enormous — but because the punishment is not calculated in years, and delay is not escape. Every extra century simply adds to the file. This is the exact futility the Quran describes elsewhere: even the whole earth in gold offered as ransom would not be accepted (3:91).

There is a broader condition being named here, and Muslims are not exempt from it. The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned that the nations would gather against this community "like diners around a platter," and when asked whether that was because of small numbers he said no — you will be many, but you will be scum like the froth of a flood, and Allah will remove fear of you from your enemies and place wahn in your hearts. Asked what wahn was, he answered: "love of this world and hatred of death."

That is the diagnosis, and it is not really about mortality. It is about how much of a person's grip is on this side. The believer is meant to hold life the way the Prophet described: "Be in this world as though you were a stranger or a traveller." A traveller uses the road, takes what he needs, and does not try to buy the hotel.

=97=

"Say: 'Whoever is an enemy to Jibril — it is he who has brought it down upon your heart by permission of Allah, confirming what came before it, and as guidance and good tidings for the believers.'"

Some of them had produced an unusual objection. They said Jibril was their enemy — the angel of harshness and punishment, as they characterised him — and that if Mika'il had brought the revelation they would have accepted it.

The Quran does not debate the characterisation. It goes past it to the structure of the thing. Jibril is not an independent operator with his own preferences; he "brought it down by permission of Allah." He is a messenger carrying a message from the Sender to the recipient. Objecting to the courier as a way of refusing the letter is not an argument, and everyone involved knows it.

That is the useful lesson in the verse, because this manoeuvre is timeless. When people do not want to accept something true, they very often attack the vehicle rather than the content. The messenger's tribe, his age, his manner, his accent, his politics, his personal failings — anything except the substance of what he said. The Makkans did the same, demanding that the Quran come to a "great man from one of the two cities" (43:31), as though revelation were a matter of social rank.

Notice too what the verse says about how revelation arrived: "upon your heart." Not merely to his ears. It was placed in the Prophet's heart, which is why the Quran was preserved perfectly by a man who could not read, and why he was told, "Do not move your tongue with it to hasten it; upon Us is its collection and its recitation" (75:16-17).

And then the description of what was delivered, in three parts: confirming what came before it, guidance, and good tidings for the believers. Someone who genuinely held the earlier scripture should have found all three welcome.

The test this verse leaves behind is a small piece of honesty anyone can practise. When you find yourself dismissing something true because of who said it, ask which is really being rejected — the message, or the person. Truth does not become false because it arrived through someone you do not like.

"Whoever is an enemy to Allah and His angels and His messengers and Jibril and Mika'il — then indeed, Allah is an enemy to the disbelievers."

The reply to their objection is now stated as a principle, and the list is arranged with care.

Allah first, then the angels as a category, then the messengers, and then two angels named individually: Jibril, whom they claimed to hate, and Mika'il, whom they claimed to prefer. Naming Mika'il alongside him closes the door on the whole manoeuvre. You cannot be at war with one and at peace with the other. They serve the same Sender, carry out the same commands, and belong to the same order. Hostility to any part of that system is hostility to all of it — which is precisely what the Quran says about the messengers too: "Those who disbelieve in Allah and His messengers and wish to discriminate between Allah and His messengers... those are the disbelievers, truly" (4:150-151).

Then the closing clause, which is one of the most frightening in the Quran: "then indeed, Allah is an enemy to the disbelievers." Notice the shift. They declared enmity to an angel; the response comes from Allah Himself.

There is a related warning in the hadith qudsi that should be read alongside this: "Whoever shows enmity to a friend of Mine, I have declared war upon him." Allah takes hostility towards those who serve Him personally.

But the verse has a positive side that is easy to miss, and it belongs to the reader. Belief in the angels is one of the six pillars of faith, and it is the one most Muslims think about least. These are real creatures in constant obedience — the two recording every word (50:18), the one who came to Maryam, the ones who descend on the Night of Decree, the ones the Prophet said fill the gatherings of remembrance and lower their wings for the student of knowledge.

Knowing that changes the texture of an ordinary day. A person who remembers that the room is not empty speaks differently in it. And the sentence has a quiet inverse: whoever loves what Allah loves and honours whom He honours finds Allah on that side too.

"And We have certainly revealed to you clear verses, and no one disbelieves in them except the defiantly disobedient."

A short verse, and it makes a large claim about why people reject revelation.

"Clear verses" — bayyinat. Signs that carry their own evidence: the Quran's language, which the finest poets of Arabia could not match; its internal consistency across twenty-three years of revelation delivered in the middle of war, migration and grief; its accounts of earlier prophets given by a man who had never read a scripture; its news of the unseen. Clear does not mean everything in it is instantly obvious to everyone — the Quran itself speaks of verses requiring knowledge to unpack. It means the case for its origin is not hidden.

Then the diagnosis, and it is the same one the surah has been building since verse 26: rejection is not caused by lack of clarity. It is caused by fisq — stepping outside the line deliberately.

That word is worth understanding, because it does two things. It removes the intellectual excuse: the problem is in the will, not the evidence. And it identifies the sequence, which most people have backwards. We tend to assume that people first stop believing and then start sinning. The Quran repeatedly describes the reverse — that persistent disobedience corrodes belief. "Then the end of those who did evil was the worst, because they denied the signs of Allah" (30:10). The sin came first; the denial arrived to make peace with it.

Anyone can verify this in miniature. A person who prays regularly finds faith straightforward. Let the prayers slide for a few months and doubts he never had begin to sound reasonable. Nothing changed in the evidence. What changed was the state of the one looking at it — and the Quran said as much in verse 2, where the Book guides "those mindful of Allah."

So the practical advice from this verse is unexpected but consistent: for most people, doubt is treated less by argument than by obedience. Clean the lens, and the same clear verses look different.

"Is it not that every time they took a covenant, a party of them threw it away? Rather, most of them do not believe."

The question is asked with a kind of weariness, and it is meant to be. It gathers up everything the surah has recounted since verse 40 and asks: is there a single covenant in this entire history that was actually kept?

The record is laid out already. The covenant at Sinai under the mountain (2:63) — turned away from. The covenant to worship Allah alone and honour parents and speak well to people (2:83) — abandoned by all but a few. The covenant not to shed each other's blood or drive each other out (2:84) — broken publicly in Madinah. And in the Prophet's own time, treaties made with him and then violated.

The word for throwing it away, nabadha, means to toss something aside as worthless — the way you discard something behind your back without looking. That is the image: not a covenant weighed and reluctantly broken, but one dropped because it had ceased to matter.

Notice again the precision: "a party of them," not all. The Quran keeps that qualification in place even at its most critical, because accuracy is part of justice. And the closing line locates the root: "most of them do not believe." Covenant-breaking is a symptom. A person who genuinely believes Allah is watching, and that he will be questioned, does not treat his word as disposable.

Which makes this verse extremely uncomfortable for Muslims, because keeping one's word is not presented in Islam as a nice trait. It is a marker of faith itself. The Prophet, peace be upon him, listed breaking a promise among the three signs of a hypocrite, alongside lying and betraying a trust. He said, "There is no faith for one who has no trustworthiness, and no religion for one who does not keep his covenant." And the standard extends to enemies: Muslims are bound by treaties even with those who fight them, and betrayal is forbidden absolutely.

The everyday form of this is small and constant. The appointment not kept. The money promised and not sent. The commitment made to a spouse or a child and quietly dropped because something else came up. Every one of those is a small nabdh — something tossed behind the back. The surah's verdict is that a habit of it says something about what a person actually believes.
