=21=

After the compelling reflection on the signs of creation, the sūrah returns to the direct address of the Prophet (ﷺ) with a command that serves as both a comfort and a clarification of his mission: “So, continue to remind all, O Prophet, for your duty is only to remind.” The Arabic fa-dhakkir innamā anta mudhakkir uses the imperative dhakkir, from the root dhikr, meaning to remind, to admonish, to cause to remember. The particle fa- (so, therefore) links this command to the preceding arguments. Because the signs of Allah’s power in the camel, the sky, the mountains, and the earth are so manifest, and because the fate of the deniers and the reward of the believers have been so clearly detailed, the Prophet is to persist in his mission of reminding. He is not to be disheartened by the rejection he faces.

The word innamā is a particle of restriction, meaning “only” or “solely.” It strictly delineates the boundaries of his responsibility. The Prophet’s duty, his primary function, is to be a mudhakkir, a reminder. He is not a controller, a compeller, a guardian over their faith, or a guarantee of their guidance. His task is to deliver the message with clarity, persistence, and compassion, and to leave the outcome to Allah.

This is an immensely liberating instruction. The Prophet deeply desired the guidance of his people, and their persistent rejection weighed heavily on his soul. This verse lifts that weight by clarifying that his success is not measured by their acceptance, but by his faithful delivery. He is a reminder, and the reminder has intrinsic value even if it appears to fall on deaf ears.

The word “continue to remind” in the present tense implies an ongoing, unceasing effort. The Prophet is to keep reminding, morning and evening, in public and in private, with hope and with warning. The reminder is the lifeline thrown to drowning humanity, and the lifeguard is not responsible if the drowning person refuses to grab it, provided he has thrown it with all his might.

The verse also applies to every caller to truth. The believer who shares the message is not responsible for converting hearts. The moment a da‘i begins to feel frustration that borders on despair, he must recall this verse: my duty is only to remind. The reminder itself is a mercy, a light, and it will not be wasted. Even if it only benefits some, as earlier surahs stated, the effort is commanded. The focus shifts from the response of the audience to the sincerity of the effort, and that shift is the source of the Prophet’s peace of mind.

The next verse will reinforce this restriction, preventing any misunderstanding that the Prophet might have the power to force belief.

=22=

The clarification of the Prophet’s role is made even more explicit in verse twenty-two: “You are not there to compel them to believe.” The Arabic lasta ‘alayhim bi-muṣayṭir uses a strong negation with lasta, “you are not.” The key word is muṣayṭir, from the root sayṭara, which means to be a controller, a dominator, a keeper, one who exercises authority and compulsion over others. The verse firmly removes any such role from the Prophet. He is not sent as a muṣayṭir over the people. He cannot and must not force them to accept faith.

This is a fundamental principle in Islam, articulated clearly in the Qur’ān and later codified in the maxim “There is no compulsion in religion.” Faith, by its very nature, is a matter of the heart and the free will. If a person is forced to utter the words of the shahādah while their heart remains unconvinced, that is not true belief but hypocrisy, and it holds no weight with Allah. The Prophet’s role is to convey, to argue in the best way, to be a beautiful example, and to warn—but not to compel. The verse was revealed in a context where the Prophet was being mocked, persecuted, and many of his followers were being tortured. A lesser person might have wished for the power to force the disbelievers into submission. The Qur’ān instructs him that such power is not part of his mission. The battle is not for bodies but for hearts, and hearts can only be won through truth and mercy, not coercion.

This teaching stands in stark contrast to the methods of many worldly powers throughout history that have sought to impose religious uniformity through force. It establishes the ethical boundaries of da‘wah. The da‘i is a witness, a herald, a lamp—not a jailer.

The verse also carries a profound theological insight: guidance is ultimately in the hands of Allah alone. Even the Prophet, the best of creation, is not able to place faith into a single heart without Allah’s will.

Therefore, he should not burden himself with what is beyond his capacity. The word muṣayṭir also carries the nuance of a guard or a watchman who keeps people under surveillance and control. The Prophet is not even that. He is not sent as a keeper over their deeds, just as earlier sūrahs told the believers that the disbelievers were not sent as keepers over them. The mutual release from the role of policing each other’s souls fosters an environment where conviction is based on genuine, free acceptance. The believer who understands this verse learns to offer the truth with love and then release the outcome, refusing to slip into the arrogance of wanting to control the spiritual destinies of others.

The next verse will describe the consequence for those who, despite this gentle, compelling-free invitation, persist in turning away.

=23=

The sūrah now turns to the other side of the equation, describing those who reject the reminder despite its clarity and the gentle manner of its presentation: “But whoever turns away, persisting in disbelief.” The Arabic illā man tawallā wa kafar uses the verb tawallā, which means to turn one’s back, to turn away, to retreat from something in aversion.

This is not a passive neglect; it is an active, deliberate turning of the face and the heart away from the truth. The second verb, kafar, means to disbelieve, to cover up the truth. The conjunction wa (and) links the external act of turning away with the internal state of disbelief. The person described here is not someone who is merely ignorant or who has not heard the message clearly.

This is someone who has been reminded, who has seen the signs, who has had the opportunity to reflect on the camel, the sky, the mountains, and the earth, but has made a conscious decision to reject. The phrase “persisting in disbelief” captures the ongoing, stubborn nature of their state. They do not have moments of reflection followed by regret; they deepen in their rejection. The verse implicitly contrasts with the earlier verses about the people of Paradise, who were pleased with their striving and received the reward. The ones who turn away are choosing the path of the people of Hell, the downcast, exhausted faces. The construction “whoever turns away” is general, applying to anyone, from the Meccan leaders to any human being until the end of time, who responds to the divine reminder with aversion and disbelief. The verse serves as a warning and also as a final demarcation. The Prophet is told: your duty is only to remind.

For those who accept, there is bliss. For those who turn away and persist in disbelief, there is a different outcome, which the next verse will describe. This segmentation of humanity based on their response to the reminder is a recurring Qur’ānic theme, and it places the ultimate responsibility on the individual. No one can claim on the Day of Judgment that they were forced to disbelieve by a compelling Prophet. They turned away by their own choice.

The word tawallā paints a vivid image of a person physically turning their back on something, a gesture of contempt and rejection. This is the posture of the disbeliever towards the truth. The believer is asked to examine his own heart: is there any trace of this turning away in my response to the commands of Allah? The verse serves as a mirror, urging a continuous turning towards the truth, not away from it. The consequence for this turning away is specified in the next verse as a major punishment from Allah.

=24=

The consequence for the one who turns away and persists in disbelief is delivered in stark terms in verse twenty-four: “then Allah will inflict upon them the major punishment.” The Arabic fa-yu‘adhdhibuhu Allāhu al-‘adhāba al-akbar uses the verb yu‘adhdhibuhu, meaning He will punish him severely. The subject is explicitly named: Allāh.

This is not a punishment from an angel or a natural force; it is the direct punishment from the Lord of all worlds. The punishment is described as al-‘adhāb al-akbar, the greatest punishment, the major punishment. This superlative form indicates that the punishment for this deliberate turning away from the clear reminder is of the utmost severity and magnitude. It is the punishment of the Hereafter, the eternal Fire, as opposed to any lesser, temporary punishment that might occur in this world, such as famine, defeat, or the torment of the grave. Some scholars have said that the “major punishment” refers specifically to the punishment of Hell on the Day of Judgment, while the “minor punishment” (mentioned elsewhere) refers to the afflictions of this world. Others said the major punishment is that of the Hereafter in general, because there is no punishment greater than the eternal wrath of Allah. The verse makes clear that this punishment is not arbitrary; it is the direct result of their own turning away. The particle fa- (then) indicates a logical sequence: they turned away, so Allah will punish them. Divine justice is a response to human choice. The severity of the punishment matches the gravity of the crime. The disbeliever rejected the Most High, the Most Merciful, the One who provided all the signs in creation and sent the clear reminder. For such a rejection, only the major punishment is fitting.

The verse also serves as a comfort to the Prophet and the believers. It assures them that the justice they long for will be executed, not by their hands, but by the Lord Himself. They do not need to seek revenge or despair at the apparent prosperity of the deniers. The major punishment is in the hands of Allah, and it is certain.

The word akbar (greatest) also implies that there are lesser punishments, but the one reserved for the obstinate, persistent disbelievers is the greatest of them all. This should fill the believer with a healthy fear and a renewed commitment to never be among those who turn away. The verse is a sobering conclusion to the section on the deniers, and the sūrah will end by emphasizing the absolute sovereignty of Allah over the entire process of return and reckoning.

=25=

The sūrah now anchors the entire warning in a foundational theological truth in verse twenty-five: “Surely to Us is their return.” The Arabic inna ilaynā iyābahum uses the emphatic particle inna and the possessive pronoun nā (Us) in the majestic plural.

The word iyāb is a noun from the root āba, which means to return, to come back. It is a return after a journey. The verse declares that the ultimate destination of all these people—the ones who turned away and the ones who believed—is back to Allah.

This is the same truth that was stated in Sūrah ‘Abasa and Al-Infiṭār, but here it is given as the direct follow-up to the threat of punishment. The disbeliever may turn away in this world, but he cannot turn away from the return. He will inevitably be brought back to his Lord.

The phrase “to Us” is intensely personal. They are not returning to an abstract principle or a distant judge; they are returning to the very Lord who created them, provided for them, and sent them the reminder. This return is the ultimate meeting, the moment when the fleeing soul is brought face to face with its Maker. The believer finds immense comfort in this: the return to the Most Merciful is the homecoming of the soul. The disbeliever finds only terror: the return is to the One whose signs he denied.

The word iyāb also carries a connotation of a final, permanent return. The journey of worldly life is over, and the soul arrives at its eternal abode. There is no further travel, no more movement. The return is the end of the wandering. The verse serves as a powerful reminder of the transience of worldly status. The disbeliever who strutted in the marketplace of Mecca as if he owned the earth will one day be returned, powerless and alone, to the Lord of the earth. The believer who was oppressed and had no advocate in this world will be returned to the best of advocates, the Lord who witnessed everything.

The verse also provides the logical foundation for the reckoning. If all must return to Allah, then there must be a reckoning. The return is not for nothing. A return to the King demands an accounting. The sūrah thus builds up to the final verse, which explicitly mentions the reckoning, completing the circle that began with the Overwhelming Event. The return is certain; the reckoning is the purpose of the return.

=26=

The sūrah concludes with the second half of the divine assurance, making the purpose of the return explicit: “then surely with Us is their reckoning.” The Arabic thumma inna ‘alaynā ḥisābahum uses thumma to indicate a sequence that is also a necessary consequence. After the return to Allah comes the reckoning.

The word ḥisāb comes from the root ḥasaba, meaning to count, to calculate, to take account. It is the precise, meticulous judgment of every deed, word, and intention.

The phrase “‘alaynā” (upon Us) is a declaration of divine responsibility. Allah has taken it upon Himself, in His justice and wisdom, to settle the accounts of every soul. No one will be overlooked, no deed will be forgotten, no oppression will remain unaddressed. This reckoning is not delegated to angels alone; it is attributed directly to Allah, emphasizing its ultimate authority and infallibility. The verse is a final, powerful answer to the disbelievers who denied the judgment. They refused to believe that their deeds would ever be counted, that the scales of justice would ever be set up. The sūrah ends by telling them that the reckoning is not just a possibility; it is a certainty, and it is upon Allah Himself. The One who created the camel, raised the sky, set up the mountains, and levelled the earth—that same One will conduct the reckoning. The believer finds in this verse the ultimate reassurance. The seeming injustices of the world are not the final word. The scales that were tampered with in the market, the mockery that was endured in silence, the faith that was held onto in the face of persecution—all of it will be weighed and recompensed in the reckoning that is with Allah.

The word ḥisāb also implies a swift and easy account for the believers, as described in earlier sūrahs, and a severe, drawn-out account for the disbelievers. The sūrah that began with the “Overwhelming Event” and painted the vivid pictures of the downcast, burned faces and the glowing, pleased faces ends with the ultimate reason for those fates: the return and the reckoning. The entire arc of the surah is complete. The listener is left with the profound awareness that life is a journey towards a meeting with the Lord, and that meeting will involve a full, just, and unavoidable accounting.
