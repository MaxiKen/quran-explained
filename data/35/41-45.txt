=41=

After the previous verse demolished any rational basis for idol worship, this verse turns our attention to the continuous, active power of Allah that sustains the very fabric of the universe. It states, “Indeed, Allah alone keeps the heavens and the earth from falling apart.

” This is a profound truth that often escapes human notice because the cosmos appears so stable. The sky above us does not crack and fall; the earth beneath our feet does not shake itself to pieces.

Yet this stability is not inherent in matter. It is a direct, ongoing act of divine maintenance. The verse uses a word that implies holding back or preventing something from collapsing. The universe, with its immense forces, is held in a perfect balance by its Creator. If He were to withdraw His sustaining command for even a moment, the entire order would disintegrate.

The verse then drives the point home: “If they were to fall apart, none but Him could hold them up.” No power in the universe—no angel, no natural law, no human technology—could restore that balance. This stark statement silences any lingering thought that other beings might share in Allah’s control. If the whole cosmic structure collapsed, the idols would be the first to be destroyed; they could not save themselves, let alone hold up the heavens.

The verse then seals this display of power with two beautiful names: “He is truly Most Forbearing, All-Forgiving.” The choice of these names at this moment is remarkable. After declaring that Allah could let the universe collapse, we are reminded why He does not. Despite humanity’s widespread ingratitude, denial, and sin, He is al-Ḥalīm—the Most Forbearing, not hasty in punishment, giving time for repentance. He is al-Ghafūr—the All-Forgiving, ready to pardon those who turn back. The universe remains intact not because we are worthy, but because of His forbearance and His plan to allow a term for testing and forgiveness.

For the believer, this verse inspires awe and gratitude. Every moment of stability in our lives, every sunrise, is a sign of divine restraint and mercy. It calls us to trust the One who holds everything together, and to seek His forgiveness before the term ends.

=42=

This verse uncovers a tragic irony in the psychology of the Makkan pagans. Before the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ was sent, the Arabs were aware of the history of previous nations. They had heard from Jews and Christians about messengers like Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus. They looked at their own idolatrous state and, in moments of reflection, they criticized those past nations for their stubbornness. “They swore by Allah their most solemn oaths” indicates that they would make emphatic, heartfelt vows—using Allah’s name, despite their idols—that if a warner were to come to them, they would be the most guided of all people. They believed they were smarter, more perceptive, and more ready for truth than the Jews or Christians, whom they saw as having scriptures but being misguided. Ibn ‘Abbās and other early commentators reported that the Quraysh used to say, “The Jews and Christians had prophets and books, and we are just like them. If a messenger came to us, we would certainly be better guided than any other community. ” This was their boast.

However, the verse exposes the outcome: “Yet when a warner did come to them, it only drove them farther away.” When Muhammad ﷺ came with the clear truth, the very people who swore they would embrace guidance reacted with the most intense rejection. Their oaths were revealed to be empty words spoken from pride, not genuine longing for truth. The warner’s arrival did not increase them in guidance; it increased them in aversion, flight, and hostility. This exposes a deep human flaw: it is easy to think of oneself as open-minded and virtuous in the abstract, but when the truth actually comes—demanding the sacrifice of status, ancestral customs, and desires—the heart’s hidden arrogance surges. The verse is a warning to all who assume that if they only saw a clear sign, they would surely believe. The Makkans saw the clearest of signs and the most noble of messengers, yet it only pushed them further into enmity.

For believers, it teaches humility: we should never assume we are immune to such hardness, and we must constantly ask Allah to keep our hearts receptive.

=43=

This verse continues describing the reaction of the disbelievers when the warner came. Instead of reflecting and accepting, they met the message with “arrogance in the land.” Their arrogance was not a passive feeling; it was an active stance of superiority, looking down on the Prophet and his humble followers. They considered it beneath their dignity to follow a man from among themselves, an orphan without great wealth. Alongside arrogance, they engaged in “plotting evil.” This included their secret meetings in Dār al-Nadwah, where they conspired to kill, imprison, or expel the Prophet ﷺ. It also encompassed their propaganda, their mockery of the Quran, and their attempts to turn the masses away.

The verse then delivers a decisive principle: “But evil plotting only backfires on those who plot. ” The Arabic word for evil plotting is makr, and the verse says the bad makr does not engulf anyone but its own schemers. This is a divine law visible across history. The plots of Pharaoh against Moses, of the pagans against the prophets, all ended up destroying the plotters while the prophets were saved and honoured. The harm they intended for others circled back upon them with perfect justice. Then the verse poses a rhetorical question: “Are they awaiting anything but the fate of those destroyed before?” The Makkans had only two options: embrace faith or await the same ruin that befell the peoples of Noah, ‘Ād, Thamūd, and others.

The phrase “You will find no change in the way of Allah” means that Allah’s established practice of eventually destroying entrenched, arrogant disbelief never changes. “Nor will you find it diverted” means this punishment cannot be shifted away from the guilty to someone else. No ransom, no protector, no clever scheme can avert the divine way.

This is both a warning to the arrogant and a comfort to the oppressed believers: the Almighty’s justice operates on fixed, unbreakable principles, and the plotters will soon witness the recoil of their own evil.

=44=

This verse issues a direct invitation to observe historical evidence. The Quraysh were a travelling merchant tribe; their winter and summer journeys took them to Yemen, Syria, and beyond. They passed by the ruins of the ‘Ād, the carved homes of the Thamūd in the mountains, the overturned cities of Lot’s people. The verse asks, “Have they not travelled throughout the land to see what was the end of those destroyed before them? ” The remains of these civilizations were not hidden; they were visible along the trade routes. The Quraysh saw them and even told stories about them.

Yet, they did not internalize the lesson. The verse then adds a crucial detail: “They were far superior in might. ” The previous nations were not weak. ‘Ād were giants of physical strength and builders of massive pillars. Thamūd carved secure homes out of solid rock. Pharaoh commanded a vast, powerful empire. Compared to them, the Makkan pagans were insignificant in material power. If those mighty nations could not resist Allah’s punishment when it came, how could the Quraysh hope to? The reason for their destruction is given: “But there is nothing that can escape Allah in the heavens or the earth.” No fortress, no strength, no hidden refuge can protect a person from divine decree. The power of Allah encompasses everything, and nothing can slip out of His grasp. Then the verse closes with two attributes that explain why this is so: “He is certainly All-Knowing, Most Capable.” His knowledge covers every hidden plot and every secret sin, so no crime goes unrecorded. His capability means that no target is too protected, no enemy too strong.

For the believer, this verse is an encouragement to explore history and archaeology with the eye of faith, seeing in ruined empires a repeated pattern of moral cause and effect. It also reframes the apparent strength of the oppressors of any age. Their might is an illusion; they are walking on the same path as Pharaoh and his like, and the end is already written in a law that never changes.

=45=

The final verse of this magnificent surah returns to the theme of divine forbearance that has shimmered throughout the chapter. It presents a terrifying yet merciful reality: “If Allah were to punish people immediately for what they have committed, He would not have left a single living being on earth.

” This is not an exaggeration. If every sin—every lie, every act of oppression, every moment of ingratitude—were met with immediate, proportionate punishment, the surface of the earth would be swept clean of life. Not a single moving creature would remain, because even the animals and the environment are affected by human sin and would be caught up in a comprehensive punishment. The Prophet ﷺ explained that even birds in their nests would not be spared. This truth silences anyone who questions why evil people seem to prosper.

The answer is that Allah does not punish instantly. He delays.

The phrase “But He delays them for an appointed term” is the key to understanding the state of the world. The term is fixed, written in the Preserved Tablet. For an individual, it is the moment of death. For a community, it is the moment when their collective evil has reached its fullness and their window of repentance is closed. This delay is an act of monumental mercy. It allows the sinner time to repent, the oppressor time to change, and the negligent time to awaken. Without this delay, humanity would have no chance at all.

However, the delay should not be mistaken for indifference or weakness. The verse ends with a solemn declaration: “And when their time arrives, then surely Allah is All-Seeing of His servants.” When the appointed term comes, judgment is rendered with perfect vision. Allah has been watching every deed, every intention, every hidden act. Nothing is forgotten, nothing is overlooked. The forbearance that held back the immediate lightning bolt will give way to a justice that sees all and compensates all.

For the believer, this verse cultivates a profound balance between hope and fear—hope in the mercy that grants us another day to turn back, and fear that the term may end while we are still in negligence. It is the perfect concluding note for a surah that began with the Originator of the heavens and the earth, reminding us that the same Creator who sustains the cosmos is the One who waits patiently for His servants to return to Him.
