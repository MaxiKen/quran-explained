=1=

The surah opens with a gentle yet profound correction directed to the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. The question, “Why do you prohibit yourself from what Allah has made lawful to you?” is not a harsh reprimand but a loving inquiry from the Most Wise. It draws attention to an incident that occurred within the Prophet’s household, a moment that reveals both his human nature and the perfection of divine guidance. The background, as reported in authentic hadiths, is that the Prophet once visited his wife Zainab bint Jahsh and was offered honey, which he enjoyed. He stayed with her longer than his usual time. This stirred a sense of jealousy in two of his other wives, Ḥafṣah and ʿĀ’ishah, who agreed to a playful plan. When the Prophet visited each of them, they said, “I smell the odour of maghāfīr on you”—a sweet but foul-smelling resin that bees sometimes collect, which leaves an unpleasant scent. The Prophet, who disliked any bad smell on his person, swore that he would never eat honey again. In doing so, he prohibited upon himself something perfectly lawful, purely out of a desire not to displease his wives.

The verse addresses this act: why would you make unlawful what Allah has permitted, seeking to satisfy your wives’ feelings? The lesson here is monumental: no one, not even a prophet, has the authority to declare permissible things forbidden. The bounds of halal and haram are Allah’s alone.

Yet the verse immediately balances this with two magnificent names: “And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” This assures the Prophet and all believers that even when a person’s loving concessions lead to an unnecessary restriction, Allah’s forgiveness and mercy are vast. The Prophet’s humanity is highlighted, showing that even the best of creation can make a choice that needs divine realignment. It also instructs husbands that while pleasing one’s wife is noble, it must not lead to transgressing Allah’s limits. The entire affair was handled with such wisdom that it became a permanent source of guidance, demonstrating that the smallest moments in a blessed household can yield universal laws.

=2=

Having gently corrected the Prophet for his oath, the surah immediately provides the solution, revealing Allah’s mercy even within the legislation. The verse declares that Allah has already ordained the means to be released from such oaths. This refers to the kaffārah, the expiation for breaking an oath, which is detailed elsewhere in the Quran and Sunnah: feeding ten needy people, clothing them, freeing a slave, or fasting three days if one cannot afford the other options.

The use of the word “ordained” indicates that this is a set, divinely sanctioned procedure, not something left to human guesswork. The Prophet later acted upon this, offering the expiation and resuming the enjoyment of honey.

Thus, a believer who makes a vow to avoid something permissible or an oath that leads to hardship is not trapped; Allah has opened a merciful exit. The verse then reinforces this comfort with “For Allah is your Guardian.

” The word Mawlā implies a protector, a patron, and a helper who looks after the interests of His servants. He does not desire their suffering. By providing the expiation, He proves His guardianship. He is “the All-Knowing, All-Wise.” He knows the intentions behind the oaths and the weaknesses that prompt them, and His wisdom decreed a practical way out rather than leaving the burden unrelieved. This also contains a subtle instruction for the community: in matters of marital harmony, one should not make rash oaths that complicate life. But if it happens, the door to relief is open. The principle extends far beyond honey; any oath that restricts a lawful thing or that is taken in a moment of emotion can be resolved through the kaffārah, preventing the hardening of a temporary decision into a permanent restriction. The verse beautifully blends law with love, showing that Islam is not a set of rigid chains but a path with merciful off-ramps provided by the All-Knowing Guardian.

=3=

This verse unveils a more private layer of the incident, touching on the sacred trust between spouses. The Prophet confided a secret to one of his wives—reports indicate it was Ḥafṣah—concerning the oath about honey or another personal matter, and he specifically asked her not to tell anyone.

However, she later disclosed it to ʿĀ’ishah. The phrase “when she disclosed it” shows that a breach of confidence occurred. Then came the divine revelation, and “Allah made it known to him.” The Prophet was informed of the entire conversation.

However, in a display of remarkable forbearance and wisdom, when he confronted his wife, “he presented part of what was disclosed and overlooked a part.” He did not reveal every detail that Allah had shown him, sparing her the full embarrassment and leaving room for dignity. This selective revelation was an act of mercy and a lesson in how to correct a loved one: you do not have to throw every mistake in their face; you can address the core issue while preserving their honour. The wife’s astonished response, “Who told you this?” was a natural human reaction—she could not imagine how her private words had reached him. The Prophet’s answer was clear and powerful: “I was informed by the All-Knowing, All-Aware.” He attributed the knowledge directly to Allah, the One whose knowledge encompasses every secret whisper, every hidden conversation. This response was not only an explanation but also a profound reminder of Allah’s constant presence. It reinforced the lesson that no plot or private disclosure can escape divine sight. The incident teaches the sanctity of marital secrets; a spouse is a trusted confidant, and breaking that trust is a serious matter. It also shows the Prophet’s honesty and his lack of any supernatural knowledge on his own; he only knew what Allah taught him. For the wider community, this is a warning against gossip and a lesson in the gentle art of correction, mirroring the divine balance of justice and mercy.

=4=

The address now shifts to a direct, dual-form appeal to the two wives, understood to be Ḥafṣah and ʿĀ’ishah. The verse begins with an invitation to repentance, a path of mercy: “It will be better if you both turn to Allah in repentance.” The phrasing is gentle, leaving the door wide open for return. The reason given is that “your hearts have certainly faltered. ” The Arabic word “sāghat” implies a deviation, an inclination away from the straight course of supporting the Prophet and maintaining his comfort. Their hearts had swerved, driven by natural feminine jealousy, into a plot that caused the Prophet to swear off something lawful and to experience domestic tension.

Yet Allah does not condemn them outright; He invites them back, showing that the station of a prophet’s wife is a matter of immense mercy, not a curse that punishes every misstep without a chance to make amends. However, the verse also contains a stern warning: “But if you continue to collaborate against him, then know that Allah Himself is his Guardian. ” The word “tazāharā” means to back each other up in an action against him. The warning is that their alliance, however close they may be, is nothing against the alliance that stands with the Prophet. The list of his supporters is awe-inspiring: Allah is his Mawla, his ultimate protector. Then comes Gabriel, the chief of the angels, the carrier of revelation. Then “the righteous believers,” the pious community that stands by him. And finally “the angels,” the entire heavenly host, are his reinforcements. This magnificent enumeration serves to dwarf any opposition. It reassured the Prophet that personal troubles in his home did not mean he was abandoned by the celestial and earthly forces of good. It also alerted the wives that their position was precious but not untouchable; they were surrounded by a divine security detail that would ensure the mission’s success irrespective of their choices. The verse masterfully combines a soft call to repentance with a firm display of divine backing, guiding the hearts back to reverence.

=5=

The warning deepens with a revelation that strikes at the heart of any complacency. “Perhaps, if he were to divorce you all, his Lord would replace you with better wives.

” The term “perhaps” when used by Allah does not imply doubt; it indicates that this is an easy and possible outcome, underscoring the reality that the Prophet’s household is a divine trust, not a personal privilege beyond recall. The wives are reminded that they are not irreplaceable. The description of the “better wives” that Allah could provide serves as both a standard and a mirror. They would be “Muslimāt,” those who are fully submissive to Allah’s will in every aspect of life, not letting personal desires override obedience. “Mu’mināt,” possessing deep, unwavering faith that manifests in their conduct. “Qānitāt,” devoutly obedient, constantly in a state of prayerful devotion. “Tā’ibāt,” repentant, always turning back to Allah when they err, unlike those who persist in fault. “ʿĀbidāt,” dedicated worshippers, and “sā’iḥāt,” which many scholars explain as women who fast frequently, as fasting is a form of spiritual journey, or women who emigrate for Allah’s sake. The list concludes with “thayyibāt wa abkārā,” including both those previously married and virgins, indicating that no particular status is an obstacle for Allah to bless His Prophet with the best. This detailed catalogue was not merely theoretical; it placed before the Mothers of the Believers a model to strive for. Reading this verse, ʿĀ’ishah and Ḥafṣah, along with the other wives, were deeply moved. It prompted them to intense introspection and, ultimately, to the sincere repentance referenced elsewhere. The verse is a masterful blend of admonition and motivation, showing that the honor of being the Prophet’s wife demands the highest caliber of inner qualities. It serves as a timeless reminder that spiritual rank is not inherited by proximity to the righteous but earned by one’s own devotion.

=6=

From the specific instruction to the Prophet’s household, the surah now widens the lens to encompass all believers. The call is urgent: “Protect yourselves and your families from a Fire.” The protection of one’s own soul begins with obeying Allah, but the command extends to the family—the spouse and children. This places a sacred responsibility on the head of the household to teach, guide, and discipline with love so that the family is not led into the path of destruction. The Fire is described with terrifying clarity: “whose fuel is people and stones.” The stones refer to the idols that were worshipped, or to sulfurous rocks that burn with intense heat, and the people are the unrepentant sinners who become kindling for the blaze.

This image is meant to shake the heart, to make one imagine the horror of being fuel for a fire alongside the very objects of false worship. The angels in charge of this Fire are “ghilāẓ shidād”—formidable, stern, and severe. There is no softness in them, for they are created for this exact purpose of exacting divine justice.

The verse adds, “who never disobey whatever Allah orders—always doing as commanded.” Their absolute obedience means that the punishment they administer is neither less nor more than what Allah has willed; it is perfectly just and inescapable.

This description serves as a powerful deterrent. It calls every believer to create a home environment steeped in the remembrance of Allah, prayer, and moral education.

The verse reminds that familial love should not be a blind love that tolerates sin; true love is to protect one’s family from the ultimate calamity. It directly connects to the earlier domestic incident: even the Prophet’s household received guidance and warning, so no family is above the need for constant vigilance. The angels’ severity highlights that on the Day of Judgment, there will be no intercession based on family ties, only one’s deeds and faith.

=7=

This verse transports the listener to the scene of the Day of Judgment, delivering the final verdict upon those who enter the Fire. The address, “O disbelievers!” is a harsh, isolating call that strips them of any pretense. The command “Make no excuses this Day” is a declaration that all the justifications they offered in the world are now worthless. In worldly life, people excuse their sins by blaming circumstances, bad company, or claiming ignorance. They argue that they were merely following their forefathers, or that they did not know any better. But on that Day, the mouths will be sealed, and the limbs will testify. No excuse will be heard because the truth has been laid bare. The reasoning is given in the second half of the verse: “You are only rewarded for what you used to do. ” This is the essence of divine justice.

The word “tujzawna” means you are being recompensed, paid back, exactly in accordance with your deeds. There is no unfairness, no exaggeration. The punishment is the direct fruit of their own actions, which they sent ahead with their own hands. This verse serves as a sobering anchor for the entire surah. It warns the believers that the Fire is not an abstract concept but a reality where excuses vanish. It also implicitly encourages the believers who might be struggling or facing injustice; they are reminded that on that Day, the oppressors will have no platform to argue, and the faithful will receive their due reward. The link to the preceding admonition is clear: protect your families, because when that Day arrives, no one will be able to excuse their failure to do so.

The verse teaches the profound principle of personal responsibility, a cornerstone of Islamic faith, which stands in sharp contrast to notions of inherited guilt or salvation by mere association.

=8=

After the images of punishment, the surah turns again with immense compassion to the believers, calling them to “sincere repentance.” The phrase “tawbatan nasūḥā” has been explained by the scholars, including ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, as a repentance that is genuine and complete: feeling remorse for the past sin, immediately abandoning it, and firmly resolving never to return to it. The outcome of such repentance is glorious: absolution of sins and admission into Gardens with flowing rivers. The forgiveness is total; the sins are erased as if they never existed. The entrance into Paradise is assured.

The verse then specifies the timing of this honour: “on the Day Allah will not disgrace the Prophet or the believers with him.” This is a beautiful promise that the Prophet and his community will not be shamed or humiliated on the plain of gathering, unlike the disbelievers whose secrets will be exposed. They will be honoured.

The description then shifts to the luminous scene of the believers crossing the bridge over Hell: “Their light will shine ahead of them and on their right.” This light is the manifestation of their faith and good deeds, illuminating the path in the terrifying darkness. As they walk, some whose light is faint will see the light of others, and in a state of humble yearning, they will pray, “Our Lord! Perfect our light for us, and forgive us. ” They do not become arrogant; even while being saved, they ask for completion and forgiveness, recognizing their shortcomings. The prayer ends with acknowledging Allah’s boundless power: “You are truly Most Capable of everything.” This verse connects directly back to the repentance invited from the Prophet’s wives. It shows that if they, and all believers, turn sincerely, their station will be among those honoured ones with the radiant light. It is a message of hope, promising that even after mistakes like those mentioned earlier in the surah, the door is open to the highest levels of divine favour.

=9=

This verse issues a directive to the Prophet, peace be upon him, regarding two distinct categories of opponents. The command to “struggle” (jāhid) encompasses all forms of striving: with the disbelievers, it could mean military struggle when necessary, as well as intellectual and daʿwah efforts. With the hypocrites, the struggle is primarily through the tongue, by exposing their plots, refuting their arguments, and implementing legal measures against their treachery, as a military fight was not waged against them. The instruction “and be firm with them” (wa-ghluẓ ʿalayhim) calls for a stance of toughness and sternness that leaves no room for the enemy to exploit weakness. This firmness is not personal cruelty but a principled posture to protect the community from their harm.

The verse then reveals their final abode: “Hell will be their home. What an evil destination!

” This is a declaration of their ultimate loss, regardless of any temporary success they might have in this world. The context of this verse within the surah is deeply instructive. After addressing the delicate internal matters of the Prophet’s household with gentleness and mercy, the command turns outward with a contrasting severity. This teaches that while within the family and the community of believers, mercy, forgiveness, and gentle correction are paramount, the attitude toward active enemies who plot to destroy the faith must be one of strength. The Prophet is to balance the softness he shows his wives with the firmness he shows those who wage war against the truth. It is also a reminder that the hypocrites of Medina, like those who may have enjoyed the internal disputes, are not to be taken lightly. The verse reassures the believers that the Prophet’s ultimate support is from Allah, and he must continue his mission with unshakeable resolve, knowing that those who oppose him are heading toward the worst home. This balanced approach—mercy inwardly, strength outwardly—defines the prophetic character.

=10=

After the call to struggle, Allah presents a striking parable to drive home a profound spiritual truth. The example for the disbelievers is taken from the closest possible human relationship: marriage. The wife of Noah and the wife of Lot were bound in matrimony to two of the greatest prophets, men who were “righteous servants” of Allah, chosen for the monumental task of calling their people to monotheism.

Yet these women “betrayed them. ” The betrayal, as the majority of scholars clarify, was not sexual immorality—for a prophet’s wife is never unchaste—but a betrayal of faith and mission. They disbelieved in the message of their husbands. The wife of Noah would expose the believers among her people, and the wife of Lot would signal to the sinful townsfolk about his guests. They aligned themselves with the disbelievers, making their physical proximity to prophethood completely void.

The verse then delivers the crushing conclusion: “So their husbands were of no benefit to them against Allah whatsoever.” Being the spouse of a prophet did not shield them from divine wrath. Their faithlessness severed any advantage that association might have brought. The command “Enter the Fire, along with the others!” is the final word, casting them into the same punishment as the rest of the disbelievers, without any reduction. This example is a dire warning to the disbelievers of all times: do not think that your lineage, your connections to holy people, or your superficial membership in a believing community will save you from the consequences of your own disbelief. It also serves as a subtle, powerful message to the Prophet’s wives, who were themselves married to the greatest of messengers. It warned them that their status depended entirely on their own faith and deeds, not on their marital tie. The lesson is clear: iman is a personal asset; no one can carry another on the Day of Judgment.

=11=

In beautiful contrast to the disbelieving wives, this verse presents the radiant example of Āsiyah bint Muzāḥim, the wife of Pharaoh. She lived in the most oppressive environment imaginable, married to the tyrant who claimed divinity for himself.

Yet her soul refused to be corrupted by her surroundings. Her faith blossomed in the darkness of that palace. When she witnessed the miracle of Moses and heard the truth, she believed. Her supplication, recorded in this verse, is a window into her pure heart. She prayed, “My Lord! Build me a house in Paradise near You. ” She did not ask for a palace on earth or to be liberated and given worldly riches. Her aspiration was a home in the immediate vicinity of her Lord. This shows her complete detachment from the dunya and her yearning for the eternal nearness to Allah.

The phrase “near You” (‘indaka) signifies the highest and most intimate station of Paradise. Then she prayed, “deliver me from Pharaoh and his evil doing.” She sought rescue not just from his person but from the system of tyranny, disbelief, and immorality that he represented. Her final plea, “and save me from the wrongdoing people,” was for complete salvation from the entire network of oppression. The hadith mentions that the Prophet said the best women of Paradise are Khadījah, Fāṭimah, Maryam, and Āsiyah. Her story is one of ultimate triumph: she was tortured by Pharaoh for her faith, but before her death, Allah showed her her palace in Paradise, and she laughed. This example is set for all believers, and especially for the wives of the Prophet: even if you are surrounded by a hostile environment or a difficult situation, your iman can raise you to the loftiest heights. Āsiyah’s marriage to a disbeliever did not harm her; her personal faith saved her. It teaches that no external circumstance can prevent a soul from reaching Allah if the heart is sincere. Her prayer also models what a believer should ask for: the highest, eternal abode, and deliverance from all forms of evil.

=12=

The surah concludes with the second luminous example for the believers: Maryam, the daughter of ʿImrān, the mother of Jesus. Her mention here completes a quartet of women in this surah—two evil and two good—creating a perfect symmetry of warning and inspiration. Maryam is praised for guarding her chastity, a quality of immense importance in the context of this surah, which deals with marital secrets and trust. Her absolute purity and modesty made her fit to receive the miraculous gift of a child without a father.

The phrase “so We breathed into her of Our spirit” refers to the angel Gabriel being sent to breathe into the opening of her garment, by which the soul of Jesus was created within her womb. This breath is attributed to Allah as an honour, showing that the spirit was a created, noble spirit from His command, not a part of divinity. Maryam’s greatness is further highlighted by her unwavering faith: “She testified to the words of her Lord and His Scriptures.” She believed in the previous scriptures and affirmed the truth that came to her, including the miraculous word of Allah that created Jesus through the command “Be. ” She accepted her role without complaint, despite the immense social trial it brought her.

The final attribute, “and was one of the sincerely devout” (al-qānitīn), indicates her constant, humble obedience and prolonged worship. The Prophet said, “Many men have attained perfection, but among women, none attained perfection except Maryam bint ʿImrān and Āsiyah...” This example, alongside that of Āsiyah, serves as a powerful encouragement for the Prophet’s wives and for all Muslim women. It shows that true honour lies in devotion, chastity, and faith, not in lineage or marital status alone. While the wives of Noah and Lot perished despite their prophetic husbands, Maryam and Āsiyah rose to the highest ranks through their own piety. The surah thus closes by inviting every believer to emulate these paragons of virtue, securing a place in the eternal nearness of Allah.
