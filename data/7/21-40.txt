=21=

After Satan planted the initial doubt by suggesting that the forbidden tree would grant angelic status or immortality, he reinforced his deception with a solemn oath. The verse says “he swore to them,” meaning he invoked Allah’s name or made a binding promise to convince Adam and Eve of his truthfulness. The content of his oath was “I am truly your sincere advisor.

” This is a terrifying portrayal of how evil can present itself in the most harmless and caring guise. The one who had just been expelled from divine mercy for his arrogance now posed as the well-wisher of humanity.

The Arabic word for “sincere advisor” (nāsih) comes from the root meaning pure or free of defects. Satan presented his counsel as untainted by selfish motives.

This is precisely the method he uses with every human being: he beautifies the sin, makes it appear reasonable, and assures the person that he only wants their good. The fact that he swore an oath is especially instructive. Even a false oath in the name of Allah did not deter him; he had no reverence left. This shows that a person who has lost true faith may still use religious language to deceive. Adam and Eve, being pure and unsuspecting, were moved by the oath. It highlights a profound weakness in human nature: we are inclined to trust strong assurances, especially when they align with our inner desires. The lesson for the children of Adam is clear: do not be deceived by fine words, impressive oaths, or the appearance of sincere advice when it contradicts the clear command of Allah. The Quran repeatedly warns that Satan and his allies whisper adorned speech to delude people. The believer must test every call against the revelation. Adam’s test was the tree; our test is the entire spectrum of lawful and unlawful. When someone encourages us to transgress a divine limit, no matter how sincere they appear, we must recognise the echo of that primeval oath.

This verse also demonstrates that Satan’s enmity is not a crude, obvious assault; it is a calculated, sophisticated campaign that often wears a mask of friendship. The antidote is knowledge and a constant connection to the guidance that exposes his plots.

=22=

The result of Satan’s scheming is described with the phrase “brought about their fall through deception.” The word deception (ghurūr) implies a gradual and skilful misleading, a false promise that led them step by step into disobedience. They did not plunge suddenly; they were lured. This indicates the power of satanic delusion to make the forbidden appear harmless and even beneficial. The moment they tasted the tree, the immediate consequence manifested: their nakedness was exposed. The garments that had covered them fell away, and they became aware of their private parts in a way they had not been before. This sudden exposure symbolises the loss of innocence, the stripping away of the protective covering of obedience. It also fulfilled Satan’s earlier stated goal, as he had intended to expose their shame. Their instinctive reaction was to cover themselves with leaves from the trees of Paradise, hastily sewing together what they could find.

This is a poignant image: the first human response to sin was shame and an attempt to cover up its effects. Then came the divine call: “Did I not forbid you from that tree and did I not tell you that Satan is your sworn enemy?” The call is both a gentle rebuke and a reminder. Allah does not ask for information; He already knows. He is making them reflect on the fact that they had been given a clear prohibition and a clear warning about Satan’s enmity. By mentioning both the forbiddance and the warning, Allah indicates that they were fully equipped to avoid the slip. The test was fair, the enemy was identified, yet they still fell. This divine call also marks the beginning of mercy: instead of immediate punishment, Allah gives them the opportunity to recognise their error and return.

The sequence teaches that sin brings shame and exposure, but Allah’s call to account is the first step towards repentance. The children of Adam would forever experience this pattern: disobedience leads to a sense of loss and vulnerability, and then the voice of conscience, which is a reflection of divine guidance, invites them to remember the original command and the ancient enemy.

=23=

This is the model of true repentance. Unlike Iblis, who responded to his sin with arrogance and argument, Adam and Eve immediately turned to Allah with humility and admission of guilt. Their words are a perfect formula for seeking forgiveness. They begin with “Our Lord,” acknowledging His lordship, care, and authority. Then they state the plain truth: “We have wronged ourselves.” There is no attempt to shift blame to Satan, to the tree, or to Allah’s decree. They take full personal responsibility.

This is the essence of tawbah: the frank recognition that sin is ultimately a self-inflicted harm. They then appeal to Allah’s attributes: “If You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we will certainly be losers.” They place their entire hope in divine forgiveness and mercy. They recognise that without these, their situation is utterly hopeless.

The word “losers” links their plea to the ultimate success and failure described earlier in the surah. By admitting they would be among the losers without forgiveness, they are aligning themselves with the reality of the Hereafter. This prayer is so profound that it became the very words Allah taught Adam, as mentioned in another surah: “Then Adam received from his Lord words, and He turned to him in mercy.” These words are a timeless gift for all humanity. Whenever a believer sins, they should immediately adopt this posture: admit the wrong without excuses, turn to the Lord, and beg for forgiveness and mercy with a heart certain that only Allah can save them from loss. The contrast with Satan’s reaction is stark and intentional. Both sinned, but one repented and was forgiven, while the other persisted and was cursed. The path of Adam is the path of salvation.

The verse also teaches that even prophets can make errors, but they are quick to repent, and this elevates their status rather than diminishing it. For us, it is a reminder that sin does not have to define our end; what matters is the sincerity and swiftness of our return.

=24=

After accepting their repentance, Allah decreed that Adam, Eve, and Iblis would descend from the garden to the earth. The command “Descend” marks the beginning of earthly life as we know it.

The phrase “as enemies to each other” refers primarily to the enmity between mankind and Satan, but some scholars also interpret it as referring to the mutual hostility that would sometimes arise among humans themselves due to satanic whispers. Life on earth would be a perpetual struggle between the forces of obedience and the forces of misguidance. The earth is described as a place of “residence” (mustaqarr) and “provision” (matā’). Residence means a dwelling place with stability, a home where humans would live out their appointed terms. Provision refers to the means of sustenance and enjoyment available for the duration of that stay.

The phrase “for your appointed stay” (ilā hīn) underscores that this earthly life is temporary. There is a specific timeframe for each individual and for humanity as a whole, after which the earth will be replaced and the Hereafter will commence. This verse holds profound lessons about the nature of this world. It is not our final home; it is a place of sojourn, a testing ground where we live as travellers. The provision is genuine but fleeting. The enmity with Satan is to be taken seriously, but it is also a manageable trial because Allah provides guidance and strength. The descent also explains why human beings experience the hardships of earthly life: it is part of the consequence of that original event, yet simultaneously a mercy, because it allows for the exercise of faith, patience, and the earning of eternal reward. Every hardship we face, every temptation we wrestle with, is connected to this ancient decision. The verse thus sets the stage for the entire human drama, reminding us that the world is not the destination but the journey, and that our time here is limited.

=25=

This verse further clarifies the nature of earthly existence by summarising the entire human lifecycle. Allah tells Adam and his descendants that the earth will be the theatre for three major events: living, dying, and being resurrected. “There you will live” acknowledges that the earth is the place of our active life, where we work, worship, build, and are tested. “There you will die” means that death will occur on this earth; our bodies will return to the soil from which they were originally created.

This statement confronts humanity with the undeniable reality of mortality. No human being can escape death on this earth.

The final phrase, “and from there you will be resurrected,” brings the focus to the Hereafter. The earth is not only the place of death but also the place from which we will be brought forth for judgment.

This promise of resurrection is central to the Quranic message. It ties the earthly life to the eternal life and ensures that every action performed on earth has consequences that extend beyond the grave. By linking life, death, and resurrection to one location, the verse emphasises the completeness of Allah’s plan. The earth is not a random habitat; it is a precisely designed arena for the human test.

The sequence also serves as a powerful psychological reminder. Knowing that we will inevitably die on this earth and then be raised from it should instil a constant awareness of accountability. The pleasures and sorrows of this life are framed within this larger arc. For the believers, this verse offers comfort: the difficulties of earth are temporary, and a just resurrection awaits. For the heedless, it is a warning: the death that everyone accepts intellectually will be followed by a resurrection they may be denying, but cannot escape. This short yet weighty verse encapsulates the entire philosophy of human existence from the Quranic perspective and functions as a bridge to the extensive moral guidance that follows in the surah, addressed directly to the children of Adam.

=26=

With the descent to earth established, Allah now addresses all humanity with a direct call: “O children of Adam!” This form of address is repeated several times in the surah, each time highlighting a specific blessing or warning. Here, the focus is on clothing. The verse acknowledges that clothing serves two primary purposes: to cover nakedness and to provide adornment. The covering function addresses the shame that Adam and Eve experienced when their nakedness was exposed, a direct consequence of Satan’s deception. Allah mercifully provided materials and the knowledge to make garments that protect human dignity. Adornment is also a legitimate purpose; looking presentable and beautiful is not condemned in itself. Islam does not demand that people neglect their appearance.

However, the verse then immediately establishes a spiritual hierarchy: “However, the best clothing is righteousness.” The Arabic term for righteousness here is taqwa, which encompasses God-consciousness, piety, and the fear of Allah that leads to obedience. Taqwa is described metaphorically as a garment because it covers the spiritual nakedness of sins and shortcomings just as physical clothing covers the body. Without this inner garment, a person may be beautifully dressed outwardly but spiritually exposed and vulnerable.

This is a profound reorientation: society often obsesses over outward fashion while neglecting the state of the heart. The verse ends by reminding that this guidance is part of Allah’s bounties, intended to make us mindful.

Thus, the provision of clothing, the permission to adorn oneself, and the emphasis on taqwa are all favours designed to lead humans to gratitude and awareness. The verse also subtly refutes the pagan Arab practice of performing tawaf around the Kaaba naked, claiming it was a religious act. True religion requires both physical modesty and inward piety. This holistic view of human dignity lays the groundwork for the subsequent warnings against Satan, who seeks to strip humans of both their physical and spiritual coverings.

=27=

This verse directly warns all humanity against Satan’s continuous threat. The address “O children of Adam” links back to the previous verse, and the warning is explicit: “Do not let Satan deceive you.” The deception is described by recalling the original trauma: Satan tempted your parents (Adam and Eve) out of Paradise and caused their clothing to be removed to expose their nakedness. The historical event is not merely a story; it is a living warning. What Satan did to the first humans he seeks to do to every descendant. His goal is to strip people of the covering of obedience and taqwa, leaving them spiritually and morally exposed.

The verse then reveals a frightening aspect of satanic operation: “Surely he and his soldiers watch you from where you cannot see them.” Satan and his forces, which include the rebellious jinn, exist in a realm invisible to human eyes. They observe us, study our weaknesses, and approach us through channels we are unaware of. This does not mean they know our thoughts, but they can sense our inclinations and exploit them. This unseen surveillance should make believers vigilant and reliant on Allah’s protection.

The verse concludes with the statement: “We have made the devils allies of those who disbelieve. ” This is a significant principle. The devils are not arbitrary in their attacks; they become the close companions and allies of those who choose disbelief and turn away from guidance. It is a two-way relationship: humans who reject faith open themselves to satanic influence, and the devils eagerly assume the role of guides and companions, ultimately leading them to ruin. This alliance is not forced; it is a natural consequence of a person’s inner orientation.

For believers, this verse is a call to conscious awareness. We must not be naive about the enemy. Just as we take precautions against unseen physical threats like viruses, we must take spiritual precautions. The best protection is constant remembrance of Allah, seeking refuge from Satan, and keeping the garment of taqwa intact.

The verse also explains why sin often feels so easy and deceptive: there is an invisible army beautifying it.

=28=

This verse exposes the irrational justifications of the polytheists and the hypocrites when confronted with their immoral practices. The shameful deed (fāhishah) refers to indecent acts, including the pagan practice of naked circumambulation of the Kaaba, as well as other moral perversions. When challenged, they offered two excuses: blind following of forefathers and the audacious claim that Allah commanded it.

The first excuse, “We found our forefathers doing it,” reflects an appeal to tradition devoid of reason. It is the refuge of those who have no proof for their actions.

The second excuse is even more outrageous: “Allah has commanded us to do it.” This is a direct lie against Allah, attributing immorality to divine decree. It is a classic example of how people can dress their desires in religious garb to silence their conscience. The verse instructs the Prophet to respond with a clear, forceful denial: “No! Allah never commands what is shameful.

” This is an absolute principle. Allah, who is most pure and perfect, cannot command indecency. Whatever He commands is inherently good and just. The idea that God would order shamelessness is a contradiction in terms and a slander against His holiness. The response continues: “How can you attribute to Allah what you do not know?

” This is a powerful logical rebuke. They had no scripture, no revelation, no rational basis for their claim. Speaking about Allah without knowledge is one of the gravest sins, mentioned repeatedly in the Quran as a satanic tactic.

This verse teaches that religious claims must be grounded in authentic revelation, not in inherited customs or personal desires. It also highlights the danger of using the name of Allah to justify cultural evils. In every age, people have invoked religion to sanction injustices; this verse is a timeless criterion: if an act is shameful and wrong, it cannot be from Allah. The believer’s duty is to distinguish between genuine divine commandments and the fabrications of human ignorance.

=29=

In contrast to the false claims that Allah commands shameful acts, the Prophet is instructed to proclaim what Allah truly commands. The verse begins with “My Lord has commanded uprightness.” Uprightness, or qist in some readings, refers to justice, balance, and straightforwardness. Allah commands a life of moral integrity and fairness, not indecency.

The second command is to “dedicate yourselves to Him alone in worship.” This means making one’s face, one’s direction and purpose, purely towards Allah without deviation. True worship must be free from idolatry and hypocrisy.

The verse then adds, “calling upon Him with sincere devotion. ” The Arabic term for sincere devotion (mukhlisīn) implies that the religion is made pure for Allah alone, with no partner associated in any act of worship. This triple emphasis on justice, exclusive worship, and sincerity encapsulates the core of the prophetic message. Then the verse shifts to a fundamental argument for this devotion: “Just as He first brought you into being, you will be brought to life again.” This links the beginning of creation to the resurrection. The One who originated you from nothing will certainly bring you back to life after death.

This is both a logical proof and a motivational reminder. Because you will return to Him, your life must be lived in uprightness and sincere worship. The rhetorical structure is beautiful: He created you, so worship Him alone; He will resurrect you, so live in anticipation of that meeting.

This verse also directly counters the Meccan polytheists’ rejection of the Hereafter. By connecting the command to worship with the certainty of resurrection, it shows that true morality is not merely a worldly system of rules but is anchored in eternal accountability. The believer’s worship and upright conduct are not just social virtues; they are an expression of gratitude for the first creation and preparation for the second.

=30=

This verse articulates the divine decree regarding guidance and misguidance. “He has guided some” means that Allah, in His infinite knowledge and mercy, has granted guidance to a group of people. Their guidance is not random; it is a response to their sincere seeking, and it is also a gift from Allah. “While others are destined to stray” indicates that some people have justly been left astray. This decree of misguidance is not arbitrary; it is the consequence of their own persistent rejection, arrogance, and preference for falsehood over truth, as established in other verses.

The second part reveals the mechanism of their straying: “They have taken devils as their masters instead of Allah.” Instead of turning to Allah as their guardian and guide, they have formed alliances with satanic forces. This could refer to literal devils from the jinn or to human agents of misguidance who act as satanic proxies. These allies lead them away from the divine path, yet they provide a false sense of security. The tragic irony is captured in the final phrase: “thinking they are rightly guided.

” This is one of the most dangerous spiritual conditions: to be deeply astray while being utterly convinced that one is on the correct path. This delusion is the ultimate success of Satan. He does not just make a person sin; he makes them believe that their sin is virtue, that their misguidance is enlightenment. The verse explains why so many people resist the call of the prophets despite its clarity. Their perception is inverted; they have taken darkness as light. This verse connects to the earlier discussion of the scales and the questioning on Judgment Day. Those who will find their scales light are precisely those who were deluded into thinking their path was correct while they followed devils.

For the believer, this is a sobering reminder to constantly check the sincerity of one’s guidance. We must ask ourselves: am I truly following Allah’s revelation, or am I crafting a comforting narrative that serves my desires? True guidance is found in the Quran and the Sunnah, not in the majority opinion or in the trends of the time, for misguidance often masquerades as enlightenment.

=31=

After the profound discussion on guidance and misguidance, the surah returns to practical instructions for the children of Adam. The verse begins with the command to “dress properly whenever you are at worship.

” The word “properly” translates zīnah, which means adornment or beautiful attire. This directly counters the pagan Arab custom of performing tawaf naked. Islam instructs believers to cover their bodies and to present themselves in a dignified and pleasant manner when standing before Allah in prayer or during pilgrimage. This not only fulfils the requirements of modesty but also expresses gratitude for the clothing that Allah has provided. The mosque and the place of prayer are not arenas for displaying wealth or fashion, but they are places where a believer should look neat and presentable out of respect for the act of worship.

The verse then expands to a more general principle of consumption: “Eat and drink, but do not waste. ” This is a comprehensive rule of moderation. Islam permits enjoyment of lawful food and drink; there is no concept of monastic deprivation.

However, the line is drawn at wastefulness (isrāf). Wastefulness includes consuming more than one needs, discarding food while others are hungry, and indulging in extravagance that harms the body or the soul. The prohibition also extends to wasting wealth on forbidden items or excessive luxuries. The reason given is clear: “Surely He does not like the wasteful.” Allah’s dislike of wastefulness means it is a sin that brings about His displeasure.

This verse teaches that Islam is a religion of balance. The believer is neither a hedonist who pursues every pleasure nor an ascetic who denies all worldly goods. The lawful is enjoyed with gratitude, and the limits are respected with humility. This instruction ties back to the story of Adam: in Paradise, he was told to eat freely but not to approach the tree. The lesson is that Allah’s permissions are vast, and His prohibitions are few, but they must be honoured. In a world facing environmental crises and gross inequality, this verse’s call to avoid wastefulness resonates powerfully, reminding us that responsible consumption is an act of worship.

=32=

This verse challenges the false piety that forbids what Allah has made lawful. The Prophet is instructed to ask a rhetorical question: “Who has forbidden the adornments and lawful provisions Allah has brought forth for His servants?” The adornments include beautiful clothing and accessories, while lawful provisions encompass the good foods and pure things that Allah created.

The question implies that no one has the authority to prohibit what the Creator has allowed. It exposes the ignorance of those who, either due to pagan customs or misguided asceticism, declared certain lawful things as forbidden.

The verse then provides the correct Islamic perspective: these enjoyments are for the believers in this worldly life. Believers are not to shun the world’s permissible pleasures; rather, they are to enjoy them with gratitude, recognising them as gifts from Allah.

However, the verse adds a crucial qualification: “but they will be exclusively theirs on the Day of Judgment. ” In the Hereafter, the full and unspoiled version of these pleasures will belong solely to the believers. The disbelievers may partake in this world, but they have no share in the eternal delights of Paradise. This transforms the believer’s relationship with worldly blessings. They become a foretaste of the greater joy to come, and their enjoyment is a sign of hope, not a cause for guilt.

The verse concludes by stating that this clarification is part of how Allah makes His signs clear for people of knowledge. Knowledge here means the understanding that comes from revelation, enabling a person to distinguish between what is truly forbidden and what is merely human innovation. This verse counters two extremes: the secular hedonism that indulges without moral restraint, and the religious excess that forbids the good things without divine warrant. The balanced path is to accept Allah’s permissions joyfully, avoid His prohibitions faithfully, and always keep the Hereafter in view.

This teaching prepares the listener for the next verse, which delineates the actual prohibitions.

=33=

Having challenged the man-made prohibitions, the Prophet is now instructed to list what Allah has truly forbidden. The verse begins with “My Lord has only forbidden,” indicating that the list is comprehensive and exclusive; no one can add to it.

The first category is “open and secret indecencies.” This covers all forms of immoral conduct, whether done publicly or hidden away.

The mention of both open and secret emphasizes that no indecency escapes Allah’s knowledge or His prohibition. The second category is “sinfulness,” a general term for all acts of disobedience and transgression against divine law. Then comes “unjust aggression,” which refers to oppressing others, violating their rights, and transgressing the boundaries set by Allah in dealings with fellow humans. The fourth and most severe prohibition is “associating others with Allah in worship. ” This is shirk, the act of setting up partners with the Creator.

The verse adds a powerful parenthetical remark: “a practice He has never authorized.” There is no scripture, no revelation, that has ever sanctioned shirk. It is forbidden not just in this law but in every divine message.

Thus, it has no basis whatsoever. The final prohibition is “attributing to Allah what you do not know.” This includes inventing lies about Allah, speaking about religion without knowledge, and making claims about what is lawful or unlawful based on speculation or cultural custom. This comprehensive list encapsulates the core moral and spiritual prohibitions. It teaches that what Allah forbids is harmful in essence, and what He permits is beneficial.

The verse also establishes the principle that forbiddance is Allah’s sole prerogative. No human authority, no cultural tradition, can override these clear boundaries. This list is a criterion for evaluating any moral claim: if it fits these categories, it is forbidden; if not, and if it is otherwise beneficial, it remains within the sphere of the permissible. The believer is thus equipped with a clear moral framework, unclouded by the arbitrary prohibitions of the ignorant or the whims of society.

=34=

This verse shifts from individual moral responsibility to the collective fate of nations. It states a universal law: every community (ummah) has a specified term, a life span decreed by Allah. This term encompasses the period during which they are given the opportunity to believe, reform, and thrive, after which judgment may come in the form of destruction or decline if they persist in rebellion.

The term is known only to Allah, and it is fixed. The verse then emphasises the absolute precision of this timing: “they can neither delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it.” When the appointed moment arrives, there is no power in the universe that can postpone it by even the smallest fraction of time, nor can they bring it forward before its time.

This is a direct response to those who mockingly asked when the threatened punishment would occur. It teaches that the timing of divine justice is not subject to human impatience or scepticism. It also contains a profound psychological balance: for the oppressors, it is a warning that their respite is finite and that the sudden seizure mentioned earlier can occur without warning when their term expires. For the believers, it is a comfort that the community of falsehood will not be allowed to persist indefinitely; there is an endpoint to their tyranny.

Moreover, on an individual level, this verse reminds each person that their own life has an appointed term. Death cannot be delayed or hastened. This awareness should lead to urgency in doing good deeds and repenting before the term ends.

The verse also connects to the broader theme of the surah: the stories of destroyed nations that will be recounted all illustrate this principle. Noah’s people, ‘Ad, Thamud, and others all reached their appointed terms and were destroyed when that term arrived, precisely as decreed. The Meccans are being warned that their term, too, is set, and they should not be deceived by the apparent delay. The verse is a call to take the opportunity of life seriously, knowing that the clock is ticking and cannot be adjusted.

=35=

This verse presents the universal path to salvation. The address “O children of Adam” is a recurring, inclusive call to all humanity. The scenario is described: “When messengers from among yourselves come to you reciting My revelations.” This encompasses the entire chain of prophethood from the earliest times to the final message. The messengers are from among human beings, not angels, making them relatable and their examples attainable. Their primary task is to recite the divine revelations, clear signs that leave no room for ignorance. The response required is twofold: “whoever shuns evil and mends their ways.” Shunning evil (taqwa) means adopting a shield of God-consciousness, avoiding what is forbidden. Mending one’s ways (islāh) means actively correcting one’s conduct, repairing the damage caused by sin, and striving to do what is right. The combination of avoiding harm and actively doing good is the essence of Islamic ethics. The reward is described in profoundly reassuring terms: “there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.” Freedom from fear means complete security from what lies ahead, including the terrors of the Day of Judgment and the punishment of the Hereafter. Absence of grief means release from sorrow over what has been left behind or missed in this world. The double negation covers both future anxieties and past regrets.

This is the ultimate peace. The verse is a compact summary of the entire message: divine communication through human messengers, the demand for moral and spiritual transformation, and the promise of eternal serenity. It ties back to the story of Adam: when he and his wife sinned, they feared and grieved, but after repentance, they were given hope. This verse extends that hope to all their descendants. Whoever follows the path of the messengers will achieve the state that Adam sought. It is also a comfort to the Prophet Muhammad, reminding him that his role is to convey these glad tidings and warnings, and that those who heed them will attain the highest success.

=36=

In stark contrast to the preceding verse, this verse describes the fate of those who reject the divine message. The phrase “receive Our revelations with denial and arrogance” highlights two fatal attitudes: takdhib (denial, treating the signs as lies) and istikbar (arrogance, considering oneself above the truth). These are not intellectual objections based on sincere inquiry; they are wilful, prideful rejections. Such people hear the clear signs recited by the messengers, but instead of opening their hearts, they respond with contempt and a sense of superiority. The consequence is unambiguous: “will be the residents of the Fire.

” The word “residents” implies a permanent abode. They will dwell in Hell as their home, not as temporary visitors. The finality is reinforced with “They will be there forever. ” This is one of the clearest verses on the eternity of punishment for disbelievers. The permanence of Hell for those who die in a state of disbelief and arrogance is a core doctrine. This verse balances the hope given in the previous verse with a sobering warning. Salvation is freely offered, but the door of mercy closes upon those who slam it shut with their own arrogance. It also explains why the sin of Iblis was unforgivable; his was the original act of arrogant denial in the face of a direct command. Those who follow his example will share his abode. The verse serves as a powerful motivator for humility. Every person must examine their heart: do I receive the words of Allah with submission, or does pride prevent me from accepting the truth? Arrogance can be subtle; it can manifest as refusing to admit a mistake, feeling too educated to follow a certain ruling, or looking down on others who are striving in faith. This verse, placed right after the beautiful promise for the God-conscious, forms a complete picture of the human choice: the path of Adam, which leads to eventual Paradise after struggle, or the path of Iblis, which leads to eternal ruin.

=37=

This verse intensifies the description of the wrongdoers and gives a graphic preview of their death. The rhetorical question “Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or deny His revelations?” establishes that such people occupy the highest rank of wrongdoing. Fabricating lies against Allah includes inventing false doctrines, claiming He has partners or children, or making up things in His name. Denying His revelations means rejecting the signs and verses that have been sent. No injustice surpasses this.

The verse then moves to their earthly experience: “They will receive what is destined for them.” This means they will enjoy their allotted share of worldly life, provisions, and perhaps prosperity, as a form of respite and gradual punishment. But this enjoyment is temporary. The scene then shifts to the moment of death. “Until Our messenger-angels arrive to take their souls.” The angels of death come to extract their souls, and they do so with a terrifying question: “Where are those false gods you used to invoke besides Allah?” At the moment of death, when the veils are lifted, these individuals see the truth. They look for their idols, saints, or spiritual intermediaries, but find them utterly absent. Their helpless cry, “They have failed us,” reveals the depth of their delusion. The false gods have abandoned them at the most critical hour.

The verse ends with a powerful statement: “and they will confess against themselves that they were indeed disbelievers. ” This confession is not an act of repentance but a testimony of self-condemnation. It echoes the earlier verse where the destroyed nations cried, “We have indeed been wrongdoers.” The scene is chilling: the disbelievers die in a state of utter humiliation, their false supports gone, and their own tongues bearing witness against them. This verse links the present life to the afterlife, showing that death is not an end but a transition where the truth becomes undeniable. It warns that the worldly possessions and false beliefs that people cling to will vanish, leaving only the stark reality of their own faithlessness.

=38=

This verse depicts the entry into Hell and the dynamics among its inhabitants. Allah commands the disbelievers to enter the Fire in the company of similar groups of jinn and humans who had passed before them. They are grouped by their common rebellion.

The verse then describes a scene of mutual hatred. Every time a new group enters, it curses the group that preceded it. This cursing reflects the blame game that occurs when misguidance ends in destruction.

However, the most intense dialogue happens once they are all gathered. The followers, those who were misled, turn to Allah and plead against their leaders: “Our Lord! They have misled us, so multiply their torment in the Fire.” They acknowledge their own error but seek to place a heavier burden on those who led them astray, such as religious leaders, chiefs, or Iblis himself. Allah’s response is one of perfect justice: “It has already been multiplied for all, but you do not know.” This means that the punishment is already doubled or multiplied for everyone according to their level of guilt. The leaders will receive additional torment for their misguidance of others, while the followers will receive theirs for their blind following and neglect of their own responsibility.

The phrase “but you do not know” indicates that the reality of the punishment is even more severe than they can perceive at that moment. This dialogue powerfully refutes the excuse of blindly following others. The Quran repeatedly emphasizes that each soul is accountable for its own choices. Even though leaders will be punished extra, followers will not escape. This scene serves as a stark warning to critically evaluate whom we follow in matters of religion. It also highlights the bitter irony that the bonds of misguidance that united them in this world turn into mutual curses in the Hereafter. The verse is a call to personal responsibility and an assurance that divine justice is nuanced and precise.

=39=

The dialogue in Hell continues as the leaders respond to the followers’ accusations. When the followers asked for the leaders’ punishment to be multiplied, the leaders retort bluntly. They say, “You were no better than us!

” This statement is a rejection of the followers’ attempt to portray themselves as mere victims. The leaders argue that the followers were not innocent dupes; they freely chose their path. They were equally rebellious, equally heedless, and equally willing to embrace falsehood. The leaders did not force them; they only called, and the followers eagerly responded.

Thus, the followers’ claim of being misled, while true, does not absolve them of their own wilful participation in disbelief. The leaders then command: “So taste the torment for what you used to commit.

” This is a harsh, vindictive statement. In the world, leaders and followers enjoyed a mutually reinforcing relationship of misguidance. In Hell, that relationship dissolves into bitter recrimination. The torment is the direct result of their own deeds, not an external imposition. This verse reinforces the Quranic principle that on Judgment Day, every excuse will be stripped away. The followers cannot hide behind their leaders, just as the leaders cannot bear the sins of the followers. The personal nature of moral responsibility is absolute. The exchange also highlights the psychology of sin: people often find comfort in numbers, thinking that if many people are doing something, it cannot be that wrong. But in the Hereafter, the majority argument collapses. This verse serves as a sobering reminder to reject the bandwagon fallacy in matters of faith. It also teaches that those who lead others to evil will find no gratitude from their followers; instead, they will receive curses. The entire scene is a powerful dramatic presentation of the consequences of rejecting the warnings delivered by the messengers. The surah is building a comprehensive picture of the afterlife, balancing the graphic descriptions of punishment with the hope-filled descriptions of Paradise that will follow.

=40=

This verse provides a vivid image of the absolute impossibility of salvation for those who die in arrogant disbelief. The subject is the same group described earlier: those who treat the divine signs with denial and arrogance.

The first consequence is that “the gates of heaven will not be opened for them.” Scholars interpret this in several complementary ways. It means that their souls, when extracted at death, will not be allowed to ascend to the heavens; instead, they will be cast back down. It also means that their deeds and prayers, being devoid of faith, will not be raised or accepted.

Furthermore, on the Day of Judgment, the gates of Paradise will be closed to them. The second image intensifies the impossibility: “nor will they enter Paradise until a camel passes through the eye of a needle. ” This proverbial expression was well-known among the Arabs. The camel was the largest animal in their environment, and the eye of a needle represents the tiniest opening. The statement means that their entry into Paradise is logically and practically impossible, just as it is impossible for a huge camel to pass through the tiny hole of a needle. Some modern commentators have speculated about alternative meanings, but the classical understanding is that this is a metaphor for absolute impossibility.

The verse ends with “This is how We reward the wicked.” The reward here is the punishment of exclusion and eternal deprivation. This closing line establishes the justice of the outcome. The wicked chose a path that leads to the denial of mercy, and they receive exactly what their actions deserve. For the believers, this verse serves as a strong deterrent against arrogance and denial. It also contrasts powerfully with the next verses, which will describe the successful believers who will enter Paradise, whose hearts will be purified, and who will inherit the eternal bliss. The impossibility of the camel through the needle’s eye highlights the vast chasm between the two destinations, motivating the listener to seek the path of humility and faith.
