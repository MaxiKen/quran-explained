=141=

Having reminded the Israelites of the honour Allah bestowed upon them, Moses now directs their memory to the immediate past—the fresh, unforgettable deliverance from Egypt. The verse begins with “And remember when We rescued you from the people of Pharaoh.” The act of rescue is attributed directly to Allah, emphasising that their freedom was not achieved by their own power or strategy but was a divine intervention. The words “rescued you” are deeply personal, addressing the Children of Israel as direct recipients of salvation.

The verse then recalls the nature of the oppression from which they were saved: “who afflicted you with dreadful torment.” The Arabic term for dreadful torment (sū’ al-‘adhāb) conveys extreme suffering and degradation. The specific form of the torment is then detailed: “killing your sons and keeping your women.” This was Pharaoh’s genocidal policy. The newborn male children of the Israelites were slaughtered to prevent the rise of a potential liberator, while the women were kept alive for enslavement and humiliation. The psychological torture of a parent seeing their child killed, combined with the systemic humiliation of their womenfolk, was a calculated policy designed to break the spirit of a people.

The verse concludes with a profound theological statement: “That was a severe test from your Lord.” The word “test” (balā’) can also mean a trial or an affliction. This reframes the entire period of slavery. It was not merely a political or social tragedy; it was a divinely permitted trial, a furnace in which the faith and patience of the Israelites were refined. The test was “severe” (‘azīm), indicating its immense difficulty. But tests from the Lord are not purposeless. They carry within them the seeds of future elevation, provided the tested respond with patience and faith. The verse is strategically placed right after the reminder of their honour, to show that the honour came after the test, and that ingratitude after deliverance is an invitation for a new test. The deliverance from such an ordeal should have been enough to bind their hearts forever to the One who rescued them, making their subsequent request for an idol all the more shocking.

This verse teaches believers to view past hardships as divine tests that, when survived with faith, become the foundation for future blessings and a reason for unshakeable gratitude.

=142=

The narrative shifts to the divine summons that would shape the spiritual destiny of the Israelites. “We appointed for Moses thirty nights” refers to a sacred appointment on Mount Sinai, where Moses was to receive the Torah.

The mention of “nights” rather than days emphasises solitude, stillness, and spiritual retreat. Allah initially set a term of thirty nights for Moses to spend in devotion and communion. “Then added another ten” indicates that the term was extended by divine will, making the complete period “forty nights.” This number appears repeatedly in scripture as a period of preparation and transformation. The extension was a test of patience for the Israelites, who were left waiting without their prophet, and an honour for Moses, granting him more time in the presence of his Lord. Before departing, Moses turned to his brother Aaron, who had been his partner in prophethood, and gave him a threefold instruction: “Take my place among my people, do what is right, and do not follow the way of the corruptors. ” The command to “take my place” (ukhlufnī) means to act as the deputy and caretaker of the community in Moses’ absence. Aaron was not a prophet in his own right at this point, though later revelation would grant him that station, but he was Moses’ trusted lieutenant.

The second command, “do what is right,” is a comprehensive directive to pursue reform, justice, and the general wellbeing of the people. It is an active command to be a source of goodness.

The third command, “do not follow the way of the corruptors,” is a prohibition against being swayed by those who cause mischief and moral decay. Moses was warning Aaron that in his absence, there would be corrupting elements among the people—those who would agitate for a return to idolatrous practices. Aaron was to resist their influence, maintain the worship of Allah alone, and keep the community on the straight path. This verse highlights the importance of righteous leadership and the responsibility of those left in charge to uphold the truth, even when the main leader is absent. It also foreshadows the tragic event that would occur during Moses’ absence: the worship of the golden calf. Aaron would do his best to stop it, but the people would overpower him. The stage is set for the momentous encounter on the mountain, which the next verse will describe in some of the most sublime passages of the Quran.

=143=

This verse describes one of the most profound and mysterious events in human history: the direct address of Allah to Moses and the request to see the Divine. “When Moses came at the appointed time and his Lord spoke to him” confirms that Moses was honoured with a direct conversation with his Creator. Emboldened by this intimacy, Moses made a request that expressed the deepest longing of the human soul: “My Lord! Reveal Yourself to me so I may see You. ” This was not a demand born of doubt but a yearning born of love. Moses desired the ultimate vision, the sight of the Divine, which is the greatest bliss of Paradise. Allah’s answer was gentle but firm: “You cannot see Me!

” This is a statement of fact about the limitations of human existence in this world. The physical eye cannot bear the unveiled vision of the Almighty in this life. But Allah did not leave Moses with a mere verbal denial. He provided a dramatic demonstration. “But look at the mountain. If it remains firm in its place, only then will you see Me.” The mountain, a symbol of strength and stability, would be the test. Then, “When his Lord appeared to the mountain, He levelled it to dust.” The exact nature of this divine manifestation, or tajalli, is known only to Allah. It was a manifestation of His glory befitting His majesty, and its effect on the mountain was total annihilation—it crumbled into dust. This demonstrated that if a solid mountain could not endure even a partial, indirect manifestation of Allah’s glory, how could a mortal human being bear the direct vision? Witnessing this cataclysm, “Moses collapsed unconscious,” overwhelmed by the sheer force of the event. When he recovered, his first words were a declaration of glorification and repentance: “Glory be to You! I turn to You in repentance and I am the first of the believers.” He declared Allah far above any deficiency, acknowledged the audacity of his request without permission, and renewed his faith. This sublime encounter taught Moses—and through him all believers—the transcendent nature of Allah, the impossibility of seeing Him in this world, and the proper response of humility, repentance, and praise when faced with the divine majesty.

=144=

After the awe-inspiring display of divine power on the mountain, Allah directly addresses Moses to console him and to remind him of the unique honour he has already received. “O Moses! I have already elevated you above all others by My messages and speech.

” The word “elevated” (istafaytuka) means chosen and selected with preference. Moses was granted a distinction unmatched by any other human being of his time: direct conversation with Allah without an intermediary.

The phrase “My messages” refers to the revelation of the Torah, the clear signs, and the wisdom entrusted to him. The phrase “My speech” refers specifically to the divine kalām, the honour of hearing Allah’s words directly.

This is why Moses is known in Islamic tradition as Kalīm Allāh—the one to whom Allah spoke. This elevation was not a reward for Moses’ request to see Allah but a clarification that what he already possessed was greater than what he had sought. The vision was withheld, but the speech was granted, and that speech was the highest honour a human can receive in this world. Allah then gave Moses a twofold command: “So hold firmly to what I have given you and be grateful.” The command to hold firmly (khudh) means to take the Tablets with strength, determination, and seriousness, implementing every teaching contained within them without hesitation or compromise. The command to be grateful (wakun min ash-shākirīn) is the natural response to such an unparalleled blessing. Gratitude is not just a feeling but an action: it means using the gift as the Giver intends. For Moses, gratitude meant faithfully conveying the Torah, ruling by it, and persevering through the difficulties that would come from his people.

This verse teaches that the greatest honour is not in seeing extraordinary visions but in receiving Allah’s guidance and speech, and that the proper response to divine favour is firm adherence and deep, active gratitude. It sets the stage for the giving of the Tablets, described in the next verse, and indirectly warns against the ingratitude the Israelites would soon display.

=145=

This verse describes the content and the significance of the Tablets given to Moses on Mount Sinai. “We wrote for him on the Tablets” indicates that the Torah was divinely inscribed on physical tablets. The substance of these writings was comprehensive: “the fundamentals of everything; commandments and explanations of all things.

” The word “fundamentals” translates maw‘izah, which encompasses admonition, moral instruction, and the essential principles of faith and conduct. The Tablets contained the core of what the Israelites needed for their spiritual and worldly life. They included legal rulings, ethical guidance, stories of previous nations, and the clear differentiation between truth and falsehood.

The phrase “explanations of all things” (tafsīlan likulli shay’) means that the Tablets provided detailed clarification for every matter they needed at that stage. It was a complete code of life for that community. Then Allah gave a command: “Hold to this firmly,” which is a call to grasp the teachings with strength, determination, and unwavering resolve. The revelation is not a set of suggestions but binding commands that require serious commitment. Next, “and ask your people to take the best of it.” This has been interpreted in several complementary ways. It could mean to act upon the best of what it contains—the highest virtues, the most rewarding deeds. Or it could mean to take the obligations and the good instructions, leaving aside the concessions only where permitted. Or it could mean to act upon its clear and excellent teachings rather than merely reciting it. In all interpretations, it encourages striving for excellence and not settling for the minimum.

The verse ends with a powerful warning: “I will soon show all of you the home of the rebellious.” The “home of the rebellious” refers to the ultimate destination of those who defy Allah’s commands—whether the destroyed remains of past nations in this world or Hellfire in the Hereafter. By mentioning this, Allah connects obedience to the Tablets with the avoidance of the fate of the rebellious. It serves as both a warning and an incentive: hold firmly to the guidance, and you will be spared the end of those who rebelled.

This verse establishes the Torah as a profound and sufficient guide, and it holds a lesson for the Muslim ummah regarding the Quran, which is an even more complete and preserved revelation, demanding the same firm adherence and pursuit of its best meanings.

=146=

This verse articulates a profound divine law regarding the spiritual fate of the arrogant. Allah declares, “I will turn away from My signs those who act unjustly with arrogance in the land.” The “signs” here refer to the verses of revelation and the signs in creation that point to the truth. To be “turned away” means that such people will be prevented from understanding, benefiting from, and being guided by these signs. Their hearts will be diverted, and their intellects will be clouded.

This is not an arbitrary decree; it is a direct consequence of their own chosen character. The description “act unjustly with arrogance” combines two grave sins: injustice (baghy) and arrogance (istikbār). Injustice here means oppressing others, transgressing bounds, and refusing to give due rights. Arrogance is the refusal to accept the truth and the disdain for those who do. When these two qualities take root in the heart, the person becomes spiritually disqualified from guidance.

The verse then describes the symptoms of this condition in vivid detail. “And even if they were to see every sign, they still would not believe in them. ” No amount of evidence, no miracle, no rational argument will penetrate their hearts because the obstacle is not intellectual but moral. Their arrogance has built a wall.

The next symptom concerns their moral compass: “If they see the Right Path, they will not take it. But if they see a crooked path, they will follow it. ” The straight path (rushd) is clearly identifiable, yet they deliberately avoid it. The crooked path (ghayy) is deviant and twisted, yet they eagerly pursue it. Their internal navigation system is inverted. What should attract repels them, and what should repel attracts them.

The verse ends by providing the root cause: “This is because they denied Our signs and were heedless of them.” Their initial denial and habitual heedlessness led to the sealing of their perception. This verse is a chilling warning against the spiritual danger of arrogance. It teaches that the punishment for persistent arrogance is the loss of the very ability to see the truth. The believer should constantly check their heart for any trace of unjust pride, especially when dealing with the truths of revelation and the advice of sincere guides.

The verse also explains why some people, despite being highly intelligent, never accept faith—the barrier is not in the mind but in the soul’s disposition.

=147=

This verse draws the final conclusion about the fate of those described in the previous verse. “The deeds of those who deny Our signs and the meeting with Allah in the Hereafter will be in vain.” The subject is twofold: they deny the revelations in this world, and they deny the ultimate accountability of the Hereafter. This combination represents complete disbelief. The result for such people is that their deeds are rendered in vain (habitat).

The word habat implies something that perishes, is nullified, or goes to waste. Even the good deeds they performed in this life—acts of charity, kindness, justice—are erased in the scale of the Hereafter because they were not built on the foundation of faith. A deed without faith is like a tree without roots; it may look green for a while, but it ultimately withers and holds no weight on the Day of Judgment.

This is a fundamental principle of Islamic theology: deeds are accepted only when accompanied by belief in Allah and the Last Day. The verse then asks a rhetorical question that serves as a confirmation of this justice: “Will they be rewarded except for what they have done?

” The answer is a resounding no. The loss they suffer is the exact recompense for their own actions. They denied the signs, so their deeds are denied. They belittled the Hereafter, so their deeds hold no value there.

This is not divine injustice; it is the natural harvest of what they sowed. The question also implicitly rejects the idea that Allah would punish someone for no reason. The punishment is a reward (jaza’) of their own doing, a precise and fair outcome.

For the believer, this verse is a powerful motivator to constantly renew and protect their faith. It warns against the illusion that one can live a life of disbelief and still expect one’s worldly good actions to save them. The indispensable requirement is iman.

The verse also provides a lens through which to understand the concept of “vain deeds” in the story of the golden calf and other sins: actions that deviate from faith are fundamentally hollow, no matter how culturally impressive or personally costly they may seem. The only deeds that survive into eternity are those rooted in the truth of the testimony of faith.

=148=

The surah now returns to the narrative of what transpired while Moses was on the mountain, fulfilling the ominous foreshadowing of his earlier instructions to Aaron. “In the absence of Moses, his people made from their golden jewellery an idol of a calf that made a lowing sound.” The absence of the prophet was the test. A man named Samiri, as detailed in Surah Taha, took advantage of the situation. He collected the gold jewellery that the Israelites had borrowed from the Egyptians and fashioned it into the shape of a calf. Through some means—whether by placing dust from the track of the angel Gabriel’s horse, as some traditions say, or through a clever mechanical construction—the statue emitted a sound resembling the lowing of a real cow. This sound was enough to captivate the simple-minded among them and to provide a veneer of the supernatural. They fell into worship, declaring, “This is your god and the god of Moses. ” The verse then presents a devastating rational critique: “Did they not see that it could neither speak to them nor guide them to the Right Path?” The false god is utterly impotent. It cannot speak a word of guidance, cannot answer a prayer, cannot point to the right way. The two fundamental functions of a deity—communication and guidance—are completely absent.

The question exposes the absurdity of their action. If they had only used their intellect, they would have immediately recognised the worthlessness of the idol. “Still they took it as a god and were wrongdoers.

” The word “still” emphasises the wilfulness of their choice. They had the evidence of their senses and their reason, yet they suppressed it and plunged into idolatry. They became wrongdoers (zālimīn), a term that in this context means those who commit the gravest injustice: shirk, associating partners with Allah. This verse is a powerful reminder of the human tendency to regress into tangible, visible objects of worship, especially when the prophetic authority is absent. It highlights the danger of valuing sensory experience over rational and revealed truth. The sound of the calf swayed them more than the memory of the parted sea. Faith must be anchored in revelation and reason, not in emotional or sensory novelty, otherwise it will falter at the first test.

=149=

This verse captures the aftermath of the golden calf episode from the perspective of those Israelites who came to their senses. It was not that every single individual worshipped the calf; a remnant, including Aaron, remained firm. But a significant portion fell into the sin. After the initial frenzy subsided, a wave of regret swept over them. “When they were filled with remorse and realized they had gone astray” describes the bitter awakening.

The Arabic word for remorse (suddiqa) carries the sense of being struck by regret, of falling down in sorrow. It was not a mild regret but a deep, painful realisation of the enormity of what they had done. They understood that they had betrayed the covenant, abandoned the teachings of Moses, and committed the worst sin. Their realisation was an act of divine mercy. Allah allowed the veil to lift from their eyes so they could see their error and have the opportunity to repent. Their response was a desperate prayer: “If our Lord does not have mercy on us and forgive us, we will certainly be losers.” This cry is remarkably similar to the prayer of Adam and Eve after their transgression. They acknowledged that their only hope lay in Allah’s mercy and forgiveness. They did not try to justify their action, blame the Samiri, or make excuses. They understood that without divine pardon, they were among the ultimate losers—those whose scale will be light and who will face eternal ruin. This prayer, though simple, contains the essence of tawbah: admission of the sin, recognition of one’s utter dependence on Allah’s mercy, and the pairing of hope for mercy with fear of loss. The fact that they used the term “our Lord” even in their state of sin shows that they had not entirely severed their connection; they still believed in Him, which is what made their repentance possible. This verse provides a timeless lesson in how to respond when one falls into sin, especially a major sin. The correct response is not to despair and run further away from Allah, nor to minimise the sin, but to feel deep remorse, to acknowledge the wrongdoing, and to turn to Allah with the hope that His mercy, which is vaster than all sins, will envelop the penitent. It sets the stage for Moses’ return and his role as intercessor and leader in the process of full reconciliation.

=150=

The scene of Moses’ return is one of the most emotionally charged moments in the Quran. He descended from the mountain carrying the sacred Tablets, having been informed by Allah of the terrible sin his people had committed. The verse describes him as “totally furious and sorrowful.” The combination of anger and grief is the mark of a true leader who cares deeply about his community’s faith. His fury was for the violation of Allah’s rights; his sorrow was for the spiritual ruin his people had brought upon themselves. He immediately confronted them with a sharp rebuke: “What an evil thing you committed in my absence!” He called the act what it was, without softening its gravity. Then he asked a piercing question: “Did you want to hasten your Lord’s torment? ” This implies that their action was so severe that it was as if they were impatient for the punishment that befalls those who commit shirk. In his overwhelming emotion, Moses threw down the Tablets—the very Tablets that contained the divine revelation written by Allah’s own hand, a priceless treasure. This act was not a disrespect to the Tablets but a spontaneous expression of shock and a prioritisation of addressing the immediate crisis. His attention then turned to his brother Aaron, whom he had left in charge. He “grabbed his brother by the hair, dragging him closer.” This was an expression of his intense disappointment, assuming perhaps that Aaron had been negligent. But Aaron’s response was immediate and deeply moving. He pleaded, “O son of my mother! ” He appealed to their fraternal bond, using the maternal connection to soften Moses’ heart. He explained, “The people overpowered me and were about to kill me.” Aaron had tried to stop them; he had warned them, but they threatened his life. He had to choose between a violent internal conflict that would have splintered the community and waiting for Moses’ return. He then pleaded, “So do not humiliate me and make my enemies rejoice, nor count me among the wrongdoing people.” He asked Moses not to treat him in a way that would give the sinful a reason to gloat over his disgrace, and he affirmed his innocence from the sin of calf-worship. This verse highlights the emotional authenticity of the prophets, the immense weight of leadership, and the importance of hearing a person’s explanation before passing final judgment. Moses’ anger was righteous, but upon hearing Aaron, he understood and relented. It teaches that even in moments of great anger, justice and mercy must prevail.

=151=

After hearing Aaron’s explanation and dealing with the immediate crisis of the golden calf, Moses turned to Allah in supplication. His prayer is a model of humility, brotherhood, and seeking divine mercy. He began, “My Lord! Forgive me and my brother! ” The request for forgiveness for himself may raise a question: what sin had Moses committed? Moses was not involved in the calf-worship. Some scholars explain that he sought forgiveness for his hasty reaction toward Aaron—grabbing him by the hair and beard before hearing the full story. Others say he sought forgiveness for any shortcoming in his leadership, or it was an expression of his profound humility and sense of shared responsibility, a feeling that he should have been present to prevent it. The fact that he included his brother Aaron shows his solidarity and his care for his fellow prophet. He immediately followed with a request of the highest aspiration: “And admit us into Your mercy.” Forgiveness is the removal of punishment, but admittance into mercy is entry into the state of perpetual divine favour, closeness, and bliss. It is a prayer that looks beyond mere pardon to the ultimate goal of the believer: to be enveloped in the mercy of Allah in this life and the next. The prayer concludes with the invocation of Allah’s name, “You are the Most Merciful of the merciful.

” This is a recognition that Allah’s mercy surpasses all other mercy. Human mercy, parental mercy, all conceivable forms of compassion are but a drop compared to the ocean of Arham ar-Rāhimīn. By invoking this name, Moses acknowledges that only such boundless mercy can cover the enormity of what has occurred. This prayer became a precedent for the believers. It teaches that after confronting a major communal sin and dealing with the practical consequences, the leader must turn to Allah in personal and collective supplication. It also shows that the prophets, despite their high station, constantly sought forgiveness and mercy, never relying on their own merits. The believer learns from Moses to always pair action with supplication, to feel responsibility for the community, and to seek not just forgiveness but the full embrace of divine mercy. The prayer also subtly reaffirms the theme of mercy that runs throughout the surah, contrasting the mercy that Allah bestows on the repentant with the wrath that befalls those who invent falsehood, as will be described next.

=152=

After Moses’ prayer and the destruction of the calf, this verse declares the divine judgment on those who actively participated in its worship and persisted in their error. “Those who worshipped the calf” specifically refers to the section of the Israelites who committed the act of shirk. The verse pronounces a double punishment for them. First, they will be afflicted with “Allah’s wrath” (ghadab).

This is the divine anger that falls upon those who knowingly violate the greatest right of Allah—His exclusive right to worship. The wrath is a spiritual and eschatological punishment, manifesting in this world through the turmoil of the heart and in the Hereafter through the fire. Second, they will suffer “disgrace in the life of this world” (dhilla). This disgrace can take many forms. For the calf-worshippers, it included the humiliation of their sin being exposed, the shame before their own community, the severe punishment that Moses administered—which included the execution of some of the guilty, as mentioned in other verses—and the permanent stain on their historical record.

The word “disgrace” is the opposite of the “honour” that comes from faith and obedience. They had been honoured with deliverance and revelation, but they traded that honour for disgrace by their own choice.

The verse concludes with a universal principle: “This is how We reward those who invent falsehood. ” The phrase “invent falsehood” (muftarīn) refers to those who fabricate lies, particularly in matters of religion. Inventing a false god, attributing divinity to a man-made object, and leading others into this falsehood is one of the gravest fabrications. The reward for such invention is wrath and disgrace.

This principle extends to all who invent and propagate false beliefs, false ideologies, and false religious practices. It is a warning that the consequences of religious innovation that opposes divine revelation are severe in both this life and the next.

The verse also serves to distinguish the fate of the unrepentant perpetrators from those who repented, as the next verse will clarify, showing the balance between justice and mercy. The believers are reminded that truth brings honour, and falsehood, no matter how popular or culturally embedded, brings nothing but eventual disgrace.

=153=

After pronouncing the severe punishment for the calf-worshippers who did not repent, the verse immediately opens the door of hope to emphasise the vastness of Allah’s mercy. “But those who commit evil” includes any evil, even the greatest of sins like shirk, the worship of the golden calf.

The word “commit” (amilū) indicates action, a deed that has been physically carried out. But the verse does not stop at the diagnosis of the illness; it provides the cure. The cure is twofold: “then repent” and “become true believers.” Repentance (tawbah) is the turning away from the sin with remorse, abandoning it immediately, and resolving never to return to it. But repentance alone must be accompanied by the renewal and strengthening of faith.

The phrase “become true believers” indicates that the repentance should lead to a deeper, more sincere state of iman than before. The sin should become a catalyst for spiritual growth, not a cause of despair.

The verse then gives the glad tidings: “your Lord will certainly be All-Forgiving, Most-Merciful.” The emphasis with “certainly” and the use of the intensive forms of forgiveness and mercy—Ghafūr and Rahīm—conveys the boundless capacity of Allah to pardon. The address “your Lord” makes the promise personal and intimate. No sin, not even the worship of a false deity, is beyond the reach of divine forgiveness if the servant turns back sincerely. This verse is a beacon of hope in the Quran. It ensures that the believer never loses hope, no matter how far they have strayed. It also refutes the notion that some sins are unforgivable. The only unforgivable sin is dying in a state of shirk without repentance. As long as a person is alive and turns back, the door of mercy is open. This verse is directly connected to the story of the calf-worshippers: those among them who truly repented and renewed their faith were forgiven.

The verse also serves as a cornerstone of Islamic theology regarding the relationship between sin, repentance, and mercy, balancing the warning of the previous verse with the assurance that Allah’s mercy is always greater than His wrath for those who seek it. It calls every sinner, no matter how grave their past, to wash away the darkness with the light of sincere repentance and to return to the Lord who is eagerly waiting to forgive.

=154=

This verse marks the resolution of the immediate crisis and the return to the primary gift of revelation. “When Moses’ anger subsided” acknowledges that the intense fury Moses felt was a natural, human response to a monumental betrayal, but it was not a permanent state. His anger passed, and with its passing, his attention returned to the precious Tablets that he had cast aside in his agitation. The fact that the Tablets had survived the fall and were now taken up again is, in itself, a subtle sign of Allah’s care for His revelation. Moses picked them up, symbolising the restoration of order, the re-centring of the community on the divine law after the chaos of idolatry.

The verse then describes the content of the Tablets: “whose text contained guidance and mercy.” The Tablets were not merely a list of rules; they were guidance (hudan), a light that shows the way, and mercy (rahmah), a manifestation of divine compassion that heals and uplifts. The guidance instructs, and the mercy forgives and encourages. The combination is the perfect description of the divine law. Law without mercy becomes harshness, and mercy without guidance becomes aimlessness. The Torah, like the Quran, balanced both.

The verse then specifies who benefits from this guidance and mercy: “for those who stand in awe of their Lord. ” The phrase “stand in awe” (yurhibūn) derives from rahbah, a reverential fear mixed with love and respect. It is the fear of the majesty and the awareness of one’s own smallness before the Almighty. Only those who have this quality in their hearts will truly benefit from the scripture. The arrogant, who dismissed the signs, or the heedless, who worshipped the calf, could not access the guidance and mercy, even though the text was in their hands.

This principle applies universally to the Quran as well. The Book is a guidance and mercy, but its doors open only to those who approach it with awe, humility, and a sincere desire to be guided.

This verse also teaches that moments of community crisis and moral failure must be followed by a return to the scripture. The solution is not to abandon the divine law in despair but to reclaim it, study it, and let its guidance and mercy heal the wounds caused by sin. Moses’ taking up the Tablets was the definitive act of redirecting the people from the false god back to the true Word of God.

=155=

The narrative now relates an event that followed the golden calf incident and was part of the process of communal repentance and renewal. “Moses chose seventy men from among his people for Our appointment.” These seventy were selected as representatives of the Israelites to accompany Moses to Mount Sinai for a solemn meeting, where they would hear the words of Allah and renew the covenant.

However, once they arrived, these men made a fateful demand: they said they would not believe until they saw Allah openly, as mentioned in Surah Al-Baqarah. In response to this audacity, “they were seized by an earthquake.” The ground shook violently, and they fell dead or were struck down in terror. This was a direct demonstration of the impossibility of seeing Allah and the consequence of making such a demand. Moses, witnessing his chosen delegation struck down, turned to his Lord in a deeply emotional supplication. He began, “My Lord! Had You willed, You could have destroyed them long ago, and me as well.” This acknowledges that everything is within Allah’s power and that the destruction of the people at any time would be a just decree. Then he pleaded, “Will You destroy us for what the foolish among us have done? ” He appeals to Allah’s mercy to not punish the righteous for the sins of the foolish few. He then reframes the entire event as a divine test: “This is only a test from You—by which You allow whoever you will to stray and guide whoever You will.” He recognises that this trial is a means by which the paths of the misguided and the guided diverge, according to Allah’s wisdom. Then comes the core of the prayer: “You are our Guardian. So forgive us and have mercy on us. You are the best forgiver.” Moses appeals to Allah’s protective lordship, asking for the specific gifts of forgiveness and mercy, and praises Allah as the supreme source of all pardon. This prayer, like many of Moses’ supplications, is a masterclass in how to plead with Allah: beginning with recognition of His power, acknowledging human frailty, interpreting events as a test, and then humbly asking for the best outcome. The prayer was answered, and the men were revived, a sign of immense mercy.

=156=

Moses continues his prayer by asking for comprehensive goodness. “Ordain for us what is good in this life and the next” is a beautiful, all-encompassing supplication.

The word “ordain” (uktub) literally means “write” or “decree.” Moses is asking Allah to destine for them a life of goodness in this world—health, provision, righteous deeds, and the enjoyment of Allah’s blessings—and the ultimate goodness of the Hereafter—Paradise and Allah’s pleasure. This prayer is the Quranic origin of the famous supplication, “Our Lord, grant us good in this world and good in the Hereafter.” He then adds, “Indeed, we have turned to You in repentance,” affirming their collective return to Allah after the trials of the calf and the earthquake. The response from Allah is one of the most profound theological statements in the Quran. “I will inflict My torment on whoever I will” establishes Allah’s absolute sovereignty and justice. Punishment is His prerogative, and it is meted out according to His wisdom, upon those who deserve it. But this is immediately followed by a much more expansive declaration: “But My mercy encompasses everything.” The mercy of Allah is so vast that it includes all things in its scope. It encompasses the believer and the disbeliever in this world through the provision of life, health, and sustenance. It encompasses the repentant sinner through forgiveness.

In the Hereafter, it encompasses the believers entirely, and its ultimate manifestation is the creation of Paradise. Some scholars, based on a hadith, explain that Allah’s mercy even extends to the punishment of Hell, for the punishment itself is a form of purification and justice. After this majestic statement, Allah specifies a particular ordainment of mercy: “I will ordain mercy for those who shun evil, pay alms-tax, and believe in Our revelations. ” Here, mercy is not a blanket amnesty but a specific reward tied to specific qualities.

The first is taqwa—shunning evil, being mindful of Allah. The second is paying zakah, which links spiritual purity with social responsibility.

The third is faith in the divine signs. These three—inward piety, outward charity, and foundational belief—are the pillars upon which the special ordainment of mercy is built. The verse beautifully balances the transcendence and immanence of Allah: He is the sovereign who punishes, but His mercy is the overarching reality, and those who align themselves with His guidance are the recipients of that overwhelming mercy.

=157=

In the midst of the narrative about Moses and the Israelites, the Quran seamlessly transitions to a prophecy about the final messenger, Prophet Muhammad. The verse continues the divine speech, specifying the identity of those who will receive the promised mercy. “They are the ones who follow the Messenger, the unlettered Prophet.” The title “unlettered” (ummī) signifies that the Prophet did not read or write, a fact that underscores the miraculous nature of the Quran and aligns him with the gentile nations, not just the learned scribes of Israel.

The verse then makes a monumental claim: “whose description they find in their Torah and the Gospel.” This is a direct challenge to the Jews and Christians. Their own scriptures, despite alterations, still contained references and descriptions of the coming prophet.

The verse then summarises the core characteristics of his mission. “He commands them to do good and forbids them from evil.

” This is the fundamental moral function of the Prophet. He is not just a preacher but a lawgiver who defines what is right and wrong. “Permits for them what is lawful and forbids to them what is impure. ” The lawful (tayyibāt) encompasses all that is good, wholesome, and beneficial. The impure (khabā’ith) includes all that is foul, harmful, and spiritually destructive. The Prophet’s law is a complete code of purity and permissibility. “And relieves them from their burdens and the shackles that bound them. ” This refers to the heavy legalistic burdens that previous nations had accumulated, either through divine decree as a punishment or through their own innovations. The message of the final Prophet came to lift these heavy yokes, offering a law that is balanced, merciful, and manageable.

The verse concludes with the condition of success: “Only those who believe in him, honour and support him, and follow the light sent down to him will be successful.” Belief must be accompanied by honouring the Prophet, providing practical support (nasr), and following the revelation—the Quran, which is described as “light. ” This verse is a bridge between the story of Moses and the universal call of Islam. It tells the Israelites, and all people, that the true followers of Moses are those who will recognise and follow the final Prophet described in their own books. It encapsulates the mission of Prophet Muhammad and establishes the criteria for eternal success, directly linking the mercy promised in the previous verse to the acceptance of the final messenger.

=158=

In a direct and powerful declaration, the Prophet Muhammad is commanded to announce the universality of his mission. “Say, O Prophet, ‘O humanity! I am Allah’s Messenger to you all.’” This is one of the clearest verses establishing that Islam is not a message for a specific tribe or nation but for every human being on earth.

The earlier prophets were sent to their specific peoples—Noah, Hûd, Sâlih, Shu’aib, even Moses was primarily sent to the Israelites. But Muhammad is sent to all of humanity, spanning all races, all times, and all geographies until the end of time. This universality is a unique feature of the final prophethood. The proclamation then grounds this messengership in the absolute sovereignty of Allah: “To Him alone belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth.” Because the entire universe is under His dominion, His message is necessarily universal. Then comes the core creed: “There is no god worthy of worship except Him. He gives life and causes death.” Tawheed, the exclusive worship of the One who holds life and death in His hands, is the foundation.

The verse then commands: “So believe in Allah and His Messenger, the unlettered Prophet, who believes in Allah and His revelations. ” The command to believe is directed at all humanity. The Messenger is described as “unlettered,” emphasising his human authenticity and the miracle of the Quran. He is also described as one who “believes in Allah and His revelations,” showing that the Prophet himself is the first and foremost believer, embodying the message he delivers.

Finally, the verse says, “And follow him, so you may be rightly guided. ” Belief must translate into practical following (ittibā‘). Guidance is the promised result. This verse is a complete summary of the Islamic call. It establishes the universality of the message, the absolute dominion of Allah, the core of monotheism, the twin requirements of faith in Allah and His Messenger, and the path of following that leads to guidance. It serves as a powerful conclusion to the long narrative of the Israelite prophets, redirecting the focus to the living, present message that demands a response from every person who hears it. The call is open, the invitation is clear, and the conditions for success have been laid bare.

=159=

After the universal call to follow the unlettered Prophet, the surah returns to the topic of the Israelites to offer a balanced assessment of their community. This verse serves as a corrective to any assumption that all Israelites were rebellious or disbelieving. “There are some among the people of Moses who guide with the truth and establish justice accordingly.

” The word “some” (ummatun) can mean a group or a community. It acknowledges that within the Israelite nation, there existed a righteous minority who remained faithful to the original teachings of Moses. These individuals did not merely believe privately; they actively “guide with the truth.” They took on the role of calling others to the straight path, using the truth of the Torah as their foundation. They were not passive believers but active guides, teachers, and reformers.

Furthermore, they “establish justice accordingly.” The word “justice” (qist) signifies fairness, equity, and balance. They not only spoke the truth but implemented it in their dealings and judgments. Their guidance was not abstract; it was practical and applied in social life.

This description perfectly fits the prophets who came after Moses among the Israelites, such as David, Solomon, and the many others, as well as the sincere scholars and righteous individuals who upheld the law despite the corruption around them. The verse is significant because it presents a nuanced picture. The Quran does not paint an entire nation with one brush. It recognises the wrongdoers, like those who worshipped the calf or killed the prophets, but it also honours the righteous. This balanced perspective is essential for justice. It also serves as a subtle encouragement to the People of the Book at the time of the Prophet: if you are truly among those who guide with truth and establish justice, then the logical next step is to recognise the truth of the final Prophet described in your own scriptures.

The verse also holds a timeless lesson for the Muslim ummah: the presence of corrupt individuals does not negate the duty of the righteous to stand up, guide, and establish justice. The true followers of the prophets are those who actively embody and propagate the truth, whatever the state of the broader community.

=160=

This verse recalls a series of divine favours bestowed upon the Israelites during their wilderness wanderings, highlighting Allah’s meticulous care for them. “We divided them into twelve tribes—each as a community.” The twelve tribes, descended from the twelve sons of Jacob, were given distinct identities and social structures, yet they formed one united people. This division was an organisational blessing, preventing chaos and fostering a sense of identity within a large population. Then the verse recounts a famous miracle: “And We revealed to Moses, when his people asked for water, ‘Strike the rock with your staff.’ Then twelve springs gushed out.” In the barren desert, thirst was a constant threat. The people complained, and Allah provided a spectacular solution. A single rock, struck by Moses’ staff, burst forth with twelve distinct springs. The number corresponds to the tribes, ensuring that each tribe had its own designated water source. “Each tribe knew its drinking place” prevented disputes and ensured equitable distribution. This miracle taught them that provision comes from Allah in the most impossible circumstances.

The next favours are listed: “We shaded them with clouds” refers to the miraculous cloud cover that protected them from the scorching desert sun during their travels. “And sent down to them manna and quails.” Manna, a sweet, gum-like substance, and quails, a type of bird, were provided as their daily food. They did not have to toil or farm; their sustenance came directly from heaven. They were told, “Eat from the good things We have provided for you.” The command was to enjoy the lawful, pure provisions with gratitude.

The verse ends with a profound statement: “They certainly did not wrong Us, but wronged themselves.” All the ingratitude, complaints, and rebellions they later showed did not harm Allah in the slightest. He is entirely self-sufficient. The harm of their sins rebounded entirely on themselves, leading to their spiritual degradation and worldly punishments. This refrain echoes throughout the Quran: sin is ultimately an act of self-harm. The favours of the twelve springs, the shade, the manna, and the quails are listed to remind them—and all of us—of the boundless mercy of Allah, and to warn that taking such favours for granted and responding with disobedience is an injustice not against the Provider, but against one’s own soul.
