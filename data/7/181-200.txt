=181=

After the profound discussion of the divine names and the warning against those who abuse them, the surah provides a balancing, hope-filled statement about the righteous among creation. This verse serves as a counterpart to the earlier description of the heedless who are destined for Hell. “And among those We created is a group that guides with the truth and establishes justice accordingly.

” The phrase “among those We created” encompasses all of humanity and the jinn. It signifies that within the multitude of created beings, there is always a remnant, a selected group, that remains steadfast upon the truth. This group is not identified by race, lineage, or worldly status but by a specific function and quality.

The first quality is that they “guide with the truth.” This means they actively call others to the path of Allah. They are not passive believers, content with their own salvation; they take up the responsibility of da‘wah, of leading people to the correct understanding of Tawheed and righteous living. Their guidance is anchored in “the truth” (al-haqq), which is the revelation of Allah, the teachings of the prophets, and the unchanging principles of the faith. They do not guide according to whims, cultural trends, or personal opinions. Their compass is fixed on the divine truth.

The second quality is that they “establish justice accordingly.” The Arabic word for justice is qist, which implies equity, fairness, and balance in all affairs. Their commitment to truth is not merely theoretical or confined to preaching; it translates into the practical establishment of justice in their dealings, judgments, and social interactions. They give each person their due, they stand against oppression, and they uphold the scales of fairness that Allah has commanded.

The word “accordingly” links their establishment of justice directly back to the truth they guide with. Their justice is not a secular concept of equity but a justice rooted in and defined by revelation. This verse is a profound reassurance for the believers. No matter how dark the times, no matter how widespread corruption and misguidance may become, there will always exist a group of people who hold the banner of truth and justice. This concept is echoed in authentic hadith that speak of a victorious group from the Muslim ummah who will remain steadfast upon the truth until the end of time. For the individual believer, this verse is both a comfort and a challenge. It comforts by affirming that the path of truth is never completely abandoned, and it challenges by inviting the listener to examine themselves: am I part of this group? Do I guide with truth, and do I establish justice in my own sphere of influence? The verse thus sets a high standard for the community of faith, linking guidance with justice as inseparable components of the righteous life.

=182=

In stark contrast to the group that guides with truth and establishes justice, this verse describes the fate of those who persist in denial. “As for those who deny Our signs” encompasses all who have been presented with the clear proofs of Allah’s existence, His oneness, and the truth of the prophets, yet willfully reject them. The signs include the verses of the Quran, the miracles, and the evidence in the natural world. The response to such denial is a specific divine strategy: “We will gradually draw them to destruction. ” The Arabic term sanastadrijuhum comes from the root daraja, meaning to proceed step by step, to descend gradually.

This is the concept of istidraj—a slow, imperceptible leading towards ruin. It is not a sudden, violent punishment but a gradual process that the victim is completely unaware of. Allah draws them by opening doors of provision, health, and worldly success. They become increasingly comfortable, arrogant, and deluded. They mistake the respite for approval, the blessings for a sign of divine favour. Every step they take deeper into sin and heedlessness is, in reality, a step closer to their own destruction. The terrifying aspect is captured in the phrase “in ways they cannot comprehend. ” They do not perceive the trap. They walk confidently into their own doom, thinking they are walking towards prosperity. Their intellects, clouded by their desires and arrogance, are incapable of recognising the signs of their impending downfall.

This is a profound psychological and spiritual punishment. The worst form of divine wrath is not a lightning bolt from the sky but a prolonged state of delusion where the sinner is lulled into a false sense of security. The Prophet warned about this when he said that when you see Allah giving a person worldly things despite their sins, know that it is istidraj. This verse serves as a chilling warning against complacency in sin. It teaches that a life of ease and prosperity is not necessarily a sign of divine pleasure; it could be the very instrument of a gradual descent into ruin.

For the believer, this verse instils a healthy fear of Allah’s subtle planning. It encourages constant self-auditing and repentance, and a refusal to measure one’s standing with Allah by material success. The true sign of safety is not wealth but a heart that is constantly turning to Allah in humility and obedience, never taking His blessings for granted.

=183=

This verse reinforces and deepens the concept of istidraj introduced in the previous verse. Allah directly speaks, using the first person, to emphasise the personal nature of this divine strategy. “I only delay their end for a while.” The disbelievers who deny the signs are not being ignored; their punishment is not forgotten. It is deliberately deferred.

The word “only” (innamā) indicates that the delay is not an indication of weakness or indifference but a specific part of a calculated plan. The respite is granted so that they may increase in sin, or so that their true nature becomes fully manifest, or so that they might still, in the period of the delay, turn back in repentance. The duration is “for a while,” which is a finite period known only to Allah. The delay is temporary, and the end is certain.

The verse then provides the reason for the confidence in this strategy: “but My planning is flawless.” The word “planning” translates kayd, which can also mean scheme or stratagem. When applied to Allah, it signifies His perfect, wise, and irresistible arrangement of events to bring about a just outcome. His planning is flawless (matīn)—solid, firm, unbreakable. There is no flaw, no loophole, no escape. The plans of the disbelievers, no matter how elaborate, are fragile webs that collapse at the slightest touch of divine power. The flawless planning of Allah ensures that the oppressor does not escape, that the delayed punishment arrives at the precisely correct moment, and that every act of patience by the believers is accounted for. This verse is an immense source of comfort for those who are oppressed and who see their oppressors thriving. It tells them that the delay is not a denial of justice. The divine plan is unfolding with perfect precision. The prosperity of the tyrant is part of the plan, not a contradiction of it. The believer is called to trust in the flawless planning of Allah, to be patient, and not to be deceived by the apparent success of the forces of falsehood. The ultimate outcome is guaranteed, for the planning of the Almighty is flawless, and no human scheme can ever outmanoeuvre it. The verse simultaneously warns the oppressor and comforts the oppressed, encapsulating the perfect balance of divine justice and wisdom.

=184=

This verse shifts from the general description of the deniers’ fate to a direct, logical defence of the Prophet Muhammad against the accusations of his Meccan opponents. Allah poses a rhetorical question: “Have they not ever given it a thought?

” The question is an invitation to reflect, to use their reason rather than blindly following their prejudices. The matter that they should reflect upon concerns the Prophet himself. “Their fellow man is not insane.” The Quraysh called the Prophet a madman, a poet possessed by jinn, or a magician. This verse asks them to simply think about the man they have known for forty years. He is “their fellow man” (sāhibuhum), their companion, who had lived among them with a reputation of absolute truthfulness and sound judgment, earning the title Al-Amin, the trustworthy. Has he suddenly lost his mind?

The question appeals to their direct, personal knowledge of his character. Insanity has signs: erratic behaviour, incoherent speech, moral inconsistency. They witnessed none of these in him. His life, his teachings, his consistency, his patience under persecution—all testify to a sound mind and a noble purpose. By negating the charge of insanity, Allah clears the ground for the positive truth: “He is only sent with a clear warning.” He is not a madman; he is a warner, a nadhīr, sent by the Lord of the worlds.

The word “only” (illā) indicates that his role is singular and unambiguous: to deliver a clear warning about the consequences of disbelief and the reality of the Hereafter. The warning is “clear” (mubīn)—it is not cryptic, confusing, or hidden. It is presented in eloquent Arabic, with logical arguments, with compelling stories, and with the testimony of a life lived in perfect moral uprightness. This verse is a masterpiece of dawah. It disarms the ad hominem attack by appealing to the very people who are making it, using the evidence of their own lived experience. It teaches the believer that the truth is often opposed not by rational arguments but by labels and slander, and the best response is to point to the self-evident reality of the messenger’s character and the clarity of the message. It also places the onus on the listener to reflect honestly and to distinguish between a divine warner and a deranged man, a distinction that any sincere person can easily make.

=185=

The surah expands the call for reflection from the character of the Prophet to the vast, observable universe. “Have they ever reflected on the wonders of the heavens and the earth, and everything Allah has created?

” This is a sweeping invitation to contemplate the malakūt—the grand dominion of Allah. The heavens with their stars, galaxies, and precise orbits; the earth with its mountains, oceans, and diverse ecosystems; and every creature that exists—all are signs pointing to the Creator.

The word “wonders” is not in the Arabic text but captures the sense of the vast, awe-inspiring nature of the universe that the verse evokes. The disbelievers are asked whether they have truly looked and pondered, or have they walked the earth with their eyes wide open but their hearts blind? The call to reflection then takes a personal, urgent turn: “and that perhaps their end is near.

” The term of their own life, or the arrival of the collective punishment, may be just around the corner. This thought should shake them from their complacency. If death is near, there is no time to waste in heedless denial. The combination of reflecting on the macrocosm and on one’s own mortality is a powerful spiritual exercise. The immensity of the universe humbles the soul, and the nearness of death awakens it.

The verse then concludes with a rhetorical question that carries a sense of sorrowful finality: “So what message after this Quran would they believe in?” If they do not believe in this Book, which is filled with clear signs, logical proofs, and responses to their objections, then what other message could possibly convince them? The Quran is the ultimate criterion, the final and most comprehensive revelation. To reject it is to shut the door on all divine guidance. There is no other scripture, no other prophet, no other sign that will come after it.

The question is a sobering reflection on the state of the hardened heart. When a person rejects the Quran, they have rejected the best and last offer of salvation. The verse is a profound call to those who are sitting on the fence, to those who claim to be waiting for a sign. The sign is already here, in the Book and in the universe, and the time for decision is limited.

=186=

After the rhetorical question about the Quran being the final message, this verse states the spiritual reality that explains why some will never believe despite the clarity. “Whoever Allah allows to stray” refers back to the divine law of guidance and misguidance. Allah’s “allowing” someone to stray is not an arbitrary act; it is the divine response to a person’s persistent, willful rejection of the truth. When an individual consistently chooses arrogance, closes their heart to the signs, and mocks the messengers, Allah withdraws His guiding light from them. They are left to stray. The consequence is absolute: “none can guide.” No human being, no preacher, no scholar, no book—no secondary cause whatsoever—can bring that person back to the path of guidance if Allah has decreed their straying. The door of guidance is ultimately in Allah’s hands. He opens it for whom He wills, and He closes it for whom He wills, based on His perfect knowledge and justice.

The verse then describes the condition of those left to stray: “leaving them to wander blindly in their defiance.” The word “wander blindly” (ya‘mahūn) paints the picture of a person stumbling in darkness, confused, with no sense of direction. They move, but they get nowhere; they think they are progressing, but they are circling in their own ignorance.

The phrase “in their defiance” (tughyān) indicates that their blind wandering is not a passive state but an active, rebellious one. They are not merely lost; they are willfully rebelling, pushing beyond all bounds, and adding to their own darkness with every act of disobedience. This verse serves as a terrifying warning about the spiritual blindness that results from persistent sin. It calls the believer to cling desperately to the guidance of Allah, to fear the state of being abandoned to one’s own devices, and to pray constantly for steadfastness. It also provides a realistic perspective for those who call others to the truth: the outcome is not in their hands. Their duty is to convey, but ultimate guidance belongs to Allah alone. The verse closes the door on despair for the preacher—he cannot force guidance—and opens the door of hope in Allah’s power alone.

=187=

The Quraysh, in their mockery, often asked the Prophet about the timing of the Day of Judgment, seeking either to catch him out or to express their scepticism. This verse provides the definitive response to such questions. The Prophet is instructed to answer with a clear declaration of the limits of his knowledge. “Say, ‘That knowledge is only with my Lord.’” The timing of the Hour is exclusively part of the unseen (ghayb), known to none but Allah. The Prophet does not have access to this information.

This is a fundamental teaching: no prophet, no angel, no human being knows when the end will come. The phrase “He alone will reveal it when the time comes” indicates that the Hour is currently hidden but will be made manifest at its appointed time by Allah’s command. Then the verse describes the awesome nature of the Hour: “It is too tremendous for the heavens and the earth. ” The weight and horror of that Day are so immense that even the vast heavens and the solid earth cannot bear its full knowledge or its impact. It is an event of such cosmic magnitude that it dwarfs all of creation.

The verse adds, “and will only take you by surprise.” The suddenness is a consistent Quranic theme. The Hour will not arrive with a long, gradual warning on that specific day; it will come abruptly, catching everyone off guard, in the midst of their daily affairs. The Quraysh’s questioning is then rebuked: “They ask you as if you had full knowledge of it.” Their persistent interrogation implied that they thought the Prophet was either hiding the answer or should be able to provide it. The instruction to the Prophet is to reiterate the point with emphasis: “Say, ‘That knowledge is only with Allah, but most people do not know.’” Most people fail to grasp the wisdom behind the concealment of the Hour. The concealment serves as a test, keeping believers in a state of constant readiness and preventing complacency.

This verse teaches the believer the proper adab regarding the unseen. It is not our task to speculate about the exact timing of the end; our task is to prepare for it.

The answer also demonstrates the perfect humility of the Prophet, who freely admitted the limits of his knowledge, a sign of his truthfulness. The verse redirects human curiosity about the future into a productive concern for the present state of the soul.

=188=

Continuing the theme of the Prophet’s limited knowledge from the previous verse, this verse expands to encompass all power and ability. The Prophet is commanded to make a profound declaration of his own human helplessness and utter dependence on Allah. “Say, ‘I have no power to benefit or protect myself, except by the Will of Allah.’” The Prophet declares that he does not own, by himself, any power to bring about good for himself or to ward off harm from himself. Every benefit that comes his way, every affliction that is averted, is solely by the will and decree of Allah. If the Prophet himself is in such a state of absolute dependence, then how can anyone else claim independent power for themselves or for their idols?

This is a devastating blow to all forms of self-reliance and to the worship of any being other than Allah. The verse then addresses the specific matter of the unseen: “If I had known the unknown, I would have benefited myself enormously, and no harm would have ever touched me.” If the Prophet possessed independent knowledge of the ghayb, he would have used it to accumulate unimaginable wealth, to avoid every danger, to predict every calamity, and to secure a life of perfect safety and prosperity. The fact that his life was one of struggle, poverty, persecution, and hardship is the clearest proof that he did not possess the keys to the unseen. He was a servant, not a fortune-teller. This argument is both logical and deeply personal. It strips away any illusion that the Prophet was a superhuman being with access to divine secrets on demand. He was, as the verse concludes, “only a warner and deliverer of good news for those who believe.” His role was strictly defined: to warn of the consequences of disbelief and to give glad tidings of Paradise to the believers. This verse is a cornerstone of Islamic theology regarding the nature of the Prophet. He is the greatest of creation, the beloved of Allah, but he is a human being and a servant. His knowledge is limited to what Allah reveals to him. For the believers, this verse fosters a correct, balanced love and respect for the Prophet, devoid of the exaggeration that leads to ascribing divine attributes to him. It also teaches the believer to place their ultimate trust not in any created being, but in the One who holds all benefit and harm in His hand.

=189=

This verse shifts to a different argument for Tawheed, drawing on the intimate, universal human experience of procreation. “He is the One Who created you from a single soul” refers to Adam, the father of humanity. All human beings, with their diverse races and languages, trace their origin back to one soul. This common origin is a sign of Allah’s oneness and power. From that single soul, Allah “made its spouse,” Hawwa (Eve), so that Adam would find comfort (yaskunu) in her.

The word for comfort implies tranquillity, love, and the deep psychological and emotional rest that a spouse provides. This is the foundation of marriage in Islam: it is not merely for physical union but for the spiritual and emotional peace that comes from the complementary nature of the pair.

The verse then traces the journey of parenthood. “After he had been united with her, she carried a light burden that developed gradually.” The woman conceives, and the pregnancy begins with a light, barely perceptible load that grows heavier as the child develops. The gradual progression is a sign of Allah’s wisdom and mercy; the burden is not imposed all at once. Then, “When it grew heavy,” meaning in the later stages of pregnancy when the physical and emotional weight is most felt, the parents naturally turn to Allah. They are driven by hope and anxiety to “pray to Allah, their Lord.” Their prayer is a conditional promise: “If you grant us good offspring, we will certainly be grateful.

” The word “good” (sālihan) means righteous, healthy, and sound. In that moment of vulnerability, when they are on the cusp of receiving a new life, they instinctively recognise that the outcome is entirely in Allah’s hands. They make a vow of gratitude. This prayer is the cry of the fitrah. Even polytheists, in moments of extreme need, often turn to the one true God. The verse beautifully illustrates the natural human recognition of Allah’s power and lordship, which surfaces in the crises of life. It sets the stage for the next verse, which will expose the tragic irony of what often happens after the prayer is answered.

=190=

This verse exposes the tragic sequel to the sincere prayer of the parents. After the child is born healthy and sound, and the blessing is fully realised, the human tendency to forget and to be ungrateful kicks in. “But when He granted their descendants good offspring, they associated false gods in what He has given them.” The pronoun “they” can refer to Adam and Eve’s descendants, or, as some scholars explain, to the polytheists of Mecca who would name their children ‘Abd al-‘Uzza (servant of the goddess Uzza) or similar idolatrous names, thereby associating partners with Allah in the very blessing of the child. The gratitude they had vowed turns into shirk. Instead of dedicating the child to the worship of the One who granted it, they dedicate the child, or their acts of gratitude, to false deities. The blessing that should have drawn them closer to Allah becomes a cause of straying further.

This is a profound insight into human ingratitude. The intensity of the prayer in the moment of distress does not always translate into lasting faithfulness when the distress is relieved. The pattern is identical to what was described about Pharaoh’s people and others who broke their promises after the removal of the plague.

The verse then declares, “Exalted is Allah above what they associate with Him!” Allah is far above and free from any partners they attribute to Him. His perfection is untouched by their shirk, and their actions harm only themselves. This verse serves as a powerful warning to all parents and to all recipients of divine blessings. When a child is born, it is a test. The gift must be met with the fulfilment of the vow of gratitude. The child should be raised upon Tawheed, given a good name, and taught to worship Allah alone. To associate partners with Allah in the blessing of a child—whether through dedicating the child to a false god, or through superstitious practices, or through forgetting the true Giver—is a grievous act of betrayal of the covenant made in the state of need.

The verse also recalls the story of Adam and Eve, where Satan’s whisperings began with the promise of good, and it reminds humanity that the cycle of prayer, blessing, and subsequent ingratitude is a recurring test that must be consciously resisted.

=191=

Having exposed the ingratitude of the polytheists who associate partners with Allah in the blessing of children, the surah launches into a powerful, rational dismantling of idol worship. This verse presents a fundamental argument against the logic of shirk by examining the nature of the objects they worship. “Do they associate with Allah those idols which cannot create anything, but are in fact created?

” The question is rhetorical, intended to provoke thought and shame. Allah is the Creator, Al-Khaliq. He brings things into existence from nothing. The idols that the polytheists worship, whether they are statues carved from stone, trees, heavenly bodies, or jinn, possess no creative power whatsoever. They did not create a single atom, a single fly, a single grain of sand. They are utterly incapable of the most essential attribute of divinity: the power to create. Not only can they not create, but they themselves are “created.” They are the product of human hands carving, or they are natural phenomena that were brought into being by Allah. A created thing, by definition, is dependent on a creator. It is weak, limited, and originated. How can a created thing, which owes its very existence to another, be worthy of worship alongside the Uncreated Creator? The logical contradiction is absolute. Worshipping a created object is the height of irrationality. It is like a pot thanking the potter’s tool instead of the potter. This argument targets the very foundation of idol worship. The pagan Arabs themselves acknowledged that Allah was the supreme Creator of the heavens and the earth. The Quran repeatedly uses this admission against them: if Allah is the Creator, then why do you worship things that do not create?

This verse teaches the believer the importance of grounding faith in clear, logical distinctions. The attribute of creation belongs exclusively to Allah. Any being that does not create, and is itself created, is disqualified from being considered divine. This simple but profound principle demolishes all forms of shirk, whether the worship of idols, of prophets, of saints, or of nature. It refocuses the heart exclusively on the One who alone has the power to bring things into existence.

=192=

This verse adds a second, equally devastating argument against the false gods. The previous verse established their inability to create; this verse establishes their inability to provide help or protection. “Which cannot help them” refers to the idols’ incapacity to assist their worshippers. The polytheists turned to their gods for aid in times of war, sickness, drought, or distress. They believed these deities could intercede, bring victory, or provide relief. The verse declares that this belief is utterly false. The idols are completely powerless to help anyone. They cannot heal the sick, enrich the poor, or protect from harm.

The second part of the verse intensifies the argument: “or even help themselves?” If the idols cannot even protect or benefit themselves, how can they be expected to do anything for others? The idols themselves are vulnerable. They can be broken, burned, stolen, or melted down. They cannot defend themselves against a fly, let alone a human being who decides to destroy them. The story of Abraham smashing the idols and placing the axe on the largest one is a perfect illustration of this helplessness. The statues could not stop him, could not cry out, could not even move. The powerlessness of the idols is so obvious that it becomes a source of mockery in the Quran. This argument appeals directly to human experience and common sense. Every human being has an innate need for a source of help that is more powerful than themselves. To turn to an object that is weaker than oneself—that cannot even defend its own existence—is a profound betrayal of the intellect. The verse is a call to recognise that true help can only come from the One who is All-Powerful, All-Self-Sufficient, the As-Samad, who needs no help and can provide all help. By highlighting the impotence of the false gods, the verse aims to free the human mind from the bondage of irrational fear and hope placed in created things. It directs the believer to place their reliance solely on Allah, the only Helper who has absolute power to benefit and to harm.

=193=

This verse moves from the general inability of the idols to the specific context of supplication and guidance. “And if you call upon them for guidance, they cannot respond to you.” The polytheists would invoke their idols, asking them to guide them to the right path, to solve their dilemmas, or to provide direction in life. The verse states the stark reality: the idols are utterly unresponsive. They cannot hear the call, and even if they could, they have no guidance to offer. They are lifeless objects or helpless beings with no knowledge of the truth. The call for guidance is the most fundamental spiritual need of a human being. To direct this call to anything other than Allah is to direct it into a void.

The verse then emphasises the totality of their helplessness with a powerful statement: “It is all the same whether you call them or remain silent.” Your invocation makes absolutely no difference to them. Whether you cry out with all your heart, perform elaborate rituals, or remain completely silent, the outcome is identical: nothing.

This is the ultimate exposure of the futility of idol worship. In any relationship, even with a weak human being, there is some form of response—a reaction, a change in expression, a word. With the idols, there is not even that. They are a complete dead end. The silence of the idols contrasts sharply with the ever-attentive Allah, who hears every whisper, sees every tear, and responds to every sincere supplication. This verse is not just a historical critique of pagan Arabs; it is a timeless warning against placing one’s ultimate hope and seeking ultimate guidance from any source other than Allah. Whether it is a political ideology, a charismatic leader, a material possession, or a human institution, the principle holds: anything other than Allah is, in and of itself, incapable of truly responding to the deepest call of the human soul. The believer learns from this to reserve their supplication, their deepest hopes, and their search for guidance exclusively for Allah, the Living, the Sustaining, who never fails to hear and to answer in the way He knows is best.

=194=

This verse delivers a final, devastating challenge that reduces the idols to the level of their worshippers, stripping them of any divine pretence. “Those idols you invoke besides Allah are created beings like yourselves.

” The phrase “created beings” translates ‘ibād, which can also mean slaves or servants. The idols, whether they are inanimate objects or spiritual beings like jinn and angels, are all servants of Allah. They are not masters; they are fellow creatures. They are “like yourselves” in the fundamental sense that they are created, dependent, and subject to Allah’s command. They have no independent divinity.

This is a humbling demotion of the so-called gods. The Arab polytheists would acknowledge their own createdness, so this argument forces them to see the logical inconsistency: if both you and your gods are equally created, why are you worshipping your equals? Worship is due only to the One who is fundamentally different—the Uncreated Creator.

The verse then throws down a direct challenge: “So call upon them and see if they will answer you, if your claims are true!” This is a bold, almost confrontational invitation. Allah is daring the polytheists to put their gods to the test. Go ahead, pray to them with all sincerity, ask them for something specific, and wait for a response. The implication is that no response will ever come. The challenge is designed to expose the emptiness of their beliefs in a practical, experiential way. The silent failure of the idols to answer will be the irrefutable proof of their falsehood.

This verse teaches the believer that faith is not a blind leap; it is based on evidence and reason. The believer is encouraged to test the claims of falsehood and to see for themselves that they lead nowhere. The stark contrast between the living, responsive Lord and the dead, unresponsive idols is a powerful motivator for exclusive devotion. It also emboldens the believer in the face of false beliefs: the truth does not fear examination, while falsehood collapses under the slightest scrutiny.

=195=

The exposure of the idols’ impotence continues with a series of rhetorical questions that appeal to basic sensory perception. “Do they have feet to walk with? Or hands to hold with? Or eyes to see with? Or ears to hear with?” These are the fundamental limbs and senses that any living being uses to interact with the world. The idols lack all of them. They cannot move, they cannot grasp, they cannot see, and they cannot hear. They are utterly deficient, lacking even the basic tools of a functioning creature. A being that lacks these faculties is completely dependent and helpless. How can such a deficient being be considered a god? A god is supposed to be superior, perfect, and all-powerful. An idol, lacking hands, cannot create or provide; lacking eyes, cannot watch over its worshippers; lacking ears, cannot hear their prayers. The questions highlight the absurdity of worshiping something that is less capable than the worshipper. The human being who carved the idol has hands, eyes, and ears, but the idol has none. So the worshipper is bowing to something that is lower than himself.

This is a profound inversion of the natural order. After this devastating dismantling, the Prophet is instructed to issue a final, defiant challenge: “Say, O Prophet, ‘Call upon your associate-gods and conspire against me without delay!’” The Prophet is told to declare his complete fearlessness. Let them gather all their false gods, all their plots, all their armies. Let them not give him any respite. He stands under the protection of the One who has feet, hands, eyes, and ears in a manner befitting His majesty, the All-Seeing, the All-Hearing, the All-Powerful. Against that protection, the helpless idols and their worshippers can do nothing. This verse is the peak of prophetic courage and reliance on Allah. It teaches the believer that when the falseness of the opposition’s foundation is clear, there is no reason to fear. The believer can face the entire machinery of falsehood with calm confidence, knowing that the power behind the truth is the ultimate power, and the power behind falsehood is nothing but an illusion.

=196=

Following the bold challenge to the idolaters, the Prophet is instructed to declare the source of his confidence and protection. “Indeed, my Protector is Allah Who has revealed this Book.

” The word “Protector” translates Waliyy, which encompasses guardian, ally, patron, and supporter. The Prophet is not alone; he is under the direct guardianship of the Lord of the universe. This guardian is not a distant, abstract deity but the One who “has revealed this Book.” The revelation of the Quran is cited as the specific proof of this protective relationship. The act of revealing the Book is an act of intimate communication, guidance, and commitment. The One who sent down the guidance will also protect the one to whom it was sent and the message itself. The connection between the revelation and the protection is profound: the Book is the rope of Allah, and whoever holds it is held by Allah. Then the verse broadens the scope: “For He alone protects the righteous.” The principle is not limited to the Prophet. Allah is the Waliyy of all the righteous (sālihīn). The righteous are those who fulfill their duties to Allah and to His creation, who live lives of integrity and faith. Their protection is not always in the form of worldly safety, for prophets and righteous people have faced harm and death. The protection is comprehensive: protection of their faith from deviation, protection of their hearts from despair, protection of their legacy from oblivion, and ultimately protection from the Fire and admission into Paradise. This verse offers immense comfort to every believer striving to walk the path of righteousness. It assures them that they are not orphans in a hostile universe. They have a Guardian who is the Almighty, the Wise. Their job is to focus on being among the righteous, and the job of protection belongs to Allah.

The verse also subtly reiterates the refutation of idolatry: why seek protection from helpless idols when the true Protector is the One who revealed the truth and who guarantees the safety of the ultimate outcome for the righteous?

=197=

This verse concludes the direct comparison between Allah and the idols, repeating the argument from earlier but now placed in the context of protection and alliance. “But those false gods you call besides Him” refers to all the beings and objects that the polytheists invoked as partners. The verse delivers a clear, double-edged verdict on their capabilities. First, they “can neither help you. ” They are utterly powerless to provide any assistance, whether in worldly matters or in the Hereafter. They cannot grant victory, provide sustenance, heal sickness, or offer intercession without Allah’s permission. All hopes pinned on them are vain. Second, they cannot “even help themselves. ” This is the ultimate proof of their impotence. The idols themselves are vulnerable. The false gods, whether they are statues, celestial bodies, or even living beings like the angels whom the polytheists worshipped, are all dependent on Allah for their own existence and protection. They cannot avert harm from themselves. How then can they be expected to help anyone else? This repetition of the argument serves to drive the point home with finality. By now, the listener should have no excuse for confusing the Creator with the created. The stark contrast between verse 196 and verse 197 is the essence of Tawheed. The true Protector is the One who sent the Book and protects the righteous. The false protectors are those who are themselves in need of protection. The believer is called to make a conscious, intellectual, and emotional break from all false attachments and to place their trust solely in the One whose help is real and whose protection is absolute. The verse serves as a powerful declaration of independence from all false powers, liberating the heart to rely only on Allah. It is the logical conclusion of the entire argument against shirk that has been woven through the surah. The only rational response to this evidence is to abandon the worship of the helpless and to turn fully to the One who holds all power.

=198=

This verse adds another dimension to the helplessness of the idols: their complete lack of perception. “If you call them to guidance, they cannot hear.” The idols are deaf. No matter how earnestly their worshippers pray, no matter how loudly they cry out, the idols perceive nothing. They cannot hear the call for guidance, let alone respond to it. This deafness is not just physical but symbolic of their utter disconnection from reality. Then the verse addresses the Prophet directly with an observation about the idols’ physical appearance: “And you may see them facing towards you, but they cannot see.” The idols are carved with painted eyes or shaped in a way that seems like they are looking. The Prophet can see these statues with their artificial gazes, but they possess no actual sight. They are blind. The illusion of a face is just that—an illusion. There is no consciousness, no awareness, no perception behind those painted eyes.

This contrast between the appearance and the reality is a powerful metaphor for idolatry as a whole. Idolatry is based on appearances, on the tangible, on the aesthetically pleasing, but it lacks any substance. The idols look like they are facing you, but they see nothing. They look impressive, but they are hollow. The believer, by contrast, worships an unseen God who sees everything, hears everything, and is fully aware.

This verse teaches the importance of not being deceived by outward forms. The true measure of worth is not in a visible, tangible presence but in actual power, knowledge, and life. The idols are dead, and worshipping them is a relationship with the dead. The relationship with Allah is a relationship with the Ever-Living, who sees and hears all. This verse serves as the final nail in the coffin of the rational argument against idol worship, preparing the ground for the moral and spiritual instructions that follow in the concluding verses of the surah.

=199=

After the intense theological critique of idolatry and the bold challenge to the disbelievers, the surah shifts its tone to provide a beautiful, concise guide to prophetic character and interpersonal conduct. The verse contains three commands. First, “Be gracious” translates the word ‘afw, which means forgiveness, pardon, and taking the easier path. The Prophet is instructed to adopt a posture of gracious leniency. He should not seek revenge for personal slights, should not be harsh in demanding his rights, and should make matters easy for people. This grace is not a sign of weakness but of strength and confidence. Second, “enjoin what is right” (‘urf).

The word ‘urf refers to what is known and recognised as good by sound human nature and by revelation. The Prophet is to command people to every form of recognised goodness—justice, kindness, honesty, modesty, and all virtues.

This is a proactive duty: not merely avoiding evil but actively promoting good. Third, “turn away from those who act ignorantly.” The Prophet is advised to disengage from the ignorant—those who mock, argue pointlessly, or refuse to engage with the truth in a sincere manner. Turning away is not an act of cowardice but a strategic and spiritual withdrawal. It preserves one’s energy and dignity for more productive engagements. Engaging with the ignorant often leads to a descent into their level, with insults replacing arguments and emotions clouding judgment. The Quran elsewhere says, “When the ignorant address them, they say words of peace. ” This threefold instruction is a complete manual for the ethical preacher. Grace softens hearts, enjoining good builds society, and turning away from ignorance protects the soul. These commands, while initially directed at the Prophet, are binding on all believers who seek to represent Islam in the world. They encapsulate the balance of mercy, active goodness, and dignified restraint that characterises the ideal Muslim personality. This verse is a breath of calm after the storm of debate, grounding the believer in practical, attainable virtues that pave the way for peaceful coexistence and effective dawah.

=200=

The final command in this set addresses the internal, spiritual battle that every believer faces. After the external instructions on dealing with others, the surah turns to the internal struggle against the whisperings of Satan. “If you are tempted by Satan” translates the Arabic nazaqa, which means an incitement, a provocation, a sudden evil impulse. This temptation can occur in any form—a flash of anger, a lustful thought, a whisper of pride, a desire to respond harshly to an insult, or a doubt about faith. No one is immune to these satanic provocations, not even the Prophet himself, though he is protected from acting upon them. The temptation is the spark; the believer’s response determines whether it becomes a fire. The divine prescription is immediate and precise: “then seek refuge with Allah.” The instruction is to turn to Allah at the very moment the temptation is felt. The believer should say, “A‘ūdhu billāh” (I seek refuge in Allah) or any similar supplication, internally or audibly, and shift their mental and emotional state towards Allah. Seeking refuge is a recognition of one’s own weakness and Allah’s complete power over the devil. It is a spiritual weapon that extinguishes the satanic spark before it catches.

The verse concludes with two divine names that are perfectly suited to this context: “Surely He is All-Hearing, All-Knowing. ” Allah is All-Hearing, so He hears the whispered prayer for refuge, even if it is silent. He is All-Knowing, so He is fully aware of the temptation, the struggle in the heart, and the sincerity of the seeking. The believer is assured that their call for help is not into a void but is heard and understood by the One who can neutralise the threat. This verse is a profound source of comfort and a practical spiritual technique. It teaches that the believer is not alone in the battle against their lower self and Satan. The cure is always at hand. The act of seeking refuge is a reaffirmation of Tawheed, for it is a turning away from the whisperer, who is a creature, to the Creator who controls all hearts. The verse bridges the external moral commands with the internal spiritual reality, reminding the believer that outward conduct depends on inward purity, and inward purity is maintained by constant recourse to Allah’s protection.
