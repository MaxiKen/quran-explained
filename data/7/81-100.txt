=81=

After Lot’s general rebuke about committing an unprecedented shameful deed, he now specifies exactly what that deed is. “You lust after men instead of women” is a direct and unambiguous condemnation of homosexual practice. Lot’s words strip away any pretence and call the act by its true nature. It is a lustful desire directed towards the same gender, abandoning the natural and divinely ordained relationship between men and women.

The phrase “instead of women” highlights the perversion of the natural order. Allah created women as partners for men, and this arrangement fulfils physical, emotional, and procreative purposes that sustain human society. Turning away from this to seek satisfaction with men is described not as an alternative orientation to be celebrated but as a deviation from the intended design. Lot’s speech demonstrates that the prophets did not shy away from naming specific sins, no matter how unpopular the message might be. Then he declares, “You are certainly transgressors.

” The word “transgressors” (musrifūn) comes from the root meaning to exceed bounds. Their crime is not just a minor error; it is a flagrant crossing of every limit set by Allah—the bounds of nature, of morality, and of decency. By labelling them transgressors, Lot indicates that their behaviour is a wilful, excessive indulgence, not a harmless preference. This verse sets the Quranic position on homosexual acts with absolute clarity. The story of Lot is consistently presented across multiple surahs as a warning against such behaviour. The punishment that follows is directly linked to this sin, along with their associated crimes of highway robbery and public indecency.

For believers, this verse serves as a timeless standard of sexual ethics, rooted not in shifting social customs but in the will of the Creator. Lot’s courage in speaking so plainly to his people is an example of speaking truth to power, even when the entire society has normalised a grave wrong.

The verse also reassures believers that the moral boundaries set by Allah are not arbitrary; they are designed for human flourishing, and transgressing them is an act of self-destruction, as the fate of Sodom will demonstrate.

=82=

The reaction of Lot’s people to his clear moral rebuke is recorded in this verse, and it reveals a society that has completely inverted its moral compass. Their “only response” indicates that they did not engage with his argument, nor did they offer any justification for their deeds. They had no defence, so they resorted to a chillingly dismissive statement: “Expel them from your land! They are a people who wish to remain chaste! ” The call to expel Lot and his followers shows their intolerance of any dissenting voice. They could not tolerate the presence of those whose very chastity was a living condemnation of their depravity.

The phrase “your land” is ironic; the chiefs spoke as if the land belonged to them, forgetting that the earth belongs to Allah, and that they were merely temporary residents. Then they describe Lot’s group as “a people who wish to remain chaste. ” This is an astonishing admission. They mock the virtue of chastity itself. Lot and his family and followers abstained from their sinful practices and maintained purity; for this, they were labelled as self-righteous nuisances who needed to be driven out. It is the classic response of a corrupt majority towards a righteous minority: they feel judged by the mere existence of the good, and they seek to silence or remove that source of guilt. This verse captures the social pressure and hostility faced by believers in a morally inverted society. The people of Lot had reached a point where vice was the norm and virtue was viewed as a threat to their way of life. Their demand for expulsion mirrors the Quraysh’s plotting against the Prophet Muhammad, who was also considered a troublemaker because he called for purity and the worship of one God. The lesson for believers is timeless: when you uphold divine standards, you may be accused of extremism, intolerance, or self-righteousness. But the truth remains the truth, and the approval of the corrupt majority is not the measure of success. Lot’s steadfastness in the face of such hostility is a model of patience and reliance on Allah.

=83=

The divine response to the crisis in Sodom is swift and decisive. “So We saved him and his family” refers to Lot and his believing household. The salvation was complete and miraculous. The angels, who had visited Lot in the form of handsome young men and were threatened by the mob, revealed their true identity and instructed Lot to leave the city by night with his family, not looking back. The salvation was an act of mercy, a direct intervention to extract the righteous from the midst of the corrupt before the punishment descended.

However, the verse includes a crucial exception: “except his wife, who was one of the doomed.” Lot’s wife did not participate in the homosexual acts of the people, but she was complicit in other ways. The Quran explains elsewhere that she acted as a spy for the sinners, informing them when guests arrived so they could harass them. Her heart was with the corrupt society, not with her prophet husband. She had no faith, and her disbelief and betrayal sealed her fate. She was told not to look back, but she did, and the punishment overtook her along with the others. This exception teaches a profound lesson: familial relationship to a prophet or a righteous person does not guarantee salvation. Faith is a personal matter. A person may live in a household of belief yet remain disbelieving at heart. No amount of proximity to goodness can substitute for one’s own sincere faith and action. The wife of Lot is mentioned alongside the wife of Noah as an example of those who rejected faith despite being married to a prophet.

For believers, this is a warning against spiritual complacency. Being part of a Muslim family or a righteous community is a blessing, but it does not automatically save one from punishment if the heart remains attached to sin and unbelief. The salvation of Lot and his daughters, while his wife perished, underscores the absolute justice of Allah, who judges each soul according to its own deeds.

=84=

This verse describes the destruction that befell the cities of the plain after Lot and the believers had departed. “We poured upon them a rain of brimstone.” The Quran uses the word “rain” (matar) ironically, because rain normally brings life and cleansing. Here, the rain was of a completely different nature: stones of baked clay, marked by Allah, raining down in a lethal torrent. Other verses specify that the cities were flipped upside down, and then the rain of stones fell upon them. This was an unprecedented punishment that matched their unprecedented sin. The “brimstone” or stones of clay were heated and hardened, each aimed precisely at the individual sinners. The rain came from the sky, demonstrating that the forces of nature are under Allah’s command and can be turned into instruments of punishment.

The verse then invites reflection: “See what was the end of the wicked!” The Arabic word “end” (āqibah) refers to the final outcome, the ultimate destination. The wicked, who mocked chastity, violated guests, and engaged in open depravity, were obliterated. Their civilisation, which had seemed so secure and permanent, was reduced to a barren wasteland—the Dead Sea area, a stark, lifeless reminder. This call to “see” urges the listener to travel, either physically or through the mind’s eye, and contemplate the ruins and the historical lesson. It also directs attention to the moral law that governs history: wickedness, especially when it becomes a societal norm, leads to destruction. For the Prophet Muhammad and his companions, this was a source of comfort and a warning to their opponents. The Quraysh passed by the remains of these destroyed nations on their trade journeys to Syria. They were not unaware of the stories, yet they failed to take heed. The verse closes the chapter of Lot with a powerful rhetorical flourish, connecting the specific punishment to the general principle of divine justice, and preparing the listener for the next story of Shu’aib, which will also end with a vivid demonstration of the end of the corrupt.

=85=

The narrative continues with the fifth major prophet story in this surah, that of Shu’aib and the people of Midian. “And to the people of Midian We sent their brother Shu’aib.” Midian was a tribe living in northwestern Arabia, known for their trade and commerce. They were descendants of Abraham through his wife Keturah, making Shu’aib their kinsman. Like every messenger, his opening call centres on Tawheed: “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him.” The message is always the same at its core. He then states, “A clear proof has already come to you from your Lord. ” This proof includes the rational arguments for monotheism and the miracles granted to him. But Shu’aib is particularly associated with economic justice, and his message expands to address the specific corruption rampant in his society.

The first command is to “give just measure and weight.” The people of Midian were notorious for cheating in trade. They would manipulate scales, giving less than what was due when selling and demanding full when buying. This dishonest practice was a form of theft and oppression that had become normalised.

The second command is to “not defraud people of their property.” This is a broader prohibition of all forms of financial exploitation, including fraud, withholding rights, and unjust dealings.

The third command is to “nor spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order.” Economic dishonesty is itself a form of corruption that destabilises society, breeds distrust, and destroys the moral fabric. Shu’aib argues that rectifying these practices is “for your own good,” meaning that honesty and justice bring tangible benefits in this life and the next. He then adds, “if you are truly believers,” tying economic ethics directly to faith. True belief in Allah demands honesty in dealings.

This verse establishes that Tawheed is not merely a private creed; it must manifest in social and economic justice. Shu’aib’s comprehensive message integrates worship of the one God with integrity in business, showing that religion encompasses every aspect of life.

=86=

Shu’aib continues his admonition by addressing another major crime of his people. “And do not lie in ambush on every road” describes a practice of waylaying travellers. The people of Midian would sit by the roads, not just to rob, but also to threaten and intimidate. The specific target is revealed: “threatening and hindering those who believe in Allah from His Path and striving to make it appear crooked. ” This shows that their highway robbery had an ideological dimension. They would stop people from approaching Shu’aib and hearing his message. They used threats, perhaps even violence, to prevent the spread of monotheism. They also engaged in a propaganda campaign to “make it appear crooked,” spreading lies and doubts about the straight path of Allah to discourage people from following it.

This is a spiritual as well as a physical crime. Then Shu’aib shifts to remind them of their past: “Remember when you were few, then He increased you in number.” The Midianites were once a small, insignificant tribe, and Allah multiplied them and granted them strength and prosperity.

This reminder is meant to stir gratitude and fear of the one who gave them their numbers. The verse concludes with a warning: “And consider the fate of the corruptors!” They are urged to look at the ruins of Noah’s people, ‘Âd, Thamûd, and the people of Lot, all of whom were corrupted and were destroyed. Shu’aib is telling them that their current power and numbers are not a guarantee of safety. The same Lord who blessed them can also bring them to account. This verse provides a comprehensive diagnosis of a corrupt society: economic cheating, physical intimidation of the righteous, ideological distortion of the truth, and arrogant forgetfulness of past blessings. The believer is reminded to neither engage in such practices nor be intimidated by those who do, for their end is already sealed by historical precedent.

=87=

After delivering his forceful admonitions, Shu’aib addresses the divided state of his people. His message has caused a split: some have believed in the truth he brought, while the majority have rejected it. The believers are likely a small, persecuted minority, while the disbelievers are the powerful chiefs. In such a tense situation, Shu’aib does not call for armed rebellion or immediate confrontation. Instead, he counsels patience. “If some of you do believe... while others do not, then be patient until Allah judges between us.

” This is a call to leave the final judgment to Allah. The believers are urged to remain steadfast in their faith and their ethical conduct, even in the face of ridicule, threats, and economic boycott. They are not to take justice into their own hands, but to wait for Allah’s decree, which will inevitably vindicate the truth and punish the falsehood.

The phrase “Allah judges between us” refers to the divine judgment that manifests either in this world through the punishment of the disbelievers and the salvation of the believers, or in the Hereafter through eternal reward and punishment. Shu’aib then declares, “He is the Best of Judges.

” This statement affirms complete trust in Allah’s wisdom and justice. Human judges may err, be bribed, or be influenced by power, but Allah’s judgment is perfect, based on absolute knowledge and fairness. This verse is a model for how to handle religious division and persecution. The believers are not to compromise their faith, but they are also not to descend into chaos or despair. Patience and reliance on Allah are the proper responses. This echoes the guidance given to the Prophet Muhammad in Mecca, where he was instructed to bear patiently the rejection of his people and to wait for Allah’s decision. The ultimate vindication came in ways no one expected. This verse instils in believers the calm confidence that truth will prevail, even if the immediate circumstances seem bleak.

=88=

The response of the elite to Shu’aib’s call for patience and justice is an ultimatum. “The arrogant chiefs” are the same type of power-holders who opposed every prophet. They had wealth, influence, and a vested interest in the corrupt status quo. They threatened Shu’aib and the believers with expulsion: “We will certainly expel you and your fellow believers from our land. ” The phrase “our land” reveals their arrogance and sense of ownership, as if the land belongs to them and not to Allah. They consider Shu’aib and his followers to be foreign elements that must be either assimilated or removed. The condition for remaining is apostasy: “unless you return to our faith.” They demand that Shu’aib abandon the monotheistic faith he has been calling them to and revert to their idolatrous religion.

The word “return” implies that Shu’aib was once upon their faith before prophethood, which is likely, as prophets were generally born into their people’s environment and only later received revelation. Shu’aib’s reply is a rhetorical question loaded with incredulity: “Even if we hate it?” He is asking them whether they truly expect him to embrace a faith that he knows to be false and that his soul detests. This question exposes the absurdity of their demand. Faith is a matter of the heart, not a political convenience. Forcing someone to profess a belief they inwardly reject is the height of tyranny and hypocrisy. Shu’aib makes it clear that even under the threat of exile, he and the believers will not pretend to accept something they abhor. This response demonstrates the unwavering commitment of the prophets. They would rather face expulsion, poverty, and danger than compromise their faith. For the believers who faced similar threats in Mecca, this was a powerful precedent.

The verse teaches that when forced to choose between conforming to falsehood or facing worldly loss, the believer’s choice must be clear: the truth is not negotiable, no matter the cost.

=89=

Shu’aib continues his response with a detailed explanation of why returning to their faith is unthinkable. He begins, “We would surely be fabricating a lie against Allah if we were to return to your faith after Allah has saved us from it.

” This is a powerful statement. Shu’aib equates embracing idolatry with inventing a falsehood against the Creator. Having been guided to the pure truth of Tawheed, abandoning it for polytheism would be the worst form of slander against God.

The phrase “after Allah has saved us from it” acknowledges that their deliverance from disbelief was an act of divine grace, not their own doing. To throw away that gift would be an act of supreme ingratitude. He then states, “It does not befit us to return to it unless it is the Will of Allah, our Lord. ” This is a profound theological statement. Shu’aib recognises that everything happens by Allah’s will, so hypothetically, if Allah willed that they fall back into disbelief, it could happen. But he immediately clarifies the character of Allah: “Our Lord has encompassed everything in His knowledge.” Allah’s knowledge is perfect and all-encompassing, and His will is always linked to wisdom. The believers are confident that Allah, in His mercy, will not will misguidance for those who sincerely seek Him. Then comes the declaration of complete reliance: “In Allah we trust.

” This is the hallmark of the believers. Having done their part, they place their full trust in Allah, not in any human power.

Finally, Shu’aib prays: “Our Lord! Judge between us and our people with truth. You are the best of those who judge.

” This is a prayer for a decisive judgment that will distinguish truth from falsehood and deliver the righteous from the oppressors. It is a prayer of total submission to Allah’s decision, whatever form it may take. This verse encapsulates the prophetic response to persecution: firm refusal to compromise, deep trust in Allah, and turning to Him in supplication for a just resolution.

=90=

Having failed to intimidate Shu’aib into recanting, the chiefs now turn their attention to the masses, attempting to dissuade them from believing. Their threat is directed at their own people: “If you follow Shu’aib, you will surely be losers!

” This is a classic tactic of the elite: they cannot refute the message with logic, so they appeal to fear and worldly interests. The word “losers” is intentionally chosen to strike at the heart of a trading society like Midian. They associate their prosperity with their current religious and economic system, a system built on cheating and idolatry. Following Shu’aib would mean abandoning those lucrative but dishonest practices. The chiefs paint this as a sure path to financial ruin and social marginalisation. They frame the choice as one between prosperity under their leadership and poverty under the leadership of a prophet.

This is the same argument used against Prophet Muhammad by the Quraysh: that abandoning the religion of their ancestors would lead to economic loss, as they would lose the pilgrimage trade and their status. The chiefs of Midian are essentially saying, “Stick with us, the winners; do not join the camp of the losers.” This threat reveals their materialistic worldview. They judge success purely by wealth, power, and numbers. They cannot see the eternal profit that comes with faith or the eternal loss that accompanies disbelief. The verse exposes the false calculus of the worldly mind. In reality, the true losers are those who sacrifice their faith and morality for temporary gains, because their worldly profit will vanish, and they will face eternal ruin. The believers who seem to lose in this world are, in fact, the ultimate winners. This verse encourages believers to see beyond the immediate threats of the powerful and to recognise that true loss and gain are determined by Allah, not by the standards of the corrupt elite.

=91=

The response to the arrogance and threats of the Midianite chiefs was swift and catastrophic. “Then an overwhelming earthquake struck them.

” The Arabic word for earthquake here is also rajfah, the same term used for the punishment of Thamûd, suggesting a violent, ground-shaking convulsion. However, other verses indicate that the people of Midian were also afflicted with a terrible cry or blast, and possibly a cloud of fire that rained down upon them. The combined effect was a total, all-encompassing destruction. The earthquake was so severe that no structure could withstand it, and no person could flee. The very land that the chiefs arrogantly called “our land” turned against them and became their grave. The verse says “they fell lifeless in their homes. ” The word “lifeless” (jāthimūn) again paints the picture of bodies crouched and motionless, frozen in the moment of death. The chiefs who had threatened Shu’aib with expulsion were themselves rooted out from their homes, not by human hands but by divine decree. The irony is complete: they told the believers to leave the land or else, but it was the believers who were saved while the oppressors were destroyed in the land they thought belonged to them. The suddenness of the punishment echoes the earlier warnings that divine torment can come by surprise while people are at their most secure. The people of Midian went to sleep as a thriving commercial society and never woke up. Their wealth, their scales, their threats—all became meaningless in an instant. This verse is a stark reminder that no earthly power can protect against the wrath of Allah when it descends. For the believers, it is a comforting confirmation that the threats of tyrants are empty when compared to the power of the Almighty. Justice may be delayed, but it is never abandoned.

=92=

The aftermath of the punishment is described with a striking image. “Those who rejected Shu’aib were wiped out as if they had never lived there.” The destruction was so complete that it left no trace of their bustling civilisation. Their homes, marketplaces, and roads became desolate. It was as though their entire existence had been a fleeting dream, erased from the landscape.

This is the ultimate negation of their arrogance. They had acted as permanent lords of the land, but now the land showed no sign of their presence. The statement “as if they had never lived there” underscores the fragility of human power and the ease with which Allah can remove it. Then the verse delivers the final verdict, echoing the chiefs’ own words back at them: “Those who rejected Shu’aib were the true losers.” The chiefs had threatened the people that following Shu’aib would make them losers. Now Allah declares that the real losers are precisely those who rejected Shu’aib. They lost their lives, their wealth, their power, and, most devastatingly, their chance at eternal salvation. Their worldly success turned out to be the ultimate illusion. This reversal of labels is a common Quranic device. The values of the worldly are inverted by divine judgment. The verse forces the listener to reconsider who the “winners” and “losers” truly are. A person may appear to be winning in this world—accumulating wealth, enjoying popularity, and successfully opposing the truth—but the end reveals the reality. True loss is not poverty or social marginalisation; it is the loss of Allah’s mercy and the eternal life of bliss. For the believers who were threatened and called losers, this verse is a powerful affirmation. It reassures them that their choice to follow the truth, even at great worldly cost, is the only truly winning strategy. The story of Shu’aib thus concludes with a profound lesson on the real measures of success and failure.

=93=

After the destruction, Shu’aib, like the prophets before him, turns away from the ruins and addresses his perished people. His words are both a testimony and a consolation for himself. He begins, “O my people! Indeed, I have delivered to you the messages of my Lord and gave you sincere advice. ” This declaration of a clear conscience is the common refrain of the messengers after their people’s destruction. Shu’aib affirms that he fulfilled his duty completely. He did not hold back any part of the revelation, nor did he compromise the truth for the sake of popularity or safety. His advice was sincere; he genuinely wanted their salvation.

This statement is a form of closure for the prophet. He bears no guilt for their fate. Then he asks a rhetorical question: “How can I then grieve for those who chose to disbelieve?

” This is not a cold-hearted dismissal but a profound recognition of moral reality. Shu’aib had grieved for them when they were alive, warning them with tears perhaps. But after they wilfully rejected every sign and forced the hand of divine punishment, his grief transforms into acceptance of Allah’s judgment. Their disbelief was not a misfortune that befell them; it was an active choice they made and persisted in. To grieve endlessly for those who chose their own destruction would be to question the justice of Allah. The prophets are models of emotional balance. They are intensely compassionate, yet they do not let their compassion override their acceptance of divine wisdom when the decree is finalised.

This verse teaches that while we should care deeply about guiding others, there comes a point where the rejection is so entrenched that we must leave the matter to Allah without allowing ourselves to be consumed by sorrow. It is a lesson in emotional resilience for all those who call others to the truth and face persistent refusal.

=94=

With the series of prophetic stories concluded, the surah now extracts a universal principle from them. “Whenever We sent a prophet to a society” indicates that what follows is a consistent divine pattern, a law of history that has operated across all nations. “We afflicted its disbelieving people with suffering and adversity.” The words “suffering” (ba’sā’) and “adversity” (darrā’) encompass a range of hardships: poverty, famine, disease, physical affliction, loss of wealth and life. These afflictions were not random; they had a clear purpose. “So perhaps they would be humbled.

” The Arabic word translated as “humbled” (yaddarra‘ūn) carries the sense of breaking down, becoming soft, turning with humility. The purpose of the hardship was to shatter their arrogance and to make them turn to Allah in repentance and supplication. When people live in ease and plenty, they often become heedless and proud. Difficulty has a way of stripping away the illusions of self-sufficiency and reminding the human being of his essential poverty before the Creator. The prophets were sent with the message, but the message was often accompanied by trials that served as wake-up calls. This method is a form of divine mercy. Before sending a devastating final punishment, Allah sends lesser hardships to give people a chance to wake up and return to the right path.

The verse teaches that suffering can be a tool of spiritual education. It is not always a punishment for past sins; it can be a corrective measure to prevent greater ruin. For the believers, this provides a framework for understanding trials: they are opportunities for humility and turning back to Allah.

For the disbelievers, the failure to respond to such wake-up calls is what leads to the final, overwhelming seizure described in the next verse.

=95=

This verse continues to unravel the cycle of human response to divine tests. After the period of hardship, Allah would change their condition to one of prosperity.

The word “prosperity” (hasanah) means good things, ease, abundance, and health. They “flourished”—they grew in numbers, wealth, and strength, spreading out and enjoying the good life. But instead of being grateful and linking the relief to their repentance, they developed a new narrative. They argued, “Our forefathers too had been visited by adversity and prosperity.” This was their way of dismissing the divine origin of the cycles. They claimed that hardship and ease are simply the natural ups and downs of life, experienced by all people across all times, and have nothing to do with Allah’s intervention or their own sins. By attributing the cycles to natural causes or chance, they neutralised the moral and spiritual lesson.

This is a classic form of denial: explaining away the signs of Allah as mere natural phenomena. Their prosperity, instead of leading to gratitude, led to arrogance and a false sense of security. So the verse concludes, “So We seized them by surprise, while they were unaware. ” When they were at the peak of their prosperity, feeling most safe and secure, the final punishment descended suddenly. They never saw it coming because they had convinced themselves that the pattern of history was just random fluctuation. This verse is a chilling warning against the secular mindset that dismisses divine involvement in the world. It teaches that the alternation of hardship and ease is a dialogue between the Lord and the servant, not a meaningless cycle. The one who reads the signs correctly repents in hardship and gives thanks in prosperity. The one who misreads them is lulled into a fatal complacency. The sudden seizure is the inevitable end of those who fail to learn the lesson.

=96=

This verse presents the lost opportunity, the hypothetical alternative history that could have been. “Had the people of those societies been faithful and mindful of Allah” sets the condition. Faith (īmān) and mindfulness (taqwa) are the two qualities that would have changed everything. If they had believed in the messengers and lived with constant awareness of Allah’s presence and commands, the outcome would have been radically different. Instead of destruction, “We would have overwhelmed them with blessings from heaven and earth.

” The word “overwhelmed” suggests a flood, an opening of gates of unimaginable abundance. Blessings from heaven include rain, appropriate weather, and spiritual light. Blessings from earth include fertile soil, abundant crops, mineral wealth, and stable living conditions. The combination encompasses every possible good, material and spiritual.

This promise is not limited to those ancient people; it is a universal principle. Righteous, faithful societies that live by divine guidance are promised prosperity and stability. The blessings are not necessarily identical to worldly conceptions of wealth; they encompass contentment, security, and the removal of anxiety. Then the verse returns to reality: “But they disbelieved, so We seized them for what they used to commit.” Their disbelief was the barrier that blocked the blessings. The seizure was the direct consequence of their actions. This verse connects morality and material wellbeing in a clear cause-and-effect relationship. It refutes the idea that faith and prosperity are unrelated. While individual believers may face tests of poverty, at the collective societal level, widespread faith and taqwa attract divine favours, while widespread corruption invites ruin. For modern readers, this verse offers a blueprint for societal reform: the path to overcoming crises—economic, environmental, social—is not only through technical solutions but through a return to faith and mindfulness of Allah, which align human behaviour with the laws that the Creator has embedded in the universe.

=97=

After laying out the principle, the surah now poses a series of rhetorical questions that call the listener to reflect on the false sense of security that leads to ruin. “Did the people of those societies feel secure that Our punishment would not come upon them by night while they were asleep?

” The question is directed at the Quraysh and, by extension, all who follow in the footsteps of the destroyed nations. Night is the time of rest and unconsciousness. People retreat into their homes, lock their doors, and feel safe from external threats. But the punishment of Allah is not an external threat that can be barred by walls. When it comes at night, it finds people at their most vulnerable, completely unaware and unable to flee. The historical examples of this include the people of Lot, who were struck at sunrise after a night of wickedness, and the people of Shu’aib, who were seized in the morning.

The question challenges the assumption that safety is guaranteed by human measures. True security comes only from Allah. When a society has earned punishment through its persistent corruption and denial, no amount of fortifications, guards, or technology can prevent the divine decree. The night is specifically mentioned because it is the time when evil deeds are often plotted and when people feel hidden from sight. The punishment coming at night demonstrates that the One who sees in the darkness is also the One who can strike in the darkness. This verse is a call to examine one’s own false sense of security. Many people live as though disaster could never befall them personally. They see others suffer and think they are immune. This question shatters that illusion and urges an immediate turning to Allah before the night comes when safety is no longer guaranteed.

=98=

This second rhetorical question complements the first by covering the other half of human activity. If night is the time of sleep and rest, day is the time of activity, work, and “play.

” The term “play” (yal‘abūn) refers to the engrossment in worldly pursuits that are devoid of eternal purpose. It does not necessarily mean frivolous games; it includes all the serious business of life that people engage in while forgetting Allah. The people who are “at play” are those who are busy with their trades, their social lives, their ambitions, and their entertainment, completely heedless of the Hereafter.

The question asks whether they felt immune from punishment descending upon them in the midst of this busy daylight activity. Historical precedent provides examples of this as well: the people of Thamûd were struck by the earthquake in the morning, and the people of ‘Âd were destroyed by a wind that raged for days, coming upon them while they were actively going about their lives. The suddenness of daytime punishment is equally terrifying. A person might be in the market, in the office, at a social gathering, and in an instant, everything is over. The verse targets the illusion that safety is tied to a particular time of day or a particular level of awareness. Allah’s punishment is not bound by human schedules. This question, paired with the previous one, covers all times: whether you are asleep or awake, passive or active, you are always within the reach of divine power. The message is that the only true security lies in living in a state of constant awareness and obedience. The “play” of worldly life should not deceive anyone into thinking that the Day of Reckoning is a distant fantasy. By posing these questions, the Quran invites the listener to internalise a state of spiritual wakefulness, never taking safety for granted.

=99=

This question moves from the specifics of timing to the overarching concept of Allah’s makr, often translated as “planning” or “stratagem.” “Did they feel secure against Allah’s planning?” refers to the idea that Allah has a deliberate, all-wise course of action regarding those who oppose Him. His “planning” is His subtle, gradual dealing with the wrongdoers, leading them step by step to their destruction without them perceiving it.

This is the istidraj mentioned in other verses: Allah opens doors of blessings for them, increasing them in wealth and health, while they sink deeper into sin and arrogance. This prosperity is not a sign of approval but a part of the plan, lulling them into complete heedlessness until the sudden seizure.

The question challenges the misinterpretation of divine delay. The disbelievers often think that the absence of immediate punishment means they are safe or that their actions are acceptable. But the reality could be the opposite: they are being given rope to hang themselves. Then the verse makes a definitive statement: “None would feel secure from Allah’s planning except the losers.

” This is a profound psychological and spiritual truth. The person who feels completely safe from Allah’s punishment, who never experiences fear of divine justice, is not a strong believer but a spiritually bankrupt one. True believers balance hope and fear. They hope for Allah’s mercy but also fear His punishment, and this fear keeps them vigilant. This verse brings the historical reflection to a direct address to the current inheritors of the earth, particularly the Meccans and all later generations. “Is it not clear to those who take over the land after the destruction of its former residents” points out that the Quraysh were living on lands that had once belonged to powerful civilisations that were wiped out—the ‘Âd, the Thamûd, the people of Lot, the Midianites. They could see the ruins with their own eyes, walk through the empty dwellings, and hear the stories.

The question asks: has not the lesson penetrated their hearts? Do they not see that the same power that destroyed those nations can destroy them as well? The specific warning is: “that—if We will—We can punish them too for their sins and seal their hearts so they will not hear the truth?” This outlines two possible forms of punishment.

The first is a direct, physical punishment similar to the earthquakes, floods, and storms that wiped out the previous nations. The second is a spiritual punishment: the sealing of the hearts. When hearts are sealed, the truth becomes inaccessible. They hear the words of the Quran, but they cannot comprehend or accept them. This sealing is a consequence of their persistent sins and rejection. It is a punishment far worse than physical destruction because it leads to eternal ruin. The verse is an urgent appeal to the living to learn from history before it is too late. The earth is full of archaeological and historical evidence of destroyed civilisations, and these are not mere accidents but divine signs.

The verse also contains a profound theological point: Allah’s punishment can take the form of spiritual blindness, which should terrify the believer. One should never assume that just because the physical punishment has not come, all is well. The sealing of the heart is a punishment that can occur in this life, imperceptibly, as a person sins and delays repentance.

The verse ends the cycle of prophetic stories and their lessons, transitioning the focus firmly onto the present audience, urging them to heed the warning and turn in faith before they become another chapter in the book of the destroyed.
