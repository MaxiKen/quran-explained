=121=

Following their instinctive prostration, the magicians now give verbal expression to their new faith. They declared openly and publicly, “We now believe in the Lord of all worlds.” This declaration is momentous for several reasons. First, it was made in the very presence of Pharaoh, the man who had promised them wealth and proximity and who claimed to be the supreme lord himself. The magicians were fully aware that this confession would enrage the tyrant and put their lives in immediate danger.

Yet they did not whisper it secretly; they proclaimed it aloud as a testimony of truth. Second, the phrase “Lord of all worlds” is precisely the title that Moses had used when he first addressed Pharaoh. By adopting this exact phrase, the magicians were affirming that the God of Moses is indeed the true Lord of everything—of Egypt, of the heavens, of the earth, and of all creation. They were directly repudiating Pharaoh’s claim to divinity. Third, the word “now” indicates a decisive break with their past. They were not hedging or delaying; they were marking a clear before-and-after in their lives. Before this moment, they were magicians in the service of tyranny; now, they were believers in the service of the Almighty. This public declaration is a model for all who embrace faith. When the truth becomes clear, the believer should not hide it out of fear of consequences. The magicians understood the risk they were taking, but the certainty of what they had witnessed made the risk irrelevant. Their declaration also serves as a profound testament to the power of divine signs. Men who had spent their lives mastering deception were instantly transformed into sincere worshippers of the Truth.

The verse teaches that genuine faith, once it enters the heart, demands to be proclaimed, regardless of the cost.

=122=

The magicians complete their declaration of faith by specifying exactly which Lord they mean. “The Lord of Moses and Aaron” identifies the God who sent these two prophets as messengers. This clarification was necessary because Pharaoh himself had claimed to be a lord, and the Egyptians worshipped a pantheon of deities. By linking the Lord of all worlds specifically to Moses and Aaron, the magicians leave no room for ambiguity. They are aligning themselves with the prophetic mission. This mention of Aaron alongside Moses is significant. Aaron was Moses’ brother, his partner in prophethood and his spokesman. The magicians recognised that both brothers were part of the same divine mission, and they honoured both. This also subtly refutes any future attempt by Pharaoh to separate the two or to claim that Moses acted alone. The magicians’ statement is a complete acceptance of the message that Moses had delivered from the beginning. They are not believing in a vague, undefined deity; they are believing in the God who revealed Himself through His chosen messengers, the God who performed these undeniable miracles through the hands of Moses. This precision in their declaration demonstrates that true faith is not a general spirituality but a specific commitment to the revealed truth and to the messengers who bring it.

For believers, this verse underscores the importance of connecting faith in Allah with faith in His prophets. One cannot claim to believe in God while rejecting the messengers He sent. The magicians understood this perfectly. Their testimony also served as a powerful witness to Pharaoh and the assembled crowds: the experts, the ones who knew the limits of human art best, were now testifying that the Lord of Moses and Aaron was the true God. This public endorsement from the most qualified witnesses was a devastating blow to Pharaoh’s entire religious and political system, setting the stage for his furious reaction.

=123=

Pharaoh’s response to the magicians’ conversion is a classic display of wounded pride and paranoia. The first words out of his mouth are, “How dare you believe in him before I give you permission?” This question reveals the heart of Pharaoh’s tyranny. He considered himself not just a political ruler but the absolute master of his subjects’ minds and souls. In his twisted worldview, even faith in God required his personal authorisation. The act of the magicians believing without his consent was, to him, an unforgivable act of insubordination.

The question also betrays his panic. He cannot refute the miracle, so he shifts the focus to the process, accusing them of a procedural violation. Then, in a desperate attempt to discredit the entire event, he invents a conspiracy theory: “This must be a conspiracy you devised in the city to drive out its people.” Pharaoh accuses the magicians and Moses of having plotted together beforehand. He claims that the entire contest was staged, that Moses and the magicians were secretly allies who planned the outcome in advance as part of a coup.

This is a bizarre accusation, given that the magicians had never met Moses before that day and had been recruited specifically to defeat him. But paranoia does not require logic. Pharaoh needed a narrative that would save face before the people, so he concocted one.

The phrase “to drive out its people” echoes the earlier fear of the chiefs—that Moses was a political threat to the ruling class. Pharaoh ends with a menacing threat: “but soon you will see. ” This is the language of a cornered tyrant. He cannot win the argument, so he resorts to threats of violence. This verse illustrates the psychology of authoritarian power when faced with truth: it denies, it fabricates conspiracies, and when all else fails, it threatens brute force. The believer learns from this to expect such reactions and not to be intimidated by them.

=124=

Pharaoh now specifies the punishment he intends to inflict on the magicians, and it is designed to be as brutal and terrifying as possible. “I will certainly cut off your hands and feet on opposite sides.

” This is a particularly cruel form of mutilation. By cutting off the right hand and left foot, or vice versa, the victim is left completely disabled, unable to use the remaining limbs effectively. It is a punishment meant to be not only physically agonising but also publicly humiliating, serving as a warning to any who might think of defying the state. Then he adds, “then crucify you all.” Crucifixion was a slow, public, and disgraceful death. The combination of severing limbs and then crucifixion represents the maximum cruelty Pharaoh’s regime could inflict. He wanted to make a spectacle of the magicians, to show the people that defection from the official religion would be met with unspeakable horror. Notably, Pharaoh singles out the magicians, not Moses and Aaron directly at this point. He could not reach Moses and Aaron because they had not committed any crime under his law—they were messengers with a sign. But the magicians, his own subjects, had committed the unforgivable sin of changing their allegiance. The threat reveals that Pharaoh’s power rested ultimately on terror. When ideology failed, when his magicians were defeated by truth, he had nothing left but the sword. This verse exposes the moral bankruptcy of regimes that prop themselves up with violence. The believer reading this verse should be struck by the contrast: the magicians had just discovered the greatest treasure—faith—and the immediate worldly consequence was a death sentence.

Yet, as the next verse will show, they did not waver. Their response is a testament to the transformative power of genuine conviction, which makes even the most gruesome threats of a tyrant seem insignificant compared to the hope of meeting the Lord.

=125=

The magicians’ response to Pharaoh’s horrific threat is one of the most powerful declarations of faith in the entire Quran. “Surely to our Lord we will all return.” Instead of begging for mercy, instead of wavering or offering to recant, the magicians respond with a statement of absolute certainty about the afterlife.

The word “surely” conveys their unshakeable conviction. They affirm that death, whether by crucifixion or natural causes, is merely a transition back to their Lord.

This statement addresses the core of the threat. Pharaoh was threatening to kill them, but they had just come to believe in a Lord who controls life and death and who will resurrect them. If they are going to return to their Lord anyway, then Pharaoh’s threat loses its terror. What is the worst he can do? Speed up their journey to the Most Merciful? This perspective transforms the situation entirely. The magicians had, moments ago, been concerned with worldly reward and closeness to Pharaoh. Now, their primary concern is their standing with the Lord of all worlds.

The phrase “we will all return” includes Pharaoh himself. They are subtly reminding him that he too will die and return to the same Lord, and that his power is only temporary.

This is a fearless proclamation that shifts the grounds of the conflict from earthly power to eternal destiny. The magicians demonstrate that true faith provides a psychological and spiritual liberation that no tyrant can crush. A person who is certain of the Hereafter cannot be controlled by threats against their worldly life. This verse is an eternal source of inspiration for believers facing persecution. It teaches that the fear of death is a weapon that only works on those who do not know what lies beyond it. For the one who knows that returning to the Lord is the ultimate homecoming, the tyrant’s worst punishment is merely the key that unlocks the door.

=126=

The magicians continue their response with a diagnosis of Pharaoh’s true grievance and a prayer to their new Lord. “Your rage towards us is only because we believed in the signs of our Lord when they came to us.” They strip away all of Pharaoh’s pretensions about conspiracy and national security. They identify the real reason for his fury: they believed in the clear signs of Allah.

This is a powerful reframing. Pharaoh wanted to portray them as traitors, but they declare that the sole cause of this conflict is their acceptance of the truth. They are not criminals; they are believers.

The word “only” emphasises that there is no other reason. Then they turn away from Pharaoh entirely and address Allah directly with a beautiful supplication: “Our Lord! Shower us with perseverance, and let us die while submitting to You.” Having declared their faith, they now ask for the two things they need most to face the impending torture and death. First, they ask for perseverance (sabr). They know that the ordeal will be severe, and they acknowledge that they cannot endure it on their own. They need divine help to remain steadfast. Second, they ask for a good ending: to die in a state of submission (Islam). They do not ask for the punishment to be averted; they ask for the quality of their response to that punishment to be perfect. They want to meet their Lord as Muslims, fully surrendered. This prayer is a model for every believer in times of trial. The goal is not necessarily to escape the trial but to navigate it with patience and to emerge from it with faith intact. The magicians’ transformation from worldly mercenaries to martyrs ready to die for their faith is one of the most dramatic and beautiful arcs in the Quran. Their prayer was answered, and they are remembered with honour among the righteous forever.

=127=

The confrontation with the magicians has ended with their conversion, but the threat to Pharaoh’s regime remains. The chiefs now turn Pharaoh’s attention back to the larger problem: Moses and the Children of Israel. They protest, “Are you going to leave Moses and his people free to spread corruption in the land and abandon you and your gods?” The chiefs frame the issue in a way calculated to provoke Pharaoh. They portray Moses as a source of corruption (fasād), which is a complete inversion of the truth—Moses was calling to the worship of Allah and justice, while Pharaoh and his chiefs were the actual corruptors. They also personalise the threat by saying Moses would cause people to abandon Pharaoh and the Egyptian gods. This plays on Pharaoh’s ego and his claim to divinity. The chiefs fear that the defeat of the magicians and their conversion will embolden the Israelites and inspire more Egyptians to follow Moses. They push for a harsh response. Pharaoh’s reply is chilling: “We will kill their sons and keep their women. We will completely dominate them.” This policy of slaughtering the male children of the Israelites and enslaving the women was not new; Pharaoh had been doing it before Moses’ birth, which is why Moses’ mother had placed him in the river. But now the policy is reinstated and intensified.

The phrase “we will completely dominate them” reveals the psychology of the oppressor: he seeks not just to control but to crush the spirit of the oppressed. This verse shows the brutal escalation that occurs when the truth begins to gain ground. The forces of falsehood, unable to defeat the message intellectually, resort to violence against the messenger’s community. It also sets the stage for the plagues that will soon afflict Egypt, as divine punishment for this systematic oppression. For the believers, it is a reminder that the path of truth is often paved with increased persecution before the dawn of victory.

=128=

In the face of Pharaoh’s renewed campaign of terror, Moses turns to his own people to fortify their spirits. He reassures them with three powerful statements.

The first is a command: “Seek Allah’s help and be patient.” The order of these two is significant. Patience (sabr) is essential, but it must be accompanied by turning to Allah for aid (isti‘ānah). Human endurance alone is insufficient; the believer must draw on divine strength through prayer, supplication, and reliance. This combination is the believer’s toolkit for any crisis. Moses then provides the theological foundation for hope: “Indeed, the earth belongs to Allah alone. ” This is a direct rebuttal to Pharaoh’s claim of ownership over Egypt. The land does not belong to Pharaoh; it belongs to Allah, and Pharaoh is merely a temporary occupant who will soon be removed.

This statement reframes the entire power dynamic. The oppressed should not view the tyrant as the owner of the land but as a usurper whose time is limited.

The third statement is a promise: “He grants it to whoever He chooses of His servants.” The inheritance of the earth is in Allah’s hands, and He bestows it according to His wisdom. This implies that the Israelites, though currently enslaved, could become the inheritors of the land if Allah wills.

The final statement is a universal principle: “The ultimate outcome belongs only to the righteous. ” The Arabic word for “outcome” is ‘āqibah, which means the final result, the ultimate victory. This victory is not guaranteed by numbers, weapons, or power, but by righteousness and taqwa. Moses is telling his people that if they remain steadfast in their faith and obedience, the final chapter of this story will be written in their favour, no matter how dark the current chapter appears. This verse is one of the great sources of hope in the Quran for all oppressed believers. It teaches that patience, prayer, and righteousness are the keys to eventual triumph, and that no tyrant lasts forever because the earth is not his—it belongs to the Righteous Owner who will give it to His righteous servants.

=129=

Despite Moses’ powerful reassurance, the Children of Israel voice their pain and frustration. Their complaint is raw and honest: “We have always been oppressed—before and after you came to us with the message.” The Israelites had suffered under Pharaoh’s brutal regime for decades. The killing of their sons and enslavement of their women was a generational trauma. When Moses arrived with his message of liberation and his signs, they had hoped for immediate relief. Instead, Pharaoh intensified the oppression. From their limited human perspective, things had gotten worse, not better. Their complaint is not a rejection of Moses’ prophethood but an expression of deep anguish and wavering hope. It reflects the human tendency to lose heart when deliverance is delayed. Moses does not rebuke them for their complaint; he understands their suffering. His response is gentle but forward-looking: “Perhaps your Lord will destroy your enemy and make you successors in the land to see what you will do.

” The word “perhaps” (asā) reflects hope, not doubt. Moses redirects their gaze from the present suffering to the future possibility. He promises that their enemy—Pharaoh and his hosts—will be destroyed. More than that, the Israelites will become the successors, the inheritors of the land. But this promise comes with a profound caveat: “to see what you will do. ” The inheritance of the land is not just a reward; it is a test. Allah will observe how they act once they are in power. Will they be grateful? Will they establish justice? Will they remain faithful? This phrase injects a note of responsibility into the promise. The Israelites are being told that their suffering is not meaningless, and their coming victory is not the end of the story. It is the beginning of a new trial—the trial of prosperity and power.

This verse teaches that believers should never despair during prolonged hardship, but they should also not assume that victory is a permanent entitlement. Every blessing is a test, and the true measure of success is what one does when the blessing arrives.

=130=

The narrative now shifts to the series of plagues that Allah sent upon Egypt as a consequence of Pharaoh’s intransigence. The first affliction mentioned is famine and a shortage of crops. “Indeed, We afflicted Pharaoh’s people with famine” indicates that this was a deliberate divine act, not a random natural disaster. Egypt was a land utterly dependent on the Nile. Its agricultural wealth was its pride and the foundation of its economy. By striking at this foundation, Allah was attacking the very source of Pharaoh’s power and the people’s comfort. The famine meant hunger, economic collapse, and suffering for the population. The shortage of crops meant the fields that normally yielded abundant harvests produced little or nothing. This was a direct challenge to the Egyptian belief that the Nile and the fertility of the land were under the control of their gods or Pharaoh himself. The purpose of this affliction is clearly stated: “so they might come back to their senses.

” The Arabic phrase yadhdhakkarūn means to remember, to take heed, to return to one’s senses. The famine was a form of divine mercy wrapped in hardship. It was a wake-up call, a loud alarm designed to shake the Egyptians out of their spiritual slumber and make them realise that their prosperity was not guaranteed by their idols or their tyrant-king, but was a gift from the Lord of Moses. The hardship was meant to humble them and drive them to seek the true God.

This verse establishes a principle that runs through all the plagues: divine punishment in this world is not merely retributive; it is reformative. Allah sends signs of distress to give people a chance to repent before the final, destructive judgment. The famine was the first tap on the shoulder, the first invitation to return. Sadly, as the next verse shows, they did not read the sign correctly.

=131=

This verse exposes the flawed logic of Pharaoh’s people in interpreting the cycles of prosperity and adversity. When times were good and the land was fertile, they attributed their prosperity to themselves or their gods: “This is what we deserve.” They saw wealth and abundance as their natural right, a confirmation of their own merit and the correctness of their ways.

This is the psychology of the spiritually heedless: in good times, they take full credit and feel no need for gratitude or humility. But when adversity struck—the famine, the shortages—they did not turn inward and examine their own sins. Instead, “they blamed it on Moses and those with him.” They saw the hardship as an evil omen brought by the presence of the Israelites and the message of Moses.

This is a classic case of scapegoating. Rather than recognising the hardship as a sign from Allah and an opportunity to repent, they projected the cause onto the innocent messenger and his followers. This shifting of blame allowed them to avoid confronting the truth. They could continue in their arrogance while vilifying the righteous.

The verse then corrects their worldview with a clear statement of faith: “Surely all is destined by Allah. ” Both prosperity and adversity are under Allah’s control. Good times are a test of gratitude, and hard times are a test of patience and a call to return. Neither is random, and neither is caused by human omens.

The verse concludes, “Yet most of them did not know. ” This ignorance was not a lack of information but a wilful blindness. They had the signs, they had Moses explaining the truth, but they chose not to understand. This verse is a mirror for all people. How often do we attribute our successes to our own efforts and blame our failures on external circumstances or innocent people? The believer is called to see all events as coming from Allah, to be grateful in ease and repentant in hardship, and to never fall into the trap of superstition and scapegoating.

The verse also shows the patience required of those who call to the truth: they may be blamed for the very calamities that are meant to wake the people up.

=132=

After the famine and the subsequent plagues that will be detailed, the people of Pharaoh reached a state of hardened defiance. This verse records their explicit declaration of unbelief. “No matter what sign you may bring to deceive us, we will never believe in you.

” This is a chilling statement of absolute rejection. They are no longer even engaging with the content of the signs. They have decided in advance that whatever Moses produces, regardless of how powerful or convincing, will be dismissed as deception.

The word “deceive” (sahara) is the root of magic, so they are persisting in the label that Moses is a magician whose signs are merely tricks. By saying “no matter what,” they are shutting the door completely. They are informing Moses that his mission, as far as they are concerned, is futile.

This is not an intellectual position; it is an emotional and volitional stance. They have made a conscious decision to disbelieve, and they are publicly announcing it. This declaration is reminiscent of the earlier verses about the sealing of hearts. When a person reaches this stage, they have passed the point of no return. They have seen the truth clearly, yet they explicitly reject it and declare that nothing will change their mind. The verse serves as a warning about the danger of persistent sin and rejection. The heart does not turn hard overnight; it is a process. Each ignored sign, each dismissed warning, adds a layer of hardness until the heart can look at the most luminous truth and call it a lie. For Moses, this declaration was a moment of profound sorrow but also a release. He had done his duty, and now the outcome was in Allah’s hands.

For the believer, this verse underscores the urgency of responding to the signs of Allah while the heart is still soft and receptive.

=133=

In response to their hardened defiance, Allah sent a series of distinct and devastating plagues. This verse lists five of them. The “floods” may refer to torrential rains that destroyed crops, or the overflowing of the Nile that wreaked havoc instead of bringing fertility. The “locusts” were swarms that devoured whatever vegetation remained, stripping the land bare. The “lice” or possibly ticks or gnats infested their bodies and homes, causing constant irritation and disease. The “frogs” multiplied to an unbearable degree, filling their houses, food, and water, making life intolerable. The “blood” likely refers to the Nile and other water sources turning into actual blood, making them undrinkable and killing the fish. These plagues were not a single event but occurred sequentially, each one a distinct and unmistakable sign. The verse says these were “all as clear signs.” The plagues were not ambiguous. They were specific, miraculous afflictions that could not be explained away as ordinary natural phenomena. Each plague targeted a different aspect of Egyptian life: agriculture, hygiene, comfort, and the very symbol of their lifeblood—the Nile.

Yet the response is tragically recorded: “but they persisted in arrogance and were a wicked people.” They refused to humble themselves. After each plague, they would beg Moses to pray for its removal, promising to believe and let the Israelites go. But as soon as the plague was lifted, they broke their promise and returned to their arrogance.

The word “persisted” indicates a repeated, deliberate pattern. “Wicked people” (qawman mujrimīn) labels them as career criminals in the moral sense. This verse demonstrates the mercy and patience of Allah, who sends escalating warnings before the final punishment. It also illustrates the irrational stubbornness of disbelief. The plagues were an opportunity for Egypt, but they treated them as inconveniences to be managed rather than signs to be heeded.

=134=

This verse reveals the cyclical pattern of Egypt’s response to the plagues. Whenever a plague descended and they were seized by torment, their arrogance vanished, and they turned to Moses in desperation. Their plea is revealing in several ways. They address Moses as “O Moses!” acknowledging him as the channel of relief. They ask him to “pray to your Lord on our behalf.” Notice they do not say “our Lord” but “your Lord.” Even in their desperation, they maintain a distance, refusing to embrace the full relationship of worship. They recognise that Moses has a special connection with his Lord, and they want to exploit that connection for their own benefit without committing to the faith themselves.

The phrase “by virtue of the covenant He made with you” refers to the special status Allah granted Moses as a prophet and the promise that his prayers would be answered. They are essentially saying, “You have a hotline to God; use it for us.” Then they make a specific and solemn promise: “If you help remove this torment from us, we will certainly believe in you and let the Children of Israel go with you.” The promise is comprehensive: they will accept Moses’ message and free the slaves. The words “certainly” and the specific mention of both belief and release indicate that they knew exactly what was required of them. This was not a vague promise; it was a detailed commitment. Moses, in his mercy, would pray, and the plague would be lifted. This verse exposes the psychology of the insincere. When the pain is present, they become instantly religious, making grand promises. But the sincerity of such promises is questionable, as they are made under duress. The true test of faith is not what a person says in the midst of a crisis but what they do when the crisis is over.

The next verse will reveal the result of their promise, and it is a pattern that continues to this day: many people cry out to God in their distress, only to forget Him when the distress is relieved.

=135=

This verse completes the pattern of broken promises. “But as soon as We removed the torment from them—they broke their promise.” The lifting of the plague was the moment of truth. Freed from the immediate suffering, they reverted to their old ways. The relief they experienced did not lead to gratitude but to a renewed sense of security and arrogance. They acted as if the plague had been a random event, not a divine warning, and they treated their own solemn promise as an empty word. This repeated cycle is a profound study in human nature. The heart that is not fundamentally transformed will return to its default state as soon as pressure is removed. True repentance is not a transaction to escape pain; it is a permanent turning of the heart towards Allah. The Egyptians wanted the pain to stop, but they did not want to change their lives, abandon their idols, or submit to the Lord of Moses. Their promises were merely a means to an end. The verse includes a cryptic phrase: “until they met their inevitable fate. ” The Arabic term ajal refers to an appointed term, the decreed time for their final destruction. This parenthetical remark indicates that the lifting of the individual plagues was temporary. The respite was not permanent; it was only a delay until the final, all-encompassing punishment—the drowning in the sea. Each broken promise added to their account and hastened them towards their inevitable end.

This is a terrifying reality: every time a person breaks a promise to Allah, they are not escaping consequences; they are merely drawing closer to the final reckoning. The verse serves as a warning against treating repentance as a bargaining chip to be used in times of trouble and discarded in times of ease. The believer is called to a consistent, sincere relationship with Allah that persists regardless of circumstances.

=136=

This verse announces the final, decisive punishment that fell upon Pharaoh and his hosts. “So We inflicted punishment upon them” is a declaration of divine retribution. After all the warnings, all the plagues, all the broken promises, the appointed term arrived. The punishment was not another temporary affliction; it was total destruction. The method of destruction is specified: “drowning them in the sea.” The famous story of the parting of the sea, the crossing of the Israelites, and the drowning of Pharaoh’s army is detailed elsewhere. Here, the surah summarises the outcome concisely. The drowning in the sea was poetic justice. The Nile and the water had been central to Egypt’s identity and power, and they had been instruments of the plagues. Now, the sea became their grave. Pharaoh, who had claimed to be the supreme lord, died like any other mortal, swallowed by the waters he could not command.

The verse then gives the reason for this punishment: “for denying Our signs and being heedless of them. ” Two sins are identified. First, they denied the signs. They saw the staff, the hand, the plagues, and they called them magic. Second, they were heedless of them. They did not give the signs the contemplation and reflection they deserved. They treated them as nuisances to be managed rather than as momentous communications from the Lord of the universe. This combination of active denial and passive heedlessness sealed their fate. The verse is a stark reminder that ignoring the signs of Allah is not a neutral act; it is a form of rejection that carries severe consequences. For the believers, the drowning of Pharaoh represents the ultimate triumph of truth over tyranny. It is the climax of the Exodus story, celebrated by Muslims as a day of deliverance. The verse reassures the oppressed that the tyrant’s power is an illusion that can be washed away in a moment by the command of Allah.

=137=

This verse describes the glorious aftermath of Pharaoh’s destruction from the perspective of the Israelites. “And so We made the oppressed people successors” refers to the Children of Israel, who had been enslaved and brutalised for generations.

The word “oppressed” (yustad‘afūn) highlights their previous state of weakness and humiliation. The transformation is stunning: from the lowest rung of society, they became the inheritors of the “eastern and western lands. ” This likely refers to the region of Greater Syria and Palestine, the blessed land promised to them.

The phrase “which We had showered with blessings” indicates that these lands were fertile, rich, and spiritually significant—a land flowing with milk and honey, as described in the Torah. The verse then explains that this transfer of power was not merely a geopolitical event but a fulfilment of divine promise: “In this way the noble Word of your Lord was fulfilled for the Children of Israel for what they had endured.” The “noble Word” refers to Allah’s decree and promise that the righteous would inherit the earth and that the patience of the oppressed would be rewarded.

The phrase “for what they had endured” acknowledges their long suffering. Their patience was not in vain; it was the very means by which they qualified for this immense reward.

The verse ends with a statement of total devastation for the oppressors: “And We destroyed what Pharaoh and his people constructed and what they established.” Their magnificent buildings, their palaces, their monuments, their irrigation systems, their entire civilisation—all of it was left behind for others to inherit or was reduced to ruins. The oppressors’ worldly achievements, which they had taken such pride in, became a lesson for history. This verse is a powerful testament to the divine law of history: the oppressed, if they remain patient and faithful, will eventually be raised, and the oppressors, no matter how mighty their structures, will be brought low. It offers profound hope to all who struggle for justice in the path of Allah.

=138=

After the miraculous crossing of the sea and the destruction of their enemy, the Children of Israel began their journey through the wilderness. The verse says, “We brought the Children of Israel across the sea,” emphasising that the salvation was entirely from Allah. During their travels, they passed by a community that was deeply engrossed in idol worship. The sight of these people, “devoted to idols,” had a shocking effect on the recently liberated Israelites. Despite having just witnessed the greatest series of miracles in history—the plagues, the parting of the sea, the drowning of Pharaoh—their first instinct upon seeing a people practising a religion was to ask for the same. They demanded, “O Moses! Make for us a god like their gods!” This request is staggering in its spiritual blindness. They had just been saved by the one true God, the Lord of all worlds, and yet the visual appeal of idolatry, the tangibility of a physical object to worship, still exerted a pull on their hearts. Generations of living among idolatrous Egyptians had left a residue in their souls. They were attracted to the sensory, the concrete, the visible representation of the divine, which is the very essence of idol worship. Moses’ response is immediate and sharp: “Indeed, you are a people acting ignorantly! ” This ignorance (jahl) is not a lack of intelligence; it is a lack of understanding of the majesty of Allah. They failed to comprehend that the God who split the sea and destroyed the superpower of the age was far beyond any man-made statue. This verse serves as a timeless warning about the human tendency to gravitate towards tangible, material representations of the sacred, and to be impressed by the religious trappings of other cultures. The freedom from Pharaoh’s physical slavery was accomplished, but the internal slavery of the mind and heart to false concepts of godhood was a much longer struggle. This incident foreshadows the later worship of the golden calf and reveals the constant need for prophetic guidance to purify the understanding of Tawheed.

=139=

Moses continues his response to the Israelites’ demand for an idol by exposing the reality of the pagan worship they had witnessed. “What they follow is certainly doomed to destruction.” The idols themselves are lifeless objects, carved from wood or stone, utterly powerless and destined to be broken, burned, or decayed. The deities they represent are false, having no real existence or power. Everything connected to such worship is doomed—the idols, the temples, and ultimately the worshippers who persist in associating partners with Allah.

This statement is a categorical rejection of any value in idolatry. Then Moses adds, “and their deeds are in vain.

” The phrase “in vain” (bātil) means false, worthless, and nullified. The religious rituals, the prayers, the sacrifices that these people offered to their idols are entirely wasted. They will receive no reward for them; they are a mirage that will vanish on the Day of Judgment. No matter how sincerely they perform their rites, if the object of worship is false, the deeds are void.

This is a fundamental principle of Islam: deeds are judged by their foundations. The most beautifully performed act of devotion, if directed to other than Allah, is spiritually empty. Moses is trying to open the eyes of the Israelites to the stark reality behind the colourful spectacle they admired. The “devotion” of those idolaters might have looked impressive, but it was devotion to a lie. Moses wants his people to understand that they have been given the truth, and the truth makes everything else obsolete. What they witnessed was not a valid alternative; it was a path to ruin.

This verse also serves as a comfort to the believers who may feel a sense of inferiority when comparing their simple, aniconic worship to the elaborate rituals of other faiths. The value is not in the external show but in the truth of the object of worship. The most humble supplication to the One True God is infinitely more valuable than the most magnificent temple built for a false deity.

Moses deepens his argument by reminding the Israelites of their special status and the immense favour they have received. The question “Shall I seek for you a god other than Allah” is rhetorical and tinged with incredulity. He is a prophet of the Most High, and they are asking him to find them a different deity. The absurdity of the request is highlighted by his tone. He then gives the reason why such a search would be not just absurd but a profound act of ingratitude: “while He has honoured you above the others?

” The Arabic word faddala means to bestow preference and excellence. Allah had chosen the Children of Israel over the other peoples of their time. This honour was manifested in many ways: He sent prophets from among them in great numbers, He saved them from Pharaoh with spectacular miracles, He provided them with manna and quails in the desert, He shaded them with clouds, and He destined them to inherit the blessed land. To receive all these honours and then to turn around and seek a god other than the One who bestowed them is the height of disloyalty and foolishness. Moses is telling them that their privileged status is not a reason for complacency but a reason for exclusive devotion. The more Allah honours a person or a community, the greater their obligation to worship Him alone and to shun all forms of false worship.

This verse teaches that blessings and honour are a test, and they carry a corresponding responsibility. The Israelites’ demand for an idol was a betrayal of the honour they had received.

This principle applies to the Muslim ummah as well, which the Quran describes as the best community raised for mankind. The honour of being a recipient of the final revelation demands the purest form of monotheism and the complete rejection of all that contradicts it. Moses’ question echoes through time, challenging every favoured community to remain faithful to the One who honoured them.
