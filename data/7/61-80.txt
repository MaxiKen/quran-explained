=61=

When the chiefs of Noah’s people accused him of being clearly misguided, he did not respond with anger or insults. Instead, he answered with calm dignity and clarity, beginning with the same affectionate address: “O my people!” This shows that despite their harsh words, his heart remained connected to them, and his sole concern was their guidance. He firmly denies their accusation: “I am not misguided! ” This is not the defensive protest of a man in doubt; it is the confident assertion of a prophet who knows he stands upon the truth. By stating it so plainly, he forces them to reconsider their charge. Then he immediately presents the correct description of himself: “But I am a messenger from the Lord of all worlds.

” This is the heart of his reply. He does not claim personal superiority or special intelligence; he points to the office that Allah has bestowed upon him. The title “Lord of all worlds” is particularly significant. Noah’s people were polytheists who worshipped various idols, each believed to govern a specific aspect of life. By invoking the Lord of all worlds, Noah declares that his message comes from the One who created and sustains everything—the heavens, the earth, and all that exists. This universal Lordship shatters the limited, fragmented authority of their false gods. The implication is clear: he is not a misguided innovator but a bearer of a divine trust. This reply also teaches an essential lesson about how to respond to false accusations. Noah does not descend into a personal quarrel; he simply clarifies the truth about his mission and shifts the focus back to the message. For the Prophet Muhammad, who faced identical accusations from the Quraysh, this verse was a direct consolation. His response should mirror Noah’s: dignified denial of false charges and a clear proclamation of prophethood.

The phrase “Lord of all worlds” also connects this story to the opening of the Quran and to the universal scope of Islam, reminding the listener that the message of Tawheed is not the invention of one man but the consistent call of all prophets to recognise the singular Creator and Sustainer.

=62=

Noah continues his clarification by detailing the nature of his mission. “Conveying to you my Lord’s messages” explains exactly what a messenger does. He is not speaking from his own mind or desire; he is a transmitter, faithfully delivering the revelations entrusted to him.

The word “messages” (risālāt) encompasses all the commands, prohibitions, stories, and truths that Allah revealed to him. This places the burden of belief or rejection on the recipients, not on the messenger. He is merely the conveyor; the content is divine. Then he adds, “and giving you sincere advice.” This reveals the motivation behind his persistence. He is not seeking power, wealth, or fame. He is a sincere advisor (nāsih) who genuinely desires their wellbeing.

The Arabic root of nāsih implies purity and freedom from deceit. Noah’s advice is untainted by hidden agendas.

This is the very quality that Satan falsely claimed to possess when he swore to Adam and Eve, “I am truly your sincere advisor.” The contrast is profound: the devil’s false sincerity leads to ruin, while the prophet’s genuine sincerity leads to salvation.

Finally, Noah says, “And I know from Allah what you do not know.” This is not a boast but a statement of fact. He has been granted knowledge of divine truths, of the unseen realities of the Hereafter, of the consequences of disbelief, and of the mercy that awaits the repentant. This knowledge is inaccessible to them through their own intellect or their idols. It is a special gift from Allah to His messengers. The statement also serves as a gentle rebuke to their arrogance. They thought they knew enough to judge him as misguided, but in reality, they were ignorant of the most essential truths. For us, this verse underscores the importance of listening to those who convey Allah’s message with knowledge and sincerity, and it reminds us that the prophets are the ultimate well-wishers of humanity, sharing secrets of the unseen for our own salvation.

=63=

Noah addresses the root of their rejection: their astonishment and indignation that a human being like themselves could be chosen as a messenger. The question “Do you find it astonishing?” directly challenges their flawed reasoning. They believed that if Allah were to send guidance, it would come through an angel or a being of a superior nature. They could not fathom that a mere man from among them, someone they knew from childhood, could be the recipient of divine revelation. This same objection was raised by the Quraysh against Prophet Muhammad. Noah dismantles this astonishment by framing the selection as an act of mercy and wisdom: “a reminder should come to you from your Lord through one of your own.” The fact that the messenger is a human being is precisely the point. A human messenger can be a relatable example; his life, struggles, and conduct serve as a practical model. He understands the weaknesses, temptations, and aspirations of his people. If the messenger were an angel, people could claim that they could not emulate him because of their different nature.

Thus, sending a human prophet is a profound mercy. The purpose of the reminder is twofold: “warning you, so you may beware and perhaps be shown mercy. ” The warning is meant to instil a healthy fear of divine punishment, motivating people to abandon their sinful ways. This fear is not an end in itself; it is a means to attain Allah’s mercy.

The word “perhaps” (la‘alla) conveys hope, indicating that if they heed the warning, they can expect to be enveloped in divine mercy. Noah’s argument is logical and compassionate. He removes the intellectual barrier by explaining the wisdom of human prophethood and replaces it with the hope of mercy.

This verse teaches believers to always trace the blessings of guidance back to Allah’s mercy and to never consider divine commands as burdens. It also reminds us that the prophets were human, making their struggles relatable and their successes attainable through faith.

=64=

After all the patient argument and sincere advice, the response of Noah’s people was persistent rejection. The verse summarises the outcome with concise finality: “But they rejected him.” This simple statement encapsulates centuries of effort met with stubbornness. Noah’s long night of calling them openly and secretly, with every possible method, resulted in only a small band of followers while the masses and the chiefs refused to believe. Then the divine judgment is pronounced: “so We saved him and those with him in the Ark.” The Ark becomes the symbol of salvation, the vessel of mercy that carried the believers to safety while the floodwaters of destruction engulfed the earth.

The mention of “those with him” includes his family members who believed, the handful of sincere followers, and the pairs of animals that were loaded onto the Ark by Allah’s command. The salvation was complete and precise; not a single believer was left behind to perish. The contrast is stark: “and drowned those who rejected Our signs.” The drowning of the disbelievers was total, leaving no survivors among the deniers.

This is the fulfilment of the “tremendous Day” that Noah had feared for them. The verse concludes with a verdict: “They were certainly a blind people. ” This blindness is not physical but spiritual. They had eyes that saw Noah, the Ark, and the signs in nature, but their hearts were blind to the truth. They could not perceive the reality of the message or the imminence of divine punishment. This blindness is the ultimate cause of their destruction. The story of Noah, placed at the head of the prophetic narratives in this surah, establishes a pattern: a messenger comes, his people reject him despite clear signs, the believers are saved, and the rejecters are annihilated. For the Quraysh, it is a warning that their rejection of Muhammad will lead to a similar fate if they do not repent. For the believers, it is a reassurance that the apparent weakness of the faithful will be followed by divine deliverance. The flood also serves as a powerful reminder that Allah’s punishment can come from a direction least expected—from the skies and the earth—overtaking the arrogant in their very strongholds.

=65=

After the destruction of Noah’s people, the surah moves to the next chapter in human history: the people of ‘Âd. “And to the people of ’Âd We sent their brother Hûd.” The ‘Âd were a powerful and advanced civilisation inhabiting the region of Al-Ahqaf, the sand dunes in what is now southern Arabia. They succeeded the people of Noah and were renowned for their towering structures, physical strength, and military might. By calling Hûd “their brother,” the Quran emphasises that he was one of them—a man from their own lineage, known to them, speaking their language. This made his message all the more compelling and removed any excuse of unfamiliarity. His opening call is identical to Noah’s: “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. ” The core message of Tawheed never changes. He commands them to direct all worship to Allah alone and negates the legitimacy of any other deity. This upfront declaration is the foundation of all prophetic missions. Then he adds a specific question: “Will you not then fear Him? ” After establishing that Allah is the only God, he appeals to their sense of self-preservation. The fear of Allah (taqwa) is the natural response to recognising His sole right to worship and His power over all things. Hûd’s question implies that if they truly understood who Allah is, they would be filled with reverential awe and would immediately abandon their false gods. This question is also a gentle rebuke; it challenges them to reflect on why they lack this fear despite acknowledging Allah in some sense, as they were not absolute atheists but polytheists who associated partners with Him. The verse sets the stage for the confrontation between Hûd and the arrogant chiefs, which will mirror the pattern seen with Noah. It teaches that the call to Tawheed is the first priority for every reformer, and that the fear of Allah is both the beginning of wisdom and the shield against destruction.

=66=

The response of the ‘Âd chiefs mirrors and intensifies the response given to Noah. They were the disbelieving elite, the ones who wielded power and influence and who benefited from the existing polytheistic system. Their reply was a two-pronged personal attack. First, they said, “We surely see you as a fool. ” The word “fool” (safīh) implies a lack of intelligence, poor judgment, and foolishness. They did not simply call him misguided; they attacked his very intellect. In their eyes, a man who would abandon the glorious traditions of their ancestors and challenge the powerful gods of ‘Âd must be mentally deficient. Second, they said, “and we certainly think you are a liar. ” This attacks his truthfulness. They accused him of fabricating his message and lying about being a messenger. The combination is devastating: if he is a fool, his words are not worth considering on their merit; if he is a liar, his motives are corrupt and he should be mistrusted. This double accusation left little room for a fair hearing among the people, as the chiefs’ words carried weight.

The verse reveals a common tactic of those who oppose the truth: instead of engaging with the arguments, they attack the character and intelligence of the messenger. This is exactly what the Quraysh did to the Prophet Muhammad, calling him a madman, a poet, a sorcerer, and a liar. By recording this, Allah comforts the Prophet and shows him that he walks the path of the noble prophets before him.

The verse also highlights the arrogance that often accompanies wealth and power. The chiefs could not accept that a man like Hûd, who did not possess their worldly status, could be right while they were wrong. The believer’s lesson is to never judge the truth by the status of the person delivering it, and to be prepared for personal attacks when standing firm on the truth.

=67=

Hûd’s reply is measured and mirrors the prophetic response of Noah. He begins again with the warm and respectful “O my people,” maintaining the connection despite their insults. He then directly refutes their first accusation: “I am no fool!

” This is not a boastful claim of intelligence but a necessary clarification. A messenger of Allah cannot be a fool because he has been entrusted with the most profound truth. His wisdom is demonstrated by his call to worship the Creator alone and to abandon what can neither benefit nor harm. He then redirects the conversation to the real point: “But I am a messenger from the Lord of all worlds.

” This is the only identity that matters. He does not ask them to judge him by his personal qualities alone; he asks them to consider the source of his message. The “Lord of all worlds” is the Lord of ‘Âd as well, the same Lord who created them, provided for them, and made them powerful. By invoking this universal title, Hûd reminds them that his authority comes from the One who governs the entire cosmos, not from any human institution. The implication is that calling him a fool is, in effect, a rejection of the wisdom of the Lord of all worlds. This response teaches profound patience and the art of dignified disagreement. Hûd did not trade insults or become enraged; he simply stated the truth and re-centred the discussion on his divine mandate.

For believers facing mockery for their faith, Hûd’s example is a timeless model: do not let the insults define you, do not descend to the level of the mockers, but calmly restate your commitment to the truth and your identity as a servant of the Lord of all worlds. The real foolishness would be to abandon this truth for the approval of people.

=68=

Hûd continues his self-definition by explaining what his messengership entails. “Conveying to you my Lord’s messages” reiterates that his role is purely that of a transmitter. He is not speaking from his own whims or inventing a new philosophy. The messages (risālāt) are a sacred trust, and his job is to deliver them faithfully without alteration or omission.

This statement places the responsibility for acceptance or rejection squarely on the people. He has done his part; the rest is up to them. Then he adds, “And I am your sincere advisor.

” This is identical to Noah’s declaration. Hûd assures his people that he has no hidden agenda. He does not want their money, their throne, or their praise. His only motive is their wellbeing in this life and the next.

The word “sincere advisor” (nāsih) carries the connotation of someone who desires pure good for the other. Hûd is essentially saying, “If you see me as a fool, know that this ‘fool’ is the only one who truly cares about your eternal fate.

” This contrast between the accusation of foolishness and the claim of sincere advice is powerful. The very person they mock is their only genuine well-wisher, while the chiefs who lead them are steering them towards destruction. This verse emphasises that the prophets are the greatest benefactors of humanity. They bring the remedy for the diseases of the soul. The believer should learn to recognise sincere advice even when it comes in a form that is not pleasing to the ego. True sincerity is not always flattering; sometimes it is the blunt warning that pricks the conscience. Hûd’s statement is also a subtle response to the charge of lying: a liar seeks personal gain, but Hûd’s only claim is that he is a conveyor and an advisor, roles that bring him nothing but hardship and rejection.

=69=

Hûd now addresses the root of their rejection, using the same logical argument as Noah but tailored to the specific history and blessings of ‘Âd. The question “Do you find it astonishing...” is a direct challenge to their prejudice against a human messenger. He reminds them that it should not be astonishing because the purpose—warning and mercy—is perfectly fulfilled through a human who understands them. Then Hûd personalises the argument by invoking their own history: “Remember that He made you successors after the people of Noah. ” The ‘Âd knew the story of the flood and the destruction of the previous mighty civilisation. They were the inheritors of the earth after that great punishment.

This reminder serves two purposes: it warns them that the same fate can befall them if they follow in the footsteps of the drowned people, and it points to Allah’s favour in granting them this succession. The second favour is that He “increased you greatly in stature. ” The ‘Âd were legendary for their physical strength and imposing bodies. They were tall, powerful, and built massive structures. This exceptional physical endowment was a gift from Allah, not a product of their own creation. Then Hûd delivers the conclusion: “So remember Allah’s favours, so you may be successful. ” Remembering favours leads to gratitude, and gratitude leads to worship and obedience, which in turn leads to success.

The word “successful” (tuflihūn) means true, lasting prosperity in this life and the next. Hûd is offering them a formula: acknowledge the blessings, trace them back to the Bestower, and respond with devotion. This argument is a masterpiece of tailoring the message to the audience. Hûd appeals to their pride in their ancestry and strength, not to flatter them, but to show them that the very things they boast about are gifts from the God they are refusing to worship.

The verse teaches that reflecting on our personal and collective blessings is a powerful path to faith and gratitude.

=70=

This verse records the defiant reply of the ‘Âd to Hûd’s reasoned arguments. Their question, “Have you come to us so that we would worship Allah alone and abandon what our forefathers used to worship?” reveals the core of their objection. It is not a lack of clarity in the message; it is a deep attachment to the ancestral religion.

The phrase “abandon what our forefathers used to worship” shows that their primary loyalty is to tradition. They see Hûd’s call to pure Tawheed as an assault on their heritage and identity. They frame monotheism as a loss, a giving up of something precious, rather than a liberation from falsehood. This argument from ancestral precedent is one of the most common refrains of the disbelievers throughout the Quran. It represents the blind adherence to custom that prevents people from embracing truth. Then, unable to refute Hûd’s logic, they resort to defiance: “Then bring us what you threaten us with, if what you say is true!

” This is a direct challenge, a demand that the punishment be hastened. It is the ultimate expression of arrogance and mockery. They do not merely reject the warning; they dare the messenger to make it happen.

This is exactly what the Meccans said to Prophet Muhammad, asking him to bring down stones from the sky or to cause the threatened torment to occur. The demand for the immediate punishment is a sign that their hearts have hardened and that they consider the warning to be an empty threat. This verse exposes the psychology of the entrenched disbeliever: they cling to tradition, refuse to reason, and finally, when cornered, they mockingly invite the punishment. The believer’s lesson is to beware of the pull of blind tradition and to always test customs against the clear standard of revelation.

=71=

Hûd responds to their challenge with a prophecy of doom, delivered not out of personal anger but as a statement of divine truth. “You will certainly be subjected to your Lord’s torment and wrath.” He does not waver or soften the warning. The torment and wrath are both “from your Lord”—the same Lord who provided them with all their blessings. Their demand for the punishment will be answered, but when it comes, they will not be able to escape it. Then Hûd turns to address their underlying argument about their forefathers. He asks a rhetorical question: “Do you dispute with me regarding the so-called gods which you and your forefathers have made up?” He dismisses their deities as mere names, fabrications with no reality or authority.

The phrase “made up” exposes the human origin of their religion. It is nothing but a tradition invented by people, passed down without any divine sanction. He then adds the critical qualifier: “a practice Allah has never authorized.

” This is the ultimate criterion for any act of worship: has Allah permitted it? If there is no divine authorization, then the worship is illegitimate, no matter how many generations have practised it. This demolishes the ancestral argument.

The verse concludes with a powerful challenge: “Then wait! I too am waiting with you. ” Hûd expresses his complete confidence in the outcome. He is not afraid of their threats or their gods. He will wait for the unfolding of events, knowing that Allah’s promise is true. This “waiting” is a form of separation and a declaration that the lines are clearly drawn. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a direct instruction on how to deal with the Quraysh’s demands. He was to warn them of the consequences, expose the falsehood of their idolatry, and then leave the matter to Allah, waiting patiently for the divine judgment.

The verse teaches the believer to stand firm, to dismantle false arguments with clarity, and to place ultimate trust in Allah’s timing.

=72=

As with Noah, the story of Hûd reaches its decisive conclusion. “So We saved him and those with him by Our mercy.” The salvation is explicitly attributed to Allah’s mercy, not merely to their own efforts. Hûd and the small group of believers who accepted his message were spared from the cataclysmic destruction that followed.

The phrase “those with him” indicates that a community of faith did form, albeit small, which provided him with companionship and support. They were saved because of their belief, and the mercy of Allah enveloped them. Then comes the fate of the rejecters: “and uprooted those who denied Our signs.

” The word “uprooted” (qata‘a dābir) means to cut off completely, to annihilate to the last one, leaving no trace. The ‘Âd civilisation was destroyed by a devastating wind that raged for seven nights and eight days, as described in other surahs, flattening their great structures and burying them in the sand. They were erased from history as a powerful nation.

The verse ends with the short verdict: “They were not believers.” This simple statement is profoundly sad. Despite Hûd’s long and patient efforts, they never became believers. Their hearts remained sealed, and their end was sealed with them. This sentence explains why they were destroyed: not because Allah is unjust, but because they persistently refused to believe, even after clear signs and warnings. This pattern of salvation and uprooting repeats in the following stories, emphasising the certainty of Allah’s decree. For the believers, it reinforces that the righteous are never truly alone and that ultimate victory belongs to them, even if it arrives after a long wait. For the deniers, it is a terrifying preview of what awaits those who follow the path of ‘Âd. The story transitions smoothly to the next nation, Thamûd, continuing the lesson.

=73=

The surah now introduces the third consecutive narrative: the people of Thamûd. “And to the people of Thamûd We sent their brother Ṣâliḥ.” Thamûd were the successors of ‘Âd, inhabiting the region of Al-Hijr in northwestern Arabia. They were known for their skill in carving magnificent homes out of the rocky mountains. Like the previous messengers, Ṣâliḥ was one of them, their brother in kinship, making his call deeply personal. His opening call is again identical: “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him.” The unchanging core of the message is the pure worship of the one God.

However, this time, a specific miraculous sign is presented immediately. “A clear proof has come to you from your Lord: this is Allah’s she-camel as a sign to you.” The people of Thamûd had demanded a miracle from Ṣâliḥ to prove his prophethood. They specifically asked for a pregnant she-camel to emerge from the rock. Allah granted their request, and a miraculous, enormous she-camel came forth from the mountain. This camel was not an ordinary animal; it was a visible, tangible sign from the Lord. It was a test for them, requiring them to respect the miracle and not harm it. The command is clear: “So leave her to graze freely on Allah’s land and do not harm her. ” The camel was to be allowed to drink from the wells on alternate days, with one day for her and one day for the people. The prohibition against harming her was a direct test of their obedience and their recognition of the sign. The warning attached is severe: “or else you will be overcome by a painful punishment.” This sets up the dramatic tension of the story. Thamûd’s response to this clear, tangible proof would determine their fate. The she-camel was a mercy and a test wrapped in one; it gave them a concrete reason to believe, but it also placed a heavy responsibility on them.

This verse teaches that miracles are not given to satisfy idle curiosity; they come with obligations. Rejecting a clear sign after demanding it invites swift and terrible consequences.

=74=

Ṣâliḥ continues his address by reminding the people of Thamûd of their own history and blessings, just as Hûd did with ‘Âd. “Remember when He made you successors after ’Âd” points to the fact that they inherited the earth after the destruction of the previous mighty civilisation. This succession was a favour and an opportunity. Allah “established you in the land,” granting them stability, power, and the resources to build a prosperous civilisation.

The verse then details their specific achievements: “you built palaces on its plains and carved homes into mountains.” Thamûd were master builders and engineers. Their skill in carving secure, elaborate dwellings into the solid rock of the mountains was legendary, and remnants of these dwellings still exist. Their ability to construct both sprawling palaces on the open plains and fortified homes in the cliffs demonstrated their advanced technology and wealth. After listing these worldly favours, Ṣâliḥ delivers a twofold command: “So remember Allah’s favours, and do not go about spreading corruption in the land.” Remembering favours is the antidote to arrogance. Instead of being proud of their own achievements, they should recognise that their skills, intelligence, and resources were all gifts from Allah. Gratitude should lead to worship and obedience. The prohibition against spreading corruption (fasād) is directly connected to their actions. Their corruption could take many forms: idolatry, oppression of the weak, cheating in trade, and eventually, harming the she-camel. By enjoying Allah’s blessings and then using them in rebellion against Him, they would be spreading corruption. Ṣâliḥ is teaching a universal principle: material prosperity is a test. If it leads to gratitude and righteousness, it is a blessing; if it leads to arrogance and sin, it becomes a curse. This verse is a mirror for every affluent society: the purpose of civilisation is not merely to build impressive structures but to establish justice and worship the One who provided the ability to build.

=75=

This verse reveals the class dynamics at play in Thamûd. The “arrogant chiefs” were the elite, the wealthy and powerful who saw themselves as superior. They addressed the “lowly who believed,” referring to the poor, the weak, and the socially marginalised who accepted Ṣâliḥ’s message. The chiefs’ question, “Are you certain that Ṣâliḥ has been sent by his Lord?” was not a sincere inquiry. It was a mocking, condescending interrogation meant to belittle both the believers and the prophet. They were implying, “You are poor and ignorant; how can you be sure about such a great matter?” Their arrogance blinded them to the possibility that truth might be with the humble. The response of the believers is a model of firm faith. They did not respond with hesitation or doubt. They declared, “We certainly believe in what he has been sent with.” Notice that they did not say, “We think” or “We hope.” Their certainty was absolute. And they emphasised that their belief was in the message itself, not just in the person of Ṣâliḥ. By saying “in what he has been sent with,” they grounded their faith in the content of the revelation, the clear signs, and the truth of Tawheed, not in blind allegiance. This response echoes the steadfastness of the followers of all prophets. The poor and downtrodden have often been the first to embrace the truth because they have fewer worldly attachments and less arrogance. The chiefs’ contempt for the poor believers is a timeless warning against judging people’s worth by their social status. In Allah’s sight, the most honoured are the most righteous. This verse reassured the Prophet Muhammad that the hostility he faced from the Meccan elite was part of a prophetic pattern, and it encouraged the believing men and women of low social status that their certainty was a badge of honour, not a mark of ignorance.

=76=

The chiefs of Thamûd, having heard the firm declaration of faith from the poor believers, respond with their own blunt declaration of rejection. Their statement, “We surely reject what you believe in,” is a clear and unambiguous line drawn in the sand.

The Arabic word for “reject” (kafara) is the root of disbelief itself. They are not merely expressing doubt or uncertainty; they are actively taking the position of kufr. They cover the truth after it has become clear to them.

The phrase “what you believe in” refers to the entire message that Ṣâliḥ brought: the oneness of Allah, the prophethood of Ṣâliḥ, the sanctity of the she-camel, and the reality of the punishment to come. Their rejection is directed not just at the prophet but at his followers as well. This verse highlights the polarising nature of truth. Once the message is clearly presented, people divide into two camps: those who believe and those who reject. There is no middle ground. The arrogance of the chiefs leads them to openly declare their enmity.

The verse also shows that the elite’s rejection was a matter of will, not intellect. They had seen the clear proof of the she-camel. Their denial was rooted in pride and a refusal to follow someone they considered beneath them.

This is a cautionary tale about the danger of arrogance. When a person’s heart is filled with pride, even the clearest signs will not penetrate. The believers, on the other hand, were rewarded with certainty. This dynamic is consistently repeated in the Quran and in human history. The lesson for believers is to take a clear stance of faith, unashamed, just as the poor believers of Thamûd did. And when faced with the arrogant rejection of others, they should not waver, because the same arrogant rejection was shown to the prophets themselves.

=77=

The climax of Thamûd’s rebellion is recorded in this verse. “Then they killed the she-camel.” This was the ultimate act of defiance. The she-camel was a living miracle, a clear sign from Allah, and they had been explicitly commanded to leave her unharmed. The killing was not the act of the entire society initially; it was carried out by a specific group of conspirators, but the whole people became complicit through their approval and their refusal to stop it.

The phrase “defying their Lord’s command” reveals the true nature of the act. It was not just cruelty to an animal; it was a direct challenge to Allah’s authority. They were telling their Lord that they did not care about His sign or His messenger. It was an act of supreme arrogance and rebellion. After committing this atrocity, they turned to Ṣâliḥ with a taunting challenge: “Bring us what you threaten us with, if you are truly one of the messengers.

” This is identical to the demand made by the people of ‘Âd to Hûd. They did not feel immediate remorse; instead, they mocked the prophet and dared the punishment to come. They believed that because the sky did not immediately fall upon them, Ṣâliḥ was a liar and his threats were empty. This false sense of security after committing a great sin is a common satanic trap. Ṣâliḥ had warned them that they had three days of enjoyment before the punishment would seize them, but they still mocked. The verse powerfully illustrates how sin, especially when done collectively and approved by society, hardens the heart to the point where the sinner no longer fears consequences but actively challenges divine justice. It is a warning that the delay of punishment is not a sign of impunity; it is a respite that can end suddenly and terribly, as the next verse will show.

=78=

The response to their defiance was swift and devastating. “Then an overwhelming earthquake struck them.

” The Arabic word for earthquake here is rajfah, which connotes a violent shaking, a tremendous convulsion that nothing could withstand. Some descriptions also mention a terrifying sound, a thunderous blast that accompanied the earthquake. The punishment seized them exactly as Ṣâliḥ had warned. They had three days after killing the she-camel, during which their faces turned pale, then red, then black, and on the fourth day, the punishment descended. The earthquake was so powerful that it shook the very mountains in which they had carved their secure homes. The very dwellings they boasted about and considered impenetrable became their tombs. The verse says, “and they fell lifeless in their homes.

” The word “lifeless” (jāthimīn) paints a picture of bodies lying prostrate, motionless, frozen in the position of death. They did not have time to flee or seek refuge. The proud civilisation of Thamûd, with its engineering marvels and its arrogance, was reduced to ruins in a single overwhelming moment. This scene is a profound meditation on the futility of human power when confronted with divine might. The mountains that they carved could not protect them from an earthquake caused by Allah’s command. The verse is also a direct warning to the Quraysh, who similarly trusted in their strongholds and mocked the Prophet’s warnings. It reminds every generation that no technology, no fortress, no wealth can shield against the decree of Allah. The suddenness of the punishment echoes the earlier verse that spoke of torment coming by night or midday while people are unaware. The people of Thamûd awoke to their last morning thinking they were safe, and by the end of it, they were no more.

This is the stark reality of divine justice when a people collectively choose rebellion.

=79=

After the destruction, Ṣâliḥ turned away from the ruins and addressed the dead, or perhaps the remaining landscape that once housed his people. His words are a poignant epitaph. “O my people!” Even in this moment of finality, he addresses them with the same affectionate term. His heart is filled with sorrow, not bitterness. He declares, “Surely I conveyed to you my Lord’s message and gave you sincere advice.

” This is the statement of a clear conscience. He fulfilled his duty completely. He held nothing back. He was not negligent or lazy in his delivery. He gave them sincere advice, meaning he genuinely desired their wellbeing and warned them out of love, not enmity. Then he states the tragic reason for their downfall: “but you do not like sincere advisors. ” This is the crux of the matter. Their destruction was not due to a lack of clear communication; it was because they had an aversion to those who told them the truth. They preferred flattery, comfortable lies, and the blind following of tradition over the hard truth that required them to change.

This statement is a profound insight into human nature. People often reject the truth not because it is unclear, but because they dislike the person who brings it or because it demands that they abandon their desires. The sincere advisor is often met with hostility, while the deceiver is welcomed. Ṣâliḥ’s final words serve as a timeless warning to all who hear the truth. The believer should cultivate a love for sincere advisors, even when their advice is painful to the ego.

The verse also concludes the story of Thamûd with the same pattern: the prophet is saved, the people are destroyed, and the prophet’s parting speech testifies to the justice of the outcome. This pattern prepares the listener for the next narrative, that of Lot, which will also involve a clear message, rejection, and a devastating punishment.

The surah moves to the story of Lot, a contemporary of Abraham who was sent to the cities of the plain, likely near the Dead Sea. The command “And remember” signals a new narrative, inviting the Prophet and the believers to reflect on this historical account. Lot was not from the people of Sodom; he had migrated there and married into them, but Allah sent him as a prophet to them. The verse says he “scolded” his people, using a word that implies a strong, sharp rebuke. This was not a gentle suggestion but a forceful condemnation of their behaviour. Lot directly confronted the men of his people with a rhetorical question: “Do you commit a shameful deed that no man has ever done before?” The “shameful deed” refers to the act of homosexuality, specifically the practice of men approaching men with lust instead of the natural relationship with women. Lot’s emphasis on “no man has ever done before” highlights the unprecedented depravity of this practice. It was not merely a sin; it was a violation of the natural order that had not been normalised by any previous civilisation to that extent. This does not mean no individual act had ever occurred, but that it had never been openly practised and accepted as a societal norm in the way Sodom did. Lot’s question is a call to their conscience. He is trying to make them see the gravity and the abnormality of what they have become accustomed to. The open, proud commission of this act, without shame, was a sign of a deeply corrupted society.

This verse establishes the primary sin of the people of Lot and sets the stage for their defiant response. For the believers, it serves as a reminder that moral boundaries are set by Allah and are not subject to changing societal trends. The unprecedented nature of the sin also shows that societies can degenerate to levels previously unthinkable, and that the role of the prophet is to call them back to the original, pure nature.
