=161=

After recounting the miraculous provisions in the wilderness, the surah reminds the Israelites of another pivotal moment in their history: the command to enter the sacred city. The instruction was clear and generous. “Enter this city” refers to Jerusalem, the blessed land promised to them after their deliverance from Egypt. They were not being asked to conquer a hostile territory by their own might; they were being invited to take possession of a land Allah had decreed for them. The command to “eat from wherever you please” indicates that the city and its surroundings would be a place of abundance and ease, a stark contrast to the barren desert they had been wandering. The permission was broad, a reward for their patience, with only one spiritual condition attached. They were instructed, “Say, ‘Absolve us,’” which translates the word hittah. This was a specific invocation, a prayer for the removal of their sins. By uttering this word, they would be humbly asking Allah to forgive them and wipe away the transgressions of the past, especially the sin of the golden calf. The entrance itself was to be an act of worship: “and enter the gate with humility.

” The Arabic word sujjadan can mean bowing down, prostrating, or simply entering in a state of humble submission. The physical posture was to mirror the inner state of the heart—grateful, penitent, and aware of Allah’s majesty. If they fulfilled these simple, clear instructions, the reward was immense: “We will forgive your sins, and We will multiply the reward for the good-doers.” Forgiveness of past sins is the greatest need of every human soul, and on top of that, Allah promised to multiply the reward for those who were already inclined to goodness. This was an offer of comprehensive mercy, a fresh start in the holy land. The command tested their obedience in a very practical way. They were not being asked to perform a difficult ritual but to enter a gate with humility and speak a single word of repentance. The richness of the promise reveals Allah’s eagerness to forgive and to bless.

Yet, as the next verse will show, many of them failed this simple test, choosing to mock the command rather than submit. This verse teaches that the conditions for divine forgiveness are not burdensome; they are acts of the heart and tongue. But for the arrogant, even a simple word of humility can be too heavy to pronounce, and they miss out on the multiplied reward that comes from sincere obedience.

=162=

The tragic response to the generous offer of entry into Jerusalem is recorded with brevity and sorrow. Instead of obeying the simple command to say “Hittah” and enter with humility, “the wrongdoers among them changed the words they were commanded to say.” This refers to a specific act of defiance. Rather than uttering the word for absolution, they substituted it with a different word, often reported as hintah, meaning a grain of wheat, or something similar, while making a joke of the matter. They twisted the divine instruction into mockery. They also altered the physical act of entry: some reports say they entered crawling on their backsides instead of bowing in humility, deliberately inverting the gesture of submission. This was not a misunderstanding; it was a wilful distortion born of arrogance and a stubborn refusal to humble themselves before Allah. They had been offered forgiveness and multiplied reward for a simple act of obedience, but their diseased hearts turned the sacred into a jest. The consequence was immediate and severe: “So We sent down a punishment from the heavens upon them for their wrongdoing.” The exact nature of this punishment is described elsewhere as a plague or a sudden torment that afflicted them. A punishment descended from the sky, a direct response to their sin.

The phrase “for their wrongdoing” links the punishment directly to their act of injustice. Wrongdoing (zulm) here encompasses their mockery of a divine command, their ingratitude for the favours they had received, and their arrogance in the face of clear instructions. This incident is a powerful illustration of the principle that the manner in which a command is fulfilled matters. It is not enough to perform the outward action; the spirit of humility and obedience must be present. Mocking a religious act or deliberately distorting it nullifies any good and invites divine wrath.

The verse also serves as a warning to those who treat the rites and commands of Islam lightly. The Israelites lost a promised blessing and brought punishment upon themselves because they could not bring themselves to utter a sincere word of penitence and bow humbly. The lesson is timeless: the doors of mercy are opened with simple acts of submission, but those doors can slam shut when arrogance and mockery take the place of humility.

The next verse will explore another segment of the Israelites who were tested with a different command and similarly failed, showing that the problem was a recurring pattern of rebelliousness.

=163=

The surah now shifts to another episode from Israelite history, one that serves as a particularly vivid lesson about the subtlety of divine tests. Allah instructs the Prophet to “Ask them,” meaning the Jews of his time, about the people of a town by the sea. This town is widely understood to be a settlement on the shore of the Red Sea, possibly Eilat or a similar location, whose inhabitants were commanded to observe the Sabbath. The Sabbath was a day set aside for rest and worship, during which work, including fishing, was strictly forbidden. The test came in a remarkable form: “During the Sabbath, abundant fish would come to them clearly visible, but on other days the fish were never seen.” The fish would surface and gather in large numbers right at the shore, so close and visible that they could almost be caught by hand, but only on the day they were prohibited from fishing. On the other six days, when fishing was lawful, the fish would disappear into the depths. This was a clear and deliberate trial designed by Allah. The temptation was immense: a livelihood, wealth, and delicious food were swimming right before their eyes, separated only by a command to rest.

The phrase “In this way We tested them for their rebelliousness” reveals the purpose. The test was not an arbitrary inconvenience; it was a precise measurement of their obedience. The fish appearing on the Sabbath was an act of Allah that laid bare their inner state. Would they honour the sanctity of the Sabbath, or would their greed and rebelliousness overcome their faith? The fact that the fish would come openly and visibly made the test all the more acute. There was no need to search or labour; the provision was presented on a platter, but on the wrong day.

This is a profound insight into how divine tests often operate. The forbidden thing is often made to appear extremely attractive and easily accessible, while the path of obedience may seem to require more effort or sacrifice. The test is precisely in that gap between immediate desire and enduring command. The verse prepares the listener for the subsequent account of how different groups among them responded, and what fate befell those who tried to circumvent the law through legal trickery. The sea-side town became a timeless example of how not to respond when Allah makes the boundaries of the lawful and unlawful unmistakably clear.

=164=

The Sabbath-breaking town was not monolithic. The people divided into three groups.

The first group were the violators who devised clever tricks to catch the fish on the Sabbath. The second group were the silent ones who kept the Sabbath themselves but did nothing to stop the violators.

The third group were the righteous, those who actively warned the sinners and tried to prevent the transgression. This verse records a fascinating dialogue between the silent ones and the righteous. The silent ones, seeing the persistent failure of the warnings, turned to the preachers and asked a seemingly pragmatic question: “Why do you bother to warn those who will either be destroyed or severely punished by Allah?” Their logic was that since the violators were clearly destined for punishment and had shown no sign of heeding the warnings, the effort was futile. Why exhaust yourself trying to save people who are bent on their own destruction? It was a counsel of despair, tinged with a desire to dissociate from a messy situation. The reply of the righteous is profound and instructive: “Just to be free from your Lord’s blame, and so perhaps they may abstain.” They gave two reasons for their persistence.

The first was to discharge their own responsibility before Allah. They knew that remaining silent in the face of open sin would make them complicit and liable to divine blame. By speaking out, they would have a valid excuse on the Day of Judgment: they had done their duty.

The second reason was hope. They did not entirely write off the sinners.

The word “perhaps” (la‘allahum) indicates that there was still a chance, however slim, that the violators might come to their senses and abstain. The duty of enjoining good and forbidding evil is not conditional on guaranteed success. It is a duty that must be performed for the sake of one’s own exoneration and out of hope for the reform of the wrongdoer. This verse provides timeless guidance for all believers. Even when the world seems completely corrupt and preaching seems to have no effect, the believer must continue to speak the truth. The silence of the good in the face of evil is a sin in itself, regardless of the outcome.

The verse also highlights the mercy inherent in the warning: the preachers did not give up on the sinners, holding onto the possibility that Allah might turn their hearts.

=165=

The story reaches its final, decisive phase. The efforts of the righteous preachers, who had persisted in their warnings despite the discouragement of the silent ones, ultimately went unheeded by the violators. “When they ignored the warning they were given” indicates that the Sabbath-breakers reached a point of no return. The reminders, the admonitions, the calls to fear Allah—all of it was treated with contempt and indifference. They were not merely forgetful; they actively ignored the warning, choosing their schemes and their greed over the clear command. At that moment, the divine judgment was executed in a precise and discriminating manner. “We rescued those who used to warn against evil” means that the preachers, the ones who had fulfilled their duty of speaking out, were saved from the punishment. Their salvation was a direct result of their righteous action. They were not grouped with the wrongdoers. The silent ones, who had neither participated in the sin nor spoken against it, are generally understood to have been included in the punishment, as their silence was a form of complicity, though some interpretations suggest they were also saved but their fate is not explicitly mentioned. The focus is on the certainty of punishment for the defiant. The verse says Allah “overtook the wrongdoers with a dreadful punishment for their rebelliousness. ” The word “dreadful” (ba’īs) means severe, painful, and harsh. The punishment was a direct consequence of their fisq, their rebellious deviation from Allah’s law. Some reports mention that they were transformed into apes and pigs, which aligns with the next verse.

This verse teaches a critical lesson about collective responsibility. It is not enough to be personally righteous while the society around you openly violates the commands of Allah. There must be an active effort to admonish and reform. The ones who speak out are safeguarded; their voice is their shield. The verse is also a source of hope for those who engage in the difficult task of da‘wah, especially when they see little result. The outcome is in Allah’s hands, and their duty is to deliver the message. The rescue of the preachers demonstrates that Allah never abandons those who stand for the truth, even when the society around them is hurtling towards destruction.

=166=

This verse completes the account of the Sabbath-breakers with a chilling divine decree. After they had ignored the warnings and were punished generally, some among them “stubbornly persisted in violation.

” The phrase indicates an even deeper level of defiance. The punishment they had already received did not humble them; instead, they carried on with their sinful ways, treating Allah’s commands with ever-greater insolence. It was this ultimate, brazen persistence that triggered a unique form of punishment. Allah said to them, “Be disgraced apes!” The command “Be” (kūnū) is the divine imperative that brings things into existence instantly. They were physically and perhaps mentally transformed into apes, a lowly state of humiliation.

The word “disgraced” (khāsi’īn) means driven away, despised, and humbled. This transformation was not an evolutionary change but a miraculous, punitive metamorphosis that served as a visible sign of their inner degradation. They had acted like animals, following their base desires without any regard for divine law, so they were made to look like animals outwardly, matching their inner state. Scholars note that these transformed beings did not procreate; they lived for a few days in that state and then perished, serving as a lesson to others. The punishment of transformation (maskh) is one that signifies a complete stripping of human dignity. The Sabbath-breakers had been given the honour of being human beings, addressed by divine law, but they threw that honour away. The verse serves as a stark warning against the habitual, unrepentant violation of Allah’s sacred limits. While the specific physical transformation is a historical event, the spiritual transformation remains a real danger: people who persist in sin can have their hearts transformed, losing the ability to distinguish right from wrong, becoming like animals in their spiritual perception.

The verse also provides a clear answer to those who argue that Allah’s mercy will automatically cover all sins without the need for obedience. Persistent, defiant rebellion can lead to a point where divine mercy is withdrawn and replaced with a humiliating punishment, either in this life or the next. The history of the Sabbath-breakers is preserved as a permanent cautionary tale, urging every generation of believers to take the commands of Allah with utmost seriousness and to never imagine that they can mock the divine law without consequence.

=167=

After recounting the specific punishments that befell the Sabbath-breakers, the surah broadens the scope to a prophetic declaration about the future of the Israelites as a whole, particularly those who persisted in rebellion. Allah instructed the Prophet to recall when his Lord declared “that He would send against them others who would make them suffer terribly until the Day of Judgment.

” This is a prophecy of ongoing subjugation. Throughout history, whenever the Israelites deviated from the covenant and became arrogant, Allah sent against them powerful oppressors who subjected them to humiliation, slaughter, and exile. This included the Assyrians, the Babylonians under Nebuchadnezzar who destroyed the First Temple, the Romans who destroyed the Second Temple and scattered them, and later powers throughout history.

The phrase “until the Day of Judgment” indicates that this state of periodic subjugation and suffering would be a recurring feature of their historical experience as a consequence of their collective misdeeds. It does not mean they are perpetually oppressed at all times, but that they would never achieve lasting, unshakeable dominance without facing severe trials, and that their ultimate deliverance lies only in true faith.

The verse then provides a profound balance between the attributes of Allah: “Indeed, your Lord is swift in punishment, but He is certainly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. ” The swiftness of punishment warns against taking Allah’s patience for granted. When the decree of punishment comes, it is executed without delay.

However, this is immediately coupled with the reminder that He is All-Forgiving and Most Merciful for those who repent and reform. The door of mercy is always open. The tragedy of those punished is not that Allah lacks mercy but that they failed to avail themselves of it. This verse carries a dual lesson. For the oppressors who are used as instruments of punishment, it is a reminder that their power is a tool in Allah’s hand, and they too will face judgment for their own tyranny. For the oppressed who are being punished for their sins, it is a call to return to the covenant, for the same Lord who punishes is also the Lord who forgives. The combination of swift punishment and encompassing mercy encapsulates the entire message of the Quran: fear and hope must always coexist in the heart, driving the believer to obedience and never allowing them to despair.

=168=

This verse continues to detail the divine decree regarding the Israelites after their periods of subjugation and scattering. “We dispersed them through the land in groups” refers to the well-known historical phenomenon of the Jewish diaspora. After the destruction of the Temple and the loss of their political sovereignty, the Israelites were scattered across the globe, forming distinct communities in various regions. This dispersion was both a punishment and a test. It stripped them of a unified territorial power base, and in this state of scattering, their true spiritual quality became manifest.

The verse then notes, “some were righteous, others were less so.” Among these dispersed groups, there was always a spectrum of behaviour. Some held firmly to the Torah, lived upright lives, and were faithful to the covenant. Others were “less so,” meaning they fell short, whether through neglect of religious duties, moral laxity, or outright corruption. This internal diversity is a sign that the divine judgment does not treat every individual in a community identically; each person is judged by their own deeds. Then the verse reveals the purpose behind the fluctuation of their circumstances: “We tested them with prosperity and adversity, so perhaps they would return to the Right Path.” The alternating cycles of good times and hard times, of honour and humiliation, were designed as tests. Prosperity tested their gratitude; adversity tested their patience and drove them to seek Allah. The ultimate goal of all these tests was their return—their repentance and their realignment with the Straight Path.

The word “perhaps” indicates that Allah, in His mercy, gave them every opportunity. The structure of history is not chaotic; it is a carefully calibrated series of examinations meant to awaken the sleepers and guide the lost. This verse offers a universal lens through which to view the events of life. The believer should see both personal and communal ups and downs as tests crafted by a merciful Lord, designed to prompt a return to Him. The tragedy is not in being tested, but in failing to recognise the test and failing to respond with the return that the test was meant to inspire.

=169=

The verse turns to the later generations of Israelites, those who came after the age of the great prophets and inherited the Torah. These successors “inherited the Scripture,” meaning they possessed the physical text and the scholarly tradition, but their relationship with it became distorted. They “indulged in unlawful gains,” referring to bribery, usury, and consuming the wealth of people unjustly, often while using religious justifications. They had developed a sophisticated system of legal loopholes and rationalisations that allowed them to live worldly lives of greed while maintaining an outward appearance of piety. The core of their self-deception is captured in their claim: “We will be forgiven after all.” They believed that their status as the chosen people, or the mere fact that they possessed the Scripture, guaranteed them divine forgiveness, regardless of their actions.

This is a dangerous delusion that severs the link between behaviour and consequence. They turned Allah’s mercy into a license for sin.

The verse then exposes their insincerity: “And if similar gain came their way, they would seize it.” The opportunity for sin would present itself again, and they would repeat the same offence without hesitation. This proves that their claim of being forgiven was not accompanied by any real remorse or intention to reform; it was a hollow slogan to quiet their consciences. Allah then confronts them: “Was a covenant not taken from them in the Scripture that they would not say anything about Allah except the truth? ” The very Torah they claimed to follow strictly commanded them to speak only the truth about Allah, yet they fabricated lies about His forgiveness while persisting in corruption. They were “already well-versed in its teachings,” so ignorance was no excuse.

The verse then redirects their focus to the eternal reality: “But the eternal Home of the Hereafter is far better for those mindful of Allah.” The petty unlawful gains of this world are nothing compared to the everlasting bliss of the next.

The final question, “Will you not then understand?” is a piercing appeal to their intellect. This verse is a powerful warning against the mindset of “cheap grace”—the belief that one can sin recklessly because God will forgive anyway. True hope in Allah’s forgiveness must be accompanied by genuine repentance and a change of conduct, not by a mechanical claim that exploits divine mercy as a cover for persistent wrongdoing.

=170=

After the severe critique of the corrupt successors who inherited the Scripture but used it for worldly gain, the verse provides a vital counterpoint by affirming the fate of the sincere. Not all later generations were corrupt. There remained a group described as “those who firmly abide by the Scripture and establish prayer.

” The phrase “firmly abide” (yumassikūn) conveys a sense of holding on with strength and determination, not letting the Scripture slip from their grasp. They clung to the original teachings, studied them, and implemented them. The specific mention of establishing prayer alongside the Scripture highlights the centrality of worship and the direct connection between possessing the Book and actualising its primary command. Prayer is the visible, repeated act that demonstrates a person’s commitment to the covenant. For these sincere ones, the promise is absolute: “surely We never discount the reward of those acting righteously. ” The Arabic uses a negation to emphasise a positive: “never discount” means Allah will never allow their reward to be lost, diminished, or forgotten.

The term “acting righteously” (muhsinīn) refers to those who perfect their deeds, doing them with excellence and sincerity. This verse provides a balanced picture of the religious community. It teaches that a community is not a monolith; within any group that has been given a scripture, there will be the corrupt and the sincere. The corrupt will be judged for their exploitation of the text, while the sincere will be rewarded for their faithful adherence.

The verse also serves as an encouragement to the believers that in times of widespread corruption and religious hypocrisy, the path of the righteous minority is seen and valued by Allah. Their reward is not contingent on the reformation of the whole society; it is individually secured.

The emphasis on “never discounting” the reward addresses the potential discouragement that can arise when the righteous see the corrupt flourishing while claiming religious legitimacy. The verse assures them that the divine accounting is precise, and not a single good deed done with sincerity will be overlooked. The contrast between the previous verse and this one sets up a choice for the listener: the path of the corrupt who delude themselves with false hope, or the path of the steadfast who secure an unfailing reward.

=171=

The surah now recalls another dramatic event from the early Israelite history, one that underscores the solemnity of the covenant. When the Torah was revealed and its comprehensive laws were presented to the Israelites, they found the obligations heavy and hesitated to accept them. In response, Allah performed a terrifying miracle to instil the gravity of the commitment into their hearts. “And remember when We raised the mountain over them as if it were a cloud. ” Mount Sinai, or a portion of it, was uprooted and held suspended above their heads, casting a shadow like a cloud. The sight was overwhelming. “They thought it would fall on them” captures the absolute terror they experienced. They were convinced that the mountain was about to crush them. This was not an empty threat but a tangible demonstration of the consequences of rejecting the covenant. With the mountain hanging over them, they were in no position to negotiate or delay. At that moment, Allah gave the command: “Hold firmly to that Scripture which We have given you and observe its teachings so perhaps you will become mindful of Allah.” The command “hold firmly” (khudhū) is the same as that given to Moses: take it with strength and determination. The Scripture is not a book to be casually perused; it is a binding contract that requires wholehearted commitment. The instruction to “observe its teachings” means to internalise and act upon its contents, not merely recite them. The ultimate goal is mindfulness of Allah (taqwa), the state of constant God-consciousness that leads to obedience and the avoidance of sin. This dramatic scene is recorded in the Quran to remind later generations, including the Jews of Medina and the Muslims, of the weight of divine revelation. The Torah was given under awe-inspiring circumstances to teach that the law of Allah is not a set of optional guidelines but a life-and-death matter.

For the Muslim ummah, the Quran was revealed gradually, without the mountain being raised, yet its authority is no less binding. The historical example serves as a spiritual wake-up call: if the earlier community required such a display to take the covenant seriously, the later community should voluntarily embrace the scripture with earnestness and fear, without waiting for a mountain to be suspended over them.

The verse also shows that the covenant was accepted, even if under duress, and its obligations became binding, holding them accountable thereafter.

=172=

From the covenant with the Israelites, the Quran expands the perspective to the primordial covenant that encompasses all of humanity. This verse describes an event that occurred before human beings were placed on earth. Allah “brought forth from the loins of the children of Adam their descendants.

” This is understood as an event in the realm of the unseen, in the spiritual pre-existence, where all human souls were extracted from the loins of Adam and assembled before their Lord. The purpose was to establish a fundamental, innate testimony within every human being. Allah asked them directly, “Am I not your Lord?” This question is the most fundamental query of existence: who is the Lord, the Creator, the Sustainer? The response of all the souls was unanimous and clear: “Yes, You are! We testify.” This testimony is embedded in the fitrah, the primordial nature of every human being. Every soul, in its deepest essence, has acknowledged Allah as its Lord.

This is why the Quran often appeals to the fitrah—the natural, inborn recognition of God that exists even in those who have never received a formal scripture. After this testimony, Allah issued a caution: “Now you have no right to say on Judgment Day, ‘We were not aware of this.’” The covenant removes the excuse of ignorance. No human being can claim on the Day of Judgment that they had no idea there was a Lord to be worshipped. The testimony is engraved in their very creation. The messengers and scriptures come to awaken and remind people of this original promise, not to introduce a completely foreign concept. This verse is of immense theological significance. It establishes the universal accountability of all human beings. It explains why belief in God is described as a “remembrance” (dhikr) in the Quran—it is the recalling of a truth already known deep within the soul. It also underscores the profound justice of Allah: no one will be punished without having first testified to His Lordship and then been given the means to honour that testimony through the guidance of prophets. The verse calls every human being to reconnect with their original, uncorrupted nature and to live in accordance with that ancient, sacred “Yes.”

=173=

This verse completes the divine caution that began in the previous one. After establishing that no one can claim ignorance on the Day of Judgment, Allah adds a second prohibited excuse. He forecloses the argument that many disbelievers have historically used to justify their idolatry: the blind following of ancestors. The verse imagines the scenario on Judgment Day and silences the excuse before it can be made. The hypothetical excuse would be: “It was our forefathers who had associated others with Allah in worship and we, as their descendants, followed in their footsteps.” The plea is that the real culprits were the first generation that invented the idolatry, while the later generations merely inherited the tradition and thus should not be held fully accountable. This argument attempts to shift blame to the dead and to present oneself as a passive product of culture.

The verse then poses their rhetorical question: “Will you then destroy us for the falsehood they invented? ” The implication is that it would be unjust for Allah to punish those who merely followed the religion they were born into. Allah preempts this argument by reference to the primordial covenant. The innate testimony to His Lordship was given to every soul directly, not mediated through ancestors.

Therefore, every individual has a personal, direct connection to that truth. The guidance of the messengers is sent to awaken that individual knowledge. When a person chooses to ignore the inner call of the fitrah and the outer call of the prophets, and instead blindly follows the customs of their forefathers, they are making a conscious, blameworthy choice. The excuse of ancestral tradition is therefore invalid. This verse is a powerful critique of cultural religion and inherited dogma. It places the responsibility for faith squarely on the individual. No one can hide behind their family, their society, or their history. Every adult human being of sound mind is obliged to seek the truth and to follow it, regardless of the path their ancestors walked. The verse calls people to break free from the chains of the past when those chains bind them to falsehood, and to honour the original covenant made with their Lord. The rejection of blind following is a cornerstone of the Quranic call to personal responsibility and intellectual honesty.

=174=

After detailing the primordial covenant and rebutting the excuses of ignorance and ancestral tradition, this verse serves as a concluding statement for this section. “This is how We make our signs clear” refers to the detailed and varied ways in which Allah has presented the truth throughout the Quran—through historical narratives, logical arguments, parables, and the deep reflection on human nature itself. The signs are not obscure puzzles; they are clarified, repeated, and illustrated from multiple angles so that they become accessible to any sincere seeker. The goal of this clarity is expressed with the phrase, “so perhaps they will return to the Right Path. ” The word “return” (yarji‘ūn) is deeply significant. It implies that the truth is the original state, the home to which the soul is being called back. The path of misguidance is a deviation from the fitrah, the natural disposition. Returning to the Right Path is not about discovering something entirely new but about coming back to what the soul already knows at its deepest level.

The use of “perhaps” (la‘alla) from the divine perspective does not indicate uncertainty on Allah’s part but rather opens up the possibility for human choice. It expresses hope and invites the listener to be among those who do return. The door is open; the signs are clear; the decision rests with the individual. This verse is a gentle but powerful conclusion to the arguments about the covenant. It reassures the reader that the Quran has been made easy to understand for the purpose of guidance. There is no excuse of confusion or ambiguity.

The verse also reinforces the theme of mercy that runs throughout the surah. Even after recounting the failures of previous nations and the stern warnings of punishment, the ultimate aim is to bring people back, to facilitate their return to the path that leads to His pleasure. The clarity of the signs is an act of mercy, a light shining in the darkness, waiting for the lost to find their way home. The stage is now set for the striking parable of the deviant scholar, which will serve as a powerful illustration of what happens when someone, having had the signs made clear, chooses to abandon them.

=175=

After establishing the clarity of the signs and the covenant, Allah instructs the Prophet to relate a story that serves as a chilling counter-example: a person who was given knowledge of the divine signs but deliberately cast them aside. The verse does not name the individual, which broadens its application, though many commentators identify him as a learned man from the Israelites named Bal’am ibn Ba’ura, who knew the greatest name of God and was a man of answered prayers, but who later used his knowledge for worldly gain and aligned himself with the enemies of Moses. The story, whether referring to a specific individual or a symbolic type, is of a person who was blessed with “Our signs”—divine knowledge, scripture, and spiritual insight. He was not ignorant; he was elevated. But he “abandoned them.

” The word insalakha conveys the image of stripping off a garment. He shed the signs from himself as one sheds clothing, deliberately and completely dissociating from them. This abandonment was not a passive forgetting but an active, willful disengagement. The immediate consequence was that “Satan took hold of him.” When a person casts off the protective covering of divine guidance, they become vulnerable. Satan, who had been waiting for such an opportunity, attached himself to the man and led him astray.

The phrase “took hold of him” implies a persistent, controlling influence. The result was that “he became a deviant” (min al-ghāwīn). From a person of spiritual stature, he fell into the ranks of the misguided, those who are lost and lead others to loss. This verse is a terrifying warning for anyone who has been granted knowledge, whether of the Quran or any sacred teaching. Knowledge is a trust. To abandon it after having received it is a far graver sin than never having known it at all. The descent from a high spiritual station to the depths of misguidance is a real possibility for the unfaithful scholar. The story serves as a mirror, prompting every believer to ask themselves whether they are holding firmly to the signs or allowing them to slip away through neglect, sin, or the pursuit of worldly desires, leaving an opening for Satan to become their companion and guide.

=176=

This verse dissects the inner cause of the deviant scholar’s downfall. “If We had willed, We would have elevated him with Our signs” indicates that the divine signs themselves had the potential to raise him to the highest spiritual ranks. The signs are inherently ennobling. But the condition for this elevation is a receptive, willing heart. This man’s will was directed elsewhere. “But he clung to this life—following his evil desires.

” The Arabic word akhlada means to incline heavily, to become attached and rooted. He clung to the earth, to its pleasures, its status, its temptations. His evil desires (hawā) became his compass, overriding the guidance of the signs.

The verse then paints a graphic and humiliating parable to capture his state. “His example is that of a dog: if you chase it away, it pants, and if you leave it, it still pants.” The panting dog is a universal image of restless, insatiable desire. The dog pants whether it is active or at rest, whether it is attacked or left alone. The panting is its constant, unchanging state. Similarly, the person who abandons divine guidance and follows his lusts is in a perpetual state of spiritual agitation and dissatisfaction. Whether he gets what he wants or not, his soul remains thirsty and exhausted. If he is warned and admonished, he pants with distress; if he is left to his desires, he pants in pursuit of more. There is no peace, no elevation, only the endless cycle of craving.

This is the end result of trading the signs for the world. Allah then states, “This is the example of the people who deny Our signs. ” The parable applies to all those who have access to the truth but reject it in favour of their lower impulses. They are reduced to the level of a panting animal, having lost the dignity that the signs were meant to grant them.

The verse concludes with an instruction to the Prophet: “So narrate to them stories of the past, so perhaps they will reflect.” These stories are not entertainment; they are mirrors. The call to reflection is the path out of the panting state. By meditating on the fate of those who fell, the living can be guided. The verse serves as a profound diagnosis of the human condition when disconnected from revelation: a ceaseless, breathless chase after the world that yields nothing but exhaustion and spiritual death.

=177=

This short verse is a powerful commentary on the parable of the panting dog and the fate of the deviant scholar. “What an evil example” is an exclamation of condemnation. The example of the dog, representing the state of those who had knowledge and cast it aside, is not just pitiful; it is profoundly evil in its implications. The evil lies in the fact that a human being, created in the best of moulds and given the capacity to know Allah, willingly reduces himself to such a state. The degradation is self-inflicted.

The verse then identifies the subject of this evil example: “those who denied Our signs.” The denial is the root cause. It includes the scholar who abandons the signs, the common person who rejects the prophets, and any who turn away after knowledge has come to them. The ultimate verdict is then pronounced: “They only wronged their own souls.” No harm whatsoever reached Allah or His messengers. The entire weight of their denial and misguidance falls squarely on themselves.

The word “only” (kānū) emphasises the exclusivity of the damage. This is a recurring Quranic theme: sin is an act of self-sabotage. The person who denies the signs is not harming the truth; they are destroying their own eternal future. They are depriving their own soul of the peace, elevation, and ultimate bliss it was created for. This verse is a profound reminder of personal responsibility. It strips away any illusion that rebellion against Allah is a form of heroic defiance against an external power. It is, rather, a tragic act of self-mutilation. The human soul is the only victim.

The verse also invites the listener to immediately check their own state. Am I, by any form of denial or neglect, wronging my own soul?

The answer determines one’s entire trajectory. The verse acts as a sobering pause before the next section, which will shift to the broader divine laws of guidance and misguidance, linking individual choices to cosmic principles. It reinforces the idea that the ball is entirely in the human court: Allah’s signs are clear, His mercy is vast, but the decision to honour or wrong the soul is a decision that each person makes for themselves, and they alone will taste the consequences.

=178=

This verse moves from the specific example of the deviant scholar to the overarching divine law that governs all guidance and misguidance. It is a concise, absolute statement of reality. “Whoever Allah guides is truly guided.” The guidance spoken of here is the special guidance of tawfīq—the divine enablement that allows a person to see the truth, accept it, and act upon it. When Allah grants this guidance to an individual, no power in the universe can mislead them. Their guidance is genuine, firm, and ultimately salvific.

The phrase “truly guided” emphasises that this is the only real guidance; all other paths that claim to lead to truth are misguidances. The second half of the verse presents the opposite: “And whoever He leaves to stray, they are the true losers. ” The phrase “leaves to stray” (yudlil) indicates that Allah allows certain people to go astray as a consequence of their own choices and the state of their hearts.

This is not an arbitrary abandonment; it is a just withdrawal of guidance from those who have persistently shown they do not want it. When Allah leaves someone to stray, they are utterly lost, and no guide can bring them back. They become the “true losers. ” The word “losers” (khāsirūn) captures the totality of their loss. They have lost the truth, lost the opportunity, lost their own souls, and will lose the eternal bliss of Paradise. The loss is not partial; it is absolute bankruptcy. This verse serves as a powerful reminder of the supreme value of guidance and the critical importance of seeking it from Allah. The believer is called to a state of perpetual neediness before Allah, constantly praying for guidance, as taught in Surah Al-Fatiha: “Guide us to the Straight Path.” The fear of being left to stray should be ever-present, for without Allah’s guiding hand, the human being is helpless against the whispers of Satan and the temptations of the self.

The verse also provides a lens through which to understand the destinies of the people mentioned in the surah—the prophets, the believers, the disbelievers. Their ends were not random; they were the outworking of this divine principle. The verse places the ultimate credit for guidance with Allah and the ultimate blame for loss on those who, by their own orientation, failed to receive it.

=179=

This verse provides a sobering description of those who are “left to stray” from the previous verse. It begins with a statement of divine decree: “Indeed, We have destined many jinn and humans for Hell.

” The word “destined” (dhara’nā) can also mean created or multiplied. It indicates that a great number of jinn and human beings, by their own freely chosen disobedience, fill the ranks of those who will enter Hell.

This is not an arbitrary pre-selection but a foreknowledge of their deeds and their end, which Allah has declared. The verse then describes the reason for this destiny by focusing on their dysfunctional faculties. “They have hearts they do not understand with.” The heart (fu’ād) is the centre of spiritual comprehension. These people possess physical hearts, but those hearts are dead to the truth. They cannot grasp the reality of Tawheed, the wisdom of revelation, or the consequences of their actions. “Eyes they do not see with” means they have physical sight, but they are blind to the signs of Allah in creation and scripture. They look at the world but see only material phenomena, never the Creator behind them. “Ears they do not hear with” means they hear sounds and words, but the message of the prophets does not penetrate their souls. The verses of the Quran enter their ears as mere noise, devoid of spiritual impact. They have the tools of perception, but they have disabled their higher functions.

The verse then compares them to animals: “They are like cattle. ” Cattle eat, drink, and follow their instincts, but they do not reflect on higher truths. However, the verse immediately corrects this: “In fact, they are even less guided!” Cattle at least follow their natural fitrah and serve their purpose. These humans, however, have the capacity for guidance but actively reject it. They are worse off because they were meant for a higher station.

The verse concludes, “Such people are entirely heedless.” Heedlessness (ghaflah) is their defining trait. They live in a fog of distraction, never waking up to the reality of their existence. This verse is a powerful call to use the faculties of the heart, eyes, and ears for their intended purpose: to know, see, and hear the truth. It warns that neglecting these faculties leads to a state worse than that of animals and a destiny that is the ultimate loss.

=180=

After the sobering description of the heedless who are destined for Hell, the surah now opens a door of light and hope by turning the attention to the beauty and majesty of Allah through His Names. “Allah has the Most Beautiful Names” (al-Asmā’ al-Husnā). These are the divine attributes that describe His perfect essence—such as Ar-Rahmān, Ar-Rahīm, Al-Malik, Al-Quddūs, Al-Salām, Al-Ghafūr, and many others. They are “Most Beautiful” because they represent the absolute perfection of every conceivable attribute of goodness, power, mercy, and majesty. These Names are not mere labels; they are pathways to knowing Allah. The command that follows is twofold: “So call upon Him by them.” This means to invoke Allah in supplication using His Beautiful Names. A believer can ask for mercy by calling upon the name Ar-Rahīm, ask for provision by invoking Ar-Razzāq, seek forgiveness through Al-Ghafūr, and so on. Each Name corresponds to a specific divine attribute and can be used to make a supplication perfectly suited to the need. This makes du‘a a profound, personal conversation with the Lord, using the very descriptions He has revealed.

The second command is: “and keep away from those who abuse His Names.” This refers to people who distort the divine names, deny them, use them for false gods, or describe Allah with attributes that do not befit His majesty. The abuse of the Names (ilhad) includes the practices of the polytheists who derived names for their idols from Allah’s Names, the Jews who used disrespectful wordplay, and any who attribute imperfection to Allah. The command to “keep away” means to distance oneself from such people and their discourse, not to engage in useless arguments that may shake one’s own faith, and certainly not to adopt their deviant practices.

The verse ends with a warning: “They will be punished for what they used to do.” The abuse of the most sacred truths carries a heavy penalty. This verse is a beautiful conclusion to the themes of the surah so far. It reorients the believer towards the positive, active worship of calling upon Allah by His perfect Names, reminding them that the ultimate reality is not the darkness of the heedless but the light of the divine attributes. It invites the soul to find comfort and strength in the Names, and to guard itself from those who would distort that pure connection.

The verse also prepares the way for the subsequent mentions of the guided and the misguided, and the importance of sincere devotion.
