=41=

After declaring that the gates of heaven will not open for the arrogant deniers and that their entry into Paradise is as impossible as a camel passing through a needle’s eye, the surah now paints a chilling picture of what awaits them instead. The verse begins with “Hell will be their bed.” A bed is normally a place of rest, comfort, and sleep. Here, the very thing that is supposed to bring repose becomes an instrument of torment. The mattress they will lie upon is the Fire itself, a surface of scorching heat that offers no comfort, only agony. Then the verse adds, “flames will be their cover.” A cover, or blanket, is meant to wrap a person in warmth and security. For these inhabitants, the cover will be layers of fire enveloping them from above. The combination of a bed of fire and a cover of flames indicates that the punishment will surround them from all directions, leaving no escape. This imagery is consistent with other Quranic descriptions where the Fire has roofs, walls, and floors of flame, completely encasing its inhabitants. The torment is both physical and psychological, for the very items that symbolise safety in this world become sources of unspeakable pain.

The verse concludes, “This is how We reward the wrongdoers. ” The term “wrongdoers” links back to those who wronged themselves through disbelief and arrogance. The reward mentioned here is not an arbitrary infliction; it is the just recompense for their own actions. There is a precise symmetry: they denied the truth and covered it with falsehood, so they are covered with fire. They took their ease in this life without gratitude, so their bed in the next life becomes a bed of torment. This verse is a severe warning, but it also affirms Allah’s justice. He does not wrong anyone; they are receiving what they earned.

For the believer, this stark description serves as a powerful motivator to avoid the deeds that lead to such a fate and to cling to the path of humility and obedience that leads to the opposite destination, which will be described in the very next verse. The contrast could not be sharper.

=42=

From the terrifying description of the Fire, Allah shifts to the beautiful promise for the believers. “As for those who believe and do good” combines the two inseparable elements of salvation: sincere faith and righteous action. Belief in the heart must be accompanied by deeds that validate it. In the midst of this statement, a profound principle of divine mercy is inserted: “We never require of any soul more than what it can afford. ” This parenthetical clause is immensely reassuring. It means that every obligation, every trial, every expectation Allah places on a human being is exactly within their capacity. No one is burdened with a command they cannot possibly fulfil or a test they cannot, with Allah’s help, bear.

This principle runs throughout the Quran and is a cornerstone of Islamic theology. It shows that the religion is not designed to break people but to elevate them within their own abilities. The implication is that those who “believe and do good” are not superhuman beings who achieved the impossible; they are ordinary people who sincerely strived and did what they could. By Allah’s grace, their efforts are accepted.

The verse then states, “it is they who will be the residents of Paradise. They will be there forever.

” The word “residents” indicates that Paradise is their true home, not a temporary lodging. The permanence is emphasised with “forever,” extinguishing any fear of departure or death. This eternal bliss is the reward for a finite life of effort. The contrast with the previous verse is complete: the bed of Hell versus the residence of Paradise, the cover of flames versus the rivers of delight mentioned next, and the eternal nature of both destinations.

The verse also teaches that the standard is fairness and mercy. Allah does not demand perfection beyond human limits, but He does demand sincerity and effort within those limits. This gives immense hope to the struggling believer, reminding them that their Lord knows their weaknesses and does not hold them to an impossible standard. The stage is now set to describe the specific pleasures and honours that await these residents.

=43=

This verse delves into the inner state of the Paradise residents and the words they will utter. “We will remove whatever bitterness they had in their hearts” refers to the complete purification of their emotions. In this world, even believers may experience feelings of resentment, jealousy, or ill-will towards one another due to human weakness. In Paradise, Allah will strip all such negativity from their breasts. They will be pure, loving brothers and sisters, facing one another with nothing but affection. The Prophet said that the people of Paradise will enter with hearts free of any hatred, just as their physical bodies will be perfected.

This is an often-overlooked blessing of the Hereafter: not only will the environment be perfect, but the inner selves will also be healed. The verse then mentions “Rivers will flow under their feet,” signifying that these purified believers will dwell in gardens with flowing water, a symbol of life, refreshment, and constant provision. At that moment, they will spontaneously proclaim, “Praise be to Allah for guiding us to this.” They recognise that their presence in Paradise is not by their own unaided merit; it is the result of divine guidance. They add, “We would have never been guided if Allah had not guided us.

” This is the epitome of humility. Even in the midst of the ultimate reward, they do not become arrogant; they attribute all good to Allah. They further acknowledge the means of that guidance: “The messengers of our Lord had certainly come with the truth.” They bear witness that the prophets conveyed the message faithfully. Then an announcement will resound: “This is Paradise awarded to you for what you used to do. ” This clarifies that while Paradise is ultimately a gift of Allah’s mercy, their deeds are the tangible reason for the award. The combination of divine grace and human effort is perfectly balanced.

The verse teaches that the believers’ joy will be doubled: they enjoy the bliss and they enjoy the knowledge that it is the fulfilment of the promise they lived their whole lives believing in. This profound scene of gratitude and divine honour creates a yearning in the listener to be among those who will utter these words on that Day.

=44=

This verse depicts a dramatic dialogue between the inhabitants of the two final abodes. The residents of Paradise, now settled in their bliss, will call out to the residents of the Fire. There is a veil or barrier between them, as will be detailed, but communication is possible. Their call is not one of gloating or mockery; it is a statement of fact that serves as a final proof. They say, “We have certainly found our Lord’s promise to be true.” This refers to all the promises of reward, victory, and eternal happiness that the messengers conveyed. They have now experienced them firsthand and are testifying to their truth. Then they ask, “Have you too found your Lord’s promise to be true? ” This question is rhetorical in one sense, but it compels the people of the Fire to make their own confession. The promise to the disbelievers was one of punishment and humiliation, and they must now admit that it has been fulfilled. Their reply is a short, undeniable “Yes, we have!” There is no argument, no denial left. The truth has been fully realised. At this point, a caller—understood to be an angel or a voice authorised by Allah—will announce to both groups, “May Allah’s condemnation be upon the wrongdoers.

” This is the formal divine curse, the declaration of expulsion from mercy. The word “condemnation” (la‘nah) signifies being distanced from all good and being subject to everlasting wrath. The public nature of this announcement, heard by both the saved and the damned, underscores the justice of the verdict. The wrongdoers are not hidden away in shame without acknowledgement of why they are there; the reason is proclaimed openly.

This verse also shows that the believers’ faith was not blind; it was based on a promise that has now been empirically verified. The dialogue erases any doubt that might have lingered about the reality of the Hereafter. It brings closure to the long struggle between faith and disbelief.

The verse ends with “upon the wrongdoers,” but the description is incomplete; the next verse qualifies exactly who these wrongdoers are, ensuring that the condemnation is precisely targeted and fully deserved.

=45=

This verse continues the announcement of condemnation by detailing the characteristics of those who deserve it. The first trait is that they “hindered others from Allah’s Way.” This refers to those who actively prevented people from following Islam. They did not simply reject the truth for themselves; they worked to obstruct others. This includes leaders who oppressed believers, spread propaganda, or used their influence to keep people in ignorance.

The second trait is that they “strived to make it appear crooked.” The path of Allah is straight, but these people put enormous effort into making it seem distorted, complicated, or unappealing. They would spread doubts, invent lies, misinterpret scripture, and attack the character of the messengers. Their aim was to make the straight way look crooked so that people would avoid it.

The third trait is that they “disbelieved in the Hereafter.” This denial of the final accountability was often the root cause of their evil. Because they did not believe they would be resurrected and judged, they felt free to commit any crime and block others from the truth. These three qualities paint a picture of the arch-enemies of the prophets: they are not passive; they are active, energetic, and systematic in their opposition. The verse makes it clear that the condemnation is not for minor faults or honest mistakes. It is reserved for those who waged war against divine guidance with full knowledge and intent. For the believers, this verse serves as a warning against any behaviour that resembles these traits. One might not be a leader of disbelief, but even on a small scale, hindering others from good or misrepresenting Islam carries a grave responsibility.

The verse also comforts those who face such obstruction. The Quraysh leaders who mocked the Prophet, blocked access to the Kaaba, and called the Quran sorcery are perfectly described here. Their fate is sealed in this declaration. This clear identification leaves no ambiguity about who the wrongdoers are, and it reinforces the justice of the punishment they have already been described as receiving.

=46=

The surah now introduces the unique scene from which its name is derived. “There will be a barrier between Paradise and Hell.” This barrier, or hijab, is a partition that separates the two realms, preventing the Fire from affecting Paradise and perhaps serving as a viewing point. “On the heights of that barrier will be people. ” The word a‘rāf means elevated places, the crests or ramparts of this divider. The identity of these people has been the subject of careful scholarly discussion. The most widely accepted interpretation is that they are a group of believers whose good and bad deeds are equal, and they are neither admitted immediately to Paradise nor cast into Hell. Instead, they are held in this intermediate place, awaiting Allah’s mercy. Another view is that they are the prophets, or martyrs, or the righteous poor who will testify against the arrogant. Regardless of their exact identity, the verse describes their state with powerful clarity: “who will recognize the residents of both by their appearance.” The faces of the people of Paradise will be bright with joy and beauty, while the faces of the people of Hell will be dark with misery and disgrace. This recognition is immediate and instinctive. They will then “call out to the residents of Paradise, ‘Peace be upon you!’” This greeting is a sign of their hopeful orientation; they are drawn towards the blessed ones and wish them well.

The verse ends with a crucial detail: “They will have not yet entered Paradise, but eagerly hope to.” This hope is not a vague wish; it is a confident expectation based on Allah’s mercy. The scholars explain that their eventual destination is Paradise, and this state of waiting is temporary. This scene provides a profound lesson about divine justice and mercy. Not every believer is of the highest rank, but as long as there is faith and hope, the final outcome is blessed. The people of the heights represent the grace of Allah extended to those who might feel they are on the boundary. It reassures believers that their hope in Allah’s mercy will not be disappointed, even if they must wait a while.

=47=

This verse describes the reaction of the people on the heights when they glance towards the other side. While they had greeted the people of Paradise with peace and hope, their gaze now falls upon the inhabitants of the Fire. The sight is terrifying: the agony, the darkness, the hopelessness. Upon seeing this, they immediately turn to Allah in supplication: “Our Lord! Do not join us with the wrongdoing people.” This prayer is a spontaneous expression of fear and humility. Even though they have not been placed in the Fire, and even though they have hope of Paradise, the mere sight of the punishment fills them with dread. They realise that only Allah’s mercy can shield them from such a fate. This prayer reveals a heart that is fully conscious of its dependence on Allah.

The word “wrongdoing people” ties directly to the earlier verses that identified the condemned. They do not want to be grouped with those who hindered others, made the path seem crooked, and disbelieved.

The verse teaches that no one should feel completely safe from Allah’s plan merely because they belong to a certain group. The correct attitude is constant humility and supplication. Even the righteous, when they witness the reality of the punishment, will plead for protection. This prayer is actually a model for believers in this world: we are commanded to ask Allah to protect us from the Fire, even if we are striving in faith. The people of the heights are in a liminal state; they see both destinations and understand more vividly than anyone the horror of the Fire and the mercy of the prayer. Their cry also serves as a powerful reminder to the listeners on earth: the time to pray “Do not join us with the wrongdoing people” is now, before the sight becomes a reality. This verse reinforces the importance of balancing hope with fear, a theme that will be explicitly commanded later in the surah. The emotional intensity of the scene builds as we anticipate the final verdict for these people.

=48=

The people on the heights are granted a role of witness and rebuke. They will call out to certain individuals in the Fire, specifically the tyrants and leaders of disbelief. The verse states that they will recognise these tyrants by their appearance.

In the Hereafter, faces will bear the marks of their deeds; the arrogant and oppressive will have a distinct, humiliated look. The call they make is a powerful statement: “Your large numbers and arrogance are of no use today!” In the world, these tyrants prided themselves on their multitude of followers, their wealth, their armies, and their social status. They believed that their worldly power was proof of their correctness and that no one could challenge them. They mocked the small, weak group of believers, thinking that numbers determined truth. But on the Day of Judgment, all those worldly measures collapse into nothingness. The large numbers have vanished as a defence; no army can protect them from the Fire. Their arrogance, which made them refuse to submit to the truth, has become the very cause of their destruction.

The phrase “are of no use today” is a devastating undercut. All that they spent their lives accumulating and boasting about is now utterly irrelevant. This rebuke from the heights is a form of justice, as these same tyrants used to scorn the believers. Now, those they considered insignificant are in a position to bear witness against them. The lesson for humanity is clear: do not be deceived by the apparent strength of the unjust. Power, popularity, and numbers are temporary and will not avail anyone if they are built on rebellion against Allah. True success lies in faith and humility, even if accompanied by worldly weakness.

The verse also serves as a comfort to the oppressed believers, assuring them that the day of accountability for the arrogant is inevitable, and on that day, the scales will be reset.

=49=

The people on the heights continue their address to the tyrants, pointing towards a group of humble believers in Paradise. They ask, “Are these the ones you swore would never be shown Allah’s mercy?” In the worldly life, the arrogant leaders would look at the poor, weak, or socially insignificant believers and swear that such people could never be honoured by Allah. They considered the believers beneath them and mocked their faith, claiming that if there were any truth, they, the elites, would have been the first to receive it. Now, the reality is starkly reversed. The very people they despised are basking in divine mercy, while the tyrants are in the Fire.

The question is rhetorical and serves to intensify their remorse. It is a poignant demonstration of how Allah elevates the humble and humbles the proud. Immediately after this scene, the verse turns to a joyful conclusion: “Finally, those on the heights will be told: ‘Enter Paradise! There will be no fear for you, nor will you grieve.’” This is the moment their hope is fulfilled. The divine command grants them entry into the eternal abode of peace.

The phrase “no fear for you, nor will you grieve” is the ultimate promise of complete security and emotional healing. They will neither fear what lies ahead nor regret what has passed. This conclusion confirms that the people of the heights are ultimately among the saved. Their wait was a purification, not a punishment. The verse beautifully ties together the themes of justice and mercy. The arrogant are rebuked and humiliated, the humble are honoured, and those in between are lifted into bliss. For the believers who were mocked and oppressed, this is a source of immense solace. It declares that worldly mockery is temporary and that the final word belongs to Allah, who knows the true worth of hearts. The entire scene from the heights is a microcosm of the Day of Judgment’s overturning of all worldly hierarchies.

=50=

The scene shifts to a direct interaction between the two permanent abodes. The residents of the Fire, in their unbearable thirst and hunger, will lift their voices towards the residents of Paradise. They will plead, “Aid us with some water or any provision Allah has granted you.” This cry is one of desperation. Water symbolises the most basic relief, and any provision represents a morsel of comfort. Their request acknowledges that the people of Paradise have been blessed with abundant provisions from Allah.

However, the response they receive is one of definitive refusal: “Allah has forbidden both to the disbelievers.” This answer is not born of cruelty from the believers but is a statement of the divine decree. The believers in Paradise have no authority to transfer their blessings to the damned; the prohibition comes directly from Allah.

The word “forbidden” indicates that the very nature of Paradise and its blessings is incompatible with those who died in a state of disbelief. The disbelievers had their chance in the world, and they rejected the One who provides. Now the sources of mercy are permanently sealed for them. This verse highlights the irreversible nature of the judgment. There is no further opportunity for charity or intercession that can alter the fate of the inhabitants of Hell. The contrast between the two groups is absolute: one enjoys the endless flow of rivers, the other begs for a drop of water and is denied.

The verse also serves as a reminder of the importance of giving charity and providing water in this life. The Prophet emphasised that giving water is one of the best deeds. The believers who gave water to the thirsty will find it as a protection for themselves on the Day of Thirst. Those who ignored the needy while enjoying Allah’s provisions will find none to give them water when they need it most. The request and its rejection complete the picture of finality and serve as a warning to value the time we have to earn the mercy that will be unattainable then.

=51=

This verse elaborates on the identity of the disbelievers for whom the provisions of Paradise are forbidden. They are described as “those who took this faith as mere amusement and play.” The religion, which is the most serious matter of life, was treated by them as a source of entertainment and mockery. They would jest about the prayers, the Quran, and the concepts of the Hereafter. They never engaged with the truth with the gravity it deserved. Life was a game to them, and they lived for passing pleasures.

The second trait is that they “were deluded by their worldly life.” The glitter of this world deceived them. They became so absorbed in wealth, status, and desires that they forgot death and accountability. They mistook the temporary for the permanent and traded their eternal afterlife for fleeting enjoyment. Then Allah’s decree is pronounced: “Today We will ignore them just as they ignored the coming of this Day of theirs.

” This is the principle of just recompense. Their neglect of the Day of Judgment is met with neglect on that very Day. Allah’s “ignoring” them means they will receive no mercy, no attention that would bring relief, no second glance of compassion. They will be left in their torment, completely forsaken.

The verse adds, “and for rejecting Our revelations,” as the foundational cause. Their mockery and delusion were rooted in their denial of the clear signs.

The verse presents a mirror: they turned away from Allah’s verses, so Allah turns away from them. This concept of mutuality is a recurring theme in the Quran: the way you treat the signs of Allah will determine how you are treated on the Day of Judgment. The verse is a profound warning against taking religion lightly. Faith is not a hobby or a cultural identity to be played with; it is the purpose of existence. Treating it as mere amusement is a sign of a heart that has not truly believed. The believer is called to a serious, committed engagement with the truth, recognising that the world is a deception only to those who let it become one.

=52=

In contrast to the neglect described in the previous verse, Allah now reminds humanity of the immense favour of the Quran. “We have certainly brought them a Book” refers to the revelation sent to the Prophet Muhammad.

The word “brought” implies that it was delivered with care and authority. The Book is described as “explained with knowledge. ” This means that its teachings, laws, parables, and accounts have been laid out clearly, with precision and wisdom. There is no ambiguity left that could excuse ignorance.

The phrase “with knowledge” indicates that the explanation is based on Allah’s perfect knowledge, covering everything needed for guidance. It is not a collection of vague philosophies but a detailed manual for life. Then the verse states that the Book is “a guide and mercy for those who believe.” Guidance and mercy are its core functions.

However, this benefit is conditional; it is only for those who believe. Those who approach the Quran with faith will find it guiding them to the straight path and showering them with mercy. Those who approach it with mockery or arrogance will gain nothing, just as the previous verse described. This verse connects to the earlier themes of the surah: the Book was sent down as a warning and a reminder, and it has been explained so that people have no excuse. The Meccan disbelievers could not claim that they did not understand; the Quran was in their own language, recited by one of their own, with clear arguments and vivid examples. The verse serves as a validation of the Prophet’s mission and a final testament to the clarity of the message. For the believers, it instills gratitude for having this Book and a sense of responsibility to study it, understand it, and live by it. It also renews the call to those who have not yet believed: the Book is here, fully explained, a mercy waiting to be embraced. But that mercy can only be accessed through the door of sincere faith.

=53=

This verse confronts the sceptics with a piercing question. “Do they only await the fulfilment of its warning?” The Book has come with clear explanations, promises, and threats. Are the disbelievers waiting for anything other than the actual occurrence of the Day of Judgment to believe?

This is a rhetorical condemnation of their stubbornness. They demand to see the punishment before they accept the truth, but that is precisely what will do them no good. When that Day arrives and the warning is fulfilled, a dramatic change will occur. “Those who ignored it before will say, ‘The messengers of our Lord certainly came with the truth.’” This admission comes too late. They will finally acknowledge what they spent their lives denying. Then, in their desperation, they will plead: “Are there any intercessors who can plead on our behalf? Or can we be sent back so we may do good, unlike what we used to do?” These two requests reveal their utter helplessness. They look for an intercessor, someone who might avert the punishment, but the intercession of that Day is only for those whom Allah permits and for whom He is pleased. They will find none. They then ask for a second chance, a return to the world to live righteously. But this request will be denied, for the test is over. The verse closes with a statement of their loss: “They will have certainly ruined themselves, and whatever gods they fabricated will fail them.” Their ruin is complete, and the false deities they worshipped have vanished, leaving them alone to face the consequences. The verse is a powerful psychological portrait of regret. It demonstrates that the time to act is now, before the warning is fulfilled. The desire to go back and do good is universal among the damned, but the Quran repeatedly states that such a return is impossible. This verse serves as an urgent call to treat the Quran as the fulfilment of the warning, not something to await, so that the scene of regret is never personally experienced.

=54=

After detailing the events of the Hereafter, the surah returns to the foundational truth of Tawheed, the oneness and supreme authority of Allah. “Indeed your Lord is Allah” is a direct declaration.

The verse then describes His creative power: He created the heavens and the earth in six Days. The word “Days” refers to stages or periods whose length is known only to Allah; they are not necessarily our twenty-four-hour days. This demonstrates deliberation and wisdom in creation. “Then established Himself on the Throne” refers to Allah’s istiwā, an attribute whose exact modality we do not comprehend, but which signifies His supreme dominion and control over the entire universe.

The verse then highlights a sign of His continuous governance: “He makes the day and night overlap in rapid succession.” The constant alternation of light and darkness, one chasing the other, is a visible manifestation of His command. Next, “He created the sun, the moon, and the stars—all subjected by His command.” These celestial bodies are not deities; they are creations, bound by laws He has set, providing light, timekeeping, and navigation for humans. Then comes a comprehensive principle: “The creation and the command belong to Him alone.” Creation is the physical bringing into existence; the command is the divine decree, the law, the moral order. Both are exclusively His. No one else can create or legislate.

This is a direct refutation of those who assign partners to Allah in worship or who follow man-made laws that contradict divine revelation. The verse concludes with praise: “Blessed is Allah—Lord of all worlds. ” The word “Blessed” indicates abundant, continuous good and majesty. This verse encapsulates the entire Islamic worldview. It establishes that the Creator of the vast universe is the same One who sent the Book, and thus He alone deserves worship and obedience.

The sequence is perfect: after warning of the Day of Judgment, Allah grounds that warning in His power to create and to command, proving that He is fully capable of bringing the Day to pass.

=55=

Following the majestic description of Allah’s creative power, the verse instructs believers on how to approach Him. “Call upon your Lord” refers to dua, supplication, and by extension all acts of worship. The manner of calling is specified: “humbly and secretly.” Humility (tadarru‘) means with a sense of utter need, brokenness, and submission. It is the posture of a servant who knows his own poverty and his Lord’s absolute richness. Secretly (khufyah) means in private, avoiding ostentation. The best supplication is often the one whispered in the depths of the night, away from human eyes, purely for Allah. This teaches sincerity and guards against the temptation to show off one’s piety.

The verse then warns: “Surely He does not like the transgressors.” Transgression in supplication can take many forms. It can mean calling upon other than Allah, inventing intermediaries, or making demands that exceed the bounds of servitude. It can also mean supplicating loudly in a showy manner, disturbing others, or asking for things that are sinful or harmful. The fundamental principle is that Allah loves humility and sincerity but detests arrogance and excess. This verse corrects the practices of the polytheists who would call upon their idols boisterously, and it also instructs Muslims in the etiquettes of dua. The combination of humility and secrecy ensures that the heart is focused on Allah alone.

This teaching is timeless: believers are to cultivate a private, intimate connection with their Lord, distinct from the noisy clamour of the world. The fact that this instruction comes right after the verse about creation and command suggests that the Creator who controls the cosmos is also intimately close and approachable. His greatness does not make Him distant; rather, it demands that He be approached with the proper reverence. This verse prepares the ground for the next instruction, which extends the idea of balance into the public sphere.

=56=

This verse seamlessly connects the inner worship of supplication to outward social responsibility. “Do not spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order” is a comprehensive prohibition. The earth was originally in a state of purity and balance, and the prophets and scriptures came to set human life in order, establishing justice, morality, and harmony. Spreading corruption (fasād) includes all forms of moral decay, environmental destruction, oppression, war, economic injustice, and rebellion against divine law. The verse forbids disturbing the spiritual and social equilibrium that Allah has ordained. Then, returning to the theme of worship, it commands, “And call upon Him with hope and fear.

” This is the balance of the believer’s heart: hope for Allah’s mercy and reward, and fear of His punishment and displeasure. Hope alone can lead to complacency, while fear alone can lead to despair. The true servant combines both, like a bird flying with two wings. This internal balance produces the right external conduct that avoids corruption.

The verse concludes, “Indeed, Allah’s mercy is always close to the good-doers.” The “good-doers” (muhsinin) are those who excel in their deeds, doing them with sincerity and excellence, and who strive to benefit others. The promise of closeness of mercy is a powerful incentive. Allah’s mercy is not far away; it is near, surrounding those who embody goodness. The three instructions—avoid corruption, balance hope and fear, and pursue excellence—form a complete framework for a righteous life. The verse is particularly relevant today, when the earth is plagued by corruption in many forms. The restoration of order begins with individuals who fear Allah inwardly and reform their conduct outwardly. The mercy that is close to the good-doers is a source of immense comfort, especially for those striving to do right in a world that often encourages the opposite. It assures them that their efforts are not in vain and that Allah’s aid and forgiveness are ever near.

=57=

From the instruction to avoid corruption and call with hope and fear, the surah turns to a natural sign that perfectly illustrates both mercy and resurrection. “He is the One Who sends the winds ushering in His mercy.” The winds are described as heralds, bringing glad tidings of the coming rain. Rain is termed “mercy” because it revives the earth, provides water, and sustains life. The process is described step by step: the winds carry heavy clouds, laden with moisture. Then, “We drive them to a lifeless land.” The land is dead, dry, and barren, seemingly beyond hope. Then the rain falls upon it, and the result is miraculous: “producing every type of fruit. ” From the dead soil, vibrant, diverse vegetation springs forth. This transformation is a direct parallel to the resurrection of the dead. Just as the barren earth is revived with water, the dead bones will be revived by Allah’s power. The verse explicitly draws this analogy: “Similarly, We will bring the dead to life, so perhaps you will be mindful. ” The entire cycle of wind, cloud, rain, and plant growth is a tangible sign pointing to the reality of the Hereafter. It is a sign that people witness repeatedly, yet often fail to reflect upon. The verse encourages mindfulness, asking people to observe the natural world and draw the spiritual lesson. This argument from nature is central to the Quranic method of proving resurrection. The One who can bring a dead land to life can certainly bring humans back to life.

The verse also beautifully ties together the themes of mercy and power. The rain is a mercy, and the resurrection is a mercy for the believers, though a trial for the deniers.

The phrase “so perhaps you will be mindful” leaves the door open for reflection. It is a gentle call to use the intellect and the senses to recognise the Creator’s ability and to prepare for the inevitable revival after death.

=58=

Continuing the agricultural imagery, this verse presents a parable of spiritual receptivity. “The fertile land produces abundantly by the Will of its Lord.

” This is the good soil that, when rain falls upon it, yields a rich harvest of diverse plants. The key phrase is “by the Will of its Lord. ” Even the fertile land does not produce on its own; its abundance is by the permission and decree of Allah. This represents the believer whose heart is pure and receptive. When the revelation falls upon such a heart, it produces abundant good deeds, noble character, and beneficial knowledge. The believer hears, understands, and acts.

In contrast, “the infertile land hardly produces anything.” This is the hard, barren ground that either does not absorb the water or yields only sparse, useless vegetation. This represents the disbeliever or hypocrite whose heart is hardened. The same rain of revelation falls on both, but the results are vastly different due to the quality of the soil.

The verse then states, “This is how We vary Our lessons to those who are thankful.” The diversity of parables and signs is a mercy for those who have the quality of thankfulness. A thankful heart is one that recognises the blessing and uses it as intended. When such a heart hears the parable, it immediately understands its application and strives to become like the fertile land.

The verse teaches that human responsibility lies in preparing the soil of the heart. The water of the Quran is the same for all, but the yield depends on the receptivity of each individual. The one who is grateful for the guidance will see their good deeds multiply. The one who is ungrateful will find their heart producing nothing of lasting value. This parable is a call to self-examination: when I hear the Quran, does it increase my humility, my charity, my patience? Or does it pass through me without effect? The goal is to be among the thankful, whose hearts are ever fertile, producing fruits that benefit themselves and humanity.

=59=

After the extensive parables and arguments for Tawheed and resurrection, the surah transitions to the stories of the prophets, beginning with Noah. This is the same narrative pattern found in many Meccan surahs, where the stories of earlier messengers are recounted to console the Prophet and warn his people. “Indeed, We sent Noah to his people.” Noah is considered one of the five greatest messengers, and he was the first messenger sent to a polytheistic people after idolatry had spread. His mission was long and arduous, spanning nearly a thousand years. His opening call is identical in essence to the call of every prophet: “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him.

” This is the core of the message. The call begins with an affectionate “O my people,” showing his deep concern and connection. He first commands them to worship Allah alone, then immediately provides the reason: there is no deity worthy of worship except Him.

This is a direct negation of their idols and false gods. Then comes the warning: “I truly fear for you the torment of a tremendous Day. ” Noah was not a tyrant threatening his people with his own punishment; he was a sincere advisor expressing fear for their wellbeing. The “tremendous Day” refers to the Day of Judgment, or it could also refer to the impending flood that would destroy them if they persisted in disbelief. In either case, his fear stemmed from love and mercy, not from malice. This opening salvo is the perfect summary of the prophetic mission: pure Tawheed, accompanied by a warning grounded in genuine care. The story of Noah is particularly poignant because of his patience and the small number of those who believed despite his centuries-long effort. By starting the series of prophetic stories with Noah, the surah emphasises the pattern that will recur: a messenger comes with clear truth, the elite reject him as misguided, the people are warned of destruction, and ultimately the believers are saved while the rejecters perish.

The response of Noah’s people is recorded in this verse, and it mirrors the reaction that every prophet received from the elite of his society. “The chiefs of his people” refers to the leaders, the nobles, the wealthy, and those with social and political influence. It is consistently the chiefs who lead the opposition against prophets, because the message threatens their status, their power, and the system that benefits them. They said, “We surely see that you are clearly misguided. ” The emphasis with “surely” and “clearly” indicates their absolute confidence in their own perspective. They project their misguidance onto Noah, calling him misguided when, in fact, they are the ones lost in idolatry.

This is a classic inversion of truth. The chiefs did not engage with Noah’s arguments; they resorted to ad hominem attacks, accusing him of error to dismiss his message without consideration.

The word “misguided” implies that he has strayed from the path of their ancestors and from rational thinking. In their view, the established tradition of idol worship was the standard of correctness, so anyone who challenged it was by definition astray. This arrogance and reliance on ancestral precedent are recurring themes in the Quran. The verse highlights the loneliness of the prophet in the face of widespread rejection. Noah, a single man, stood against the entrenched beliefs of an entire society, and the leaders used their influence to turn the masses against him.

The verse also serves as a direct parallel to the Prophet Muhammad’s experience in Mecca. The Quraysh chiefs similarly called him a madman, a poet, a sorcerer, or misguided. By recounting this, Allah reassures the Prophet that he is not the first to be so accused, and that the outcome will be the same as it was for Noah: the truth will ultimately prevail, and the deniers will face their doom. The stage is set for Noah’s patient and logical reply, which will be detailed in the verses to come.
