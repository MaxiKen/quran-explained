=101=

After the sweeping historical reflections and the warning that the present inheritors of the earth are not immune from divine punishment, the surah now turns directly to the Prophet to reassure him and to summarise the lesson. “We have narrated to you some of the stories of those societies” refers to the accounts of Noah, ‘Âd, Thamûd, Lot, and Shu’aib that have just been recounted.

The word “some” indicates that these are selected examples from a much larger tapestry of human history. The Prophet is being told that he has been given enough to warn his people; he does not need to know every detail of every destroyed nation. The essential pattern is clear.

The verse then identifies the consistent factor in their destruction: “Surely, their messengers came to them with clear proofs, but still they would not believe in what they had already denied.” The messengers did not come empty-handed; they came with bayyināt, clear signs, miracles, and rational arguments that left no reasonable doubt about the truth of their message.

Yet the people, confronted with these clear proofs, did not merely hesitate or ask for time; they actively disbelieved in something they had already denied. This implies that their denial was a settled disposition, a stubborn stance that preceded even the arrival of the proofs. When the proofs came, they merely reinforced their rejection rather than breaking it. This psychological observation is crucial: disbelief is often not an intellectual conclusion reached after examining evidence; it is a prior state of the heart that interprets all evidence through a filter of rejection. Then the verse declares the divine law that results from such persistent denial: “This is how Allah seals the hearts of the disbelievers. ” The sealing of the heart is the spiritual consequence of chronic rejection. When a person repeatedly turns away from the truth, the heart gradually loses its ability to perceive and accept it. This sealing is not an arbitrary act of cruelty; it is the natural outcome of the person’s own choices, which Allah then formalises as a punishment. For the Prophet Muhammad, this verse was a comfort: he should not blame himself for the Quraysh’s rejection, for they were following the same pattern as the people of old.

For the believer, it is a stark warning to cherish and protect the softness of the heart by responding to the truth immediately whenever it appears.

=102=

This verse delves deeper into the root cause of the destruction of those nations by focusing on two fundamental moral failures. “We did not find most of them true to their covenant.” The covenant here refers to the primordial covenant mentioned elsewhere in the Quran, where all human souls testified that Allah is their Lord, as well as the covenant of faith that each people implicitly makes by recognising truth when it comes. It may also refer specifically to the covenant that a people make with a prophet once they accept his message. To be “true to their covenant” means to fulfil the obligations of loyalty, worship, and obedience that arise from knowing the truth. The verse declares that when Allah examined the overall moral record of those nations, most of them were covenant-breakers. They had pledged allegiance to the truth, perhaps in moments of crisis, but reneged when ease returned.

This is a devastating verdict. It means their destruction was not merely a matter of intellectual disbelief but of moral perfidy.

The second failure is stated even more strongly: “Rather, We found most of them truly rebellious.” The word “rebellious” (fāsiqīn) implies a going out of the bounds, a deliberate and habitual transgression. They were not simply mistaken or ignorant; they were actively defiant, choosing to live outside the limits set by Allah. This dual diagnosis—covenant-breaking and rebelliousness—forms a comprehensive picture of a corrupt heart. Covenant-breaking signifies disloyalty and untrustworthiness; rebelliousness signifies active lawlessness and moral chaos. The combination is lethal to both individual souls and societies.

This verse also serves as a mirror for the Muslim community. It prompts the question: are we keeping our covenant with Allah, which we renew every day in prayer? Are we living within His bounds, or have we normalised transgression?

The mention that “most” were like this is a realistic acknowledgment that the majority often follow the path of corruption, while the righteous are a minority. This should not discourage the believer but rather reinforce the determination to be among the few who remain faithful. The verse transitions the narrative from the earlier stories to the detailed story of Moses, which will now be recounted, showing that even a mighty prophet like Moses had to deal with a people who frequently broke their covenants and rebelled.

=103=

Having summarised the pattern of previous nations, the surah now embarks on the most detailed prophetic narrative in this chapter, the story of Moses and Pharaoh. “Then after them” indicates a chronological sequence. After the eras of Noah, Hûd, Sâlih, Lot, and Shu’aib, Allah sent Moses. This places Moses within the same prophetic continuum. “We sent Moses with Our signs” refers to the nine clear signs or miracles that Moses was given—his staff turning into a serpent, his hand shining white, and the plagues that would later afflict Egypt such as the flood, locusts, lice, frogs, and blood. These signs were not mere magic tricks; they were divine proofs intended to convince Pharaoh and his people of the truth of Moses’ message and to compel the release of the Children of Israel. The targets of the message are specified: “to Pharaoh and his chiefs.” Pharaoh was the tyrannical ruler of Egypt who had declared himself a god. His chiefs, or mala’, were the elite class—the nobles, advisors, priests, and military leaders who supported his regime and benefited from the oppression of the Israelites. The verse states that “they wrongfully rejected them.” The rejection was wrongful, meaning it was not based on any genuine doubt or lack of clarity. The signs were manifest, yet they responded with denial and arrogance. This echoes the pattern of all earlier nations.

The verse then issues a powerful invitation to reflect: “See what was the end of the corruptors!” The addressee is the Prophet, the believers, and by extension every human being who hears the Quran. The end of Pharaoh and his hosts—drowning in the sea while pursuing Moses—is well-known and serves as the ultimate cautionary tale.

The word “corruptors” (mufsidīn) identifies their core crime: they spread corruption through tyranny, disbelief, and the enslavement of a whole people. This verse sets the stage for the epic confrontation that will unfold in the following verses, reminding the listener that the outcome is already known and that it conforms perfectly to the divine law of history.

=104=

This verse captures the opening declaration of Moses before Pharaoh, and it mirrors the opening words of every prophet in this surah. The address “O Pharaoh” is direct and without any title of honour, because Moses is not there to flatter the king but to deliver the word of the Almighty. He immediately declares, “I am truly a messenger from the Lord of all worlds.

” The emphasis with “truly” (innī) indicates absolute certainty and seriousness. He is not a rebel, a magician, or a political agitator; he is an official envoy of the Supreme Authority.

The phrase “Lord of all worlds” is monumental. Pharaoh claimed to be the lord of Egypt, and in his megalomania, he even claimed to be the highest god. By invoking the Lord of all worlds, Moses instantly demotes Pharaoh. He is telling the tyrant that there exists a Lord to whom even Pharaoh is a mere slave, a Lord who governs not just Egypt but all the worlds—the world of humans, jinn, angels, animals, and the entire cosmos. This assertion challenges the very foundation of Pharaoh’s power and religion. Moses is not asking for a share of authority; he is declaring the complete sovereignty of Allah and his own role as His messenger.

This is the prophetic model of speaking truth to power. There is no apology, no hesitation, no negotiation on the core principle. The message must be delivered clearly and boldly, regardless of the worldly power of the listener. For the Prophet Muhammad, who was facing the Quraysh chiefs, this was a direct lesson in how to present the call: with uncompromising clarity on the absolute Lordship of Allah. For all believers, this verse teaches that when conveying the message, we must ground it in Tawheed—the recognition that Allah is the Lord of everyone, including the most powerful people on earth. No human authority can override the authority of the Creator.

=105=

Moses continues his address by establishing his own credibility and the nature of his mission. He declares, “obliged to say nothing about Allah except the truth.

” The word “obliged” (harīs) conveys a sense of duty and a heavy responsibility. Moses is not a free agent; he is under a divine mandate that compels him to speak only the truth about Allah.

This is a declaration of his integrity. A true prophet cannot lie or distort the message. This sets him apart from the sycophants and magicians who surrounded Pharaoh and told him what he wanted to hear. Moses is asserting that his message is not personal opinion but divine revelation, and he cannot alter it to please the king. He then states, “Indeed, I have come to you with clear proof from your Lord.” The “clear proof” (bayyinah) refers to the tangible signs and miracles that Allah had bestowed upon him, such as the staff and the shining hand, which he would soon demonstrate. By saying “from your Lord,” Moses reminds Pharaoh again that even this tyrant has a Lord, and that the proof comes from that very Lord, not from a foreign deity.

Finally, he delivers the specific demand: “so let the children of Israel go with me. ” The Children of Israel were the descendants of Jacob, who had settled in Egypt generations ago and were now enslaved and oppressed by the Pharaohs. The demand for their release is not just a political request for freedom; it is a religious command. The Israelites needed to be freed so they could worship Allah properly, away from the corrupting environment of Egyptian idolatry and servitude. This demand strikes at the economic and military foundation of Pharaoh’s state, because the Israelites were a massive slave labour force.

Thus, the request is a direct challenge to the entire oppressive system. This verse teaches that the call to Allah is inseparable from the call to justice and the liberation of the oppressed. Faith demands action against tyranny, and the messenger is the voice that must courageously articulate that demand.

=106=

Pharaoh’s response to Moses’ bold declaration is not an immediate violent outburst but a calculated challenge. He does not engage with the theological claims about the Lord of all worlds; instead, he latches onto the mention of a “clear proof.” He asks, “If you have come with a sign, then bring it if what you say is true.” This question is a trap and a test. On the surface, it appears reasonable: if you claim to be a messenger with divine backing, show us the evidence. But in reality, Pharaoh is setting the stage for a confrontation that he believes he will win. Egypt was the land of magic, and Pharaoh was surrounded by the most skilled magicians of the age. He assumed that whatever “sign” Moses could produce would be a form of magic that his own experts could easily counter. By challenging Moses to produce the sign, Pharaoh thinks he is luring the prophet into a public humiliation that will discredit him and reaffirm Pharaoh’s own status as the supreme authority. The condition “if what you say is true” carries a tone of scepticism and arrogance. It is the same phrase used by earlier disbelieving nations when they dared their prophets to bring on the punishment. The demand for a sign, when made with a sincere heart, is legitimate; but when made from arrogance and a desire to debunk, it becomes a means of sealing one’s own fate. For Moses, this challenge is exactly what he expected. He had been given the signs precisely for this moment. The stage is now set for the dramatic demonstration of divine power. This verse highlights the psychology of the tyrant: when confronted with truth, he does not bend; he escalates. He seeks to turn the situation into a contest of power because that is the language he understands. But the contest will not go as he anticipates, because the power behind Moses is not of this world.

=107=

In direct response to Pharaoh’s challenge, Moses performs the first of his two immediate signs. “So Moses threw down his staff.” The staff was an ordinary object, the tool of a shepherd, which Moses used for walking, leaning, and herding. It was not a magical artefact; it was the most mundane of items. This ordinariness is part of the miracle: it proves that the power lies not in the object but in the One who gives the command. When Moses cast it to the ground, the transformation was instantaneous and absolute: “behold!—it became a real snake.

” The Arabic word used for snake here is thu‘bān, which denotes a large, swift, and terrifying serpent, not a small harmless reptile. Other verses describe it as appearing to run and devour things. The snake was not a mere illusion or a trick of the eye; it was a real, living creature that moved and inspired fear. This was the first of the two signs that Allah had given Moses on Mount Sinai to convince him of his own prophethood, and now it was being displayed as a proof for the most arrogant tyrant on earth. The sudden transformation of a lifeless stick into a living, powerful creature is a sign of the Creator’s power over life and matter. It challenges the very notion that nature operates independently of Allah’s will. For Pharaoh, who claimed to be a god, this was a direct refutation of his divinity, because only the true God can give life to inanimate matter. This miracle also foreshadows the eventual swallowing up of the magicians’ illusions, as will be described later. The verse is a powerful reminder that Allah can turn any weak, ordinary means into an instrument of overwhelming victory. The believer should never underestimate what Allah can do with the simple tools placed in their hands, provided they are used in obedience to Him.

=108=

Moses now demonstrates the second sign. “Then he drew his hand out of his collar.” Moses would insert his hand into the opening of his garment at the chest or under his arm, and when he withdrew it, a stunning transformation had occurred. The hand “was shining white for all to see. ” The whiteness was not a symptom of disease, as might be feared; it was a radiant, luminous whiteness, a brilliant light that shone without causing harm. Some reports describe it as being like the rays of the sun, illuminating the surroundings. This sign was a direct contrast to the serpent, and together they demonstrated two different aspects of divine power. The serpent inspired terror and awe; the shining hand inspired wonder and beauty. Both were beyond human ability. This sign had a personal dimension for Moses as well. When he was first given this miracle on the mountain, his hand had turned white as a reassurance for him personally. Now, it served as a public proof. The fact that the hand would return to its normal state when he placed it back showed that this was a controlled, deliberate sign from Allah, not a random affliction. For Pharaoh and his court, these two signs were undeniable. They had never seen anything like them.

Yet, as the next verse will show, the response of the chiefs was not to believe but to spin the narrative, calling Moses a skilled magician. This illustrates a painful truth about the human heart: when it is determined to reject the truth, it will find a way to explain away even the clearest evidence. The shining hand also symbolises the light of prophecy and divine guidance that Moses carried, a light that Pharaoh’s darkness could not extinguish.

For believers, this sign is a reminder that Allah equips His messengers with proofs suitable for their time, and that His signs are both powerful and beautiful.

=109=

After witnessing the two stunning signs, the immediate reaction of Pharaoh’s inner circle is recorded. The “chiefs” were the elite of Egypt, the council of nobles and advisors who surrounded the throne. They could not deny what their eyes had seen—the staff had become a serpent, and Moses’ hand had shone with light. But instead of acknowledging the signs as proof of Moses’ prophethood, they interpreted them through the lens of their own cultural and religious framework. Egypt was saturated with magic. The magicians were a powerful, respected class, and their arts were highly developed. So the chiefs, eager to protect the status quo and dismiss the threat, said, “He is indeed a skilled magician.

” The word “skilled” indicates that they considered Moses not a novice but a master of the magical arts. By labelling him a magician, they could neutralise the theological implications of the signs. If Moses was just a very good magician, then his challenge to Pharaoh’s authority was not a divine mission but a professional rivalry. There was no need to rethink their gods or their politics. This tactic of labelling a prophet as a magician or a sorcerer is a recurring theme in the Quran. It is a way for the powerful to reassure themselves and the masses that the status quo is not actually under divine threat. This reaction also reveals the arrogance of the elite. They were not willing to entertain the possibility that a shepherd from the oppressed Israelite minority could be a genuine messenger of the Creator. Their class prejudice and national pride formed an impenetrable barrier. The verse exposes how intellectual dishonesty works: when evidence contradicts one’s worldview, rather than revising the worldview, one reinterprets the evidence to fit the existing framework. The believers are taught to recognise this pattern and not be shaken when clear truth is dismissed with clever labels.

=110=

The chiefs did not stop at calling Moses a magician; they added a political dimension to their accusation to inflame Pharaoh’s fear and anger. “Who seeks to drive you from your land” is a masterful piece of manipulation. They framed Moses’ religious message as a political conspiracy aimed at overthrowing Pharaoh and expelling the ruling class from their homeland. This was a direct appeal to Pharaoh’s deepest insecurity: the loss of power and territory. By saying “your land,” they reminded Pharaoh that Egypt was his kingdom, his possession, and that Moses was a threat not just to religion but to national sovereignty.

This is the same tactic used against every reformer: paint him as a foreign agent, a destabiliser, a threat to national security. It distracts the masses from the actual message and unites them against a common enemy. The chiefs’ accusation was not entirely baseless in one sense: Moses was demanding the release of the Israelites, which would indeed disrupt the economic system. But Moses was not seeking the throne for himself; he was fulfilling a divine command. The chiefs conveniently ignored the possibility that Moses was a true prophet and focused solely on the perceived worldly threat. After hearing this counsel, Pharaoh turned to his advisors and asked, “What do you propose?” This question shows that Pharaoh, despite his absolute power, relied on the advice of his council. He was not a lone madman; he was supported by a system of enablers who reinforced his delusions.

This verse reveals the mechanics of tyranny: the ruler is propped up by an elite class that benefits from the oppression and actively works to suppress the truth. For believers, it is a reminder that the opposition they face is often not just personal animosity but a systemic defence of a corrupt order. The response to such opposition, as Moses will demonstrate, is not to retreat but to trust in Allah and continue presenting the truth with the proofs He has provided.

=111=

The chiefs now present their strategic plan to Pharaoh. They had identified Moses as a threat, and they proposed a course of action designed to counter him on the same terms he was perceived to be operating on—magic. “Let him and his brother wait” means to delay the confrontation. They advised Pharaoh not to act hastily against Moses and Aaron, who had come together as the prophetic duo.

The phrase “let him wait” suggests a policy of strategic patience. They wanted time to gather their own resources and stack the deck in their favour. By delaying, they also hoped to create a sense of anticipation among the public, turning the event into a grand spectacle where the state’s power could be fully displayed.

The second part of the plan was to “send mobilizers to all cities. ” The “mobilizers” were officials or messengers who would be dispatched throughout the land to recruit the very best magicians Egypt had to offer. This was a nationwide dragnet. The goal was to assemble a team of magicians so skilled that they would easily overpower whatever Moses could produce, thereby humiliating him publicly and reinforcing Pharaoh’s image as the protector of the established order. This plan reveals a mixture of fear and confidence. They feared Moses enough to prepare extensively, but they were confident that Egypt’s magic was supreme. They did not consider the possibility that Moses’ power came from a source that could not be matched by any human art. This verse illustrates how worldly power prepares to confront truth: with organisation, publicity, and a show of force. But it also shows a critical miscalculation: they thought the conflict was one of human skill versus human skill, whereas in reality it was human arrogance versus divine omnipotence.

For the believer, this verse offers comfort: the enemy may appear to be making elaborate preparations, but all their planning is utterly futile against the will of Allah.

=112=

This verse completes the plan of Pharaoh’s chiefs. The mobilizers were to scour “all cities” to gather “every clever magician.

” The word “clever” translates an Arabic term that implies expertise, cunning, and mastery of the art. The magicians of Egypt were not mere street performers; they were an elite class of learned men who had studied the occult sciences, illusion, and trickery to the highest degree. They were the scientists of their day, respected and feared. The chiefs wanted the absolute best, the most skilled practitioners in the entire kingdom, so that Pharaoh’s victory would be total and undeniable. The fact that they needed to gather them from all cities indicates that the state was taking this challenge with the utmost seriousness. This was to be a national event, a showdown between the old order and the new challenger. The chiefs’ confidence rested on the assumption that magic, no matter how impressive, had limits and that they possessed the peak of that field. They could not conceive that Moses’ signs were from a category beyond magic—the category of miracle, which operates on a different plane altogether. The magicians themselves would later recognise this distinction when they saw the serpent swallow their illusions and immediately fell into prostration, declaring belief in the Lord of Moses and Aaron. This verse highlights a profound truth: human expertise, no matter how advanced, cannot replicate divine creation or withstand divine power. The best of human skill is still within the realm of illusion and manipulation, whereas divine signs are real, transformative acts of the Creator.

For believers, the lesson is to never be intimidated by the impressive expertise of those who oppose the truth. The most sophisticated arguments, technologies, or systems that man can build are no match for the simple but real power of Allah.

=113=

The plan was executed, and the magicians were assembled and brought before Pharaoh. This verse gives us a fascinating insight into the character of the magicians and the nature of their relationship with the state. They were not ideologically committed to Pharaoh’s godhood; they were professionals seeking worldly compensation. Their first question to Pharaoh was, “Shall we receive a suitable reward if we prevail? ” They were willing to use their skills in the service of the king, but they wanted to ensure they would be paid handsomely for their success. This request reveals their confidence in their own abilities. They assumed victory was likely, and they wanted to negotiate their prize in advance. They were mercenaries in a spiritual battle, not true believers in the cause they were about to defend.

This is in stark contrast to Moses, who sought no reward from Pharaoh or his people and whose only motivation was obedience to his Lord. The magicians’ request for a reward also highlights the worldly incentives that often prop up corrupt regimes. The elite and their skilled servants are bound together by mutual material interest. Pharaoh relied on the magicians to defend his ideology, and the magicians relied on Pharaoh for wealth and status. This symbiotic relationship of falsehood is a recurring pattern.

However, the request also sets the stage for the dramatic irony that will follow. These magicians, who came seeking gold and favour from a tyrant, will soon be the very ones who fall in prostration to the Lord of all worlds, declaring their faith with a sincerity that no amount of money could buy. Their encounter with the real miracle of Moses will shatter their worldly ambitions and open their eyes to a truth far greater than any reward Pharaoh could offer.

For the believer, this verse teaches that worldly incentives can never produce genuine faith. True guidance, when it comes, can transform even the most materialistic heart into a heart that willingly sacrifices everything for the truth.

=114=

Pharaoh’s response to the magicians’ request is immediate and generous. He confirms the reward: “Yes.” The simple affirmative indicates that payment was never in question; the state’s resources were at their disposal. But Pharaoh goes further and offers a prize even more valuable than money: “and you will certainly be among those closest to me. ” This was a promise of high status, proximity to the throne, and inclusion in the inner circle of power. In the court of a tyrant, being among those closest to the king was the ultimate worldly ambition. It meant influence, honour, security, and access. Pharaoh was essentially offering the magicians a permanent upgrade in their social standing, from hired experts to royal confidants. This offer reveals how desperately Pharaoh wanted to win this confrontation. He was willing to elevate these magicians to the highest echelons of his regime if they could discredit Moses. It also shows Pharaoh’s deep-seated need to be surrounded by those who validated his claims to divinity. By making the magicians his close ones, he would be co-opting their victory as his own.

This promise of closeness is a direct inversion of the closeness that the believers seek. The magicians were offered closeness to a mortal tyrant, while Moses was offering closeness to the Lord of all worlds. The irony is that the magicians, once they tasted the truth, would reject Pharaoh’s promised closeness in favour of Allah’s mercy, even at the cost of their lives. This verse highlights the nature of worldly temptation: power offers proximity, privilege, and recognition, but all of it is temporary and built on a foundation of falsehood. The believer must constantly weigh the offers of the world against the promise of Allah, remembering that the closeness that truly matters is the one that leads to eternal salvation, not the fleeting favour of a human ruler.

=115=

The day of the great confrontation has arrived. The magicians, having secured their reward and their place near Pharaoh, are ready for the contest. They address Moses directly: “O Moses! Will you cast, or shall we be the first to cast? ” This question is delivered with a tone of confidence and perhaps even politeness, reflecting the professional etiquette of the magicians. They give Moses the choice of who goes first, which could be seen as a gesture of sportsmanship or a subtle way of showing that they are not afraid and are fully in control. In typical contests, the one who goes first might have an advantage in setting the tone, but the magicians are so sure of their skills that they do not mind giving Moses the option.

The question also adds to the dramatic tension. The crowd is watching. Pharaoh is on his throne. The magicians have their ropes and staffs. Moses is standing with his brother. The atmosphere is charged. The magicians’ courteous query contrasts sharply with the arrogance of Pharaoh, showing that they, as technicians, have a certain respect for their craft and their opponent. But they have no idea of the true nature of the force they are about to confront. This moment marks the final point before the unveiling of truth.

For the believer, this verse illustrates that when the truth and falsehood stand face to face, falsehood often appears confident, well-prepared, and even gracious. But appearances are deceptive. The real power is with the one who relies on Allah. The magicians’ question also serves a narrative purpose: it shows that the sequence of events was not dictated by Moses’ strategy but by the unfolding of Allah’s plan. Moses’ response in the next verse will demonstrate his complete trust in that plan.

=116=

Moses’ reply to the magicians’ question is short and decisive: “You first.” This decision was not born of fear or hesitation; it was a strategic choice that allowed the full extent of the enemy’s power to be displayed before the divine truth overwhelmed it. It ensured that there would be no room for excuses after the miracle. The magicians then cast their ropes and staffs. The verse describes the effect of their magic with three powerful expressions: “they deceived the eyes of the people.” This refers to the visual illusion they created. Through a form of trickery, perhaps by coating their ropes with some substance that made them move in the sunlight, or by utilising jinn, as some reports suggest, their sticks and ropes appeared to slither like live serpents.

The second description is “stunned them. ” The sheer scale and impressiveness of the illusion filled the onlookers with awe and fear. The entire field seemed to be crawling with snakes. Even Moses, as a human being, felt a natural sense of alarm, though Allah reassured him.

The third description is “made a great display of magic. ” This was no amateur performance; it was a spectacular, top-tier exhibition of the most advanced magical arts of the ancient world. The magicians had more than proven their skill. To the average observer, it seemed clear that Pharaoh’s side had won. This verse sets up the moment of truth. The magicians had thrown everything they had. They had deceived, stunned, and dazzled. But as the next verse will show, what they had was mere illusion, and illusion, no matter how impressive, cannot stand against reality.

The verse teaches that evil can be extremely impressive and convincing. It can deceive the masses and create the illusion of power. But the believer must remember that behind the illusion lies emptiness, and that the truth, when it comes, will swallow it whole.

=117=

At the climax of the magicians’ dazzling display, when all eyes were fixed on the writhing mass of serpents, Allah intervened. “Then We inspired Moses” indicates a direct, sudden divine communication.

The word “inspired” (awhaynā) suggests a swift, intimate command delivered to Moses’ heart at the precise moment needed. The instruction was simple: “Throw down your staff. ” There was no elaborate ritual, no secret incantation. Just a shepherd’s staff, thrown to the ground in obedience. And the result was instantaneous and catastrophic for the forces of falsehood: “behold!—it devoured the objects of their illusion!” The staff transformed into the same massive serpent as before, but this time its purpose was not merely to display itself but to actively consume. It swallowed every single one of the magicians’ ropes and staffs, one after another, until nothing remained of their grand display.

The word “devoured” (talaqqafu) conveys a rapid, hungry consumption. The serpent moved swiftly, taking in the illusions as if they were mere snacks. This was the decisive blow. The magicians’ art had produced illusions, but Moses’ staff had produced a real, living creature with the power to consume physical objects. The illusion was devoured by the reality. The magicians, who knew the secrets of their craft better than anyone, immediately understood that what they had witnessed was not magic. Magic can be countered by greater magic, but magic cannot produce a real creature that consumes physical matter. This was something from a different realm entirely: a miracle from the Creator. The verse powerfully demonstrates that the truth obliterates falsehood. No matter how impressive or overwhelming falsehood may seem, it is ultimately hollow. When the command of Allah comes, the truth stands clear, and the falsehood vanishes.

For the believer facing the seemingly overwhelming propaganda and power of the world, this verse is a timeless reassurance: the staff of truth will always swallow the snakes of illusion.

=118=

This verse is a concise, definitive summary of the outcome of the confrontation. “So the truth prevailed” (wawaqa‘a al-haqq).

The word “prevailed” carries the meaning of being established, standing firm, and becoming manifest. The truth that Moses brought—the oneness of Allah, the reality of prophethood, the power of the Creator over all creation—was proven beyond any doubt. The evidence was not merely intellectual; it was witnessed by the eyes of thousands. The serpent had physically consumed the magicians’ apparatus, leaving no trace. This was a victory that could not be spun or denied by anyone who had seen it.

The second part of the verse states, “and their illusions failed” (wabatala mā kānū ya‘malūn). The word “failed” translates a verb that means to be rendered futile, to be shown as false, to collapse into nothingness. All the elaborate work of the magicians, their preparation, their art, their confidence—all of it vanished in moments. The illusion was not just defeated; it was exposed for what it was: a lie. The magicians themselves would soon acknowledge this openly. This verse captures the fundamental Quranic promise: truth and falsehood are not equal, and their confrontation always ends with the triumph of truth. The triumph may not always be immediate in human perception, but the divine law is that truth has inherent strength and falsehood has inherent weakness.

The verse also served as a direct reassurance to the Prophet Muhammad. The Quraysh were putting on their own “great display” of opposition—with their poetry, their propaganda, their economic boycott, and their mockery. This verse told him that just as Moses’ truth prevailed over the magic of Pharaoh, so too would the Quran prevail over the falsehood of Mecca.

For believers in every age, this verse provides an unshakeable optimism rooted in faith. No matter how powerful the forces of illusion appear, they will ultimately fail, and the truth will stand.

=119=

This verse describes the immediate aftermath of the miracle for the camp of disbelief. “So Pharaoh and his people were defeated right there.” The defeat was not just a technical loss in a contest; it was a comprehensive humiliation. Pharaoh, who had positioned himself as a god and had confidently assembled the best magicians in the land, was now exposed as weak and his championed experts rendered useless.

The phrase “right there” (hunalika) emphasizes the locality of the defeat; in that very arena, in that very moment, before the eyes of the gathered crowds, Pharaoh’s power crumbled. The second part says “and put to shame” (wanqalabū sāghirīn).

The word “shame” (sāghirīn) implies being humbled, belittled, and disgraced. The arrogance with which they had entered the contest was turned upside down. They left the scene not as triumphant defenders of the state but as the humiliated losers of a cosmic showdown. This defeat was not merely personal for Pharaoh; it was a blow to the entire system of idolatry and tyranny that he represented. The magicians, who were supposed to be the instruments of that system, would soon become its most vocal opponents, having witnessed the truth firsthand. This verse highlights the principle that arrogance before Allah always leads to disgrace. The proudest moments of the tyrant are often the prelude to their most spectacular falls. For the believers who were watching this drama unfold—the oppressed Israelites—this was the first tangible sign of their approaching liberation. The defeat of Pharaoh’s magicians was the crack in the edifice of oppression. For the Prophet Muhammad and his companions, who were still a small, persecuted minority, this verse was a prophecy that the proud Quraysh leaders would similarly be defeated and shamed, just as Pharaoh was.

The verse teaches believers to never be overawed by the apparent might of the oppressor. Their power is an illusion that can be shattered by a single divine command, leaving them disgraced in the very arena where they once boasted.

This verse records the most extraordinary outcome of the contest: the conversion of the magicians. “And the magicians fell down, prostrating.” The very men who had been recruited to defeat Moses, who had secured their reward and the promise of closeness to Pharaoh, were the first to publicly declare their faith. The act of falling into prostration is the ultimate physical expression of submission and worship. It was an instinctive, uncontrollable response to what they had witnessed. They did not simply nod in agreement or verbally concede; their bodies collapsed to the ground in sujood, the posture that is reserved exclusively for the worship of the One True God. The reason for their immediate prostration was their expert knowledge. As masters of the magical arts, they understood the limits of illusion better than anyone else. They knew that no trick could have produced a real serpent that physically devoured solid objects. The transformation of Moses’ staff was not a manipulation of perception; it was a genuine alteration of reality. In that instant, they recognised that Moses was not a magician but a true messenger of the Creator. Their prostration was a testimony that they had seen divine power and could not deny it. This conversion was a devastating blow to Pharaoh’s campaign, more humiliating than the loss of the contest itself. The very instruments of his propaganda had become believers, publicly defecting in front of the assembled masses. This verse demonstrates the power of clear truth to transform hearts instantly, even hearts that had been steeped in falsehood. It also shows that sincere seekers, when confronted with overwhelming evidence, do not hesitate to abandon their previous lives, no matter the cost. The magicians’ prostration foreshadowed their coming steadfastness in the face of Pharaoh’s brutal threats.

For believers, this verse is a celebration of the victory of guidance over misguidance, and a reminder that no one is too far gone in error to be reached by the light of Allah when it shines.
