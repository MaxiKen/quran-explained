=1=

The surah opens with a divine command to the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him: “Say.” He is to convey this extraordinary event to the people. The command immediately establishes that what follows is not a human story but revelation from Allah.

The phrase “It has been revealed to me” underscores the direct source of this knowledge. The Prophet could not have known on his own that a group of jinn had listened to the Quran; jinn are unseen beings, hidden from human perception in their natural state. The event occurred, according to authentic reports, when the Prophet was returning from the painful mission to Ta’if. He stopped at a place called Nakhlah and recited the Quran during the night or dawn prayer. A group of jinn from Nasibin, sometimes identified as seven or nine, passed by and were captivated by the recitation. They stopped to listen, and their hearts were transformed. They then returned to their people as warners, sharing what they had heard. The jinn themselves describe the Quran as “a wondrous recitation.

” The Arabic word “‘ajaban” means something amazingly astonishing, far beyond ordinary speech. It captivates the listener with its eloquence, its truth, its rhythm, and its profound meaning. The jinn, who possess sharp faculties and have existed for centuries, could immediately sense that this was no human poetry or magic. They recognized its divine origin. This opening is a powerful rebuke to the Quraysh. While the unseen creatures believed at the first hearing, the Quraysh, who shared the Prophet’s language and homeland, rejected it. The surah Al-Jinn thus contrasts the response of two different creations.

The previous surah, Nuh, told of a people who rejected for 950 years; here, a group of jinn accepted in a single night. The lesson is clear: belief is not about time but about the state of the heart.

The phrase “a group of jinn” reminds us that not all jinn are devils; many are upright beings with free will, accountable to Allah. They listened, they understood, and they acted.

=2=

The jinn immediately declare their verdict on the Quran: “It leads to Right Guidance.” The word “yusaddiqu” in this context means it guides to the truth, to the straight path, to everything that is morally and spiritually correct. The Quran is not just information; it is a transformative guide. It leads to right guidance in belief, in worship, in character, and in dealings. The jinn’s statement is a testimony of faith from another realm of creation. Then they affirm the consequence of that guidance: “so we believed in it.” Belief is not passive; it is the active, wholehearted acceptance of the truth. This immediate belief of the jinn shames those who hear the Quran day after day yet harden their hearts. Following their belief, they make a firm resolution: “and we will never associate anyone with our Lord in worship.

” This is the core of tawheed. The jinn, who previously may have been involved in shirk, as later verses will explain, now utterly renounce it. They declare that they will never commit shirk—not now, not ever.

The use of “our Lord” (rabbina) is tender and personal. They have claimed Allah as their own Lord, entering into a relationship of devotion and submission.

The phrase “never associate anyone” is an absolute negation. Shirk is the unforgivable sin if one dies upon it, and the jinn recognize its gravity.

This verse also refutes the polytheists of Mecca who associated idols, angels, and jinn themselves with Allah. The jinn themselves are now declaring pure monotheism. It dismantles the Qurayshi claim that the jinn are Allah’s partners or that they deserve worship.

The verse teaches that the moment genuine guidance is heard and understood, it demands an immediate response: belief and the total rejection of all false gods.

=3=

The jinn continue their confession of purified faith by specifically refuting false claims about Allah. “Our Lord—Exalted is His Majesty” translates “jadd rabbina.

” The Arabic word “jadd” can mean majesty, glory, or greatness. Some early scholars also understood it as “riches” or “self-sufficiency,” meaning that Allah is far too exalted, rich, and perfect to need a mate or child. The jinn magnify Allah, declaring His absolute transcendence. Then they deny two core falsehoods: that He has “taken a mate” (sahibah, meaning spouse or female companion) and that He has “offspring” (walad, meaning child). Among the pagan Arabs, some claimed that the angels were Allah’s daughters. Certain jinn and deviant humans spread the idea that Allah had a spouse from among the jinn, producing offspring. The jinn who believed now categorically reject these blasphemies. They affirm that Allah is above such needs; He is the Creator who neither begets nor is begotten.

This is identical to the message of Surah Al-Ikhlas. The mention of “mate” and “offspring” reflects the depth of the corruption that had infiltrated the unseen realm. Iblis and his followers had propagated these lies, as mentioned in the next verse. The jinn are now publicly repenting and setting the record straight.

This verse teaches that proper exaltation of Allah requires purifying one’s concept of Him from all anthropomorphic or mythological ideas. The phrase “Exalted is His Majesty” is a form of glorification that every believer should internalize. It echoes the teaching of Noah in the previous surah, where he asked, “What is the matter with you that you are not in awe of the Majesty of Allah?” The jinn have now answered that call; they are in awe and they proclaim that awe by denying any deficiency in the Divine.

=4=

The jinn now openly admit the errors of their own kind. “The foolish of us” refers to the ignorant, reckless, and unwise among the jinn—specifically Iblis and his followers, the devils who spread lies.

The word “safih” denotes a person who lacks sound judgment, who is driven by whims and arrogance rather than truth. These foolish ones “used to utter outrageous falsehoods about Allah. ” The Arabic “shatatan” means statements that are extreme, far from the truth, and utterly unreasonable. These falsehoods include the very claims denied in the previous verse: that Allah has a wife, that He has children, that there are beings who share His power or deserve worship alongside Him. Some commentators mention that Iblis even proclaimed that Allah had taken a spouse from the jinn and produced offspring. The believing jinn are now disassociating themselves from these blasphemies. Their confession is a model of humility. They do not deny their past misguidance; they acknowledge that the foolish among them fell into grave error. This teaches humans that acknowledging the mistakes of one’s forefathers or community is not a betrayal but a necessary step toward truth. The Quraysh were asked to abandon the idols their ancestors worshipped, and they refused out of pride. The jinn, however, willingly call their own past “foolishness. ” The verse also warns that among any creation, there will be those who go to extremes and lead others astray. Believers must always measure claims about Allah against the clear revelation, not against tradition or speculation. By exposing the source of the lies, the jinn dismantle the foundation of idolatry. If the very beings whom humans worshipped as intermediaries now admit that their own foolish ones lied about Allah, what justification remains for shirk?

=5=

This verse reveals a mistaken assumption the jinn had held for ages: “We certainly thought that humans and jinn would never speak lies about Allah.” Before hearing the Quran, the jinn assumed that no rational creature would dare fabricate falsehoods about the Creator. They had a certain naive trust that beings with intellect would never be so audacious.

However, the emergence of shirk and the outrageous claims about Allah—a spouse, offspring, partners—proved them wrong. Both humans and jinn had indeed told enormous lies about the Divine. This realization shattered their previous worldview and opened their eyes to the depth of deception that Satan had spread. The verse carries an important lesson: assumptions based on what one thinks others would or would not do are not reliable guides to truth. The only reliable guide is revelation. The jinn’s admission also highlights that evil is real and widespread. Many people today assume that all religions are fundamentally sincere or that no one would deliberately invent false doctrines about God. History shows otherwise. The jinn’s awakening is a call for critical reflection; believers must not blindly trust inherited beliefs but must test them against the clear message of the Quran and the prophets.

The phrase “humans and jinn” also shows the unity of the two species in responsibility. Both are subject to the same spiritual dangers. They can both be misled by the foolish among them. This verse connects to the previous surahs’ themes: the disbelievers in Al-Ma‘arij thought the Day of Judgment was impossible because they had not been taught correctly. The jinn now understand that widespread belief does not equal truth. Lies can become so common that they are mistaken for reality. The antidote is the “wondrous recitation” that exposes the falsehood.

=6=

The jinn now recount a specific pre-Islamic practice that deepened misguidance: “some men used to seek refuge with some jinn.” In pre-Islamic Arabia, when a person travelled and stopped in a desolate valley at night, he would call out, “I seek refuge in the master of this valley from the foolish among his people.” He was essentially asking the chief jinn of that area for protection against any harm from lesser jinn. This act of seeking refuge in other than Allah was a form of shirk. It was based on the belief that the jinn had power independent of Allah to protect or harm. The result was devastating: “so they increased each other in wickedness.” The humans, by seeking refuge with jinn, committed shirk and lost their reliance on Allah. The jinn, being invoked and worshipped, became arrogant and more emboldened in their evil. The humans’ fear of the jinn grew, and the jinn’s tyranny over the humans expanded. It was a vicious cycle of mutual reinforcement of sin. This verse is a timeless warning against turning to any created being—whether jinn, saint, astrologer, or amulet—for protection that should only be sought from Allah. The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught the believers to recite specific supplications for protection, such as Ayat al-Kursi and the Mu‘awwidhatayn, which direct one’s heart to Allah alone. The jinn, in recounting this, are admitting how both species were drawn into deeper misguidance. They now recognize the evil of that practice.

The verse also explains why the jinn might have felt superior; humans were grovelling to them. But Islam restored the balance by commanding humans to worship only Allah and teaching that all creation, including jinn, are His servants. The increase in wickedness was a direct result of bypassing the Creator and clinging to creation. This lesson remains urgently relevant: reliance on anything other than Allah—be it money, status, or occult practices—only compounds one’s problems.

=7=

The jinn now address their fellow jinn, pointing out a shared false belief: “those humans thought, just like you jinn, that Allah would not resurrect anyone for judgment.” Many humans, especially the pagan Arabs, denied the resurrection and the Day of Reckoning. The jinn, as a community, had also fallen into this delusion, influenced by the devils among them. Both groups assumed that death was the end and that there would be no accountability. This denial of the Hereafter was the root of much of their moral corruption. If there is no judgment, then one can live for pleasure, power, and self-gratification without fear of ultimate consequence. The jinn now realize how wrong they were. The Quran’s powerful arguments for the resurrection, its descriptions of Paradise and Hell, and its logical reasoning from the creation of the heavens and the earth awakened them to the truth. This verse demonstrates that denial of the afterlife is a universal error that crosses species, not just a human folly. The jinn’s confession affirms that the message of all prophets, including the Quran, is essential to correct this misconception.

The phrase “just like you” indicates that the jinn speaking are now distancing themselves from that past ignorance. They are calling their fellow jinn to abandon the old belief. This connects directly to Al-Ma‘arij, where the disbelievers “truly see this Day as impossible” and to Noah’s preaching of the resurrection. The jinn who heard the Quran now understand that the One who created them in stages can indeed bring them back. The verse is a powerful reminder that the Hereafter is not a negotiable part of faith; it is a central pillar, and denying it leads to utter loss.

=8=

The jinn now shift to a cosmic sign that deeply affected them and signaled the arrival of a new prophetic era: “Earlier we tried to reach heaven for news.” Before the advent of the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, the jinn used to ascend toward the lowest heaven to eavesdrop on the conversations of the angels. They would snatch snippets of divine decrees—future events, fortunes, misfortunes—and pass them on to fortune-tellers and soothsayers on earth, who would then mix them with a hundred lies and gain fame and influence. But after the Prophet’s mission began, they attempted this again “only to find it filled with stern guards and shooting stars. ” The “stern guards” are powerful angels stationed to protect the heavens. The “shooting stars” (shuhub) are flaming meteors hurled at any jinn attempting to steal a hearing. This was a dramatic change in the cosmic order. The sky, which was once accessible for their eavesdropping, was now a fortified barrier. This sign, mentioned also in Surah Al-Hijr and As-Saffat, indicated that the era of soothsaying had come to an end. The truth was now descending through a protected channel: the angel Jibril bringing revelation to the Prophet. No unauthorized leaks could contaminate the pure message. The jinn recognized this event as a major turning point. It caused them to search for the reason, leading some of them to the Quran. The verse highlights the miracle of the Quran’s preservation and the end of the chaos of false oracles. For the believers, it is a reassurance that the divine message they receive is pure and uncontaminated by demonic interference. The universe itself was rearranged to honour and protect the final revelation.

=9=

The jinn provide further detail about their old practice: “We used to take up positions there for eavesdropping.” They would sit in specific stations, one above another, forming a chain to catch the sound and relay it down. It was a calculated effort, a sort of cosmic espionage. But now, “whoever dares eavesdrop now will find a flare lying in wait for him. ” The Arabic word “shihab” is a bright, blazing flame—a meteor that is specifically aimed at the intruder.

The phrase “lying in wait” (rasadan) suggests that the angels are placed precisely to monitor and intercept any such attempt. The jinn are met with a vigilant and relentless defense. Any jinn who tries is immediately targeted and burned. This destroyed their ability to gather secret knowledge. The jinn who heard the Quran now explain this to their fellows, acknowledging the futility of even trying. The verse underscores the absolute sovereignty of Allah; He guards His unseen knowledge. It also nullifies the credibility of fortune-tellers and magicians who previously relied on stolen information. Their source is now permanently cut off. For the believers, this is a call to trust only in the revealed Quran and to shun all forms of divination. The jinn’s candid admission is a powerful testimony that something monumental had occurred in the heavens, and the only sensible response was to seek out the new messenger and submit. The “flare lying in wait” is a physical manifestation of the divine wrath against those who attempt to trespass on the realm of the unseen. It shows that the unseen is not a realm of chaos but is meticulously governed by Allah’s command.

=10=

With the eavesdropping blocked, the jinn are plunged into a state of ignorance about the future: “Now, we have no clue whether evil is intended for those on earth, or their Lord intends for them what is right.” Before, they could snatch fragments of information and speculate about impending disasters or blessings. Now, they are completely cut off. They candidly confess their uncertainty. This admission is profoundly important. It demolishes the illusion that jinn have innate knowledge of the unseen or can predict the future. They are as ignorant as humans when it comes to Allah’s plans.

The phrase “evil is intended” could refer to a destructive punishment, perhaps a flood or earthquake. “What is right” refers to mercy, guidance, and prosperity. The jinn heard the strong warning in the Quran and saw the heavens fortified; they wondered if a catastrophe was about to strike the earth. But they could not be sure; only Allah knows. This uncertainty drove them to embrace the clear revelation they had heard, because the Quran provides the answers that stolen whispers could never give.

The verse teaches the believer not to seek knowledge of the future from any creature. The unseen is Allah’s exclusive domain. The jinn’s humility in admitting “we have no clue” is a model for all intelligent beings. It contrasts with human soothsayers who pretend to know what will happen.

The verse also connects to the earlier surahs where the punishment was a central theme; the jinn are now aware that a warning has come, and they choose to believe rather than risk facing an unknown calamity unprepared. Their statement reflects a healthy fear of the Lord’s plan, which leads them to take the safest path: embracing the guidance.

=11=

The jinn now describe the diversity within their own species, a fact often overlooked by humans who stereotype all jinn as evil. “Among us are those who are righteous.

” The word “salihun” means good, upright, obedient to Allah. These are the believing jinn who accept the truth and live by it. “And those who are less so” translates a phrase indicating those who are not righteous—some are mediocre, some are outright evil. This spectrum of morality mirrors the human condition. The jinn then say, “We have been of different factions.

” The word “shiya‘” means diverse groups, sects, and paths. Before Islam, the jinn were divided into various religions and ideologies, just like humans. Some were Jews, Christians, Magians, or idolaters, influenced by the cultures near which they resided. Now, the Quran has come to unite the righteous among them on the single path of Islam. The verse serves multiple purposes. It corrects the false notion that all jinn are demons; they have free will and are accountable. It also warns humans that the jinn have their own struggles with faith and divisions, so no one should invoke them as gods or intermediaries, because they themselves are in need of guidance. The fact that the righteous jinn openly acknowledge their community’s diversity shows their sincerity. They are not hiding their internal problems.

For believers, the lesson is to avoid generalizations and to understand that the struggle between good and evil exists in all intelligent species. The jinn’s testimony makes their embrace of Islam even more compelling; they chose unity over factionalism, truth over inherited division.

=12=

Having witnessed the cosmic events and heard the Quran, the believing jinn have attained a deep conviction: “we truly know that we cannot frustrate Allah on earth, nor can we escape from Him into heaven.” The word “lan nu‘jiza Allah” means we can never render Him incapable, we can never outrun His power or thwart His decree. Whether on earth or attempting to flee to the heavens, there is absolutely no escape. This knowledge is the fruit of belief. Before, the jinn roamed the skies and thought they had access to hidden realms. Now they realize that even the heavens are under strict divine control, and their previous eavesdropping was only by permission.

The phrase “nor can we escape from Him into heaven” is a direct reference to their failed attempts to reach the heavenly assembly. If they try to flee upward, they are met with shooting stars. If they stay on earth, they are still within His grip. There is no corner of the universe that lies beyond Allah’s authority. This admission is a declaration of total submission. It echoes the message of Al-Ma‘arij, where the angels ascend through the pathways to a Lord whose punishment cannot be averted. It also parallels Noah’s people who found no help against Allah. The jinn have learned the lesson that many humans ignore: no one can outmaneuver the Almighty. This knowledge produces a healthy fear and a commitment to obedience.

For the believer, reflecting on this verse instills a sense of awe and accountability. Whether one is in the depths of the sea or the heights of the sky, Allah’s knowledge and power encompass everything. True freedom lies not in running but in surrendering to the only inescapable reality.

=13=

The jinn return to the pivotal moment of their transformation: “When we heard the guidance of the Quran, we readily believed in it.” The word “guidance” (huda) captures the essence of the Quran; it is not merely a book of laws but a guiding light. Their belief was immediate and without hesitation, indicated by the emphatic “readily.” They did not demand miracles, they did not argue, they did not stall. This contrasts starkly with the Quraysh, who were offered the same guidance but engaged in endless disputes. The jinn then explain the benefit of such belief: “For whoever believes in their Lord will have no fear of being denied a reward or wronged.

” The phrase “no fear of being denied a reward” means they will not suffer any decrease in the good deeds they earned. Allah will not shortchange them; every good act, however small, will be accounted and rewarded fully. “Or wronged” means they will not be unjustly punished for sins they did not commit, nor will their bad deeds be exaggerated. The believer is secure in Allah’s perfect justice. This assurance is a powerful motivator. The jinn, who previously lived in a world of uncertainty and fear of the unknown, now find security in their Lord’s promise. The verse highlights that faith in Allah brings ultimate fairness. Human systems of justice are flawed; people often lose rights or face oppression. But with Allah, no injustice exists. The believer can rest assured that his efforts are not wasted. This message would have been a balm for the persecuted early Muslims in Mecca, who were denied their rights and wronged daily. The jinn’s testimony affirms that the ultimate court is with Allah, and there, no injustice will be tolerated.

=14=

The jinn restate the division within their community, now with clearer terminology: “among us are those who have submitted to Allah and those who are deviant.” Submission (islam) is the voluntary surrender of one’s will to Allah, which is the very definition of the religion brought by all prophets. The “deviant” (qaasitun) are those who turn away from the straight path, who incline toward injustice and falsehood. Then a definitive verdict is given: “So as for those who submitted, it is they who have attained Right Guidance. ” This is an unambiguous statement that true guidance is only found in Islam, in the submission to the One God. The deviants are excluded from guidance by their own choice. The jinn are acting as preachers to their own kind, making the truth clear. They do not promote pluralism; they declare that right guidance is exclusive to those who submit.

This is not arrogance but a sincere warning born from their own experience of finding the truth. The verse also implicitly addresses the human listeners. If even the jinn can discern that submission is the only way to guidance, how can the pagans of Mecca remain in their deviance?

The phrase “it is they who have attained Right Guidance” uses the Arabic “fahuwa” to emphasize that these are indeed the rightly guided. The word “rashad” means maturity, sound judgment, and hitting the target. The submitter has reached spiritual maturity. The verse connects to the core message of Al-Ma‘arij and Noah: success lies in faith and good deeds, not in wealth, clan, or power. The jinn who submitted are now among the blessed, while the deviant among them face the same fate as deviant humans.

=15=

The jinn conclude their speech with a stark warning to their own species: “And as for the deviant, they will be fuel for Hell.” The word “deviant” (qaasitun) describes those who stubbornly turn away from the truth. Their ultimate destination is Hellfire, where they will serve as its “fuel” (hatab).

This is the same imagery used in the Quran for human disbelievers. The jinn are not a different class of creation with a separate destiny; they will be thrown into the same Fire along with sinful humans. This verse clearly establishes that jinn are accountable and will be judged. The righteous among them enter Paradise, and the deviant enter Hell. This demolishes any notion that jinn are gods or that they have a special status exempting them from divine justice. Their mention as “fuel” indicates they will burn and suffer the torment, contributing to the intensity of the Fire. The lesson for humans is that if the jinn, despite their powers and their ability to roam the heavens, cannot escape Hell if they deviate, then humans should certainly not feel secure. The jinn’s own testimony becomes a powerful tool of da‘wah to both species.

For the believer, this verse deepens the fear of deviation and the gratitude for the guidance that leads to safety. It also reassures that the oppression and mockery faced from the deviant, whether human or jinn, will not go unpunished. The justice of Allah is all-encompassing. The speech of the jinn ends here, and the surah pivots back to addressing the human deniers of Mecca, drawing a direct lesson from what the jinn said.

=16=

The focus now shifts entirely to the human deniers, the Quraysh and their ilk. Allah declares a conditional statement: “Had the deniers followed the Right Way, We would have certainly granted them abundant rain to drink.” The “Right Way” (tariqah) refers to the path of Islam, the straight religion of monotheism and righteousness. The promise attached to it is abundant rain. In the arid climate of Arabia, rain was synonymous with life, prosperity, and survival. The connection between spiritual uprightness and material blessings is a recurring theme. Just as Noah promised his people that seeking forgiveness would bring rain and wealth, here Allah affirms the same principle.

The word “ma’an ghadaqan” means plentiful, gushing water. It signifies not just a single shower but a continuous, generous supply.

The verse teaches that the struggles of the Quraysh, including the drought they sometimes faced, were linked to their rejection of the truth. If they would only accept tawheed, the heavens would open.

This is not a superstitious belief but a divine law: when societies obey Allah, He blesses them with the resources of the earth. When they rebel, those blessings are withheld or become a trial. The verse serves as both an invitation and a warning. The invitation is clear: embrace the truth and experience material ease as a sign of Allah’s pleasure. The warning is implicit: if you continue in denial, do not expect sustained prosperity.

This verse also connects to the jinn’s earlier words about not knowing whether evil or good was intended for earth. Here, Allah clarifies that the good would have come had they chosen the right path. The deniers are given a glimpse of what they lost.

=17=

The verse continues with a crucial qualification: “as a test for them.” Even the abundant rain and worldly blessings would not be an unconditional reward but a test. Would the people be grateful and increase in faith, or would they become arrogant and attribute the prosperity to their own efforts or to their idols? Wealth and ease are heavy trials. Many are ruined by abundance more than by poverty. Allah would test the deniers with the very blessings they desired to see if they would live up to their responsibility. Then the verse delivers a stern warning: “And whoever turns away from the remembrance of their Lord will be admitted by Him into an overwhelming punishment.” The “remembrance of their Lord” refers to the Quran, the message of truth. Turning away from it after hearing it is an act of rebellion. The consequence is an “overwhelming punishment” (adhab sa‘d).

The word “sa‘d” describes a punishment that is severe, ascending in intensity, or climbing over the one who suffers it. It is a torment that crushes all resistance and leaves no escape. Some commentators say it implies a punishment that is uphill, a painful climb into increasing distress. This connects to Al-Ma‘arij, where the punishment was a raging Flame that rips scalps.

The verse establishes that worldly blessings, had they come, would not have been a sign of final approval but a test before the ultimate judgment. And for those who reject the message, no amount of rain in this world will save them from the drought of mercy in the Hereafter. The turning away is a conscious choice, and the punishment is the inevitable result of that choice. The verse calls the listener to think beyond immediate comfort and consider the eternal outcome.

=18=

This verse lays down a fundamental principle of worship: “The places of worship are only for Allah.” The word “masajid” is plural of masjid, the place of prostration. It refers primarily to the physical mosques where prayers are offered, but its meaning extends to any place on earth where one prostrates to Allah, as the Prophet, peace be upon him, said, “The whole earth has been made a masjid for me.” The mosques are to be dedicated exclusively to the worship of the One God. No idols, no statues, no shrines for saints, no invocations to any being other than Allah should be found within them. The command “so do not invoke anyone besides Him” applies within the mosques and beyond them. The act of invocation (du‘a) is worship itself, as the Prophet taught, “Du‘a is worship.” Calling upon someone else, whether a prophet, an angel, a jinn, or an idol, is an act of shirk that nullifies the purpose of the mosque. This verse was revealed in Mecca, where the Kaaba, built by Abraham as a pure house of worship, had been filled with 360 idols. The Quraysh had turned the sacred mosque into a pantheon. The verse is a direct assault on that practice, declaring that these places belong solely to Allah. It also connects to the jinn’s testimony of pure monotheism. The jinn affirmed they would never associate anyone with their Lord, and now the human deniers are commanded to ensure that even the architecture of their worship reflects that same purity. The practical lesson for all believers is to guard the masjids from innovations and shirk, and to fill them with the remembrance of Allah alone. The heart is also a masjid where Allah should be worshipped exclusively, free from the idols of desires and worldly attachments.

=19=

This verse describes a stark contrast to the command just given. While the mosques are for Allah alone, when the greatest servant of Allah, the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, “stood up calling upon Him alone” in prayer and recitation, “the pagans almost swarmed over him.

” The Arabic “yakaduna yakununa ‘alayhi libad” paints a vivid image: they crowded around him so densely, they were almost a compact mass, pressing in on him. This occurred when the Prophet prayed openly near the Kaaba or in other locations, reciting the Quran and calling upon Allah. The disbelievers would gather from all sides, as mentioned in Al-Ma‘arij where they rushed “from the right and the left, in groups to mock you.” Here, their physical aggression nearly reached the point of crushing him.

The phrase “servant of Allah” (‘abd Allah) is the most honourable title any human can have. It signifies complete submission and devotion. The Prophet is called the servant of Allah in the context of prayer, highlighting his perfect worship. The Quraysh could not tolerate seeing someone pray to Allah alone, because it exposed the falsehood of their idols and their traditions. Their reaction was not intellectual debate but animalistic mob fury. This verse reassures the Prophet that his pure monotheism is what provokes them, but he is on the right path. It also contrasts the Quraysh’s behaviour with that of the jinn. The jinn listened quietly, believed, and warned their people. The Quraysh listened only to attack. The verse is a timeless lesson: when a believer stands firm upon tawheed, the forces of falsehood may swarm, but the believer must stand his ground, for the mosques belong to Allah, not to the mob.

=20=

In the face of the swarming pagans, Allah commands the Prophet to make a clear, unwavering declaration: “Say, O Prophet, ‘I call only upon my Lord, associating none with Him in worship.’” This is a statement of absolute monotheism and a direct answer to the polytheists who wanted him to compromise. The verb “call” (ad‘u) encompasses all forms of worship and supplication. The Prophet declares that his worship, his prayers, his requests, his reliance—all of it is directed solely to his Lord. He then explicitly negates any shirk: “associating none with Him. ” The phrase “in worship” clarifies that this association pertains to any act of devotion. This public testimony places a clear boundary between him and the idolaters. There is no middle ground; he will not call upon their idols alongside Allah, nor will he invoke angels, jinn, or saints.

The repetition of the first-person possessive “my Lord” is intimate and exclusive. It is as if he is saying: my Lord is the only One I call; your lords and idols have no share. This verse is the Quranic response to all pressures to syncretize faith or dilute monotheism. It echoes the jinn’s earlier declaration: “we will never associate anyone with our Lord.” The same words are now placed on the Prophet’s tongue. The lesson is that the essence of Islam is pure tawheed, and it must be proclaimed courageously, even when the opposition is ready to swarm.

For the believer, this verse is a model of steadfastness. In a world that often demands religious compromise, the response must remain: I call only upon my Lord, no one else. The surah’s argument now builds toward reminding the Prophet of his limited role and the ultimate accountability before Allah.
