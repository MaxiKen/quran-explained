# **SURAH 3: ĀL-ʿIMRĀN**

## **3:1**

> Alif-Lãm-Mĩm

### **MEANING**

The surah opens with three Arabic letter names pronounced one after another rather than joined into a word: ***“Alif-Lãm-Mĩm”***. The verse supplies no gloss of its own, so a reader is left with a deliberate opening whose recited sound is preserved but whose decoding is not given. That combination should shape how the verse is read. It is not noise inserted before the real subject, and it is not a cipher the verse invites each reader to crack. What follows immediately is a declaration about God, so the letters stand at the threshold of the surah’s first theme rather than as a puzzle detached from it.

The same combination of letters opens other surahs, which gives the opening a recognizable function in the Qur’an: it marks the start of a fresh passage of revelation. Within this surah the movement is swift. The letters are followed by the affirmation that there is no god but Him, and then by the statement that He has sent down the Book. The opening therefore leads into the subject that occupies the surah’s first long section without asking the reader to solve anything first.

### **THE OCCASION AT THE OPENING OF THE SURAH**

A report preserved through Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq, from Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, explains why the surah begins as it does. A delegation of the Christians of Najrān came to the Prophet in Medina: sixty riders, among them fourteen of their notables. Affairs returned to three of them. Al-ʿĀqib, named ʿAbd al-Masīḥ, was their commander, the man of their counsel, whose opinion they did not override. Al-Sayyid, named al-Ayham, was responsible for their provisions and their travelling arrangements. Abū Ḥārithah ibn ʿAlqamah was their bishop, their scholar, their prayer-leader, and the master of their place of study; the Byzantine kings had honoured him, funded him, and built churches for him because of what reached them of his learning.

The delegation entered the mosque at Medina after the afternoon prayer, wearing fine embroidered Yemenite cloaks and wraps. When their time of prayer came, they stood to pray in the Prophet’s mosque facing east, and he said, *“Leave them.”* Two of their scholars then spoke with him, and he called them to submit. They answered that they had already submitted. He replied that they had not, and told them that what kept them from submission was *“your claiming a child for God, your worship of the cross, and your eating of pork.”* When they asked who Jesus’ father was, the Prophet did not answer them, and God sent down concerning their sayings and their differences the opening of this surah, some eighty-odd verses of it.

The same account sets out the positions they defended. Some said *“he is God,”* arguing from his raising the dead, healing the sick, telling of unseen things, and shaping a bird from clay and breathing into it. Some said *“he is the son of God,”* arguing that he had no known father and spoke in the cradle, something no child of Adam had done before him. Some said *“he is third of three,”* arguing from the divine sayings *“We did,”* *“We commanded,”* *“We created,”* *“We decreed,”* on the reasoning that a single speaker would have said *“I did.”*

### **THE ARGUMENT PUT TO THE DELEGATION**

Al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas, in a report transmitted through Ibn Abī Jaʿfar from his father, puts the exchange in the form of questions. The delegation asked the Prophet who Jesus’ father was and spoke against God with lies and slander. The Prophet asked whether they knew that no child is born without resembling his father; they said yes. Whether they knew that our Lord is living and does not die, while perishing comes upon Jesus; they said yes. Whether they knew that our Lord watches over everything, guarding, preserving, and providing it; they said yes, and agreed that Jesus owns nothing of that. Whether our Lord knows everything in the earth and the heavens while Jesus knows only what he was taught; they said no. He asked whether they knew that our Lord shaped Jesus in the womb as He willed, that our Lord does not eat food, drink, or relieve himself, while Jesus was carried by his mother as a woman carries, born as a woman gives birth, then nourished as an infant is nourished, and ate, drank, and relieved himself. They said yes to all of it. *“So how can he be as you claim?”* The account says they understood, then refused everything except denial.

### **WHAT THE OPENING ESTABLISHES**

The declaration that divinity belongs to God alone is explained as His informing His servants that worship is neither valid nor permissible for any other, because He is unique in lordship and singular in divinity. Everything below Him is His possession and everything besides Him is His creation, with no partner in His authority or dominion. From that premise the conclusion follows: since every object of worship other than Him is owned by Him, and every being revered instead of Him is His creature, the owned owe undivided obedience to their Owner. The verse is thus described both as an argument against people who persist in worshipping an idol, an image, the sun, the moon, a human being, or an angel, and as a warning to whoever takes a lord, god, or object of worship other than God that he is standing in error and has left the clear path.

The opening therefore does two things at once. It clears God of what the delegation claimed, and it identifies the ground on which the claim fails. What the letters withhold in detail, the declaration that follows supplies in substance: the One who is worshipped is the One who creates and owns, and no creature formed in a womb belongs on the other side of that divide.

## **3:2**

> Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining

### **MEANING**

After the opening letters, the surah names the One to whom worship belongs and describes Him with two of His names: ***“Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining”***. The negation is absolute, and the two names explain why. He is living in a way that has no end, and He is the One who stands over everything, managing and sustaining it. A being whose life ends, or who is himself sustained and guarded, cannot be the god to whom worship is owed. The verse is addressed to no one in particular and therefore to everyone: it states what is true of God before any argument is made.

The order of the two names matters. Life without end is named first and sustaining everything second. That sequence also anticipates the argument developed in this surah’s opening: a creature carried in a womb, fed, and subject to perishing does not share the divine life, and a creature who is himself watched over and provided for does not himself watch over and provide for others.

### **HOW IT WAS READ**

The readings preserved for the second name differ slightly. The readers of the major cities read al-Qayyūm, and the same is reported from ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb and Ibn Masʿūd. Two other forms are reported from ʿAlqamah ibn Qays through al-Aʿmash, from Ibrāhīm, from Abū Maʿmar: one report has him reading al-Qayyim, and another, through Shaybān from al-Aʿmash, has him reading al-Qayyām. The reading preferred is the one carried by the Muslims’ reciters through widespread transmission, without collusion or agreement to agree, and written in their codices: al-Qayyūm.

The three forms are explained as intensive patterns built from the verb *“qāma yaqūmu,”* to stand. The original of al-Qayyūm is given as *“al-qaywūm,”* on the pattern *“fayʿūl”*: the first *“waw,”* being a vowel-carrying letter preceded by a vowelless *“yāʾ,”* was changed into a *“yāʾ”* and merged with it into a doubled letter. Al-Qayyām is explained the same way from *“al-qaywām”* on the pattern *“fayʿāl,”* and al-Qayyim from *“fayʿil,”* the pattern seen in *“sayyid”* from *“sāda yasūdu.”* The reason such forms are used is given as the intention to intensify praise: those patterns praise more forcefully than the simple *“al-qāʾim.”* It is also suggested that ʿUmar’s choice of al-Qayyām reflects the speech of the people of the Hijaz, who turn such root letters into *“yāʾ.”*

### **WHAT AL-ḤAYY MEANS**

The transmitted explanations of al-Ḥayy differ in emphasis without contradicting one another. A report through Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq, from Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, explains it as the One who does not die, adding that Jesus died, and was crucified, in the claim of the scholars of Najrān who argued with the Prophet. Al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas, through Ibn Abī Jaʿfar from his father, says simply *“living, who does not die.”* A second view takes the name to mean that the direction of everything He wills and wants is easy for Him, and that nothing He wills is beyond Him, unlike the gods and rivals who direct nothing. A third understands it as the life that has always belonged to Him and always will: He is described as living because He has life, just as He is described as knowing because He has knowledge and powerful because He has power.

These converge on one point. The life named here is not the borrowed, temporary life of creatures. It is life without cessation or interruption, which removes from God everything that afflicts every living thing He has made: perishing, and the cutting off of life when its term arrives. The inference drawn is explicit. Whoever perishes, passes away, and dies cannot be a god deserving to be worshipped beside the God who neither perishes nor dies.

### **WHAT AL-QAYYŪM MEANS**

Explanations of ***“All-Sustaining”*** also vary. Mujāhid, in a report through Ibn Abī Najīḥ, glosses the two names together with the words *“the One standing over everything.”* Al-Rabīʿ, through the route already mentioned, says that al-Qayyūm stands over everything, guarding it, preserving it, and providing for it. Another view takes it as standing in His place, understanding the name as permanent standing with no movement or transfer in it, and reading it as God’s denying of Himself change and movement from place to place. A report through Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq, from Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, explains al-Qayyūm as *“the One who stands in His place in His dominion over His creation and does not pass away,”* adding that Jesus, in the claim of those scholars, passed from the place he had to another.

The preferred understanding is the first. It is a description by which God describes Himself as the One who undertakes the affair of everything: its provision, its defence, its guarding, its direction, and its turning about within His power. Arabic speakers say of a man that he stands over the affair of a town, meaning that he manages it, and the name is explained on that model. The two names thus work together. The living God who does not die is also the One upon whom everything else depends for its standing.

## **3:3**

> He has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book in truth, confirming what came before it, as He revealed the Torah and the Gospel

### **MEANING**

The verse turns from who God is to what He has sent: ***“He has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book in truth, confirming what came before it, as He revealed the Torah and the Gospel.”*** The address is to the Prophet, and the claim is that the revelation he has received comes with the truth and stands in a confirming relationship to what preceded it. ***“The Torah and the Gospel”*** are named in the same breath, so the verse places this Book within one continuous history of revelation rather than setting it over against its predecessors. Its truth is not a private standard; it is the truth that settles what earlier communities disputed.

The shape of the sentence guards against two opposite errors. Revelation is not a series of disconnected messages, so the Book’s agreement with earlier scripture is part of its credibility. Nor is that agreement a licence to treat every later claim as continuous with earlier ones, since the verse says the Book confirms what preceded it and does not say that everything attributed to earlier scriptures has been preserved intact.

### **THE BOOK AND WHAT IT CONFIRMS**

The explanation given is that the Book is the Qur’an, revealed with the truth, meaning with truthfulness concerning what the people of ***“the Torah and the Gospel”*** differed about and concerning what the Christian delegation of Najrān and other idolaters argued against the Prophet. That confirmation means that it confirms the earlier books God sent down to His prophets and messengers and verifies what His messengers brought from Him. Since the One who sent down all of it is one, there is no discrepancy within it; had it come from anyone other than Him, there would have been much discrepancy in it.

Mujāhid, in a report through Ibn Abī Najīḥ, explains ***“confirming what came before it”*** as *“what was before it of book or messenger.”* Qatādah, through Saʿīd, says it means the Qur’an confirms the books that passed before it. Al-Rabīʿ, through Ibn Abī Jaʿfar from his father, reports the same pairing of book and messenger. A report through Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq, from Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, glosses the opening as *“He sent down to you the Book with the truth,”* meaning with truthfulness in what they differed over.

The phrase is therefore understood in two registers at once: the scriptures that preceded it and the messengers who brought them. That breadth is why the verse reads as a statement about continuity of source rather than only about textual corroboration. The oneness of the sender is offered as the reason no real contradiction can appear between one part of His revelation and another.

### **THE TORAH, THE GOSPEL, AND THEIR PURPOSE**

The second half of the verse assigns each earlier scripture to its recipient. The Torah was sent down to Moses and the Gospel to Jesus, *“previously,”* meaning before the Book sent down to the Prophet. A report through Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq, from Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar, says plainly that the Torah came to Moses and the Gospel to Jesus, as books had been sent down to those before them.

The words rendered in the following verse as guidance for people are explained here as a clarification from God concerning what people differed about: His oneness, the affirmation of His messengers, and His description of Muḥammad as His prophet and messenger, along with the other ordinances of His religion. Qatādah, through Saʿīd, describes the two scriptures as *“two books God sent down, in which there is clarification from God, and protection for whoever takes them, believes them, and acts by what is in them.”*

That last clause matters for how the verse balances its claims. Earlier revelation was genuine guidance, and its guidance was effective for those who held to it and lived by it. Naming them as guidance is not a concession to every later interpretation of them; it is an affirmation of their source and function. The verse thus prepares the reader for what follows. The same God who sent guidance earlier has now sent the Book that confirms it, and the dispute about Jesus is to be settled by what He has sent down.

### **ONE SENDER, ONE TRUTH**

The logic of the verse rests on a single premise: the sender is one. Because the same God sent the Torah, the Gospel, and this Book, the later revelation can confirm the earlier without ceasing to be itself, and the earlier can be acknowledged without being treated as a rival authority. This is why the verse faces both directions at once, backward toward Moses and Jesus and forward toward the argument the surah is about to conduct.

For the reader, the practical consequence is that no party in the dispute can claim revelation for itself while denying the source of revelation. The Book confirms what preceded it, and the God who sent both knows what each community has done with what it received.

## **3:4**

> previously, as a guide for people, and ˹also˺ revealed the Standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺. Surely those who reject Allah’s revelations will suffer a severe torment. For Allah is Almighty, capable of punishment

### **MEANING**

The verse completes the sentence begun in the one before it and then turns to those who refuse what has been sent. The Torah and the Gospel came ***“previously, as a guide for people,”*** and He also sent down the Standard, the criterion that separates truth from falsehood. That term is not simply another name for a book; it names the function by which the dispute is decided. Immediately after, the verse warns that ***“those who reject”*** God’s signs face ***“a severe torment”***, because God is mighty and able to requite. Guidance sent, criterion given, and consequence named all belong together, so the warning is not arbitrary but follows from what has been made clear.

### **WHAT THE STANDARD IS**

Explanations of the Standard divide in a way worth tracing. A report through Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq, from Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, explains it as *“the separation between truth and falsehood in what the factions differed about concerning Jesus and otherwise.”* Qatādah, through Saʿīd, takes it to be the Qur’an, which God sent down to Muḥammad and by which He separated truth from falsehood: declaring what is lawful and what is forbidden, laying down its ordinances, setting its limits, imposing its obligations, giving its clarification, commanding obedience to it, and forbidding disobedience. Al-Rabīʿ, through Ibn Abī Jaʿfar from his father, says that the Standard is the Qur’an, which separated truth from falsehood.

The argument offered in favour of the first reading is that the Qur’an has already been mentioned in the preceding verse, in the words about the Book revealed in truth, and there is no question that the Book there is the Qur’an. Repeating it again in the same breath would serve no purpose that mentioning and reporting it in the first place did not already serve. On that reading, what is meant here is God’s decisive separation between His prophet Muḥammad and those who argued with him about Jesus, by the conclusive argument that cuts off their excuse and the excuse of others like them. The reasoning is strengthened by what follows: the verse goes on to mention people who deny God’s signs, which fits a separation that is itself a proof for the people of truth against the people of falsehood.

Both readings agree on the substance even where they differ on the reference. Either the Qur’an is itself the criterion, or the criterion is the decisive judgement God has delivered through it. In both cases the point is that the dispute about Jesus is not left without an arbiter.

### **THE WARNING THAT FOLLOWS**

***“Surely those who reject Allah’s revelations”*** is explained as those who deny God’s signs, proofs, and arguments for His oneness and His divinity and for Jesus being His servant: those who took the Messiah as a god and a lord, or claimed he was God’s child. For them there is a severe punishment from God on the Day of Resurrection.

The word rendered *“revelations”* is thus understood in this setting as signs and evidences, not merely as verses of a text. That sense matches the sequence. The Standard has just separated truth from falsehood, so rejecting it is rejecting the evidence that settles the dispute rather than overlooking a text one never encountered. The description of the punishment as severe is paired with two descriptions of the One who sends it. God is *“Almighty”* in His dominion: no one can prevent Him from punishing whom He wills, no barrier can come between Him and that person, and no one is able to oppose Him in it. And He is ***“capable of punishment”*** against those who deny His proofs after they have been established against them, made clear to them, and known by them. A report through Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq, from Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar, says exactly this: God takes vengeance on whoever disbelieves in His signs after knowledge of them and of what came in them.

The verse therefore closes the opening movement of the surah by joining revelation to accountability. The God who is living and self-sustaining, who sent the Book in truth and gave the criterion, is also the One whose might cannot be blocked and whose requital falls on those who knowingly deny what has been made clear to them.

## **3:5**

> Surely nothing on earth or in the heavens is hidden from Allah

### **MEANING**

The verse states a fact about God’s knowledge and turns it into a question aimed at the people disputing with the Prophet: ***“Surely nothing on earth or in the heavens is hidden from Allah.”*** Nothing that exists in the earth and nothing that exists in the heavens is concealed from Him. Since that is so, how could what these people say be concealed? The verse is not offering a detached claim about omniscience. It presses that claim against a specific audience that has just argued in front of the Prophet, and it prepares for what follows, where the same God is described as the One who forms every human being in the womb.

The scope is deliberately total. Earth and heavens together leave nothing outside what He knows, so no private thought, closed consultation, or unstated motive escapes Him. That scope is what gives the verse its edge: the disputants were not arguing beyond His knowledge, and the verse implies that they were aware of it.

### **NOTHING IS HIDDEN FROM HIM**

A report through Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq, from Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, explains the verse as God’s saying that He already knows what these people want, what they scheme, and what they set up as rivals to Him when they make Jesus a lord and a god, while they possess knowledge that says otherwise. The account calls that combination *“deceit against God and disbelief in Him.”*

The explanation brings knowledge and intent together. It is not only that God sees what is done publicly; the claims were advanced in His full view by people who held knowledge contradicting them. That is why the verse is shaped as a question rather than a bare assertion. The Prophet is told, in effect, how could their claim be hidden from Me when I know everything? The wording implies that nothing in the earth or the heavens escapes the One who is addressing him.

This also binds the verse to what precedes it. The Standard that separates truth from falsehood, mentioned in the previous verse, is backed by a knowledge that cannot be evaded. A dispute conducted before the God who knows everything is not a dispute that can be won by rhetoric, and the verse removes any suggestion that it was conducted out of His sight.

### **WHY THE KNOWLEDGE MATTERS HERE**

The question of hiddenness is not raised for its own sake. The opening of the surah answers claims made about Jesus by a delegation that had come to argue, and the argument turns on what God knows about him. If nothing is hidden from God, then the formation of that human being in the womb is known to Him in every detail, and the claim that he is divine cannot be shielded by the ignorance of those making it.

The verse therefore performs a narrowing function in the passage. It removes the possibility that the dispute is occurring in a region God does not see, and it moves the reader toward the next statement, where God is described as the One who forms people in the wombs of their mothers as He wills. Knowledge and formation belong to the same subject, and the surah will use the second to settle what the first has raised.

For a reader, the weight of the verse is practical as well as doctrinal. The argument about Jesus is being conducted before the One who formed him, by people who have been told how he was formed. Nothing in that argument is out of God’s view, and nothing about what they intend, plot, or say is concealed from the God they are claiming to describe. The verse thus stands between the warning of the previous verse and the demonstration of the next: the God who punishes denial is the God whose knowledge nothing escapes, and He is about to name the act of creation that settles the dispute.

## **3:6**

> He is the One Who shapes you in the wombs of your mothers as He wills. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Almighty, All-Wise

### **MEANING**

The verse joins an act of creation to a declaration of oneness and to two divine names: ***“He is the One Who shapes you in the wombs of your mothers as He wills. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Almighty, All-Wise.”*** Formation in the womb belongs to God alone and happens ***“as He wills.”*** That is the ground of the declaration that follows: the One who forms is the One who is worshipped, and nothing formed is. The verse then closes with might and wisdom, so the claim about Jesus is answered by an argument rather than by force alone.

The address is plural and direct. Everyone hearing it is among those who were shaped in a womb, so the statement is not remote from the reader. It begins where every human life begins and draws its conclusion about divinity from there.

### **SHAPING IN THE WOMBS**

A report through Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq, from Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, explains what the clause is meant to establish. God’s forming people in the wombs ***“as He wills”***—making this one male and that one female, this one dark and that one fair—is a way of making His servants recognize that everyone whom women’s wombs have enclosed is someone He formed and created ***“as He wills.”*** Jesus son of Mary is among those He formed in his mother’s womb. Were he a god, he would not be among those a womb enclosed, because wombs enclose the created, not the One who creates what is in them. The report puts the point as a question: *“How could he be a god when he was in that station?”*—a station, the account adds, that they neither denied nor disputed. Al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas, through Ibn Abī Jaʿfar from his father, says simply that God formed Jesus in the womb ***“as He wills.”***

Other transmitted material describes the shaping itself. A report through al-Suddī, from Abū Mālik and from Abū Ṣāliḥ from Ibn ʿAbbās, and from Murrah al-Hamdānī from Ibn Masʿūd, and from a number of the Prophet’s Companions, says that when the drop settles in the womb it moves through the body for forty days, then becomes a clot for forty days, then a lump of flesh for forty days. When it is ready to be formed, God sends an angel who brings dust between his fingers, mixes it into the lump, kneads it with it, then forms it as he is commanded, asking whether male or female, wretched or felicitous, and what its provision, its lifespan, its trace, and its afflictions will be. God answers and the angel writes, and when that body dies it is buried where that dust was taken from.

Qatādah, through Saʿīd, draws out the attribute rather than the procedure: *“Our Lord is able, by God, to form His servants in the wombs ***“as He wills”***—male or female, dark or fair, complete in creation or not.”* The two accounts complement each other. One sets out the stages and the writing of the decree; the other states the power behind them.

### **NO GOD BUT HIM, THE ALMIGHTY, THE ALL-WISE**

The declaration ***“There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him”*** is explained here as God’s clearing Himself of having any rival or like in His lordship, and of divinity being permissible for anyone other than Him. It is at the same time a denial of what the delegation of Najrān said about Jesus, of everyone who held their view, and of everyone who claims an object of worship alongside God or acknowledges lordship in another.

The two names that follow explain why the denial cannot be answered. *“The Almighty”* means that no one can rescue a person God wills to punish, and no refuge or shelter can save him, because of the might before which every creature is humbled. *“The All-Wise”* means that He is wise in His governing, in His leaving His creatures without excuse, and in the succession of His arguments against them, so that whoever perishes does so after clear evidence and whoever lives does so after clear evidence. A report through Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq, from Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar, states it compactly: God is *“mighty in His vengeance against those who disbelieved in Him at His will, and wise in His excuse and His argument to His servants.”* Al-Rabīʿ, through the route already mentioned, says *“mighty in His vengeance, wise in His command.”*

The verse therefore completes the opening argument of the surah. The passage has named the living, self-sustaining God, the revelation He sent in truth, the criterion He gave, and the knowledge that misses nothing. It now adds that the same God forms every human being in the womb, that worship belongs to Him alone, and that His might and wisdom stand behind the claim.

## **3:7**

> He is the One Who has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book, of which some verses are precise—they are the foundation of the Book—while others are elusive. Those with deviant hearts follow the elusive verses seeking ˹to spread˺ doubt through their ˹false˺ interpretations—but none grasps their ˹full˺ meaning except Allah. As for those well-grounded in knowledge, they say, “We believe in this ˹Quran˺—it is all from our Lord.” But none will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason

### **MEANING**

The verse turns from the opening declaration about God to the character of the Book He has sent, and explains why people respond to it so differently. ***He is the One Who has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book, of which some verses are precise—they are the foundation of the Book—while others are elusive.*** The Book is not uniform in the kind of clarity it offers. Some of it is decisive and stands as the core to which the rest is referred; some of it admits more than one construal. That difference belongs to the revealed text itself rather than being a defect in it.

The verse then divides its readers by how they handle what is not settled. ***“Those with deviant hearts follow the elusive verses”***, using them to sow confusion, while ***“the well-grounded in knowledge”*** receive both kinds as coming from their Lord. The closing sentence makes the difference a matter of judgement rather than of information.

### **THE PRECISE VERSES AND THE FOUNDATION**

The precise verses are explained as those made firm by clarification and detailed exposition, so that their proofs and evidences are established for what they were made to indicate: the lawful and the forbidden, promise and threat, command and prohibition, report and parable, exhortation and lesson. Calling them ***“the foundation of the Book”*** means that they are the core of the Book, holding the support of the religion, the obligations, the limits, and everything people need for their religion and are charged with in this life and the next.

The word rendered *“foundation”* is explained through Arabic usage, in which the thing that gathers the greater part of something is called its mother: the banner that gathers an army, and the manager of a town’s main affairs. The precise verses are the Book’s mother for the same reason. Yaḥyā ibn Yaʿmar illustrates this with *“the mother of towns”* for Mecca, and Ibn Zayd, through Ibn Wahb, is reported calling them *“the sum of the Book.”* One grammatical point is discussed: the word for foundation is singular although the verses are plural, because what is meant is that the precise verses together are that foundation, not that each one of them is. A parallel is drawn with Qur’an 23:50, where Jesus and his mother are called *“a sign”* rather than two signs.

### **WHAT THE ELUSIVE VERSES ARE**

The transmitted explanations differ substantially. Several early authorities identify the precise verses as those by which one acts, meaning the verses that abrogate, and the elusive ones as those whose ruling was abrogated, which one believes but does not act upon. This is reported from Ibn ʿAbbās through ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah, transmitted through al-Suddī from Ibn ʿAbbās and Ibn Masʿūd and other Companions, and reported from Qatādah, from al-Rabīʿ, and from al-Ḍaḥḥāk. Mujāhid, through Ibn Abī Najīḥ, takes the distinction differently: the precise verses are those containing the lawful and the forbidden, and everything besides them is *“elusive”* in that its parts confirm one another.

A third view, reported from Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, says the precise verses hold *“the argument of the Lord, protection for the servants, and the repelling of opponents and falsehood,”* admitting no turning about, while the elusive ones are true yet admit turning and interpretation, and God tests His servants through them. A fourth, from Ibn Zayd through Ibn Wahb, identifies the precise verses with the clarified accounts of earlier nations and the elusive ones with those accounts as they recur with varying wording but one meaning. A fifth defines the precise as what the scholars know and the elusive as what only God knows. These are not all compatible as definitions, and the wording is broad enough to hold more than one; what they share is the distinction between the settled and the unsettled, with the settled governing how the rest is read.

### **WHO FOLLOWS THE ELUSIVE ONES**

The deviance named here is explained as a heart’s inclining away from the truth, glossed as deviation by Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar and as doubt by Mujāhid, by a report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah, and in an explanation transmitted through al-Suddī from Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Masʿūd, and other Companions. Their following ***“the elusive verses”*** is explained as taking up what is ambiguous in order to confirm by false construals the error they have already chosen. A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah says they carry the precise over to the elusive and the elusive over to the precise, and confound others, so God confounds them.

Whom the verse addresses is itself disputed. Al-Rabīʿ, through Ibn Abī Jaʿfar from his father, says it was the delegation of Najrān, who seized on the Prophet’s own description of Jesus as *“the word of God and a spirit from Him”* and drew conclusions from that wording. Another view names Abū Yāsir ibn Akhṭab, his brother Ḥuyayy, and the men who disputed with the Prophet about the length of his term and his community’s term, which they tried to derive from the disconnected letters. Qatādah extends it to every innovator who argues from an ambiguous verse against what God has clarified, and Ibn ʿAbbās is reported saying of a sectarian group that they *“believe in its precise and perish at its elusive.”* ʿĀʾishah, through Ibn Abī Mulaykah, reports that the Prophet said of those who dispute about it, *“they are the ones God meant, so beware of them.”*

### **DOUBT, INTERPRETATION, AND KNOWLEDGE**

Two explanations of the doubt they seek are reported: al-Suddī and al-Rabīʿ take it as idolatry, while Mujāhid, Ibn Jurayj, and Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar take it as confusion and obscurity. The reason given for preferring the second is that those addressed were already idolaters, so the point was that they wished to cast confusion on the believers and argue them off the truth they held. The interpretation they seek is likewise explained as the hidden term they tried to compute from the disconnected letters, or as the future consequences of the Qur’an, or as the construal of ambiguous verses to suit the deviation already in their hearts.

Whether the well-grounded know that interpretation is itself a reported difference. ʿĀʾishah through Ibn Abī Mulaykah, Ibn ʿAbbās through Ibn Ṭāwūs from his father, Hishām ibn ʿUrwah from his father, ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz, and Mālik are all reported holding that they do not, and that their distinction is saying ***“We believe in it—all is from our Lord”***; Ibn ʿAbbās is reported reciting the verse so that a new sentence begins at the mention of the well-grounded, and similar readings are attributed to Ubayy and Ibn Masʿūd. Mujāhid, al-Rabīʿ, and Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar are reported taking the phrase as joined to God’s name. The well-grounded are described as scholars who have mastered their knowledge so that no doubt enters it, the root sense being something fixed firmly in something else.

### **PEOPLE OF REASON**

The closing clause is explained as meaning that no one takes heed and restrains himself from saying about the ambiguous verses what he does not know ***“except people”*** of intellect and judgement. Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar connects that heedfulness to referring the interpretation of what is ambiguous back to the known interpretation of what is precise. The verse thus ends where it began, with the foundation.

## **3:8**

> ˹They say,˺ “Our Lord! Do not let our hearts deviate after you have guided us. Grant us Your mercy. You are indeed the Giver ˹of all bounties˺

### **MEANING**

The verse gives the well-grounded in knowledge something to say besides their declaration of belief: ***“Our Lord! Do not let our hearts deviate after you have guided us. Grant us Your mercy. You are indeed the Giver ˹of all bounties˺”***. They have just acknowledged that the Book contains what they cannot settle and that God alone knows its interpretation. Their response to that acknowledgement is not curiosity but prayer. The petition has two halves. They ask God not to turn their hearts away now that He has guided them, and they ask Him to grant them mercy from Himself. The verse ends by naming Him the Giver, so the whole request rests on what He gives rather than on what they have secured.

The sequence matters. Guidance has already happened; the danger is what follows it. Steadfastness is therefore presented as something asked for and given, not as a possession held independently of the One who gave it.

### **THE PRAYER IN ITS SETTING**

The verse is explained as continuing the speech reported in the previous one. The well-grounded say they believe in the ambiguous verses and affirm that both they and the precise verses are their Lord’s revelation, and their words are ***“Our Lord! Do not let our hearts deviate after you have guided us”***. What they are asking, on this reading, is that God avert from them the very thing that afflicted those whose hearts swerved: pursuing ambiguous verses in order to sow confusion and to reach for knowledge that belongs to God alone. The prayer asks not to be made like the people who turned away from His path.

The second half is explained in the same direction. ***“Grant us Your mercy”*** means: give us from Yourself the success and firmness to stand by what we now hold, our affirmation of both kinds of verses. The closing name means that He is the One who gives His servants success and rightness in standing firm on His religion and in affirming His Book and His messengers. A report through Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq, from Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, glosses the first clause as a request that their hearts not be turned even though they themselves may turn through what they do.

### **HEARTS THAT TURN**

The verb rendered *“deviate”* is explained in the lexical discussion as inclining and swerving away from the truth, with the derived form used in this surah’s prayer meaning ***“do not incline our hearts away from the truth.”*** The same root appears in the previous verse in the description of *“deviant hearts,”* so the two verses are tied together by one word: what is described there as an existing condition is asked against here as a future danger.

Transmitted material makes the point through practice rather than definition. Umm Salamah, through Shahr ibn Ḥawshab, reports that the Prophet often said in his supplication, *“O Turner of hearts, make my heart firm upon Your religion,”* and then recited this verse. She asked him whether hearts really are turned, and he answered that every human being’s heart is between two of the fingers of the Most Merciful: *“if He wills He sets it straight, and if He wills He turns it,”* and he added the prayer that God not turn their hearts after having guided them and grant them mercy from Himself. She also asked him to teach her a supplication, and he told her to say, *“O God, Lord of the prophet Muḥammad, forgive my sin, remove the rage of my heart, and shelter me from trials that lead astray.”*

Jābir, through Abū Sufyān from al-Aʿmash, reports the same supplication and the Prophet’s reply that the heart lies *“between two of the fingers of the Most Merciful,”* which he indicated with his own fingers. Anas is reported to the same effect. Al-Nawwās ibn Samʿān reports that he heard the Prophet say that every heart is between two of the fingers of the Most Merciful, and that he used to pray, ***“O Turner of hearts, make our hearts firm upon Your religion,”*** adding that the balance is in the hand of the Most Merciful, who raises some people and lowers others until the Day of Resurrection. Samurah ibn Fātik reports the same image of the heart between two fingers. ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ reports that the Prophet said the hearts of all the children of Adam are *“between two of the fingers of the Most Merciful, like one heart, which He turns as He wills,”* and then prayed, ***“O God, Turner of hearts, turn our hearts to Your obedience.”***

### **ASKING THE GIVER**

Calling God the Giver at the close of the petition is what makes the request coherent. Mercy here is not an abstraction but the gift of being kept firm, and the verse puts that gift in the hands of the One who gives rather than in the resolve of the one who asks. That is why the prayer is reported as the Prophet’s own habitual supplication despite his station: the acknowledgement that hearts are turned by God is not a deduction drawn from weakness but a description of where hearts stand before Him.

The verse also closes the movement begun with the precise and the elusive. Those whose hearts have swerved use ambiguity to unsettle others; the well-grounded answer by asking to be kept from swerving. Their knowledge that God alone knows the interpretation leads them to pray rather than to speculate, and the surah presents that prayer as the mark of someone who has understood.

## **3:9**

> Our Lord! You will certainly gather all humanity for the ˹promised˺ Day—about which there is no doubt. Surely Allah does not break His promise.”

### **MEANING**

The prayer begun in the previous verse continues: ***“Our Lord! You will certainly gather all humanity for the ˹promised˺ Day—about which there is no doubt. Surely Allah does not break His promise.”*** The well-grounded are not only asking to be kept from swerving. They are grounding that request in what they know about the future. All people will be assembled for a Day whose coming is certain, and God never fails in what He promises. Because that gathering is certain, steadfastness now matters.

The verse is a confession shaped as a statement about the future. It names a Day whose coming is certain and a God true to what He promises, and by naming them it gives the previous petition a horizon. What is asked for is not escape from that Day but what the promise holds for those who arrive at it having believed and held firm.

### **WHAT IS LEFT UNSAID**

The explanation given is that this belongs to a kind of speech in which what is mentioned makes clear what has been left out. The sense is: O Lord, God will gather people for the Day of Resurrection, so forgive us on that Day and pardon us, for He never fails in what He has promised—that whoever believes in Him, follows His messenger, and acts by what He commanded in His Book will be forgiven by Him then.

That reading explains why a sentence shaped as a report is classified as a request. On this understanding, the well-grounded are asking their Lord to keep them firm in the insight they already have—faith in God and His messenger and in what has come to them of His revelation—until He takes them in the best state of their deeds and faith. If He does that for them, the Garden becomes due to them, because He has promised that whoever is in that state will be admitted to it.

The point is not that the sentence hides a secret meaning. A petition can be expressed by stating the premise it rests on, and anyone who says to God that He will gather people and keeps faith is asking for what the promise contains.

### **THE DAY AND THE PROMISE**

***“No doubt”*** is explained as no uncertainty. The preposition rendered *“for”* is understood as *“on”*: a day on which God gathers His creation to decide between them at the standing of presentation and reckoning. The word rendered *“promise”* is explained as a noun pattern derived from the word for promising.

This clarifying clause is what makes the verse a foundation for what follows. If the gathering is certain and the promise kept, then wealth, children, and numbers cannot alter the outcome. The next verse draws exactly that conclusion, and the passage after it points to Badr as evidence that God gives victory to whom He wills. The prayer that opens with ***“Our Lord”*** therefore closes by placing this life under a Day that is not in doubt, which is why the request for mercy in the previous verse is a request to be kept firm until it arrives.

## **3:10**

> Indeed, neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah—and they will be the fuel for the Fire

### **MEANING**

The address turns from the prayer of the well-grounded to those who reject: ***“Indeed, neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah—and they will be the fuel for the Fire.”*** The verse denies the two things people most rely on. Wealth and children are real advantages in this life, and the Qur’an elsewhere describes them as a trial, but here they are declared useless at the point where they matter most. Whatever a person has accumulated or produced cannot stand between him and God.

The second clause sharpens the first. Such people are not merely unprotected; they become what burns. The image is not of someone thrown into a fire but of someone who is the fire’s fuel, so the consequence is described as arising from the people themselves rather than as something brought against them from outside.

### **WHO IS MEANT**

The explanation given identifies those meant here as the people who denied the truth they had come to know concerning the prophethood of Muḥammad: the Jews of the Children of Israel, their hypocrites, and the hypocrites and disbelievers among the Arabs. What marks them out is not ignorance of the claim but rejection of it after it had become clear to them.

The description is then tied directly to Qur’an 3:7, which speaks of those in whose hearts is deviance, following what is ambiguous in the Book, seeking to sow confusion and to reach for an interpretation that is not theirs to reach. Their possessions and their offspring will not rescue them from God’s punishment when He brings it down on them, and those things will not push it away or stand in for them in any part of it. In the Hereafter they are ***“the fuel for the Fire,”*** meaning its firewood.

### **WHY POSSESSIONS AND CHILDREN FAIL**

The pairing is deliberate. Wealth and children are the two forms of strength a person can point to: what one owns, and what one leaves behind. The verse sets the limit of both in a single sentence. They operate within this life and have no purchase on the reckoning, so trusting them as protection against God is not a miscalculation of degree but a mistake about what kind of thing they are.

The wording also guards against a narrower reading. What is denied is not that wealth and children can be good in themselves, but that they can serve as a defence on the Day the previous verse has just described. Their usefulness is bounded by the life in which they exist, and the verse's point is about the boundary, not about the things.

That is why the image of fuel follows so naturally. What a person treated as his support becomes the material of his own punishment. Qur’an 3:5 has already said that nothing in the earth or the heavens is hidden from God; this verse applies that to the individual. The next verse then supplies the precedent, comparing their case to the people of Pharaoh and those before them, who also denied the signs and were seized for what they had done.

## **3:11**

> Their fate will be like that of the people of Pharaoh and those before them—they all rejected Our signs, so Allah seized them for their sins. And Allah is severe in punishment

### **MEANING**

The comparison completes the warning: ***“Their fate will be like that of the people of Pharaoh and those before them—they all rejected Our signs, so Allah seized them for their sins. And Allah is severe in punishment.”*** Those named in the previous verse are told that their case is not new. Earlier communities denied the signs and were seized, and their possessions and children did not help them either. The point of the comparison is that denial followed by seizure is a pattern rather than an accident, and that the audience addressed stands inside it.

The verse names the cause precisely. What brought the seizure was not weakness or bad fortune but the rejection of signs that had been given. Severity in punishment is therefore presented as a response to what people did with what they had been shown, not as an arbitrary exercise of power.

### **WHAT THE COMPARISON MEANS**

The word rendered *“fate”* is explained in several ways. Al-Rabīʿ, through Ibn Abī Jaʿfar from his father, takes it as *“like their wont”*; al-Ḍaḥḥāk, and Ibn Zayd through Ibn Wahb, as *“like their deed”*; ʿIkrimah and Mujāhid, through Abū Ḥamzah from Jābir, as *“like the doing of ***the people of Pharaoh***, like their affair”*; and Ibn ʿAbbās, through Abū Rawq from al-Ḍaḥḥāk, as *“like their making.”* Al-Suddī takes it as *“like the denial of ***the people of Pharaoh***,”* so that the comparison concerns the act of denial specifically.

The lexical discussion traces the word to persistence: one says of a person that he kept at a matter, meaning he persisted in its work and toil, and Arabic then carried the sense over to affair, matter, and habit. Imru’ al-Qays uses the word in a poem addressed to the traces of a campsite, saying *“as was your wont with Umm al-Ḥuwayrith before her,”* where the sense is a person’s matter, doing, and habit. An unattributed verse is also cited for an alternative pronunciation of the root with a vowel on the hamza.

The differences among these explanations are narrower than they first appear. Wont, deed, doing, and denial describe the same thing from different sides: what such people habitually did, and the specific act of rejection that defined them.

### **THE PRECEDENT BEFORE PHARAOH**

The verse looks backward past Pharaoh as well. Earlier communities—the people of Noah, the people of Hūd, the people of Lot, and others like them—denied the signs and were destroyed when they did, and their wealth and children were of no use to them when the punishment arrived. The description of God as ***“severe in punishment”*** is explained as severe in punishing those who disbelieve in Him and deny His messengers after the argument has been established against them.

The comparison works in two directions. It warns the audience that what befell earlier deniers can befall them, and it reassures the believers that the outcome the next verses recall belongs to the same governance. God takes people to task for what they did with what they were given, and neither numbers nor possessions alter that.

## **3:12**

> ˹O Prophet!˺ Tell the disbelievers, “Soon you will be overpowered and driven to Hell—what an evil place to rest!”

### **MEANING**

The Prophet is instructed to deliver a warning in plain words: ***“Tell the disbelievers”*** that ***“Soon you will be overpowered and driven to Hell—what an evil place to rest!”*** The message has two parts. They will be overcome, and they will be gathered to Hell. The first concerns what will happen to them in this life; the second concerns where they end up. The closing exclamation turns the destination into something to recoil from: the resting-place prepared for them is evil.

The command to say it aloud matters. This is not a private prediction but something to be spoken to their faces, which is what gives the warning its edge and explains the reaction reported below.

### **HOW IT WAS READ**

The readings differ over the person of the two verbs. Many readers read it as a direct address, ***“You will be overpowered”*** and gathered, and this is the reading of most of the reciters of the Hijaz and Basra and some of Kufa. A group of the reciters of Kufa read it in the third person, meaning: tell the Jews that the idolaters of the Arabs will be overcome and gathered to Hell.

The reading preferred is the direct address. The reason given is that the following verse addresses the same people with *“there was a sign for you in the two armies,”* so joining like speech to like is more fitting than breaking into a report about someone else. It is also noted that where the command to speak was revealed to someone other than the immediate addressees, either person is possible in Arabic, and a parallel is cited from a reading attributed to Ibn Masʿūd in which the same shift occurs.

### **THE OCCASION**

Reports connect the verse to Badr. Ibn ʿAbbās, in a report through Muḥammad ibn Abī Muḥammad from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr or ʿIkrimah, says that after the victory over Quraysh at Badr the Prophet gathered the Jews in the market of Banū Qaynuqāʿ and told them to submit before something like what had befallen Quraysh befell them. They answered that he should not be deceived by himself: he had killed a few men of Quraysh who were raw and knew nothing of fighting, and *“if you fight us, you will know that we are the people.”* ʿĀṣim ibn ʿUmar ibn Qatādah reports the same gathering and the same boast, with the Jews adding that they knew him to be a messenger sent, as they found it in their own scripture and in God’s covenant with them. ʿIkrimah, through Ibn Jurayj, names Finḥāṣ the Jew, who said on the day of Badr that Muḥammad should not be deluded by having defeated Quraysh, since Quraysh did not know how to fight.

These accounts are cited for a reason beyond the story itself: they identify who is being addressed, and that identification is what settles the reading. On this basis, the direct address is preferred, since the people warned are the very people told in the next verse to take a lesson from what they witnessed.

### **THE EVIL RESTING-PLACE**

The word rendered ***“place to rest”*** is explained as a bed or couch, so the clause means: evil is the bed to which they are gathered. Mujāhid, in a report through Ibn Abī Najīḥ, explains it as *“evil is what they spread for themselves.”* That gloss ties the image back to the verse’s opening. The resting-place is not assigned arbitrarily but prepared by what they did, and the defeat they are promised is of a piece with it.

## **3:13**

> Indeed, there was a sign for you in the two armies that met in battle—one fighting for the cause of Allah and the other in denial. The believers saw their enemy twice their number. But Allah supports with His victory whoever He wills. Surely in this is a lesson for people of insight

### **MEANING**

The warning is now backed by an event: ***“Indeed, there was a sign for you in the two armies that met in battle—one fighting for the cause of Allah and the other in denial. The believers saw their enemy twice their number. But Allah supports with His victory whoever He wills. Surely in this is a lesson for people of insight.”*** A sign here is not a marvel but something to be read. Two forces met, one small and one large, and the smaller prevailed. The verse names the reason in the middle of the description: God grants His help to those He chooses. Numbers are therefore not the deciding factor, and the outcome is meant to be understood rather than merely recorded.

The audience is addressed directly. The sign is for them, so the verse asks its hearers to draw the conclusion themselves, and it ends by saying that only ***“people of insight”*** do so.

### **THE TWO FORCES**

The two sides are identified as the Prophet and his Companions at Badr on one hand and the idolaters of Quraysh on the other. Reports attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through Saʿīd ibn Jubayr or ʿIkrimah, to ʿIkrimah through Ibn Jurayj, and to Mujāhid through Ibn Abī Najīḥ all place the encounter at Badr. Qatādah, through Saʿīd, explains *“a sign”* as *“a lesson and something to think about”*; al-Rabīʿ, through Ibn Abī Jaʿfar from his father, adds *“and something to reflect upon.”*

A grammatical point is raised about why the second side is put in the nominative after the mention of two parties, with poetic parallels cited: Arabic either repeats a repeated term in the case of the first or begins it afresh, and the accusative and genitive are also possible. The reading followed by the reciters is the nominative, and departing from it is not permitted despite being allowable in Arabic.

### **SEEING TWICE THE NUMBER**

The clause about seeing is read in two ways. The reciters of Medina read it as an address to the Jews, and most reciters of Kufa and Basra read it as a report about the believers. The explanation preferred is that the idolaters appeared to the Muslims as double their own number, God having made them appear few to them, and then made them fewer still so that they appeared equal in number, and fewer again after that.

Ibn Masʿūd, through al-Suddī from Murrah al-Hamdānī, describes exactly this: *“We looked at the idolaters and saw them as doubling our number; then we looked at them again and did not see them as a single man more than us”*—which he connected with Qur’an 8:44. Through Abū ʿUbaydah, Ibn Masʿūd is also reported saying that they were made to appear few until he asked the man beside him whether he saw seventy, and was told, *“I see a hundred.”* They then took a captive who told them they had been a thousand.

Other estimates are reported. Al-Rabīʿ gives the idolaters as nine hundred and fifty and the Prophet’s Companions as three hundred and thirteen; Ibn Jurayj gives between nine hundred and a thousand against three hundred and some; ʿAlī and Ibn Masʿūd are reported through Abū Isḥāq giving a thousand, inferred from the camels the enemy slaughtered each day; and Yazīd ibn Rūmān, from ʿUrwah, reports the same method yielding between nine hundred and a thousand. One report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās giving a lower figure is noted as conflicting with the others.

### **SUPPORT AND INSIGHT**

The verb rendered *“supports”* is explained from the phrase *“I supported so-and-so with such-and-such,”* meaning I strengthened and helped him, the root sense being strength, as in the description of David as possessed of strength. The conclusion drawn is that God informed the Jews of these shifting estimates to show that He strengthens the believers with His help, so that they would not be deluded by their own numbers and would fear that what befell the idolaters at Badr might befall them at the believers’ hands. Qatādah, through Saʿīd, reads the closing clause as *“there was in them a lesson and something to think about; God strengthened them and gave them victory over the idolaters.”*

## **3:14**

> The enjoyment of ˹worldly˺ desires—women, children, treasures of gold and silver, fine horses, cattle, and fertile land—has been made appealing to people. These are the pleasures of this worldly life, but with Allah is the finest destination

### **MEANING**

The passage turns from the fate of deniers to what attaches people to this life in the first place: ***“The enjoyment of ˹worldly˺ desires—women, children, treasures of gold and silver, fine horses, cattle, and fertile land—has been made appealing to people. These are the pleasures of this worldly life, but with Allah is the finest destination.”*** The verse lists what people love and then evaluates it. Desire for these things has been made attractive, and that attraction is not denied. What is denied is that the list constitutes the good. Everything named is provision for a limited life, while the return that matters is with God.

The structure is a comparison held in a single sentence. The things named are real and desirable; the destination with God is better; and the reader is left to put the two in order. The next verse makes the comparison explicit.

### **WHY THE LIST IS GIVEN**

The explanation given is that the verse rebukes the Jews who preferred this world and the love of leadership in it to following Muḥammad after they had come to know that he was truthful. The list is therefore not a general description of human nature but a diagnosis: these are the attachments that kept a particular audience from responding to what they knew.

Al-Ḥasan is reported saying of this world that *“no one blames it more severely than its Creator.”* A report through Abū Bakr ibn Ḥafṣ from ʿUmar relates that when the verse came down he said, *“Now, Lord, when You have made it attractive to us!”* and that the following verse was then revealed.

### **MEASURING THE TREASURES**

The word rendered *“treasures”* is the plural of qinṭār, and the transmitted explanations of its measure differ widely. Muʿādh ibn Jabal is reported giving twelve hundred ounces, as are Ibn ʿUmar, ʿĀṣim, Abū Hurayrah, and Ubayy ibn Kaʿb in a report from the Prophet. Al-Ḥasan and Ibn ʿAbbās are reported giving twelve hundred dinars, or twelve hundred weight of silver. Other reports give twelve thousand dirhams or a thousand dinars; eighty thousand; seventy thousand, as from Mujāhid and Ibn ʿUmar; the fill of a bull’s hide in gold; or simply *“much wealth, some of it upon the rest,”* which is how al-Rabīʿ and Qatādah explain it.

The conclusion drawn is that the word means abundant wealth and that no fixed weight should be assigned to it, since Arabic usage is reported not to set a defined measure for it, and the disagreement among early authorities shows that none was handed down. The intensive form is explained as doubled or multiplied.

### **HORSES, CATTLE, AND TILLAGE**

The horses are described as marked, and the explanations again divide: pasturing, from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ḥasan, al-Rabīʿ, and Mujāhid; beautiful and well-formed, from Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah, and al-Suddī; marked, from Ibn ʿAbbās through ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah and from Qatādah, who explains it as the blaze on their faces; and prepared for fighting, from Ibn Zayd. The view preferred is that they are marked by their markings and beautiful, since the verbal noun in Arabic means marking, and lines by al-Nābigha and Labīd are cited using the word of marked horses. The view that they are merely pasturing is traced to a different root meaning letting animals graze, as in Qur’an 16:10.

*“Cattle”* is explained as the eight pairs named elsewhere in the Qur’an—sheep, goats, oxen, and camels—and ***“fertile land”*** as cultivation. The closing words are explained as meaning that all of it is what the people of this world enjoy while they live, using it to get by and to satisfy the desires whose love has been made attractive to them, rather than as provision for the return or a means of nearness to God, except what is spent in His way. Al-Suddī explains ***“the finest destination”*** as *“a good return,”* which is the Garden, and it is noted that the good return belongs specifically to those who are mindful of their Lord.

## **3:15**

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Shall I inform you of what is better than ˹all of˺ this? Those mindful ˹of Allah˺ will have Gardens with their Lord under which rivers flow, to stay there forever, and pure spouses, along with Allah’s pleasure.” And Allah is All-Seeing of ˹His˺ servants

### **MEANING**

The answer to the previous verse is now given. The Prophet is told to ask ***“Shall I inform you of what is better than ˹all of˺ this?”*** and to answer it himself: ***“Those mindful ˹of Allah˺ will have Gardens with their Lord under which rivers flow, to stay there forever, and pure spouses, along with Allah’s pleasure. And Allah is All-Seeing of ˹His˺ servants”***. The question is addressed to the very people for whom the love of desires was made attractive, and it accepts the comparison they are living by in order to reverse it. Those things are attractive; here is the better thing.

The structure does two things at once. It identifies who receives the better thing—***“those mindful”***—and it closes with a name of God that explains why the comparison is not rhetorical. He sees ***“His servants”***, so the choice each person makes between the two lists is known to Him.

### **WHERE THE QUESTION ENDS**

Grammarians differed over where the question ends. Some held that it ends at ***“better than ˹all of˺ this,”*** after which a fresh statement begins about what is reserved for the mindful, which is why the word for gardens is in the nominative. Others allowed both the nominative and the genitive, the latter treating the gardens as an apposition to *“the better.”* A third view took the question as running as far as ***“with their Lord,”*** so that the gardens answer an implied *“what is it?”*

The view preferred is the first: the question ends at ***“better than ˹all of˺ this,”*** and what follows is a fresh statement about those who will have the gardens. On that reading the sentence is shaped as a report but functions to spell out what the *“better”* consists in, and nothing needs to be supplied to complete it.

### **WHAT IS PROMISED**

Those meant are explained as the people who feared God and obeyed Him by discharging His obligations and avoiding what He prohibited. The gardens are explained as orchards, the rivers beneath them as flowing beneath their trees, and remaining in them as an abiding that does not end.

***“Pure spouses”*** is explained as the women of the Garden, purified of every impurity that affects the women of this world. The word rendered *“pleasure”* is explained as a verbal noun derived from the verb *“God was pleased with so-and-so,”* with an alternative vocalization attributed to the dialect of Qays and to the reading of ʿĀṣim.

The reason His pleasure is named alongside the gardens is given explicitly: it is the highest station of honour for the people of the Garden. Jābir ibn ʿAbd Allāh, in a report through Sufyān from Muḥammad ibn al-Munkadir, relates that when the people of the Garden enter it, God will say, *“I will give you what is superior to this,”* and when they ask what could surpass it, He will say, *“My pleasure.”*

### **THE CLOSING NAME**

The final clause is explained as God’s being possessed of sight of the servant who is mindful of Him, fears Him, obeys Him, and prefers what He has prepared over the love of what has been made attractive to him in this life—and equally of the one who is not mindful, who disobeys Him, obeys Satan, and prefers the desires of this world to the lasting bliss with Him. He knows each group, so that He may repay every one of them at their return to Him: the doer of good for his good, and the doer of evil for his evil. The verse thus answers the list in verse 14 without denying it. The better thing is not the absence of desire but a destination, and the two are set before the reader by the One who sees which is chosen.

## **3:16**

> who pray, “Our Lord! We have believed, so forgive our sins and protect us from the torment of the Fire.”

### **MEANING**

The description of the mindful continues with what they say: ***“who pray”***—***“Our Lord! We have believed, so forgive our sins and protect us from the torment of the Fire.”*** Their faith is not a silent state. It speaks, and what it says is a request. The two petitions are joined: forgiveness for what is past, and protection from what is feared. The verse places this supplication at the centre of the portrait, so that the people described are identified by what they ask for as much as by what they do.

The order within the prayer matters. They begin by affirming that they have believed, and only then ask. The request rests on the affirmation rather than replacing it, and the thing asked for is named twice over—covered, and kept away.

### **WHAT THE PRAYER MEANS**

The explanation given is that these are people who say: we have believed in You, in Your prophet, and in what he has brought from You. ***“Forgive our sins”*** is explained as covering their sins with His pardon and by His leaving off punishing them for them, and ***“protect us from the torment of the Fire”*** is explained as averting His punishment, so that He does not punish them by it.

The reason they single out this request is given: whoever is pushed away from the Fire on that Day has won deliverance from God’s punishment and the good return. The verse therefore names in a single petition the whole of what matters, and it does so in the words of the people it describes rather than in a description of them.

### **PRAYER AT THE CENTRE OF THE PORTRAIT**

The placement of this supplication is what gives the passage its shape. The previous verse promised gardens, abiding, pure spouses, and God's pleasure to the mindful, and the verse after this one will describe them as patient, sincere, obedient, and charitable. Between the promise and the description sits a prayer. Being mindful is therefore presented as something that speaks, and the thing it asks for is precisely what the promise has just held out: to be kept from the Fire and brought safely to what is with God.

A reader who follows the sequence sees that the portrait is not built from achievements alone. What these people do is real and named, but what defines them first is what they say to their Lord, and the saying is a request rather than a report.

### **HOW THE CLAUSE IS CONSTRUED**

The phrase rendered *“who pray”* admits two grammatical readings. It may be in the genitive, as an apposition to the mindful mentioned in the previous verse, or in the nominative as the beginning of a new statement, since it opens a fresh verse. A parallel is drawn with Qur’an 9:111 and 9:112, where a description of the believers is followed in the next verse by a fresh nominative description of the repentant and the worshipping. Both readings are allowed, and the sequence in the following verse implies the genitive.

The verb rendered *“protect”* is traced to the root meaning to ward something off from someone, so the request is that He ward the Fire off from them. That sense gives the petition its precision: what is asked for is not the removal of consequences in general but to be kept from the one thing that would undo the deliverance described in the previous verse.

## **3:17**

> ˹It is they˺ who are patient, sincere, obedient, and charitable, and who pray for forgiveness before dawn

### **MEANING**

The portrait of the mindful is completed with five qualities: ***“˹It is they˺ who are patient, sincere, obedient, and charitable, and who pray for forgiveness before dawn.”*** Four of these describe how such people conduct themselves, and the fifth returns to what they say. The list is a single description, so patience, truthfulness, obedience, and giving are not alternatives from which one may choose. The final clause brings the portrait back to speech, and the time it names is specific: ***“before dawn.”***

### **THE FOUR QUALITIES**

The patient are explained as those who were patient in hardship, in suffering, and at the moment of battle. The sincere are explained as those who were true to God in what they said, making their affirmation of Him, of His messenger, and of what came from Him real by acting on what He commanded and refraining from what He prohibited. The obedient are explained as those who obey Him, and the charitable as those who give the alms due on their wealth, placing it where God commanded it to be given, and spending in the ways He permitted.

Qatādah, through Saʿīd, describes them in the same terms: the sincere are *“a people whose mouths, hearts, and tongues were upright, and who were true in secret and in public”*; the patient are *“a people who were patient in obeying God and patient in keeping away from what He prohibited”*; and the obedient are *“those who obey God.”* These are not separate virtues so much as one life described from four angles: endurance, honesty, obedience, and generosity.

### **SEEKING FORGIVENESS BEFORE DAWN**

The transmitted explanations of the closing clause, ***“before dawn,”*** differ. Qatādah, through Saʿīd and through Ibn Abī Jaʿfar from his father, takes those meant to be the people of prayer, who pray in the pre-dawn hours. Others take them to be those who ask forgiveness. In a report through Ḥurayth ibn Abī Maṭar, from Ibrāhīm ibn Ḥāṭib from his father, a man was heard in a corner of the mosque at the pre-dawn hour saying, *“Lord, You commanded me and I obeyed You, and this is the pre-dawn hour, so forgive me,”* and when the narrator looked, it was Ibn Masʿūd. Through Sulaymān ibn Mūsā, from Nāfiʿ, Ibn ʿUmar is described keeping the night alive in prayer and then asking, *“Nāfiʿ, have we reached the pre-dawn hour?”* When the answer was no he returned to prayer; when it was yes, he sat asking forgiveness and supplicating until morning. Anas is reported saying, *“We were commanded to seek forgiveness seventy times at the pre-dawn hour,”* and Jaʿfar ibn Muḥammad is reported saying that whoever prays at night and then seeks forgiveness seventy times at the end of the night is written among those who seek forgiveness at dawn. Zayd ibn Aslam, asked who they were, answered: those who attend the dawn prayer in congregation.

The view preferred is that they are those who ask their Lord to cover their shame, since the word in that phrase is the plural of the word for the pre-dawn hour, and the most apparent sense of the clause is supplication, though it can also be understood as exposing oneself to His forgiveness through action and prayer. What the reports agree on is the setting: the end of the night, when the day's claims have not yet begun.

## **3:18**

> Allah ˹Himself˺ is a Witness that there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—and so are the angels and people of knowledge. He is the Maintainer of justice. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Almighty, All-Wise

### **MEANING**

The argument returns to the surah’s opening theme and states it in the strongest available form: ***“Allah ˹Himself˺ is a Witness that there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—and so are the angels and people of knowledge. He is the Maintainer of justice. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Almighty, All-Wise.”*** God’s own testimony is placed first, and the testimony of the angels and of those who know is joined to it. The verse then names Him as the One who upholds justice, and repeats the negation a second time, now carried by two names.

The repetition is not redundant. The first negation is carried by the testimony; the second is carried by His might and wisdom. What is witnessed to by God, angels, and the knowledgeable is also what His power and wisdom establish, so the verse joins evidence to attribute.

### **WHO BEARS WITNESS, AND TO WHAT**

The angels and those described as ***“people of knowledge”*** are explained as joined to God’s name as co-witnesses, so that the statement means: God testifies to His own oneness, the angels testify to it, and those possessed of knowledge testify to it.

The purpose of the verse is explained as denying what the Christians who argued with the Prophet about Jesus attributed to him, and what idolaters attributed to God of partners and of lords taken besides Him. God informs them that He is the creator of everything other than Him and the Lord of everything any disbeliever or idolater has taken as a lord besides Him, and that this is what He, His angels, and those possessed of knowledge among His creation testify to. He begins with Himself, magnifying Himself and clearing Himself of what they ascribed to Him, just as He has taught His servants to begin their affairs with mention of Him before anyone else.

A report through Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq, from Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, states the point compactly: *“God bears ***witness that there is no god*** but He, and the angels and those possessed of knowledge, in contradiction to what they said”*—meaning the delegation of Najrān. Mujāhid, through Ibn Abī Najīḥ, explains *“justice”* as justice. A reading attributed to some Basran authorities is noted taking the verb to mean *“God has decreed,”* which is rejected on the grounds that testifying and decreeing are not the same thing.

### **THE MAINTAINER OF JUSTICE**

The phrase rendered ***“the Maintainer of justice”*** is explained as meaning that He is the One who undertakes justice among His creation, the word for justice being derived from the expressions *“he is equitable”* and *“he has been just,”* meaning he acted justly. Grammarians differed over what the word describes: some Basran grammarians took it as a circumstance of the pronoun in ***“except Him,”*** and some Kufans as a circumstance of God’s name, so that the sense would be that the God who upholds justice bears witness. A reading attributed to Ibn Masʿūd is cited in which the word appears as a definite description of those possessed of knowledge.

The view preferred is that it is a description of God, since the angels and those possessed of knowledge are joined to Him, so the description of upholding justice belongs to Him as well. The closing names are then explained: the Almighty is the One whom nothing He wills can resist and from whom no one He punishes can be defended, and the All-Wise is the One in whose governance nothing is flawed. The verse therefore closes the circle opened at the beginning of the surah: the living, self-sustaining God is the One whose oneness heaven and its knowers attest.

## **3:19**

> Certainly, Allah’s only Way is Islam. Those who were given the Scripture did not dispute ˹among themselves˺ out of mutual envy until knowledge came to them. Whoever denies Allah’s signs, then surely Allah is swift in reckoning

### **MEANING**

The testimony of the previous verse produces a conclusion: ***“Certainly, Allah’s only Way is Islam. Those who were given the Scripture did not dispute ˹among themselves˺ out of mutual envy until knowledge came to them. Whoever denies Allah’s signs, then surely Allah is swift in reckoning.”*** The way that counts with God is submission, and the verse states this immediately after the testimony to His oneness. The two belong together: since there is no god but Him, the way that belongs to Him is the way of submitting to Him.

The verse then accounts for disagreement: ***Those who were given the Scripture did not dispute ˹among themselves˺ out of mutual envy until knowledge came to them.*** When that knowledge reached them, they differed anyway. The timing is the point. The disagreement is not traced to an absence of evidence but to something in them the evidence could not remove.

### **WHAT SUBMISSION MEANS**

The word rendered *“Way”* is explained in this setting as obedience and lowliness, and poetic usage is cited for that sense: an Arabic poem uses it of people who were *“obedient in a state of abasement,”* al-Quṭāmī uses the verb of being humbled, and al-Aʿshā uses it of a people humbled in war. Submission is explained as yielding with lowliness and humility, the verb meaning to enter into submission, just as one says of a people that they entered into drought or into springtime.

The verse is therefore explained as meaning: the obedience that counts as obedience with Him is obedience to Him, the affirmation of tongues and hearts of servitude and lowliness toward Him, and their yielding in what He commanded and forbade without arrogance toward Him or turning away from Him, and without associating any of His creation with Him in servitude or divinity.

Qatādah, through Saʿīd, explains it as *“the testimony that there is no god but God, and affirming what came from God”*—the religion He legislated for Himself, sent His messengers with, and guided His friends to, accepting no other and rewarding by nothing else. Abū al-ʿĀliyah, through al-Rabīʿ, describes it as *“sincerity to God alone, worshipping Him with no partner, establishing prayer and giving the alms, with the rest of the obligations following.”* Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar explains it as *“what you are upon of the oneness of the Lord and the affirmation of the messengers.”*

### **WHY THEY DIFFERED**

The dispute is explained as concerning Jesus. Those who had received the Scripture differed about him and invented falsehoods about God, and the explanation states that they did so after they knew the truth of what they were disputing and were certain that what they were saying about him was a monstrous fabrication. Their disagreement was transgression of some of them against others, and a seeking of leadership, dominion, and authority.

Abū al-ʿĀliyah, through al-Rabīʿ, says they acted *“out of envy for this world and the pursuit of its dominion and authority, so that they killed one another over the world after having been the scholars of the people.”* Ibn ʿUmar is reported reciting this verse often and saying that from that very cause they were afflicted. Al-Rabīʿ relates that Moses, at his death, entrusted the Torah to seventy of the scholars of the Children of Israel, and that after three generations division fell among their descendants until they shed one another’s blood. Al-Rabīʿ understood those meant to be the Jews; Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar, through Ibn Isḥāq, understood them to be the Christians who were given the Gospel.

### **SWIFT IN RECKONING**

The closing clause is explained as meaning that ***“whoever denies”*** God’s proofs and signs, which He set up as a reminder for whoever reasons and as evidence for whoever takes heed, will find that God has enumerated his deeds and will repay him for them in the Hereafter. ***“Swift in reckoning”*** is explained as swift in enumeration: He preserves every deed without the effort of counting by hand or by heart that His creatures need. Mujāhid, through Ibn Abī Najīḥ, glosses it as *“His enumeration of them.”*

## **3:20**

> So if they argue with you ˹O Prophet˺, say, “I have submitted myself to Allah, and so have my followers.” And ask those who were given the Scripture and the illiterate ˹people˺, “Have you submitted yourselves ˹to Allah˺?” If they submit, they will be ˹rightly˺ guided. But if they turn away, then your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. And Allah is All-Seeing of ˹His˺ servants

### **MEANING**

The Prophet is told how to answer if the argument continues: ***So if they argue with you ˹O Prophet˺, say,*** ***“I have submitted myself to Allah, and so have my followers.”*** The instruction covers both the response and the counter-question. He is to state his own submission and that of those with him, and then put the same question to the People of the Book and to those who have no book: ***“Have you submitted yourselves ˹to Allah˺?”***

The verse then settles what counts as success: ***If they submit, they will be ˹rightly˺ guided. But if they turn away, then your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. And Allah is All-Seeing of ˹His˺ servants.*** The outcome is not placed in the Prophet’s hands. He delivers; the response belongs to others, and it is seen by the One who sees every servant.

### **WHAT SUBMITTING ONESELF MEANS**

The explanation takes the address to refer to the delegation of Najrān, who argued about Jesus with falsehood. The response he is told to give is explained as meaning: I have yielded to God alone with my tongue, my heart, and all my limbs. A reason is given for why the wording singles out the self: it is the noblest part of a human being and holds his beauty and dignity, so that when it humbles itself before something, everything less noble in the body has humbled itself with it.

***“And so have my followers”*** is explained as meaning that those who follow him have yielded themselves to God with him, the phrase being joined to the first person in ***“I have submitted.”*** The joining matters: the messenger does not submit alone, and what he declares is not a private position.

### **WHO IS ASKED**

The explanation identifies ***“those who were given the Scripture”*** as the Jews and the Christians, and ***“the illiterate ˹people˺”*** as the idolaters of the Arabs, who have no scripture. Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar, through Ibn Isḥāq, glosses the latter as those who have no scripture, and Ibn ʿAbbās, through Ibn Jurayj, is reported explaining them as those who do not write. The question put to both groups is the same, which is itself part of the point: those with a scripture and those without are asked one question.

That question is explained as asking whether they have singled out God’s oneness and made worship and divinity exclusively for the Lord of the worlds, setting aside the rivals and associates they join with Him in worship, while knowing that there is no lord besides Him and no god other than Him. ***“They will be ˹rightly˺ guided”*** is explained as their having found the path of truth and followed the clear way.

### **A QUESTION THAT IS ALSO A COMMAND**

A grammatical point is raised: how can ***“if they submit”*** follow a question? The answer given is that where speech carries the sense of a command though it is shaped as a question, this is normal. Parallels are cited from Qur’an 5:91, where the words rendered *“will you then desist?”* mean desist, and from Qur’an 5:112, where the disciples’ question to Jesus is in fact a request. A reading attributed to Ibn Masʿūd of Qur’an 61:10–11 is cited in which the command form appears where the standard reading has the statement.

### **THE LIMIT OF THE MESSENGER’S DUTY**

The closing clause is explained as meaning: ***“if they turn away”*** from what you call them to of submission and sincere devotion to God, then you are only a messenger who conveys, and nothing is required of you beyond delivering the message to those you were sent to and discharging the obedience you were charged with. The final name is explained as God’s knowing who accepts from among His creatures what you have been sent with and obeys you by submitting, and who turns away from it and rejects it. The verse therefore closes where it began, with the Prophet’s task defined: to say it, and to ask.

## **3:21**

> Indeed, those who deny Allah’s signs, kill the prophets unjustly, and kill people who stand up for justice—give them good news of a painful punishment

### **MEANING**

The passage now names a specific crime: ***“Indeed, those who deny Allah’s signs, kill the prophets unjustly, and kill people who stand up for justice—give them good news of a painful punishment.”*** Three acts are joined: denying the signs, killing the prophets, and killing those who call for justice. The announcement of punishment is framed as ***“good news,”*** which is the sharpest available way of saying that the outcome is certain and that those addressed have chosen it themselves.

The three acts are not random. Denying the signs removes the standard by which killing could be judged, and the second and third killings are the removal of the people who applied it. The verse therefore describes a single progression rather than three unrelated offences.

### **WHO IS MEANT**

The explanation identifies those meant as the people of the two scriptures, the Torah and the Gospel. A report through Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq, from Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, says that the verse gathers the people of both scriptures together and mentions what Jews and Christians alike originated and invented.

The killing of the prophets is explained as their killing the messengers God sent to them, who forbade them the acts of disobedience they were committing and the courses they were following after He had already prohibited them in their scriptures. Zachariah and his son John are named as examples of those killed.

### **HOW THE VERB IS READ**

The readings differ over one of the verbs. The generality of the reciters of Medina, the Hijaz, Basra, and Kufa, and of the reciters of the other cities, read it as killing. One later reader of Kufa read it as fighting, following a reading he attributed to the codex of Ibn Masʿūd. The reading preferred is killing, on the grounds of the consensus of the reciters and because the interpretation transmitted from the early authorities is to that effect.

### **THOSE WHO ENJOIN JUSTICE**

Reports explain who the third group are. Maʿqil ibn Abī Maskīn, through Ibn Abī Najīḥ, describes revelation coming to the Children of Israel, who would remind their people and be killed for it, after which men who had followed and believed them would stand and remind their people and be killed as well—those are the ones who enjoin justice among the people. Qatādah, through Ibn Abī Jaʿfar from his father, says that the followers of the prophets used to forbid them and remind them, and they killed them for it. Ibn Jurayj reports something similar, describing people among the Children of Israel who had not studied the scripture but to whom revelation came, who reminded their people and were killed for it.

Abū ʿUbaydah ibn al-Jarrāḥ, through Qabīṣah ibn Dhuʾayb from Makhūl, reports asking the Prophet who will be most severely punished on the Day of Resurrection, and the Prophet answering: *“A man who killed a prophet, or a man who commanded what is wrong and forbade what is right,”* then reciting this verse. He added that the Children of Israel killed forty-three prophets within a single hour of one morning, after which a hundred and twelve men of their worshippers stood and commanded right and forbade wrong, and were all killed at the end of that same day.

The reports agree on the pattern. Prophets were killed, and then those who merely repeated what the prophets had said were killed for saying it. The verse names both killings because the second shows what the first was really about.

## **3:22**

> They are the ones whose deeds are wasted in this world and the Hereafter. And they will have no helpers

### **MEANING**

The announcement is followed by the reason it is deserved: ***“They are the ones whose deeds are wasted in this world and the Hereafter. And they will have no helpers.”*** Their deeds come to nothing in both lives, and no one will come to their aid. The sentence is short and absolute, and it explains why the good news of the previous verse is really bad news: nothing good is left over from what they did, and no one will intervene for them.

The word rendered *“They”* is explained as referring to the people described in the verse immediately before it: those who deny the signs, kill the prophets, and kill those who enjoin justice. This verse therefore does not introduce a new group; it pronounces on the one already named.

### **WASTED IN THIS WORLD**

The explanation of wasted deeds in this life is that they gained through them no praise and no good mention among people, because they were upon error and falsehood. God did not raise any remembrance for them by those deeds; instead He cursed them, tore away their coverings, and exposed the ugliness of what they had concealed through the tongues of His prophets and messengers in the scriptures He sent down to them, leaving them a lasting disgrace for as long as this world remains. That, the explanation says, is how their deeds come to nothing here.

The point is that the waste is visible even before the reckoning. Deeds done in opposition to what God sent produce no honour and no lasting name, and what was hidden is brought into the open.

### **WHY THE DEEDS COME TO NOTHING**

Two things are worth holding together here. The first is that the verse does not say their deeds were never done. Killing prophets and silencing those who call for justice are real acts with real consequences, and the previous verse records them. What is said is that they come to nothing—that they yield no profit and no standing for the people who did them.

The second is that the waste is attributed to what the deeds were in themselves rather than to a later reversal, and the deeds in question are the ones Qur’an 3:21 has just named. Because they were disbelief in God, there is no reward attached to them that could be restored. The explanation does not describe a balance that tips the wrong way; it describes deeds that carry no weight at all.

### **WASTED IN THE HEREAFTER, AND NO HELPERS**

In the Hereafter, He has prepared for them the punishment He has described in His Book, and He has informed His servants that their deeds become worthless, carrying no reward, because those deeds were disbelief in God—and the requital of their people is abiding in the Fire.

The closing clause is explained as meaning that these people ***“will have no”*** helper to rescue them from God when He takes vengeance on them for the crimes they committed and the insolence they showed toward Him, and so to deliver them from Him. The two halves of the verse belong together. What they did yields nothing, and what they face cannot be averted by anyone else. A person with void deeds and absent helpers has nothing left to stand on, which is exactly what the previous verse announced to him in advance.

## **3:23**

> Have you not seen those who were given a portion of the Scriptures? Yet when they are invited to the Book of Allah to settle their disputes, some of them turn away heedlessly

### **MEANING**

The argument turns to a specific refusal: ***“Have you not seen those who were given a portion of the Scriptures? Yet when they are invited to the Book of Allah to settle their disputes, some of them turn away heedlessly.”*** The question is put to the Prophet to make him—and the reader—look at something. People who had been given a share of revelation are called to be judged by it, and a party of them refuses.

The refusal is the point, not the invitation. They were not ignorant of what they were being called to; the closing words say they ***“turn away”*** while disregarding it. The verse therefore describes people who possess scripture and decline to be bound by it at the moment it is applied to them.

### **WHICH BOOK THEY WERE CALLED TO**

The transmitted explanations differ over which book is meant. Ibn ʿAbbās, in reports through Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and ʿIkrimah, relates that the Prophet entered the house of study where a group of Jews were gathered and called them to God. Nuʿaym ibn ʿAmr and al-Ḥārith ibn Zayd asked him what religion he followed, and he answered that he followed the religion of Abraham. They said that Abraham had been a Jew, and he said to them: bring the Torah, for it is between us and you. They refused, and this verse was revealed about them.

Others take the book meant to be the one God sent down to Muḥammad. Qatādah, through Saʿīd, says that those meant are the Jews, called to God’s Book and to His prophet to judge between them, while they find him written down with them in the Torah and the Gospel, and then they ***“turn away”*** in disregard. Ibn Jurayj reports that the People of the Scripture were called to a book to judge between them with truth in matters of legal penalties, and that the Prophet called them to Islam, and they turned away from it.

### **WHY THE TORAH READING IS PREFERRED**

The view preferred is that a party of the Jews, who had been given knowledge of the Torah, were called to the scripture they professed to accept in order to settle something they had disputed with the Prophet. It is allowed that the dispute concerned his prophethood, or the religion of Abraham, or the call to Islam, or a particular legal penalty, since they had disputed with him about all of these and he had called them to the judgement of the Torah on each occasion, and ***“some of them”*** refused and concealed it.

The reason given for preferring this is that they denied the Qur’an while claiming to affirm the Torah, so the argument against them from what they themselves professed was more decisive and cut off their excuse more completely. The verse does not specify which dispute it was, and the explanation states that there is no need to know, because in every case what they were called to was something they were obliged by their own religion to answer.

The closing description is explained as their turning their backs on the scripture to whose judgement they had been called, disregarding it and turning from it while knowing its truth and its argument. The passage thus sets up the next verse, which supplies the belief that made such a refusal seem safe to them.

## **3:24**

> This is because they say, “The Fire will not touch us except for a few days.” They have been deceived in their faith by their wishful lying

### **MEANING**

The verse supplies the reason for the refusal just described: ***This is because they say,*** ***“The Fire will not touch us except for a few days.”*** ***They have been deceived in their faith by their wishful lying.*** The refusal to be judged is traced to a claim about the Fire, and that claim is traced in turn to something they invented. The chain matters: a false expectation about the outcome produced a refusal to be judged now.

The word rendered *“deceived”* carries the sense of being lulled into security. What deceived them was not an argument but their own fabrication, and the verse locates the deception ***“in their faith”***—in the very thing by which they expected to be safe.

### **THE NUMBERED DAYS**

The explanation given is that their refusal of the judgement of the Torah came from their saying that the Fire would not reach them except for a limited number of days, and that their Lord would then bring them out of it. The days are identified as forty: the days in which they worshipped the calf.

Qatādah, through Saʿīd, reports them saying that the Fire would not reach them except for the discharge of the oath, meaning the period in which they set up the calf, after which both the oath and the punishment would come to an end. Al-Rabīʿ, through Ibn Abī Jaʿfar from his father, reports the same: they said they would not be punished in the Fire except for forty days, the days in which they set up the calf.

The reports are consistent on the number and on what it referred to, which is worth noticing: the limited punishment they claimed for themselves was tied to a specific episode of their own history, not to a general promise. They took a bounded event and turned it into a standing exemption.

### **WHAT THEY INVENTED**

The falsehood that deceived them is explained as their claim to be *“the children of God and His beloved,”* together with their claim that God had promised their father Jacob that He would not enter any of his descendants into the Fire except for the discharge of an oath. God declared every part of that false, and informed His prophet Muḥammad that they are the people of the Fire, abiding in it, rather than those who believe in God, in His messengers, and in what they brought from Him.

Mujāhid, through Ibn Jurayj, is reported saying that what deceived them in their religion was their own claim that the Fire would not reach them except for numbered days. The verse therefore identifies the mechanism precisely. An inherited privilege was claimed without evidence and repeated until it shaped what they thought they could risk, and it was risked at exactly the point where the scripture they professed was being applied to them.

The connection back to the previous verse is what makes the warning sharp. Their refusal was not grounded in doubt about the Torah. They refused it because of a belief that they were safe regardless of what it said, and the verse names that belief as the thing that will fail them.

## **3:25**

> But how ˹horrible˺ will it be when We gather them together on the Day about which there is no doubt—when every soul will be paid in full for what it has done, and none will be wronged

### **MEANING**

The warning closes with a question: ***“But how ˹horrible˺ will it be when We gather them together on the Day about which there is no doubt—when every soul will be paid in full for what it has done, and none will be wronged.”*** The question is left open because the answer is meant to be felt rather than stated. What will their state be, on a Day whose coming is not in doubt, at which each person receives full payment and no one is wronged?

The verse is explained as a severe threat and a heavy warning addressed to those who said what they said and did what they did: turning away from God’s Book, deluding themselves about their Lord, and inventing falsehood. The greatness of what they will meet is measured by what God will do on that Day: bring them all together, and pay each soul fully according to what it earned.

### **A THREAT LEFT OPEN**

The question is deliberately unfinished, and that is part of how it works. A stated punishment can be measured and compared with what a person thinks he can bear; an unanswered question about a Day on which everything is settled cannot. The explanation calls it a severe threat and a heavy warning, and it is delivered to people who, in Qur’an 3:24, had just been described as untroubled by the prospect of the Fire.

What makes it heavy is its certainty. The Day is named as one whose coming is not in doubt, and the gathering is attributed to God Himself, so the only thing left open is the condition of those who arrive at it.

### **WHY THE PREPOSITION MATTERS**

A grammatical question is raised: why does the verse say they will be gathered *“for”* a Day rather than *“in”* it? The answer given is that the two prepositions do not carry the same sense here. With *“in,”* the sense would be: what punishment will they receive while they are in the Day of Resurrection. With *“for,”* the sense is that they are gathered for what will take place on that Day—for what God will decide between His creatures—so that an action is implied and an unstated predicate is supplied by the preposition itself. It is for that reason that the preposition meaning *“for”* is chosen.

The point is not merely grammatical. Being gathered for what occurs on that Day puts the emphasis on the judgement that will take place rather than on the interval that contains it.

### **PAID IN FULL, AND NOT WRONGED**

The phrase rendered ***“no doubt”*** is explained as no uncertainty about its coming. The clause about payment is explained as meaning that God will pay each soul the full due for what it earned of good and of evil, and that the closing words mean that the one who did good is not shortchanged of the reward for his good, nor is anyone who did evil punished for a crime he did not commit.

The verse thus closes the argument that began with the refusal to be judged. The people who trusted that the Fire would touch them only briefly are told that they will be gathered for a Day on which every deed is settled in full and no one is treated unjustly. The threat is not that the reckoning will be harsher than they deserve, but that it will be exactly what they have earned—and that the exemption they invented will not be produced.

## **3:26**

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O Allah! Lord over all authorities! You give authority to whoever You please and remove it from who You please; You honour whoever You please and disgrace who You please—all good is in Your Hands. Surely You ˹alone˺ are Most Capable of everything

### **MEANING**

The Prophet is told to open with an address rather than an argument:

***“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O Allah! Lord over all authorities! You give authority to whoever You please and remove it from who You please; You honour whoever You please and disgrace who You please—all good is in Your Hands. Surely You ˹alone˺ are Most Capable of everything***

The opening words are explained as meaning *“say, Muhammad: O God.”* What follows is one unbroken attribution. He possesses the dominion, He grants it, He takes it back, He grants might, He brings low, and every good lies with Him. None of it is credited to anyone besides Him, and the reason is stated at the close: He has power over every thing.

The verse therefore answers a claim by denying its premise. Those who attributed divinity to a creature were attributing to him what belongs only to the One who disposes of kingship itself, and the argument of the whole passage is that the disposal of kingship was never handed over.

### **THE OPENING ADDRESS**

Arab grammarians differed over the final letter of the divine name used here. It is a vocative, and a single unattached vocative normally takes the nominative, so the question was why this one carries an added letter at the end.

One view held that the letter was put in place of the vocative particle. Names without the definite article are called with that particle, as in *“O Zayd”* and *“O ʿAmr,”* and so, on this account, the letter replaced it at the end of the name, the way a letter replaces a dropped consonant in words like *“fam”* and *“ibnum.”*

Others rejected this, saying that the Arabs are heard calling with both the particle and the letter together. An Arabic poem is cited for it, *“whenever you prayed or magnified: O God, return our elder to us safe,”* with a variant reading *“whenever you glorified or magnified.”* A second is cited in which the name appears with a lightened final letter, and a third in which the particle and the name stand together. The alternative account is that a word was joined to it, the sense being *“O God, intend us with good,”* which then fused through frequency of use, its vowel shifting onto the letter before it. A parallel is adduced in the imperative meaning *“come here,”* said to be composed of an interrogative particle and the same word. Some Arabs, when the final letter is dropped, begin the name with a glottal stop, while others begin it without; those who drop it treat the initial as the definite article, and those who pronounce it suppose it belongs to the root.

### **THE OCCASION NAMED**

Qatādah, through Saʿīd, relates that the Prophet asked his Lord to place the dominion of Persia and Byzantium within his community, and that this passage came down in answer to that request. A second route from Qatādah gives the same account with the phrasing *“it was mentioned to us.”*

### **WHAT THE DOMINION MEANS**

Mujāhid, through Ibn Abī Najīḥ, is reported giving the sense of kingship in this place as *“prophethood”* — so that granting it is granting prophecy and withdrawing it is withdrawing it. Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, through Ibn Isḥāq, explains the address as *“Lord of the servants who owns the dominion; none decrees concerning them other than You.”*

The granting is explained as giving kingship to whom He wills, making him king and giving him authority over whom He wills; the withdrawing as stripping kingship away.

### **THE ELIDED WORDS**

A grammatical point is raised about the clause on withdrawing. It says that He withdraws kingship from whom He wills, without naming the withdrawal, because the sense is carried by what is already said: He withdraws it from whom He wills to withdraw it from. The parallel given is the idiom *“take what you wish,”* meaning *“take what you wish to take,”* and Qur’an 82:8, *“in whatever form He willed did He assemble you,”* understood as *“in whatever form He willed to assemble you in.”*

### **HONOURING, HUMBLING, AND WHAT LIES WITH HIM**

Honouring is explained as granting kingship and authority and extending power; humbling as stripping his kingship and setting his enemy over him. The clause about good is explained as meaning that all of it is with Him and returns to Him, and that no one is able to do any of it, because He has power over every thing — setting this against those the associators among the People of the Book and the unlettered Arabs took as a god and a lord besides Him, such as the Messiah, and such as the rivals the unlettered took as a lord.

## **3:27**

> You cause the night to pass into the day and the day into the night. You bring forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living. And You provide for whoever You will without limit.”

### **MEANING**

The prayer continues with four acts that no creature can perform:

***“You cause the night to pass into the day and the day into the night. You bring forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living. And You provide for whoever You will without limit.”***

The first pair describes alternation rather than replacement: what is shortened in one is added to the other, so that the two lengthen and shorten against each other across the year. The second pair describes two opposite productions, each of which yields the other’s source. The closing clause describes giving that is measured by nothing outside the giver.

### **ENTERING ONE INTO THE OTHER**

The verb is explained as causing to enter, from the same root as a man entering his house. What enters is the deficit: what He shortens of the night’s hours is put into the day’s hours, and what He shortens of the day’s hours is put into the night’s hours, so that the increase of one is the decrease of the other.

The explanations given converge on that reading with different emphases. Al-Suddī puts numbers on it: the night reaching fifteen hours and the day nine, and then the reverse. Ibn ʿAbbās, through ʿIkrimah, is reported saying that whatever is lacking from the day He places in the night, and whatever is lacking from the night He places in the day. Mujāhid, through Ibn Abī Najīḥ, describes the two as taking from each other in the hours, and Qatādah describes it as the deficit of one in the increase of the other. Al-Ḍaḥḥāk and Ibn Zayd stress the observable result: each is sometimes longer than the other, because one has taken from the other.

### **BRINGING FORTH LIVING FROM DEAD**

Three readings are recorded, and they are not treated as equally close to the surface of the wording.

The first is that He brings the living human being, and living cattle and beasts, out of the dead sperm-drop, and brings the dead sperm-drop out of the living human being and living cattle. This is the reading Ibn Masʿūd gives through Ibrāhīm, and it is the one Mujāhid, al-Ḍaḥḥāk, al-Suddī and Qatādah are reported giving. Ibn Zayd extends it to the grain: a dead seed from which a living plant comes, and a living plant from which a dead seed comes.

The second is that it means the date-palm from the stone and the stone from the palm, the ear from the grain and the grain from the ear, the egg from the hen and the hen from the egg. ʿIkrimah is reported giving this.

The third is that it means the believer from the disbeliever and the disbeliever from the believer, the believer being alive of heart and the disbeliever dead of heart. This is given by al-Ḥasan, through Qatādah and through Maʿmar. Salmān — or Ibn Masʿūd, the transmitter being unsure — is reported describing the clay of Adam as kneaded for forty nights, after which every good particle came into one hand and every bad particle into the other, and the two were mixed, and that this is why the believer comes out of the disbeliever and the disbeliever out of the believer. Al-Ḥasan also asks the question directly: *“do you not know that a disbeliever begets a believer, and that a believer begets a disbeliever?”*

The first reading is preferred, on the ground that what separates from a living body is dead, and the sperm-drop is dead by its separation, and God then produces from it a living person and living animals. The parallel adduced is Qur’an 2:28, *“how can you disbelieve in God when you were dead and He gave you life.”* The other two readings are said to have a comprehensible sense but not to be the dominant, familiar sense in ordinary usage, and the meanings of scripture are carried to the familiar rather than to the rare.

### **THE TWO READINGS OF THE WORD FOR DEAD**

The reciters differed over whether the word is read with a doubled middle letter or a light one. The distinction drawn is that the doubled form covers both what has not yet died but will die and what has already died, while the lightened form covers only what has died; for what is still to come the Arabs use the participial form, as in *“you will die tomorrow.”*

The doubled reading is preferred, and the reason given is that God brings the living out of the sperm-drop after it has separated and become dead, and will also bring it out while it is still in the man’s loins and alive — so the doubled form is wider in praise and more complete in commendation.

### **PROVISION WITHOUT RECKONING**

The closing clause is explained as His giving generously to whom He wills of His creatures without reckoning against the one He gives to, since He does not fear any diminution entering His stores or any perishing of what He holds. Al-Rabīʿ, through his father, is reported saying: *“provision goes out from Him without reckoning; He does not fear that what He has will decrease.”*

### **WHAT THE WHOLE PASSAGE IS DOING**

Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, through Ibn Isḥāq, gathers the argument. Even granting that Jesus was given power over the things they cite as proof of divinity — reviving the dead, healing the sick, shaping a bird from clay, and telling of hidden things — that was given to him as a sign for the people and as confirmation of the prophethood to which he was sent. There remains, of the dominion and power of God, what was never given to him: making kings, the placing of prophethood wherever He wills, the alternation of night and day, the bringing forth of the living out of the dead, the bringing forth of what is dead out of the living, and providing for whom He wills without reckoning. None of that was placed under the authority of Jesus, and so, the argument runs, there is no proof in the former for what they claim — since were he divine, all of it would be his, while he is known to them as one who fled from kings and moved from one country to another.

## **3:28**

> Believers should not take disbelievers as guardians instead of the believers—and whoever does so will have nothing to hope for from Allah—unless it is a precaution against their tyranny. And Allah warns you about Himself. And to Allah is the final return

### **MEANING**

A prohibition is issued to the believers, and it is stated in the strongest available form:

***“Believers should not take disbelievers as guardians instead of the believers—and whoever does so will have nothing to hope for from Allah—unless it is a precaution against their tyranny. And Allah warns you about Himself. And to Allah is the final return”***

The prohibition is not about ordinary contact. It is about taking them as backing and helpers, aligning with them in their religion, aiding them against the Muslims, and exposing the weak points of the Muslims to them. Anyone who does that has, in the words of the verse, no share with God at all: God is quit of him and he is quit of God, because he has left the religion and entered unbelief.

The exception is then stated, and it is narrow. It applies when believers are under the authority of the disbelievers and fear for their own lives: in that case they may display alliance with their tongues while concealing enmity, not supporting them in the unbelief they are upon and not assisting them against any Muslim by deed.

### **HOW THE PROHIBITION IS WORDED**

A grammatical note is recorded: the verb is jussive because it is a prohibition, but the final radical carries a short vowel because it meets another non-vocalised letter.

The word rendered *“guardians”* is explained as helpers, supporters and backing. Ibn ʿAbbās, through ʿAlī, is reported explaining the verse as a prohibition against being soft toward the disbelievers or taking them as intimates in place of the believers, with the qualification that if the disbelievers are in a position of dominance over them, they may show them softness while differing from them in religion — and that this is the meaning of the exception.

### **THE OCCASION GIVEN**

Ibn ʿAbbās, through Muḥammad ibn Abī Muḥammad, from ʿIkrimah or from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, gives the setting. Al-Ḥajjāj ibn ʿAmr, an ally of Kaʿb ibn al-Ashraf, of Ibn Abī al-Ḥuqayq and of Qays ibn Zayd, had been insinuating himself with a group of the Anṣār in order to turn them from their religion. Rifāʿah ibn al-Mundhir ibn Zanbar, ʿAbd Allāh ibn Jubayr and Saʿd ibn Khaythamah told those men to avoid those Jews and to beware of keeping their company, lest they be turned from their religion. The men refused and persisted in their close association, and this passage is said to have come down about them.

### **WHAT THE EXCEPTION ALLOWS**

The exception is explained in two ways, and the difference matters.

The first, and the one most of the early explanation follows, is that it permits speech only. Ibn ʿAbbās, through Ibn Jurayj, is reported glossing the phrase as *“speaking with the tongue while the heart is at rest in faith.”* Abū al-ʿĀliyah, through al-Rabīʿ, states it as *“fear is with the tongue and not with deeds.”* Al-Ḍaḥḥāk says the same and adds the condition that it applies to one who is forced to say something that is disobedience to God, and says it out of fear for his life while his heart is at rest in faith: no blame attaches to him, because the fear is on the tongue. ʿIkrimah sets a limit in the other direction: it holds only so long as no Muslim blood is shed and no Muslim property is made lawful. Mujāhid, through Ibn Abī Najīḥ, describes it as civility in worldly dealings and fair company. Al-Suddī describes the permitted display as showing alliance in their religion and disavowal of the believers, while stressing that this is a matter of the tongue.

The second is Qatādah’s, given through Saʿīd and through Maʿmar: he read the exception as kinship. On his reading it means that one may maintain a tie of blood with a disbeliever without taking him as an ally in religion, and through Maʿmar the gloss is *“that there be kinship between you and him, so you maintain it for that reason.”* Al-Ḥasan, through ʿAbbād ibn Manṣūr, is reported in the same direction: *“keep their company in this world honourably, kinsman or other; but in religion, no.”*

An objection is raised to this second reading. It has a coherent sense, but it is not the sense the plain wording carries: the phrase says *“from them,”* and the fear described is fear of the disbelievers and not of anyone else. On the reading that makes it kinship, the sense has to become fearing God on account of the kinship between you, and that is not what the dominant, familiar meaning of the words is. The rule applied is that the meaning of the Qur’an is carried to the apparent and familiar usage among the Arabs, not to the remote one.

### **THE TWO READINGS OF THE FINAL PHRASE**

The reciters differed over the last word of the exception. The general reading of the cities is on the pattern of a noun like *“tukhama,”* derived from the verb meaning to fear; a variant reads it on the pattern of a longer form. The reading transmitted by the mass of reciters is preferred, on the ground of its being the established and widely transmitted reading, and a report that cannot be mistaken is not to be set aside in favour of an isolated one.

### **THE CLOSING WARNING**

The final sentence is explained as God frightening His servants from Himself: that they commit no acts of disobedience and take no enemies of His as allies, since to Him is their return and their destination after death, on the Day they are gathered to the standing of account. The sense given is: when you come to Him having opposed what He commanded and done what He forbade — taking them as guardians in place of the believers — there will reach you of His punishment what you have no power to bear. So fear Him, and beware of that reaching you from Him, for His punishment is severe.

## **3:29**

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Whether you conceal what is in your hearts or reveal it, it is known to Allah. For He knows whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And Allah is Most Capable of everything.”

### **MEANING**

The command to speak is renewed, and what is to be said concerns what cannot be hidden:

***“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Whether you conceal what is in your hearts or reveal it, it is known to Allah. For He knows whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And Allah is Most Capable of everything.”***

The address is to those who had just been told not to take the disbelievers as guardians. What they hide of attachment to them, and what they show of it with their tongues and their hands, is equally known to God. The conclusion drawn for them is practical: do not conceal affection for them and do not display alliance to them, for there will then reach you of your Lord’s punishment what you cannot bear, since your secret and your open conduct are known to Him, and none of it escapes Him.

### **WHAT IS HIDDEN AND WHAT IS SHOWN**

The verse is built on a pair, and the pair is exhaustive. Concealing is explained as holding attachment to the disbelievers inwardly and keeping it secret; revealing is explained as making it appear from yourselves through your tongues and your acts.

Al-Suddī is reported saying that the purpose was to inform them of *“what they kept secret of that and what they made public.”* The point is not that one of the two is more visible to Him, but that neither is beyond Him — so that the concealed attachment cannot be treated as safe simply because no one saw it.

### **FROM THE WIDER TO THE NARROWER**

The second clause widens the claim before narrowing it again. ***“God knows whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth.”*** He is not ignorant of anything that is in a heaven or an earth or wherever it may be. The argument then runs from the greater to the lesser: if nothing in a heaven or an earth is concealed from Him, how could what is in your breasts of inclination toward them, in affection and love, or what you show them of aid in word and deed, be concealed from Him?

The structure is worth holding on to, because it explains why the verse moves the way it does. The knowledge asserted is not offered as an abstract attribute. It is offered as the reason the warning before it and the warning after it are serious: nothing in the matter has gone unrecorded, and so nothing in it has been left unpunished.

### **THE CLOSING ATTRIBUTE**

The verse ends by pairing knowledge with power. The explanation given is that God is able to bring forward the punishment for your taking them as allies and for your backing them against the believers, and able over everything else He wills. Nothing He intends is difficult for Him, and nothing He seeks is withheld from Him.

That pairing is what makes the verse a warning rather than a statement of fact. Knowledge alone would mean the attachment is recorded; power added to it means the consequence can be brought forward, in this life as well as in the one to which the preceding verse pointed. The same pairing closes the earlier verse of this passage, and it is repeated here because the warning it supports has been repeated: the prohibition on taking guardians has been restated in terms of what the heart conceals, and the ability to act on what the heart conceals is what makes the restatement bite.

### **WHERE THE VERSE SITS**

The instruction to speak is directed to the Prophet, but what he is to say is addressed to the believers, and it takes up the prohibition of the verse immediately before it. Qur’an 3:28 had dealt with the outward act of taking guardians and had allowed a narrow exception for the tongue under duress. This verse removes the comfort that exception might have given. The exception concerned what may be said when life is at risk; it said nothing about what the heart may hold. Here the heart is placed under the same scrutiny as the tongue, and the alliance that is neither spoken nor acted upon is declared known all the same.

The closing attribute then does the remaining work: the One who knows it is the One able to punish it, and the return to Him named at the end of the previous verse is where that will be settled.

## **3:30**

> ˹Watch for˺ the Day when every soul will be presented with whatever good it has done. And it will wish that its misdeeds were far off. And Allah warns you about Himself. And Allah is Ever Gracious to ˹His˺ servants

### **MEANING**

The warning is given a date, and then the scene on that date is described:

***“˹Watch for˺ the Day when every soul will be presented with whatever good it has done. And it will wish that its misdeeds were far off. And Allah warns you about Himself. And Allah is Ever Gracious to ˹His˺ servants”***

Two things are said about that Day. The first is that every person will find the good he did brought forward and kept complete for him. The second concerns the evil: he will long for a great distance to lie between himself and it. The distance wished for is not a distance in space but a distance in time and in consequence — a wish never to meet what he did.

### **PRESENTED, AND COMPLETE**

The word rendered as being presented is explained by Qatādah, through Saʿīd, with a single word: *“complete,”* or *“paid in full.”* Nothing of the good has been allowed to fall away, and nothing of it has been reduced.

A grammatical observation is recorded alongside this. The clause has the form of a command to remember, and it is said that the Qur’an came for command and for reminder, so that the sense is *“remember the day on which…”* — the same pattern found elsewhere in phrases of the form *“fear a day on which…”* A further point is made about the relative word: it carries the sense of *“that which,”* and cannot be read as a conditional particle, because the verb of finding falls upon it. The clause about evil is conjoined to the same relative, and the verb inside it is a relative clause in the nominative position, because of what follows it.

### **THE DISTANCE WISHED FOR**

The word rendered as ***“far off”*** is explained as a limit or a term that is far away. Al-Suddī glosses it as *“a distant place,”* and Ibn Jurayj as *“an appointed term.”*

A line of Arabic poetry is cited for the sense, by al-Ṭirimmāḥ: *“every living being completes the full count of life, and perishes when its term expires”* — where the word means the limit of his appointed span.

Al-Ḥasan, through ʿAbbād ibn Manṣūr, describes the wish itself: *“one of them would be pleased never to meet that deed of his at all; that becomes his hope, whereas in this world it was a sin he used to enjoy.”* The contrast he draws is the whole force of the clause. What was pleasurable to do becomes unendurable to face, and the wish is not for a lighter punishment but for separation from the deed.

### **THE WARNING, AND THE GRACIOUSNESS BESIDE IT**

The verse then repeats the warning of the preceding passage: God cautions you concerning Himself, so that you do not earn His anger by doing what angers Him and then meet Him on that Day while He is angry with you, and there reaches you of His painful punishment what you cannot bear.

It is then followed by the statement that God is ***“gracious to His servants,”*** and the juxtaposition is explained rather than softened. Al-Ḥasan, through ʿAmr from Ibn ʿUyaynah, is reported saying: ***“part of His graciousness to them is that He warned them about Himself.”*** The warning is placed on the same side as the mercy. He cautions them against His own punishment, frightens them of it, and forbids them the disobedience He has forbidden — and that cautioning is named as an instance of the graciousness, not as an exception to it.

### **HOW THE TWO HALVES HOLD TOGETHER**

The verse is doing two things at once, and the sequence matters. It first makes the record complete: every good is kept intact and brought forward, and every evil is placed before its doer in a way he cannot evade. Then it names the feeling that will accompany the second of those, and it is a wish for distance rather than a plea for mercy — which is why the closing words are needed.

For without them the verse would end in a threat with nothing to turn to. With them, the threat itself is located inside the mercy: the warning is issued precisely because there is still time to act on it, and the One who issues it is the One described as gracious toward the servants to whom it is addressed. The good kept complete and the evil wished away are, in the end, the same record seen from two directions.

## **3:31**

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “If you ˹sincerely˺ love Allah, then follow me; Allah will love you and forgive your sins. For Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

### **MEANING**

A claim is put to the test, and the test is a single instruction:

***“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “If you ˹sincerely˺ love Allah, then follow me; Allah will love you and forgive your sins. For Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”***

The claim of loving God is not dismissed; it is made answerable to something observable. Following the Prophet is set down as the sign by which the claim is verified, so that love of God is not left as a feeling someone announces about himself.

The result promised is reciprocal. The one who follows is loved by God, and his offences are forgiven — the forgiveness covering what has passed of them, with the closing names confirming it.

### **WHO IS BEING ADDRESSED**

Two settings are given for why this was revealed, and they differ.

The first is that it was revealed about people who said, in the Prophet’s lifetime, that they loved their Lord. Al-Ḥasan reports it in that form, and Ibn Jurayj gives it in nearly the same words: there were people who claimed to love God, saying *“we love our Lord,”* and they were commanded to follow Muḥammad, with following him made the mark of that love.

The second is that it is an instruction to the Prophet to say this to the delegation of the Christians of Najrān: if what they say about Jesus is said out of veneration and love of God, then they should follow Muḥammad. Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, through Ibn Isḥāq, gives it in that form, glossing the forgiveness as covering *“what has passed of your unbelief.”*

The second is preferred, and the reason given is textual rather than external. No group is mentioned in this sūrah before this verse, or after it, who claimed to love God or to venerate Him, and so there is nothing in the passage for the verse to answer. What surrounds it, from the opening of the sūrah onward, is an argument against the claims made about the Messiah, and a verse placed in the middle of that argument is best read as continuing it. The first account is not rejected outright; it is noted that if al-Ḥasan meant by *“those people”* the delegation from Najrān, then his reading is the same as the one preferred.

### **HOW THE VERSE IS THEN READ**

On that reading the sense runs: say, Muḥammad, to the delegation from the Christians of Najrān — if you are as you claim, loving God, and if you venerate the Messiah and say about him what you say out of love for your Lord, then make your words true, if you are truthful, by following me. You know that I am sent to you by God, just as Jesus was sent to those he was sent to. If you follow my call and believe what I have brought you from God, He will forgive you your offences, pardoning the punishment for them and overlooking what has passed of them, for He forgives the offences of His believing servants and is merciful to them and to others of His creation.

### **WHY THE SIGN IS A DEED**

The structure of the verse is worth noticing, because the whole force sits in it. A conditional is stated — *“if you love God”* — and its response is not another statement of feeling but an act: ***“follow me.”*** What is promised in return is also not a feeling but a standing with God, and a clearing of the record.

That is why the verse can be used as a test at all. An inward state that produces nothing observable cannot be checked, and a claim that cannot be checked cannot be argued with. By attaching the claim to following, the verse makes it answerable; by promising love in return, it makes the answer worth having.

### **THE CLOSING NAMES**

The verse closes with two names: the one who forgives, and the one who shows mercy. Their placement matters. A demand that turns a claim into a deed, and then holds the deed to account, would be a hard thing to leave unqualified — the effect would be to expose everyone who has ever made the claim. The closing names do the opposite: the same One who sets the test is the One who forgives what fails it and is merciful to those who come to it.

The forgiveness is specified as covering what has passed, which is why the promise is addressed to people whose past was precisely the problem. The delegation being argued with in this passage had a history of rejecting what came to them, and the verse offers the following of the Prophet as the point at which that history is closed rather than held.

## **3:32**

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Obey Allah and His Messenger.” If they still turn away, then truly Allah does not like the disbelievers

### **MEANING**

The instruction to speak continues, and what is to be said is a demand with a consequence attached:

***Say, ˹O Prophet,˺*** ***“Obey Allah and His Messenger.”*** ***If they still turn away, then truly Allah does not like the disbelievers***

Two objects of obedience are named together, and they are not separable: obeying God and obeying the one He sent. The consequence is then stated in the negative form — not that they will be punished, but that God withholds His love from those who disbelieve. The withholding of love is the substance of the threat.

### **WHO IS TOLD TO OBEY**

The command is explained as addressed to the Prophet, to be delivered to the delegation from the Christians of Najrān. Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, through Ibn Isḥāq, gives the sense as: obey God and the Messenger Muḥammad — *“and you recognise him,”* meaning the delegation — *“and you find him written down with you”* in your scripture.

The appeal therefore rests on something they already possess. They are not being asked to accept a stranger; they are being asked to acknowledge what their own book is said to contain about him.

### **WHAT TURNING AWAY MEANS HERE**

The conditional clause is explained as their turning their backs on what they were called to and abandoning it, persisting in their unbelief. The verse then instructs the Prophet to inform them that God withholds His love from the one who disbelieves by denying a truth he has come to know and rejecting it after knowing it.

That last qualification is the point of the sentence. The disbelief named here is not ignorance; it is rejection in the face of recognition. The explanation states it plainly: they deny his prophethood and reject the truth he is upon after having known that his affair is sound and his prophethood real.

### **THE PAIRING OF GOD AND MESSENGER**

The verse joins obedience to God with obedience to the Messenger without distinguishing between them, and that joining is the substance of the demand. It is the same joining made in the previous verse, where following the Messenger was set down as the sign of loving God; here it is stated as a command rather than as a test.

What follows from it is that the demand cannot be met partly. One cannot accept the One who sent while refusing the one who was sent, because the second is how the first is obeyed at all. This is why the address to the delegation presses on recognition: the obstacle is not that they lack grounds, but that they have grounds and turn from them.

### **WHY THE CONSEQUENCE IS STATED AS IT IS**

The closing clause is worth pausing on, because a threat of punishment would have been the expected form and is not what is given. What is stated is that God withholds His love from those who disbelieve.

The difference is real. Punishment is an event that may be delayed; not being loved is a standing condition, and it describes what the person is now rather than what will happen to him later. It also matches the opening of the verse: the demand was for obedience, which is a relationship, and what is withdrawn is the corresponding relationship rather than a benefit.

It is also the mirror of the promise in Qur’an 3:31, where following brings the love of God and the forgiveness of offences. The two verses stand as a pair — one stating what comes of following, the other what is withheld from those who turn from it — and between them the choice is left with the delegation the passage has been arguing with since its opening.

## **3:33**

> Indeed, Allah chose Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of ’Imrân above all people ˹of their time˺

### **MEANING**

The argument now turns from the delegation to a lineage, and the names are given in order:

***“Indeed, Allah chose Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of ’Imrân above all people ˹of their time˺”***

Two individuals are named first, and then two households. The choosing is explained as selection for their religion: God chose them for the religion they were upon, since they were people of submission, and in naming them He declared that religion chosen over every other religion that opposed it.

### **WHAT THE TWO HOUSEHOLDS MEAN**

The explanation is specific about the scope of the phrase *“family.”* A man’s family, in this usage, means his followers and his people and those who are upon his religion. So the households of Abraham and of ʿImrān mean the believers among them, not everyone descended from them.

Ibn ʿAbbās, through ʿAlī, is reported giving exactly that: ***“they are the believers from the family of Abraham, the family of ʿImrān, the family of Yāsīn and the family of Muḥammad”*** and the proof he cites is Qur’an 3:68, *“the people most entitled to Abraham are those who followed him”* — and they are the believers. Qatādah, through Saʿīd, describes Adam and Noah as two prophets whom God chose over all humankind; through Maʿmar he speaks of two sound houses and two sound men being favoured over all humankind, noting that Muḥammad was of the household of Abraham. Al-Ḥasan, through ʿAbbād ibn Manṣūr, explains the favouring as being by prophethood, over all humankind — *“they were the prophets, God-fearing, chosen for their Lord.”*

### **WHY THESE FOUR**

The selection is not arbitrary, and the sūrah has prepared for it. The passage has been arguing against claims made about Jesus on behalf of his mother’s house, and it will go on to tell the story of that house in detail. Naming the household of ʿImrān here places what follows inside a chosen lineage rather than outside it — the honouring of that household is asserted before its story is told, so that the story is read as the fulfilment of the assertion rather than as its evidence.

Adam and Noah are named first because the choosing begins where humanity begins, and is renewed after the flood. The two households are then named together, and the next verse will say what joins them.

### **THE LIMIT ON THE PHRASING**

The verse says they were chosen over all humankind, and the explanation carries a qualification: over humankind of their era. Ibn Jurayj is reported giving that reading explicitly.

The qualification matters for how the claim is to be understood. It is not a comparison across all of history in which later figures are ranked below earlier ones; it is a statement about the standing of these households among those who were contemporary with them. Read the other way, the verse would be making a claim it does not need to make and that the narrative which follows does not support.

### **WHAT IS BEING CHOSEN**

The choosing is for religion, and the religion is submission. That is the thread running from this verse through the rest of the passage: Adam and Noah, along with the two households of Abraham and ʿImrān, are named together because they stand on the same thing, and the argument with the Christians of Najrān is an argument about whether Jesus belongs to that thing or stands outside it.

The verse answers before the story begins. Jesus will be told, in the verses to come, to say that God is his Lord and theirs and to worship Him — and that, the sūrah will say, is the straight path. The naming of the households here is what makes that claim continuous rather than novel.

## **3:34**

> They are descendants of one another. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing

### **MEANING**

The verse just read is compressed into a single clause that carries the whole argument:

***“They are descendants of one another. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing”***

The first sentence says what joins the households named before it, and it says it in the strongest available way: they are offspring, each from the other. The second names two attributes, and both are directed at something specific that the next verse will tell.

### **HOW THE CLAUSE IS CONSTRUED**

A grammatical note is recorded about why the word for offspring is in the accusative. It is explained as a severance from the two households named in the previous verse, because the word for offspring is indefinite while the households are definite.

An alternative is then allowed: that it stands as a second object of the choosing, so that the sense is *“He chose offspring, some of them from others.”* Both are said to be sound, and the difference does not affect the meaning.

### **WHAT ‘ONE OF ANOTHER’ MEANS**

The phrase is explained as describing alliance in religion and mutual support upon submission and the truth — not descent alone. The proof-texts cited are Qur’an 9:71, *“the believing men and believing women are allies of each other,”* and Qur’an 9:67, *“the hypocrites, men and women, are of each other”*: in both places the phrase means that their religion is one and their way is one.

So the sense given is: offspring whose religion is the religion of each other, whose word is one and whose community is one in the oneness of God and obedience to Him. Qatādah, through Saʿīd, glosses it as *“in intention, and deed, and sincerity, and singling Him out.”*

This is the point on which the verse turns. If the bond were descent, the claim would be trivial — of course one generation comes from another. The claim being made is that the two households named in Qur’an 3:33 hold one religion, and that this is what makes them a single line rather than two.

### **WHY THE TWO ATTRIBUTES ARE NAMED HERE**

The closing names are explained with reference to what follows immediately: God is the one who hears the speech of the wife of ʿImrān, and the one who knows what she keeps hidden in herself, when she vows to Him what is in her womb as one set free.

The verse is therefore a hinge. It looks back to the households it summarises, and it looks forward to the vow that is about to be reported in full, and the two attributes named in it are precisely the two that the vow will call upon. The woman in the next verse addresses God as the one who hears and the one who knows, and this verse has already said that He is both.

### **WHAT THE VERSE DOES IN THE PASSAGE**

The passage has been arguing against those who claimed a special standing for Jesus on the basis of his mother’s house. This verse answers that claim in a way that does not deny the honour but relocates it. The household of ʿImrān is honoured — but what honours it is the religion it holds, and that religion is the same one held by the household of Abraham before it and by Adam and Noah before both.

That is why the bond is stated as religion rather than blood, and why the next move the sūrah makes is to tell the story of that household from inside: a vow made by a woman, a child born, a dedication, and a prayer for protection. The honour is real, and the narrative shows what it consisted of.

## **3:35**

> ˹Remember˺ when the wife of ’Imrân said, “My Lord! I dedicate what is in my womb entirely to Your service, so accept it from me. You ˹alone˺ are truly the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.”

### **MEANING**

The narrative opens with a vow, and the vow is reported in the woman’s own words:

***“˹Remember˺ when the wife of ’Imrân said, “My Lord! I dedicate what is in my womb entirely to Your service, so accept it from me. You ˹alone˺ are truly the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.”***

The sense given is: I have made it an obligation upon myself, for You, that ***“what is in my womb”*** be set free for Your worship. Setting free is explained as confining the child to His service and the service of His sanctuary, released from the service of anything else, and made solely for Him.

The request that follows is the natural one: that He receive it from her. And the two names she closes with are the two the previous verse has just attributed to God — the one who hears what she says and the one who knows what she intends and wills, nothing of her affair, open or hidden, being concealed from Him.

### **WHO SHE WAS**

She is named as the mother of Mary, the daughter of ʿImrān, the mother of Jesus. Her own name is given as Ḥannah, the daughter of Fāqūdh, and the genealogy of her husband ʿImrān is traced back through the line of Solomon to David.

The opening word of the verse — *“when”* — is explained as attaching to the attribute of hearing named in the verse before, so that the two verses are read together: He hears her saying this.

### **WHY SHE MADE THE VOW**

The account given is that she had been prevented from bearing children until she was advanced in years, and that she was of a house that held a station with God. She was sitting in the shade of a tree and saw a bird feeding its chick, and her soul stirred for a child, so she called upon God to grant her one, and she conceived Mary — and ʿImrān died while she was carrying her. When she knew there was a child in her womb she made it a vowed offering to God.

The vowed offering is explained as devoting the child to God, making it confined to the sanctuary, with no benefit taken from it in any of the affairs of this world. Another report adds the detail that she and the mother of John were sisters, married to ʿImrān and to Zachariah respectively, and that ʿImrān died while she was pregnant.

### **WHAT ‘SET FREE’ MEANT IN PRACTICE**

The explanations converge on a single practice. Mujāhid, through al-Naḍr ibn ʿArabī, is reported glossing the term as *“a servant for the church,”* and through Ibn Abī Najīḥ as *“for the church, to serve it.”* Al-Shaʿbī gives it as *“I freed her for worship,”* and through Ismāʿīl as *“I placed her in the church and freed her for worship.”* Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, through ʿAṭāʾ, says *“for the synagogue and the church,”* and through Sālim *“set free for worship.”* Mujāhid, through Khuṣayf, gives *“pure, with nothing of the affairs of this world mixed into it.”*

Qatādah, through Saʿīd, describes the practice itself: *“they used to set free only males, and the one set free, once freed, was placed in the church and did not leave it; he attended to it and swept it.”* Al-Rabīʿ, through his father, gives the same. Al-Suddī says she thought what was in her womb was a boy and so dedicated it, expecting it would not work in this world. Al-Ḍaḥḥāk gives it as *“I made her child for God and for those who study the scripture and learn it.”*

ʿIkrimah, through al-Qāsim ibn Abī Bazza, adds that she was an old barren woman who used to envy women their children, and that she said: *“O God, it is binding upon me as a vow of gratitude, if You provide me with a child, that I give him in charity to the Jerusalem sanctuary, to be one of its keepers and servants.”*

### **THE TWO NAMES SHE CLOSES WITH**

The closing of her prayer is explained as: *“You are the One who hears what I say and supplicate, and the One who knows what I intend in myself and will.”* The pairing is deliberate, and it covers the whole of what a vow is. A vow is speech, and so it needs to be heard; it is also an intention formed before the speech, and so it needs to be known. She names both because she is about to discover that the vow she made does not match what she will be given.

## **3:36**

> When she delivered, she said, “My Lord! I have given birth to a girl,”—and Allah fully knew what she had delivered—“and the male is not like the female. I have named her Mary, and I seek Your protection for her and her offspring from Satan, the accursed.”

### **MEANING**

The vow is answered, and the answer is not what was vowed:

***When she delivered, she said,*** ***“My Lord! I have given birth to a girl,”*** ***—and Allah fully knew what she had delivered—*** ***“and the male is not like the female. I have named her Mary, and I seek Your protection for her and her offspring from Satan, the accursed.”***

The birth is announced as a disappointment, and the reason is given in her own words: ***“the male is not like the female.”*** What had been vowed was a child set apart for the sanctuary, and a female, in the practice of that time, could not serve there. Her second act is to name the child, and her third is to place the child, and those who would come from her, under God’s protection.

The intervening clause is then read in the way that matters most: God knew best what had been born to her.

### **THE READING OF THE MIDDLE CLAUSE**

A small grammatical point carries theological weight here. The verb is feminine because it refers to the vowed child, not to the word for *“what”* in the previous verse — had it referred to that, the wording would have been different. The word for delivering is simply the word for giving birth.

The reciters differed over the clause about God’s knowledge. The general reading of the reciters makes it a statement by God about Himself — that He knows best what was delivered to her — rather than part of her speech. Some early reciters read it as her own words, so that she is saying that God knows best what has been born to her. The reading transmitted by the mass of reciters is preferred, and an isolated reading is not to be set against it.

### **WHY SHE SAID WHAT SHE SAID**

Her words are explained as an apology to her Lord, since the vow had been made while she was pregnant and the child dedicated to His service — and the reason she gives is that males are stronger for that service and better able to perform it, while a female is not suited in some of her conditions to entering the sanctuary and attending the church, because of what befalls her of menstruation and the bleeding after birth.

Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq states it as: males are stronger for that than females. Qatādah, through Saʿīd, reports that *“a woman could not be made to do that”* — meaning being set apart for the church, placed in it, attending to it and sweeping it without leaving — *“because of what afflicts her of menstruation and harm,”* and that her words on that account were ***“the male is not like the female”***. Al-Rabīʿ, through his father, adds that her hope had been for a boy, because a woman cannot do it. Al-Suddī says her thought was that the child she carried was a boy and had dedicated it on that basis. ʿIkrimah, through al-Qāsim ibn Abī Bazza, gives it as referring to menstruation, and to a woman not being fit to be among men — and notes that it is her mother who says this.

### **THE PRAYER FOR REFUGE**

She then says that she places her refuge, and the refuge of those who will come from her, with God against the accursed Satan. The word for refuge is explained at its root as a shelter, a place of resort and a fortress.

The explanation then states that God answered her: He granted refuge to her and to those who came from her, and gave Satan no way against them.

### **WHAT IS SAID ABOUT THAT PROTECTION**

A number of accounts are given on this point, and they are attributed differently.

Abū Hurayrah is reported relating, through several routes, that no child of Adam is born without Satan touching him — or pricking him, or squeezing him — and that the child cries out at birth from that touch, except Mary and her son. In one version a veil was struck and the prick landed on the veil instead of reaching them; in another, he was prevented from them by a barrier.

Ibn ʿAbbās, through ʿIkrimah, is reported saying that no child is born without crying out, except the Messiah son of Mary: Satan was not given authority over him and did not prick him.

Qatādah gives the same account and adds that the two of them did not commit offences as the rest of Adam’s children do, and that Jesus used to walk on water as one walks on land, out of what God had given him of certainty and sincerity. Al-Rabīʿ, through his father, reports both of these and adds a saying attributed to Jesus praising his Lord: *“He granted refuge to me and my mother from the accursed Satan, and there was no way for him against us.”*

Wahb ibn Munabbih, through al-Mundhir ibn al-Nuʿmān, gives an account set at the birth: the devils came to Iblīs saying the heads of the idols had been turned down; he flew to the ends of the earth and found nothing, then to the seas and found nothing, then flew again and found the child born at a donkey’s manger with the angels surrounding him. He told them that a prophet had been born that night and that *“no female has ever conceived or borne a child but that I was present with her — except this one,”* and told them to despair of the idols being worshipped after that night, and to approach the children of Adam instead through levity and haste.

These accounts differ in detail and in whose authority they are given on, and the differences are worth preserving rather than smoothing out. What they agree on is the claim that the prayer made in this verse was answered.

## **3:37**

> So her Lord accepted her graciously and blessed her with a pleasant upbringing—entrusting her to the care of Zachariah. Whenever Zachariah visited her in the sanctuary, he found her supplied with provisions. He exclaimed, “O Mary! Where did this come from?” She replied, “It is from Allah. Surely Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit.”

### **MEANING**

The acceptance is stated twice, once as an act and once as a process:

***So her Lord accepted her graciously and blessed her with a pleasant upbringing—entrusting her to the care of Zachariah. Whenever Zachariah visited her in the sanctuary, he found her supplied with provisions. He exclaimed,*** ***“O Mary! Where did this come from?”*** ***She replied,*** ***“It is from Allah. Surely Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit.”***

The vow that had been made for a male child is accepted anyway, and accepted well. The child is then made to grow well and placed with a guardian. What follows is a scene repeated often enough to be described as habitual: her guardian enters and finds provision with her that he did not bring, and her answer locates the source.

### **TWO UNUSUAL FORMS**

A grammatical note is recorded on the word for acceptance. It is a verbal noun, but not formed on the pattern of the verb it belongs to; on the verb’s own pattern it would have taken another shape. The Arabs often bring verbal nouns on the root form even where the verb has been augmented, as in *“so-and-so spoke a speaking.”* The same is said of *“made her grow a growing.”*

A second note concerns that first word. Abū ʿAmr ibn al-ʿAlāʾ is reported saying, through al-Yazīdī from Abū ʿUbayd, that he had not heard the Arabs pronounce its first letter with the short vowel that analogy requires, as in words of the same pattern meaning entering and leaving, and that he knew of no other word in Arabic speech resembling it.

The growing is explained as her growing in His nourishment until she was complete — a full-grown woman. Ibn Jurayj states it as *“she grew in the nourishment of God.”*

### **WHO TOOK HER IN**

The reciters differed over the verb. The general reading of the reciters of the Ḥijāz, Medina and Basra reads it lightly, meaning that Zachariah took her in, and they support this from Qur’an 3:44, *“which of them should take charge of Mary.”* The general reading of the Kufans reads it with a doubled middle letter, meaning that God made Zachariah her guardian.

The doubled reading is preferred. The argument is that Zachariah took her in only because God awarded her to him by the casting of lots and the sign He produced for his rivals. The objection is raised that the other side’s proof-text argues against them: it is normal in Arabic to say *“so-and-so made someone take charge of a thing and then he took charge of it,”* so the two are not in conflict.

The reciters also differed over the name itself: the Medinans read it long, the Kufans short. Both are known dialects and both readings are widespread, so either is correct; a third dialect shortening it to a form resembling a relational adjective is not permitted in recitation, because it differs from the copies.

The word is explained from poetry: *“he is a gatherer of what strays of grazing beasts,”* with a variant *“of what strays and flees”* — meaning that what has wandered off he gathers to himself.

### **HOW THE LOTS WERE CAST**

Two accounts are given of the sign, and they are not reconciled. One is that Zachariah’s arrow stood fixed in the water and did not move while the current carried the others away. The other is that his arrow travelled upstream while the others went down with the flow. Whichever it was, the point made is that it was a judgement from God in his favour against his rivals.

Al-Suddī gives the fullest account. Those who wrote the Torah, when someone was brought to them, used to cast lots over who would take him and teach him. Zachariah was the best of them at the time, and Mary’s maternal aunt was married to him. When they brought her they cast lots, and Zachariah said *“I have the most right to her; her aunt is married to me.”* They refused, so they went out to the Jordan and cast the pens they wrote with — and the pens floated away while Zachariah’s pen stood upright on its end as though in clay, so he took her. ʿIkrimah, through al-Qāsim ibn Abī Bazza, adds the mother’s words: *“here is the vowed one; I have set her free, and she is my daughter, and no menstruating woman enters the church, and I will not take her back to my house.”* Qatādah notes that she was the daughter of their chief and imam and that their scholars contended over her; Mujāhid describes it as winning the lot with his pen.

A different account is also recorded: that Zachariah took her in without any casting of lots, because her mother died after her father while she was small and her aunt, the mother of John, was with him. On this account the casting came later, when a hardship struck them and Zachariah grew too weak to bear her upkeep.

### **THE PROVISION FOUND WITH HER**

The provision is explained in two ways. The first is that it was winter fruit in summer and summer fruit in winter. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported mentioning grapes out of season, and in another version *“the fruits of Paradise”*; Ibrāhīm and Qatādah say fruit out of its season; al-Ḍaḥḥāk and al-Suddī describe the two seasons inverted. Al-Rabīʿ adds that Zachariah had put seven doors between himself and her. Al-Ḥasan says it was provision from heaven, not from people.

The second is that what was found with her exceeded what he used to bring her. Ibn Isḥāq gives this at length. When she came of age they placed her in the church for her mother’s vow. Then a famine struck, and Zachariah told the Children of Israel he had grown too weak to carry her upkeep; they said they too were exhausted; and they pushed her from one to another until they cast lots, and the lot fell to a carpenter named Jurayj. Mary told him, *“think well of God, for God will provide for us.”* Jurayj then began to be provided for on her account, and brought her each day from his earnings what would suffice her; and when he brought it in, God made it grow, so that Zachariah would come in and find provision beyond what had been brought.

### **THE QUESTION AND THE ANSWER**

The question is explained as one of wonderment: from what source did you get this? Al-Rabīʿ and others add that he used to lock seven doors behind him and go out, then come in and find the fruit of one season in another, and would marvel and ask her, and she would say: from God.

Her answer is explained as: God is the one who provided it for me, and sent it to me and gave it to me.

The closing words are explained at length. God sends provision to whom He pleases without counting and without a number by which He reckons against His servant — because sending it does not diminish His stores, and His giving and reckoning add nothing to what He holds. Reckoning belongs to one who fears that his dominion will diminish, or to one who gives without knowing what he gives.

## **3:38**

> Then and there Zachariah prayed to his Lord, saying, “My Lord! Grant me—by your grace—righteous offspring. You are certainly the Hearer of ˹all˺ prayers.”

### **MEANING**

The scene produces a prayer, and the prayer is dated by a single word:

***Then and there Zachariah prayed to his Lord, saying,*** ***“My Lord! Grant me—by your grace—righteous offspring. You are certainly the Hearer of ˹all˺ prayers.”***

The opening phrase means *“at that”* — at the point of seeing what he saw. What he saw was provision reaching Mary without any human being causing it, including fresh fruit at a season when it does not exist on earth. From that he conceived hope for a child despite his own age and his wife’s barrenness.

The reasoning is stated explicitly: the One who provided Mary with summer fruit in winter and winter fruit in summer, while she was cut off from people, is able to provide a child from a woman past the age of bearing. Both are departures from the customary course, and that is precisely the connection.

### **WHY THE HOPE AROSE WHERE IT DID**

Al-Suddī is reported giving the inference in Zachariah’s own words: ***“a Lord who gave her this out of its season is certainly able to grant me righteous offspring.”*** He then desired a child, stood and prayed, and called upon God secretly.

Ibn ʿAbbās, through Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, gives it in nearly the same form: when Zachariah saw that at Mary, he said that the One who brings this to Mary out of its time is able to grant him a child — and that is when he called. ʿIkrimah reports that he entered the sanctuary, locked the doors, and whispered in private to God.

A reason is added for why the request was urgent: his household had, at that time, died out.

The prayers quoted alongside this one are Qur’an 19:4–6, where he describes his bones having weakened and his head glowing with white hair and his wife being barren, and Qur’an 21:89, ***“My Lord, do not leave me alone, though You are the best of inheritors.”***

### **WHAT HE ASKED FOR**

The word for offspring is explained as meaning progeny, and *“righteous”* as *“blessed”* — al-Suddī gives it with that single word. The phrase *“from Yourself”* is explained simply as *“from You.”*

A grammatical point is then raised, because the word for offspring is collective but is used here of one. The proof given is that God relates his prayer elsewhere with the singular — Qur’an 19:5, ***“grant me from Yourself an heir”*** — not the plural, which shows that what he asked for was one child. The feminine ending on *“righteous”* is for the feminine form of the word for offspring, not for the child.

Two lines of Arabic poetry are cited for that usage. In the first, a poet says of a man’s father, *“your father was a ruler — another bore him”* — using a feminine verb for the word meaning ruler although the person is male — and then *“and you are a ruler; that is the perfection.”* In the second, a poet says *“do not despise a mountain snake”* using the feminine for the word meaning snake, and then returns to the meaning with the masculine *“when it bites.”* The rule stated is that this is permitted only for words that are not proper names for persons.

### **THE CLOSING NAME**

The closing words are explained as meaning *“You hear the prayer,”* with the observation that the form used is more emphatic, since it carries the sense of possessing hearing.

A view attributed to a grammarian of Basra is mentioned — that the sense is *“You hear what You are called upon with”* — and then set aside. The sense preferred is the plain one: You possess hearing of the prayer of whoever calls upon You.

### **HOW THE PRAYER FOLLOWS FROM THE SCENE**

Two things are worth holding together. The first is that the prayer is prompted by evidence rather than produced in its absence: Zachariah asks because he has just seen something that would otherwise have been impossible, and the verse presents the two as parallel impossibilities.

The second is the shape of the request. He asks for offspring from God *Himself*, and the word he uses for it is the word for blessing. That is the note the sūrah has been sounding since the opening of this narrative: what is asked is not merely that the customary course be interrupted, but that what comes of it be good. The answer, in the next verse, will name the child and say what he will be.

## **3:39**

> So the angels called out to him while he stood praying in the sanctuary, “Allah gives you good news of ˹the birth of˺ John who will confirm the Word of Allah and will be a great leader, chaste, and a prophet among the righteous.”

### **MEANING**

The prayer is answered while it is still being made:

***So the angels called out to him while he stood praying in the sanctuary,*** ***“Allah gives you good news of ˹the birth of˺ John who will confirm the Word of Allah and will be a great leader, chaste, and a prophet among the righteous.”***

The call comes in a stated posture and place: he is standing at prayer in the sanctuary. What is announced is a son, and the announcement immediately says what he will be: he will affirm a word from God, he will be a master, he will be chaste, and he will be a prophet, counted with the righteous.

### **WHO CALLED**

The reciters differed. The general reading of Medina, and of some in Kufa and Basra, uses the feminine verb, treating *“the angels”* as a plural whose verb precedes it — which the Arabs do, especially with words of feminine form. Some Kufan reciters read it with the masculine, taking the caller to be Gabriel, and a reading to that effect is attributed to the copy of Ibn Masʿūd.

The question is then raised directly: how can *“the angels”* be said of one? The answer given is that Arabic permits speaking of the one in the manner of the plural where a single individual is not what is being specified. A man who rode one mule is described as going out on the mules of the post; asked from whom he heard something, a man says *“from people”* when he heard it from one man. Qur’an 3:173 is cited, where *“the people”* said to them that the people had gathered against them and the speaker was one.

The position taken is that both readings are known and either is correct, since the meaning does not differ. On interpretation, however, the apparent sense is preferred: God informed that the angels summoned him, and the apparent sense of a plural is a group rather than one, Gabriel being one.

### **HOW THE CALL IS WORDED**

A further difference is recorded over the particle after the call. The general reading opens it, so that the call falls directly upon what follows: the angels summoned him with the glad announcement. Some Kufan reciters read it as the direct speech of the angels, on the ground that calling is speech. The opening is preferred, since the widespread reading of the cities is a proof and an isolated reading is an opinion.

A third difference concerns the verb of announcing. The Medinans and Basrans read it with a doubled middle letter, meaning that God gives him the announcement of a child. Some Kufans read it lightly, meaning that God gladdens him with a child He grants him, and a poem is cited for that sense: *“I gladdened my family when I saw a letter that had come to you from al-Ḥajjāj, its writing being recited.”* The lightened form is described as the dialect of Tihāma, of Kināna and others of Quraysh.

The doubled reading is preferred, both because it is the widespread and familiar usage and because the reciters of the cities agree on the doubled form at Qur’an 15:54. A distinction between the two attributed to Muʿādh al-Kūfī — that the lightened form means gladness and the doubled one means an announcement — is rejected: the scholars of Arabic speech are said not to know it from any sound route.

### **THE NAME**

The name is explained as originally a verb meaning *“he lives.”* The reason given for choosing it is that God gave him life through faith. Qatādah is reported saying it means *“a servant whom God gave life through faith.”*

### **CONFIRMING A WORD FROM GOD**

The description is explained as meaning that he confirms Jesus son of Mary. Grammatically it is a severance from the name, since the name is definite and the description indefinite.

The accounts converge on that, with different details. Mujāhid is reported relating that the wife of Zachariah said to Mary, *“I find that which is in my womb stirring for that which is in your womb,”* so that *“confirming a word from God”* means John affirms Jesus. Ibn ʿAbbās, through Ibn Jurayj, reports that she used to say, *“I find that which is in my womb prostrating to that which is in your womb,”* and that this prostration in the womb is his confirmation of him. Al-Rabīʿ says he was *“the first man to affirm Jesus”*; al-Ḍaḥḥāk says he was the first to testify that he is a word from God, and notes that the two were maternal cousins, with John the elder. Qatādah adds that he was upon his way and his path.

A reading attributed to a grammarian of Basra — that *“a word from God”* here means a scripture of God, on the analogy of saying *“so-and-so recited to me such-and-such a kalima”* meaning an ode — is rejected as ignorance of the term and as audacity in rendering the Qur’an by personal opinion.

### **MASTER, AND CHASTE**

*“A master”* is explained as noble in knowledge and worship, and it is conjoined to the preceding description. The glosses cluster on a small set of qualities. Qatādah is reported saying *“a master in worship, and forbearance, and knowledge, and piety”*; elsewhere, *“the forbearing one.”* Saʿīd ibn Jubayr says *“the forbearing”* and *“the God-fearing.”* Mujāhid says *“the one noble with God.”* Ibn Zayd says *“the noble”*; Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyab says *“the jurist, the learned”*; and ʿIkrimah is reported with the definition *“the one whom anger does not overcome.”*

*“Chaste”* is explained as one who refrains from approaching women. The root sense given is prevention and holding back, and it is illustrated from speech: one says a man was prevented from a thing when he was held back from it; one says of a reader who cannot read that he is *“halted”* in his reading; and an enemy’s *“besieging”* is the holding of people back from moving. Two Arabic poems are cited, one describing a drinking companion as *“not one who withholds, nor one who passes the cup round,”* and one in which the poet says the slanderers found him *“keeping your secret closely guarded.”*

The explanations are unanimous on the sense. Ibn Masʿūd is reported giving it as *“the one who does not approach women”*; Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Mujāhid, Qatādah, Ibn Zayd and al-Ḥasan give the same or nearly the same; al-Ḍaḥḥāk says *“the one who has no offspring and no fluid”*; al-Suddī says *“the one who does not want women.”*

A saying about him is reported through Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyab from Ibn al-ʿĀṣ, that every child of Adam will come with an offence except John son of Zachariah — the speaker then taking a small twig from the ground and saying that he had of what men have only the like of that twig, and that this is why God named him master and chaste.

### **A PROPHET AMONG THE RIGHTEOUS**

The final description is explained as meaning a messenger of his Lord to his people, telling them of His command and His prohibition, His lawful and His unlawful, and conveying to them what He sent him with. ***“Among the righteous”*** is explained as: among His righteous prophets.

## **3:40**

> Zachariah exclaimed, “My Lord! How can I have a son when I am very old and my wife is barren?” He replied, “So will it be. Allah does what He wills.”

### **MEANING**

The answer is met with a question, and the question is answered by a principle:

***Zachariah exclaimed,*** ***“My Lord! How can I have a son when I am very old and my wife is barren?”*** ***He replied,*** ***“So will it be. Allah does what He wills.”***

He asks how the child is to come to him: a man who has reached the age he has reached does not have children born to him, and his wife was barren. The reply does not explain the mechanism. It states that God does whatever He intends, and leaves the matter there.

### **THE WORDS USED**

*“Barren”* is explained as a woman who does not bear, and the note is added that the same word is used of a man. A line of ʿĀmir ibn al-Ṭufayl is cited for it: ***“what a wretched man, if I am one-eyed and barren and a coward — what excuse have I then at every gathering?”***

The word for old age is a verbal noun, and the construction is explained: saying that old age has reached him is the same as saying he has reached old age, since what reaches you, you have reached. The sense is simply that he has grown old, as one says *“hardship has reached me”* meaning that one is in hardship.

### **WHY HE ASKED**

The question is raised directly, because it looks like doubt: how could Zachariah, a prophet of God, ask this after the angels had given him the good news by God’s command? Was he doubting their truthfulness? That cannot be attributed to the people of faith in God, still less to the prophets and messengers. Or was he denying his Lord’s power? That would be worse still.

Two answers are given. The first, given by al-Suddī and by ʿIkrimah, is that an insinuation intervened. Al-Suddī reports that when he heard the call, Satan came to him and said that the voice he had heard was not from God but from Satan mocking him, and that had it been from God, He would have revealed it to him as He reveals other matters to him — and that he wavered at that point and asked how a boy could be his, meaning *“from where?”* ʿIkrimah gives the same in a different form: Satan came wanting to spoil his Lord’s favour upon him, and asked whether he knew who had called him; he said yes, his Lord’s angels had called him; and he was told it was rather Satan, since had it been from his Lord it would have been concealed to him as his own call had been concealed.

On this reading his words were a request for confirmation: he wanted a sign by which it would be settled for him that the voice he had heard was an announcement from God on the tongues of His angels. That is why the next verse has him ask for a sign.

The second answer offered is that the question was about the means rather than about the possibility: by which way would the child he had been given news of come — from his wife, barren as she was, or from another woman? On this reading nothing in the question touches the power of God at all. Both readings are left standing, and the difference between them is real rather than verbal.

### **THE REPLY**

***“So will it be”*** is explained as: the matter is as He has described Himself — creating a child from an old man who has despaired of children, and from a barren woman from whom no birth is hoped, is easy for Him. The comparison offered is with Zachariah himself: He created him before He created any child from him, when he was not yet anything.

The reason given is that He is God, and the creation of nothing He intends is difficult for Him, and no act He intends is withheld from Him, because His power is a power that no power resembles. Al-Suddī is reported condensing this into a single clause: ***“thus does God what He wills — and He created you before, when you were nothing.”***

### **WHAT THE EXCHANGE ESTABLISHES**

The reply deliberately declines the terms of the question. Zachariah asked *“how,”* which invites an account of means, and the answer gives none; it states a will instead. That refusal is the substance of the verse, and it is the same refusal the sūrah has been building toward since its opening, where God was described as doing all these things — giving and withdrawing authority, entering night into day, bringing the living out of the dead, providing without reckoning — and each of them was named as something no creature does.

It also settles the question raised about the asking. Whatever the state in which the question was put, the answer is not a rebuke. It is a reminder of the sort of agent being spoken about, and it is followed immediately, in the next verse, by the sign he asked for.

## **3:41**

> Zachariah said, “My Lord! Grant me a sign.” He said, “Your sign is that you will not ˹be able to˺ speak to people for three days except through gestures. Remember your Lord often and glorify ˹Him˺ morning and evening.”

### **MEANING**

The sign that was asked for is given, and it is given as a restraint:

***Zachariah said,*** ***“My Lord! Grant me a sign.”*** ***He said,*** ***“Your sign is that you will not ˹be able to˺ speak to people for three days except through gestures. Remember your Lord often and glorify ˹Him˺ morning and evening.”***

The sign is not a spectacle. It is a silence, limited to a three-day span, and limited further by what remains open inside it: he may still gesture, and he is told to remember his Lord much and to glorify his Lord at the two ends of the day. The sign is doubled — it confirms the announcement, and it removes the doubt that prompted the request.

### **WHY THE SIGN WAS WANTED**

The request is explained as a request for a mark: if the call he had heard was truly from God and a good news from Him, then let a mark be given, so that what had been whispered to him — that the voice was not the voice of the angels and the news was not from his Lord — would be cleared away.

Al-Suddī is reported giving it in that form: ***“O Lord, if this voice is from You, then appoint for me a sign.”***

### **THE SIGN AS A DISCIPLINE**

The explanation then takes a direction worth stating plainly, because it changes how the verse reads. The sign is described as combining two things: the mark he asked for, by which the reality of the announcement would be made clear to him, and a purging of the slip and the mistake involved in asking.

Qatādah, through Saʿīd and through Maʿmar, states it directly: *“he was only punished for that because the angels had addressed him face to face and given him the good news of John, and then he asked for a mark after the angels had spoken to him — so he was taken by his tongue and could not speak except by gesturing and signalling.”* Al-Rabīʿ, through his father, gives the same, adding that this was *“it was mentioned to us”* on his authority. Jubayr ibn Nufayr describes the physical form of it: *“his tongue swelled in his mouth until it filled it, then God released it after three.”*

The point being made is not that asking is blameworthy in itself. It is that the asking came after direct address, and the sign that answered it was therefore fitted to the faculty involved: the tongue that had asked was the tongue that was held.

### **A GRAMMATICAL NOTE ON THE SIGN**

A question is raised about the form of the word for sign, since a word of its shape would normally carry a glottal stop. Three answers are recorded and each is answered in turn.

The first is that it originally had a doubled consonant, which the Arabs found heavy, so they converted it into a long vowel because of the preceding open vowel — as in the oath form *“by whatever so-and-so is, may God disgrace him.”* The second is that it is a defective form of the feminine agent pattern; against this it is objected that the Arabs form its diminutive with the first vowel rather than the second, and the objection is itself answered by pointing out that the diminutive of a feminine agent noun takes that shape only when it functions as a personal name. The third is that it is a form whose first semi-vowel was converted into a long vowel, as in words for *“need”* and *“stature”*; against this it is objected that the Arabs do that only in derivatives of three-letter roots, and the answer given is that on that basis one would expect the same of words for *“date-stone”* and *“life”* — which does not happen.

### **WHY THE VERB IS IN THE FORM IT IS**

A second grammatical point is recorded. The reciters chose the form that governs the following verb, because the sense is that he will address no one for a three-day span in the time to come, and the particle chosen is the one that accompanies what is to come, not the one that governs nouns. Had the sense been that he is in that state for a three-day span, the other form would have been correct, since the particle would then be the lightened form of the emphatic one — but that is not the sense here.

### **WHAT THE GESTURE WAS**

The word for gesture is explained first by its dominant sense in Arabic: movement of the lips. It is also used for movement of the eyebrows and the eyes, though not often, and for speech kept low like a whisper. A poem of Juʾayya ibn ʿĀʾidh is cited for the latter: *“the speech of the champions became gesturing, and a muttering from them like the low rumbling of camels.”* A second is cited for the verb meaning to collapse from a blow: *“I fell from it onto the back of my neck, convulsing.”*

The early explanation differs on what was meant here. One reading is movement of the lips without forming speech with the tongue; Mujāhid is reported giving *“moving the lips”* and *“gesturing with his lips.”* The other is gesturing and signalling generally: al-Ḍaḥḥāk gives *“signalling,”* and elsewhere *“to signal with his hand or his head without speaking.”* Ibn ʿAbbās is reported saying that his tongue was held so that he spoke to people with his hand. Ibn Isḥāq gives it as *“gesturing is signalling.”* Ibn Zayd adds the qualification that he would address no one for a three-day span except by gesture, *“except that he remembered God”* — the gesture being a signal he made to them. Al-Ḥasan describes him as holding his tongue and gesturing with his hand to his people: ***“glorify morning and evening.”***

### **WHAT REMAINED OPEN**

The closing instruction is explained with emphasis. The sign is a silence toward people only: it is not dumbness, nor a defect, nor an illness. He is told to remember his Lord much, because nothing prevents him from that, and nothing stands between him and that glorifying.

Muḥammad ibn Kaʿb, through Abū Maʿshar, is reported drawing the conclusion: *“had God granted anyone a concession to leave off remembrance, He would have granted it to Zachariah”* — at the very moment of His saying it to him.

The two times named are then explained. *“The evening”* runs from when the sun passes its height until it sets, and a poem is cited for it: *“you cannot enjoy the shade of the forenoon, nor taste the returning shade of the evening”* — the returning shade beginning at the sun’s decline and ending at its setting. *“The morning”* is a verbal noun from setting out early for a need, between the rising of dawn and the time of forenoon. A line of ʿUmar ibn Abī Rabīʿa is cited for one form and a line of Jarīr for the other, and it is noted that the same verb is used of a palm coming early into fruit, and that the earliest-ripening of fruit is called by a related word. Mujāhid states it compactly: *“the morning is the first part of dawn, and the evening is the sun’s declining until it sets.”*

## **3:42**

> And ˹remember˺ when the angels said, “O Mary! Surely Allah has selected you, purified you, and chosen you over all women of the world

### **MEANING**

The address is repeated, this time to Mary rather than to Zachariah:

***“And ˹remember˺ when the angels said, “O Mary! Surely Allah has selected you, purified you, and chosen you over all women of the world”***

Three things are said of her, and the third is built on the first two: she has been selected, she has been purified, and she has been preferred above all womankind. The verse is attached to what precedes it by the word *“when,”* so that it continues the account that began with the wife of ʿImrān.

### **WHAT THE THREE VERBS MEAN**

*“Selected you”* is explained as: He chose you and picked you out for His obedience and for what He singled you out for of His honour.

*“Purified you”* is explained as purifying her religion of doubt and of the filths that are found in the religions of the women of the children of Adam. Mujāhid, through Ibn Abī Najīḥ, gives it as *“He made you good in faith.”*

***“Chosen you over the women of the worlds”*** is explained as His choosing her over the women of the worlds of her own time, by reason of her obedience to Him, so that He favoured her above them. Ibn Jurayj is reported restricting it explicitly: *“that is for the worlds of that day.”*

### **WHAT IS SAID ABOUT HER RANK**

A number of sayings are cited on this point, and they come through different routes and with different wordings.

ʿAlī is reported saying, through ʿAbd Allāh ibn Jaʿfar from Hishām ibn ʿUrwa from his father, that he heard the Prophet say: *“the best of its women is Mary the daughter of ʿImrān, and the best of its women is Khadīja.”* Another route, through Hishām from his father from ʿAbd Allāh ibn Jaʿfar, gives it as *“the best of the women of Paradise is Mary the daughter of ʿImrān, and the best of the women of Paradise is Khadīja the daughter of Khuwaylid.”*

Anas ibn Mālik, through Thābit al-Bunānī, gives a list of four: *“the best of the women of the worlds are four: Mary the daughter of ʿImrān, Āsiya the daughter of Muzāḥim the wife of Pharaoh, Khadīja the daughter of Khuwaylid, and Fāṭima the daughter of Muḥammad.”* Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī, through Murra al-Hamdānī, is reported with a different formulation: *“many of the men reached perfection, and none of the women reached perfection except Mary, Āsiya the wife of Pharaoh, Khadīja the daughter of Khuwaylid, and Fāṭima the daughter of Muḥammad.”*

Qatādah gives several. He reports that the Prophet used to say *“enough for you, of the women of the worlds: Mary the daughter of ʿImrān, the wife of Pharaoh, Khadīja the daughter of Khuwaylid, and Fāṭima the daughter of Muḥammad.”* He also gives the saying *“the best of women who have ridden camels are the righteous women of Quraysh, most tender to a child in his smallness and most careful of a husband in what he owns,”* with the rider *“had I known that Mary had ridden a camel, I would not have preferred anyone over her.”* Abū Hurayrah is reported giving the same saying about the women of Quraysh and adding *“Mary never rode a camel at all.”*

A longer account is given through Fāṭima the daughter of Ḥusayn, from Fāṭima the daughter of the Prophet. She said that the Prophet came in one day while she was with ʿĀʾisha and whispered to her, and she wept; then he whispered to her again and she laughed. ʿĀʾisha asked her about it afterwards and she said that she had been told that Gabriel used to go over the Qur’an with him once each year and had now gone over it twice; that no prophet lives but half the life of the one before him; that Jesus, his brother, had lived a hundred and twenty years and that he was sixty and thought he would die that year; and that *“no woman among the women of the worlds has been tried with what you will be tried, so do not fall short of any woman in patience”* — and she wept. Then he said *“you are the mistress of the women of the people of Paradise, except Mary the virgin”* — and she laughed. He died that same year.

### **HOW THE ANGELS SPOKE**

Ibn Isḥāq is reported giving the setting in which these words were heard. Mary was confined in the church, and with her in it was a youth named Joseph, whose parents had made him a vowed one confined there. When their water ran out, the two of them would take their jars and go out to the open country where the water they drew from was, fill their jars and come back to the church. And the angels, during all of that, would come to Mary with these words. When Zachariah heard it he would say: *“the daughter of ʿImrān has a great affair.”*

### **WHY THIS IS SAID BEFORE THE ANNUNCIATION**

The sequence matters. Mary is told that she has been selected and purified and preferred before she is told what is going to happen to her. The next verse will command her to devotion, and the verse after that will place the whole narrative in the mouth of a prophet who was not present at any of it.

That ordering is what makes the announcement that follows bearable to read. The honour is asserted first and in general terms, and only then is the specific and much harder thing disclosed. The purification named here is also the answer already given, in advance, to the accusation that the coming of the child will provoke.

## **3:43**

> O Mary! Be devout to your Lord, prostrate yourself ˹in prayer˺ and bow along with those who bow down.”

### **MEANING**

The address continues, and it becomes a command:

***“O Mary! Be devout to your Lord, prostrate yourself ˹in prayer˺ and bow along with those who bow down.”***

Three acts are named. The first is inward and general — devotion directed to her Lord. The second and third are named separately and in that order: prostration, and bowing, and the bowing is qualified by the company in which it is to be done.

### **WHAT DEVOTION MEANS HERE**

The opening verb is explained as devoting obedience to her Lord alone. Beyond that, the explanations differ, and the differences are worth keeping side by side rather than merging.

The first is that it means to lengthen the standing. Mujāhid is reported giving *“lengthen the standing”* — meaning devoutness — and, through Ibn Jurayj, *“lengthen the standing in prayer, meaning devoutness.”* Layth reports from him that when she was told this she stood until her ankles swelled, and in another version until her feet swelled. Al-Rabīʿ, through his father, explains it as *“devoutness is the standing — stand for your Lord in prayer; stand firm for Him in prayer.”* Al-Awzāʿī is reported saying that she used to stand until matter ran from her feet.

The second is that it means to be sincere to her Lord — given through Sharīk from Sālim from Saʿīd.

The third is that it means to obey her Lord. Qatādah and al-Suddī are both reported with *“obey your Lord.”* A saying is also cited to the effect that every place in the Qur’an where this word occurs, it means obedience to God.

The fourth is that it means to worship her Lord, given from al-Ḥasan.

The summary given draws them together: devote the worship of your Lord to His face purely, and be lowly in obedience and worship to Him together with those of His creatures who are lowly to Him — in thanks for what He has honoured her with of selection, of purification from filths, and of preferment over womankind of her time.

### **WHY THE THANKSGIVING IS ATTACHED**

That framing is the point of the verse. The command is not free-standing; it is set directly after the threefold honour of the previous verse, and the explanation reads it as the response that honour calls for. She was selected, purified and preferred — and what is asked of her in return is worship.

This also explains why the last of the three acts is qualified. Prostration and bowing are named plainly, but the bowing is to be done ***“with those who bow.”*** The worship asked for is not private only. It is to be performed in the company of others who do the same, which is why the summary speaks of being lowly *together with* those who are lowly.

### **WHAT THE VERSE DOES IN THE PASSAGE**

The narrative so far has moved from a vow, through a birth, to a childhood in the sanctuary and provision arriving from an unexpected source. At each point Mary has been acted upon — vowed by her mother, accepted by her Lord, placed with a guardian, fed by God. This verse is the first in which she is addressed and asked to do something.

It comes in the middle of the sūrah’s argument, and it comes immediately before the announcement that will turn her life upside down. The command to stand long, to prostrate and to bow with the congregation is given before the thing that will require all of it — and the ordering is the same ordering as in the previous verse, where the honour was asserted before the burden was disclosed.

## **3:44**

> This is news of the unseen that We reveal to you ˹O Prophet˺. You were not with them when they cast lots to decide who would be Mary’s guardian, nor were you there when they argued ˹about it˺

### **MEANING**

The narrative breaks off, and the reader is told why it can be trusted:

***“This is news of the unseen that We reveal to you ˹O Prophet˺. You were not with them when they cast lots to decide who would be Mary’s guardian, nor were you there when they argued ˹about it˺”***

Two claims are made. The first is that what has just been recounted belongs to the unseen and is being revealed. The second is that the one to whom it is revealed was absent from every scene in it, and is told so twice — once for the casting of lots and once for the dispute that surrounded it.

### **WHAT ‘THIS’ GATHERS UP**

The opening word is explained as gathering the reports God has given His servants: about the wife of ʿImrān and her daughter Mary, about Zachariah and his son John, and everything recounted in the verses from *“God chose Adam and Noah”* onward. All of it is collected into that one word.

*“Of the unseen”* is explained as meaning that these are reports of the hidden past of those people — reports that neither the Prophet nor his people had any access to, and that only a few of the scholars and monks of the two scriptures knew. The word for unseen is a verbal noun from a person’s being absent from something.

### **THE POINT OF THE REVELATION**

The purpose is then stated. God informs His prophet that He has revealed this to him as a proof of his prophethood, a confirmation of his truthfulness, and a cutting off of the excuse of those among the disbelievers of the two scriptures who deny his message.

The argument is spelled out: they know that he could not have arrived at knowledge of these reports given how hidden they are, and could not have come to know them given how obscure they are even among the people to whom they belong — except by God making him know them. It is known among them that he was unlettered, not writing so that he could read the books and reach this knowledge through books, and not a companion of the people of the books so that he could take his knowledge from them.

### **WHAT REVEALING MEANS**

The verb is explained as sending down. The root sense given for the noun is a caster’s casting something to the one it is cast to, and the explanation then shows how wide that is. It may be by writing, by gesture and signal, by inspiration, or by message.

Qur’an 16:68 is cited, *“and your Lord revealed to the bee,”* meaning He cast that to it and inspired it. Qur’an 5:111 is cited, *“and when I revealed to the disciples,”* meaning I cast knowledge of that to them by inspiration. A line of rajaz poetry is cited: *“He cast to it stability, and it became stable”* — meaning He cast that to it as a command. Qur’an 19:11 is cited, *“so he signalled to them: glorify at dawn and in the evening,”* meaning He cast that to them by gesture. Qur’an 6:121 is cited for the devils casting to their allies by whispering, and Qur’an 6:19, *“this Qur’an has been revealed to me that I may warn you with it and whomever it reaches,”* for its being cast by Gabriel’s bringing it from God.

The noun is then explained as what passes from the caster to the one cast to, which is why the Arabs call a written line and a piece of writing by that name — since it is fixed and established in what has been written. A line of Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr is cited: *“odes of his reached the non-Arabs and the horizons, remaining as writing remains in hard stone,”* meaning writing fixed in stone. A line of Ruʾba is cited for the verb used of a scribe’s fine writing.

### **THE LOTS AND THE DISPUTE**

***“You were not with them”*** is explained as: you were not in their presence, so as to know what We teach you of reports you did not witness — you know it only by Our teaching you. ***“With them”*** means in their presence; ***“when they cast”*** means at the time of their casting; and ***“their pens”*** are the arrows with which the Children of Israel drew lots over who would be her guardian.

Mujāhid, through Ibn Abī Najīḥ, identifies those who cast as Zachariah and his companions, casting lots with their writing-reeds over Mary when she entered upon them. Qatādah says she was the daughter of their imam and chief, that the Children of Israel contended over her and drew lots with their arrows, and that Zachariah won — he being married to her aunt. Al-Ḍaḥḥāk and Ibn ʿAbbās give the same, with Ibn ʿAbbās placing the casting at the time she was placed in the mosque, while they were writing the revelation. Al-Ḥasan is reported saying it refers to where they drew lots over her, and that it was unseen to the Prophet when God told him.

A grammatical point is then raised: the phrase ***“which of them should take charge of Mary”*** implies something left unsaid — the casting was so as to see which of them would take charge, and to make that clear and know it. It is then noted that someone might suppose the interrogative should be in the accusative, and that this is an error: seeing, making clear and knowing take this interrogative as a question, and the interrogative in a question takes the initial position with the government of the question suspended from it. To say *“I will surely see which of them stood”* is to say *“I will ask the people: which of them stood?”* — and likewise *“I will surely know.”*

### **THE SECOND ABSENCE**

***“Nor were you with them when they disputed”*** is explained as: you were not with Mary’s people at the time of their dispute over who had the most right to her and was worthiest of her.

The explanation then notes that although the address is to the Prophet, it is a rebuke from God to those among the people of the two scriptures who deny him. How can they doubt him, when he tells them these reports and did not witness them, and had not been present at these things, and is not someone who read the books and so learned their account, nor someone who sat with the people of those books and heard their report?

Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, through Ibn Isḥāq, states it as: He tells him of the hidden knowledge they had concealed from him, in order to confirm his prophethood and as a proof against them for what he brings them of what they had hidden.

## **3:45**

> ˹Remember˺ when the angels proclaimed, “O Mary! Allah gives you good news of a Word from Him, his name will be the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary; honoured in this world and the Hereafter, and he will be one of those nearest ˹to Allah˺

### **MEANING**

The annunciation is now made to Mary herself, and what is announced is named in full:

***“˹Remember˺ when the angels proclaimed, “O Mary! Allah gives you good news of a Word from Him, his name will be the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary; honoured in this world and the Hereafter, and he will be one of those nearest ˹to Allah˺”***

The child is described first by what he is — ***“a word from Him”*** — then by the name given to him, then by his descent, which is traced through his mother alone. Two further things are said of him: he holds honour in both lives, and he is among those brought near.

The verse is tied to the one before it: the Prophet was not with them when they disputed, and he was not present at the angels saying this either.

### **WHAT ‘A WORD FROM HIM’ MEANS**

Announcing is explained as informing a person of news that pleases him. The phrase ***“a word from Him”*** is explained as a message from God and news from Him, from the ordinary saying *“so-and-so cast a word to me that pleased me,”* meaning he told me something I was glad of. Qur’an 4:171 is cited, *“and His word which He cast to Mary,”* meaning the announcement God gave Mary of Jesus, which He cast to her.

Two readings are then given. Qatādah, through Maʿmar, is reported saying that the word is His saying *“Be.”* On that reading he is called God’s word because he came to be through that word, just as one says of what God has decreed, *“this is the decree of God and His judgement,”* meaning it happened by His decree and judgement — and as Qur’an 4:47 says, *“the command of God is always carried out,”* meaning what God commanded, the thing commanded that came about by His command.

The other reading is that it is simply a name God gave him, as He named the rest of His creation with whatever names He willed. Ibn ʿAbbās, through ʿIkrimah, is reported saying: *“Jesus is the Word from God.”*

The first is preferred, and the reason given is grammatical. The angels gave Mary the announcement of Jesus by God’s message and His word, which He commanded them to cast to her — that God would create from her a child with no husband and no male. And that, it is said, is why the next clause uses the masculine and says ***“his name”*** rather than the feminine: the word here is not intended as a personal name in the way a proper name is, but in the sense of the announcement, so its pronoun is treated as masculine — the way the pronouns of words for offspring and beast and of epithets are treated.

Two grammarians are then cited and answered. A Basran explained the masculine by saying that in meaning it refers to a male, and compared a verse where a soul is addressed and the address then shifts, and the description of a man as *“the one with the little breast.”* A Kufan agreed that the pronoun refers to the word but gave a different reason: that the Arabs do this with epithets, titles and names not coined to identify a person, which is why one may say *“offspring”* with either a feminine or a masculine adjective, but may not say of a man named Ṭalḥa that *“she came.”* Some rejected the argument from *“the one with the little breast,”* saying the feminine ending there was used because a piece of the breast was meant, as one says of a portion of meat or a drink.

### **THE NAME AND THE DESCENT**

The clause naming him is explained as God informing His servants of his lineage — that he is his mother Mary’s son — and thereby denying both what the heretics among the Christians attributed to him of sonship to God and what the slanderers among the Jews accused his mother of. Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, through Ibn Isḥāq, states it as: *“so was his affair, not what they say about him.”*

The title is then explained. It is said to be an adjectival form shifted from the passive participle, meaning *“wiped”* — that God wiped him and purified him of offences. Ibrāhīm is reported giving it as *“the Messiah is the truthful one.”* A different account, through Saʿīd, is that *“he was only named the Messiah because he was wiped with blessing.”*

### **HONOURED, AND BROUGHT NEAR**

The word rendered *“honoured”* is explained as possessing standing and a high rank with God, and nobility and honour. It is noted that the word is used of a man who is noble and whom kings and people hold great, and that a related word for standing is a metathesis of it — the initial letter having been moved to the position of the middle radical.

Grammatically, the word is in the accusative as a severance from the name, since the name is definite and the word indefinite, being an attribute of it; it is added that the genitive, referring it back to *“word,”* would also have been permissible.

Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar is reported glossing it as ***“honoured in this world and the Hereafter with God.”***

The final clause is explained as meaning that he is among those whom God will bring near on the Day of Resurrection, settling him in His proximity and drawing him close. Qatādah gives it as *“among those brought near to God on the Day of Resurrection,”* and al-Rabīʿ, through his father, gives the same.

### **WHAT THE VERSE SETTLES**

The sūrah has been arguing about Jesus since its opening, and this verse states the terms on which he is to be understood. He is a word proceeding from God — brought about by His word, on the preferred reading, rather than being a name for a pre-existent person. He is ***“the son of Mary,”*** and the descent is stated through her alone. He is honoured and brought near, which is a great deal. And he is not what either party in the dispute claimed: not the son of God, and not the child of the accusation brought against his mother.

## **3:46**

> And he will speak to people in ˹his˺ infancy and adulthood and will be one of the righteous.”

### **MEANING**

The description of the child continues with two more things said of him:

***“And he will speak to people in ˹his˺ infancy and adulthood and will be one of the righteous.”***

The speech is placed at two points in a single life: in the cradle, and in maturity. The announcement closes by placing him among the righteous.

### **HOW THE CLAUSE IS CONSTRUED**

A grammatical note is recorded on the verb. It has the form of an independent verb, since nothing governs it, but its position in the sentence is that of a circumstantial qualifier. A line of poetry is cited for the same construction: *“I spent the night giving her a blade that cuts cleanly, striking straight in its shanks and swerving.”*

The word for the cradle is explained as the place where an infant lies while being nursed. Ibn ʿAbbās, through Ibn Jurayj, is reported glossing it as *“the resting-place of the infant in his nursing.”*

The word for maturity is explained as one who has passed youth and has not yet reached old age; one says of a man and of a woman alike that they are in that stage. A line of rajaz poetry is cited for the feminine form: *“and I shall not return after it to dealing with the mature woman and the girl.”*

### **WHY THE SPEAKING IS MENTIONED TWICE**

The purpose of the double mention is stated plainly. His speaking is as an infant in the cradle — as a demonstration of his mother’s innocence of what those who invented falsehood against her accused her of, and as a proof for his prophethood. And the later speaking is that of one who has reached maturity after passing youth, coming by the revelation God sends to him, and His command and His prohibition, and what He sends down to him of His book.

The point being made is that the second is as much a sign as the first, because of what it says about him. God informs His servants of this about the Messiah — that it was so — even though the usual course among people is that they speak in maturity and old age. It is given as an argument against those among the Christians who say about him what is false: he was, from the moment he was created, a newborn infant and then a mature man, turning through states and changing as times and days passed over him, from smallness to greatness and from one condition to another. Were he what the heretics say he is, that would not be possible for him.

So the verse is also a refutation of the delegation from Najrān who argued with the Prophet about him, and an argument made by God on behalf of His prophet: he was like the rest of the children of Adam, except for what God singled him out for of the honour by which He distinguished him from them.

Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, through Ibn Isḥāq, states it as: *“he informs them of the states he passes through in his life, like the turning of the children of Adam through their lives, small and great — except that God singled him out by speaking in the cradle, as a sign of his prophethood and to make the creatures know the places of His power.”*

### **WHAT THE EXPLAINERS SAY**

Qatādah and al-Rabīʿ are both reported giving the sense as his speaking to them small and great. Ibn Jurayj gives it as *“he spoke to them small and great, and in maturity,”* and adds that Mujāhid glossed the word for maturity as *“the forbearing one.”* Al-Ḥasan is reported saying that he spoke to them in the cradle as a child and spoke to them when grown.

A different reading is reported from Ibn Zayd, who took the second term as still future: that Jesus spoke to them in the cradle, and that he speaks to them when he kills the Dajjāl, being then in that stage of life.

### **AMONG THE RIGHTEOUS**

The closing phrase is explained as meaning that he is of their number and of their allies, since the people of righteousness are of one another in religion and in merit.

The word is in the accusative, conjoined to the position of the clause about speaking — so that the three descriptions given in the previous verse and this one stand together: he is honoured, he is brought near, and he is of the righteous. What the sūrah has been insisting on throughout is that all three are said of a servant.

## **3:47**

> Mary wondered, “My Lord! How can I have a child when no man has ever touched me?” An angel replied, “So will it be. Allah creates what He wills. When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, ‘Be!’ And it is

### **MEANING**

The announcement is met with a question, and the question is about means rather than possibility:

***Mary wondered,*** ***“My Lord! How can I have a child when no man has ever touched me?”*** ***An angel replied,*** ***“So will it be. Allah creates what He wills. When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, ‘Be!’ And it is***

She asks by what route the child is to come to her. The answer does not describe a route. It describes an agent: ***“Allah creates what He wills,”*** and what follows explains how little is required for it — a decree, and a word, and the thing is.

### **WHAT SHE ASKED**

The question is explained as a question about the way, put in two alternatives: will there be a husband whom she marries and a spouse she weds, or will He originate his creation within her without husband and without a male, without any human being touching her?

That framing matters, because it settles what kind of question this is. She is not asking whether it can happen, and the answer she receives is not a rebuke for asking. She is asking which of the two ways it will be, and the answer redirects her from the way to the One who chooses it.

### **THE ANSWER GIVEN**

***“So will it be”*** is explained as: thus God creates from you a child for you, without any human being touching you, making him a sign for people and a lesson. His creating accords with His purpose, and His doing with His intent.

The explanation then states the scope of that in both directions, and this is the part that answers her alternatives directly. He gives the child to whom He pleases, with a male and without a male; and He withholds it from whom He pleases among women, even if she has a husband. Both of the ways she asked about are within it, and so is neither of them.

The reason given is that the creation of nothing He purposes to create is difficult for Him. Rather, it is that when a thing is purposed by Him, He commands it, saying to it *“Be,”* and it is — whatever He purposes, however He purposes.

Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, through Ibn Isḥāq, gives it as: ***“He does what He intended and creates what He wills, from a human being or other than a human being; when He decrees a matter, He only says to it, ‘Be,’ of what He wills and however He wills, and it is what He intended.”***

### **THE SAME ANSWER GIVEN TWICE**

The wording here is the wording of Qur’an 3:40 almost exactly, where Zachariah asked how a son could be his when old age had reached him and his wife was barren, and was told that God does whatever He purposes. The two questions are different in substance — his concerned two people past the ordinary means, hers concerns the absence of any means at all — and both receive the same kind of answer.

That repetition is the sūrah’s method in this whole passage. Each of the impossibilities narrated in it — a barren woman, a woman untouched by any man, provision arriving out of season, a child speaking in the cradle — is met with the same attribution. The point accumulated across them is not that any one of them is remarkable, but that the One who does them does all of them.

### **WHY THE ANSWER IS NOT A MECHANISM**

It is worth noticing what the reply declines to give. Mary asked *“how,”* and *“how”* is exactly the word that would invite an account of causes. What she is given instead is a description of what decrees are like when God makes them: a word, and the thing.

That is also why the verse ends where it ends. *“And it is”* is not a flourish; it is the claim the verse is making. Between the decree and the thing there is no interval and no instrument, and the sūrah will go on in the following verses to name what that word brings about — a messenger who shapes a bird from clay, heals the unhealable, gives life to the dead, and tells of what is hidden.

## **3:48**

> And Allah will teach him writing and wisdom, the Torah and the Gospel

### **MEANING**

The announcement turns from what he is to what he will be given:

***“And Allah will teach him writing and wisdom, the Torah and the Gospel”***

Four things are named, and they are not all of one kind. The first two are capacities given to him. The last two are scriptures, and one of them already exists at the time this is said while the other does not.

### **HOW THE VERSE IS READ**

The reciters differed over the opening verb. The general reading of the reciters of the Ḥijāz and of Medina, and of some Kufans, reads it in the third person, connecting it to what immediately precedes: ***“thus God creates what He wills”*** and *“He only says to it, ‘Be,’ and it is.”* The general reading of the Kufans and of some Basrans reads it in the first person plural, conjoining it instead to *“We reveal it to you”* in Qur’an 3:44 — as though the sense were: ***“that is of the tidings of the unseen which We reveal to you… and We will teach him the Book.”***

The position taken is that these are two readings that differ in wording but not in meaning, so that either is correct: both of them report that God teaches Jesus the Book and what is mentioned that He teaches him.

### **WHAT THE FOUR THINGS ARE**

This verse is described as the beginning of a report to Mary of what God will do with the child He has given her good news of — of honour, of high rank and of merit.

*“The Book”* is explained as the writing he writes with his own hand; Ibn Jurayj is reported giving it as *“with his hand.”*

*“Wisdom”* is explained as the sunna — the way — which God reveals to him in other than a book. Qatādah is reported saying simply *“the wisdom is the sunna.”*

*“The Torah”* is the Torah that was sent down upon Moses, and which was among them from Moses’ time.

*“The Gospel”* is the gospel of Jesus, which did not exist before him — and yet God told Mary about it before creating him, that He would reveal it to him.

Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, through Ibn Isḥāq, states it as: God informed her of what He intended for him — ***“and He will teach him the Book and wisdom, and the Torah”*** which was among them from the time of Moses, *“and the Gospel”* — another book that He originated for him, *“the knowledge of which they did not have, except the mention of it, that it would be from the prophets before him.”* On this reading Qatādah is reported adding that Jesus used to read both of them.

### **WHY THE GOSPEL IS NAMED TO HER**

A reason is given for why the fourth item is mentioned at all, since it did not yet exist. Mary already knew, from what had come down of the scriptures, that God would send a prophet to whom a book would be revealed, called the Gospel. So God informed her that this prophet — the one whose description she had heard, and whom His prophets before had been promised He would send down the book called the Gospel upon — is the child He has granted her and given her good news of.

That is what makes the naming an answer rather than a prediction. She is not being told about a book; she is being told who her son is, identified by the terms she already had.

### **WHAT THE VERSE DOES IN THE PASSAGE**

The sūrah has taken some care over what Jesus is taught. Immediately after this verse he will be described as a messenger to the Children of Israel, and the signs he brings will be listed. Before any of that, the claim is made that he was taught — writing, wisdom, and two scriptures, one of which is the scripture already in the hands of the people he is being sent to.

The order is deliberate and it bears on the argument the sūrah has been making since its opening. What is being described is a prophet within a lineage of prophets: taught as they were taught, holding the Torah as they held it, and given a book of his own as they were given theirs. The next verse will say what he was sent to say.

## **3:49**

> and ˹make him˺ a messenger to the Children of Israel ˹to proclaim,˺ ‘I have come to you with a sign from your Lord: I will make for you a bird from clay, breathe into it, and it will become a ˹real˺ bird—by Allah’s Will. I will heal the blind and the leper and raise the dead to life—by Allah’s Will. And I will prophesize what you eat and store in your houses. Surely in this is a sign for you if you ˹truly˺ believe

### **MEANING**

The description of Jesus now turns to what he will be sent to do:

***“and ˹make him˺ a messenger to the Children of Israel ˹to proclaim,˺ ‘I have come to you with a sign from your Lord: I will make for you a bird from clay, breathe into it, and it will become a ˹real˺ bird—by Allah’s Will. I will heal the blind and the leper and raise the dead to life—by Allah’s Will. And I will prophesize what you eat and store in your houses. Surely in this is a sign for you if you ˹truly˺ believe”***

Four signs are listed. Three are acts on the natural world, and each carries the qualification ***“by Allah’s Will.”*** The fourth is a telling of what is hidden. The verse closes by naming all four together as a sign, conditional on their being believers.

### **HOW THE SENTENCE IS BUILT**

A grammatical note is recorded first. The words ***“and We will make him”*** are understood before *“a messenger,”* omitted because the sentence already indicates them. The words he is to proclaim are then explained as: he has brought them a mark from his Lord confirming his claim to be a messenger to them from their Lord. Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, through Ibn Isḥāq, gives it as: *“it confirms thereby my prophethood, that I am a messenger from Him to you.”*

### **THE BIRD FROM CLAY**

The word for bird is the plural of the singular for a bird, and the reciters differed over whether both occurrences are read in the singular or both in the plural. The plural in both places is preferred: that is how the act is described, and it agrees with the written text of the copy.

Ibn Isḥāq gives an account of the occasion. Jesus sat one day with boys from the school, took clay, and said *“shall I make for you from this clay a bird?”* He shaped it, blew into it, and said *“be a bird by God’s permission,”* and it went out flying between his hands. Ibn Jurayj adds that he asked them *“which bird is strongest in creation?”* and they said *“the bat, for it is only flesh.”*

A grammatical question is raised: why does the verse say he blows *“into it”* in the masculine, when the preceding word is grammatically feminine? The sense is *“I blow into the bird”*; the feminine would also have been correct, referring back to the word for form, as in Qur’an 5:110.

### **HEALING THE BLIND AND THE LEPER**

The verb is explained as meaning *“I cure”*; one says that God cured the sick person when He healed him, and that the sick person recovered — with two dialects noted for the latter.

The explanations of the word for the blind differ. Mujāhid is reported saying it means one who sees by day and does not see by night. Qatādah says *“we used to be told that it is the one born blind, with the eyes closed over,”* and the same is given from Ibn ʿAbbās. Al-Suddī, Ibn ʿAbbās through Ibn Jurayj, Qatādah through Maʿmar, and al-Ḥasan give it as *“the blind.”* ʿIkrimah gives it as *“the one with weak sight.”*

The known sense of the root in Arabic is given as blindness, and a poem of Suwayd ibn Abī Kāhil is cited for it: *“it blinded his eyes until they turned white, and he blames himself now that he has desisted.”*

An argument is then made for preferring that sense, and it is an argument about the nature of a proof. Blindness and leprosy have no treatment — and that is why they are among his proofs. Were what he produced the curing of a dim-sighted person, or of one who sees by day and not by night, his opponents could have answered that among them were those who treat such things and who are neither prophets nor messengers of God. So the correct sense is the one who sees nothing, by night or by day.

### **GIVING LIFE TO THE DEAD**

Wahb ibn Munabbih is reported, through ʿAbd al-Ṣamad ibn Maʿqil, giving the setting. When Jesus was twelve, God revealed to his mother, while she was in Egypt, to go up with him to Syria. They remained there until he was thirty, and his prophethood lasted three years, after which God raised him to Himself. Wahb adds that as many as fifty thousand sick would gather around him at once — those who could reach him came to him, and those who could not, he walked to them — and that he used to treat them by supplication to God.

### **WHAT THEY EAT AND WHAT THEY STORE**

The telling is explained as: he informs them of their eating, of what he did not witness with them at the time of their eating; and ***“what you store”*** means that which you set aside and do not eat.

The glosses are consistent. Mujāhid gives it as *“what you ate last night, and what you put away of it,”* and al-Rabīʿ gives the same. ʿAṭāʾ ibn Abī Rabāḥ describes it as the food they keep in their houses — *“unseen, which God taught him.”* Al-Ḥasan gives it as *“what you hoard for fear that the one who withholds will not replace it.”* Qatādah gives a different setting: a table that came down to them with fruit of Paradise, about which they had been told not to betray, nor hoard, nor keep anything for the morrow.

### **AN OBJECTION AND ITS ANSWER**

The objection is put in its strongest form: what proof is there in telling people about their food and their stored provisions, when astrologers and diviners tell such things and are right?

The answer given is the distinction the passage turns on. The astrologer and the diviner are known — to the very people they tell — to arrive at it by extraction, through some of the means that lead to knowing it. Jesus, and the rest of the prophets and messengers, used to tell of it without extraction and without seeking knowledge of it by any device, but by initiation: by God’s informing him, with no prior basis that he followed or built on or resorted to, the way the astrologer resorts to his calculation and the diviner to his familiar.

That is the difference between the knowledge of the unseen that the prophets have and the knowledge of those who claim it. The prophets bring their proofs by means that can also be reached by some device, but on a basis no one else shares — one on which the creation knows that it cannot be reached by that means, except from God.

### **THE CLOSING CONDITION**

The closing words are explained as: in My making of ***“the bird from clay”*** by His permission, and My healing of ***“the blind and the leper,”*** and My giving life to the dead, and My telling you ***“what you eat and what you store”*** — by initiation, without calculation or soothsaying — there is a lesson for you, that you may learn from it that I am true in saying that I am a messenger to you from your Lord.

***“If you ˹truly˺ believe”*** is explained as: if you are those who attest the proofs of God and His signs, affirming His oneness and His prophet Moses and the Torah he brought you.

## **3:50**

> And I will confirm the Torah revealed before me and legalize some of what had been forbidden to you. I have come to you with a sign from your Lord, so be mindful of Allah and obey me

### **MEANING**

The speech Jesus is to deliver continues, and it ends where the sūrah’s argument has been heading all along:

***“And I will confirm the Torah revealed before me and legalize some of what had been forbidden to you. I have come to you with a sign from your Lord, so be mindful of Allah and obey me”***

Two things are said about his mission in relation to what came before it: he confirms the Torah, and he makes lawful a portion once forbidden. Then the sign is named again, and with it the double demand — be mindful of God, and ***“obey me.”***

### **WHY THE WORD IS IN THE FORM IT IS**

A grammatical point is recorded that turns out to carry meaning. The word rendered *“confirming”* is in the accusative as a circumstantial phrase belonging to ***“I have come to you”*** — not conjoined to *“honoured”* in the earlier verse.

The reason given is decisive: the phrase says ***“what is before me,”*** not ***“what is before him.”*** Had it been conjoined to the earlier description, the wording would have had to be *“confirming what is before him of the Torah”* and *“that he may make lawful for you.”* The first person settles it: these are the words of Jesus about his own mission.

### **CONFIRMING WHAT CAME BEFORE**

The reason it is said that he confirms the Torah is that he believed in the Torah, affirmed it, and affirmed that it is from God.

The principle stated alongside this is broader than the case. All the prophets affirm whatever scriptures and messengers of God came before them, even where some of the rulings of their laws differ — the difference being God’s, who made them differ in it.

And Jesus, it is said, acted by the Torah and opposed nothing of its rulings, except what God lightened for its people in the Gospel of what was made hard for them in it.

Wahb ibn Munabbih is reported, through ʿAbd al-Ṣamad ibn Maʿqil, putting this in Jesus’s own words: *“I have not called you to differ from a letter of what is in the Torah, except to make lawful for you a portion once forbidden to you, and to lift from you some of the burdens.”*

### **WHAT WAS MADE LAWFUL**

The explanations name the same categories with different detail.

Qatādah is reported saying that what Jesus brought was gentler than what Moses brought, and that there ***“had been forbidden”*** to them in what Moses brought the flesh of camels and the fat of the entrails, along with some birds and fish.

Al-Rabīʿ, through his father, gives it at greater length: what Jesus brought was gentler than what Moses brought; the flesh of camels and the fat of the entrails ***“had been forbidden”*** to them in the Torah and were made lawful for them through Jesus; fats ***“had been forbidden”*** and were made lawful in what he brought; and likewise some fish, and some birds without spurs, and other things that ***“had been forbidden”*** and made hard for them — *“so Jesus came to them with relief from it in the Gospel.”*

Ibn Jurayj names the flesh of camels and fats, and adds that he was sent to the Jews, and that they differed and split.

Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr, through Ibn Isḥāq, explains the phrasing itself: ***“confirming what is before me of the Torah”*** means what preceded me of it; and ***“to make lawful for you some of what was forbidden to you”*** means that he tells them it ***“had been forbidden”*** and they had left it, and then it is made lawful for them as relief, so that they attain its ease and leave its consequence. Al-Ḥasan is reported saying that things ***“had been forbidden”*** to them and Jesus came to make lawful what was forbidden, seeking their thanks by that.

Two things are worth drawing out. The first is that the change is partial: ***“some,”*** not all, of that which was forbidden. The second is that it is a relief rather than a reversal: the earlier prohibition was real, and the later permission is described as lightening a burden, not as correcting a mistake.

### **THE SIGN, AND THE DEMAND**

***“I have come to you with a sign from your Lord”*** is explained as: he has brought them a proof and a lesson from their Lord, by which they know the truth of what he says to them. Mujāhid is reported glossing it as *“what he made clear to them of all the things, and what his Lord gave him.”*

The demand that follows is then explained in two parts. *“Be mindful of God”* — in what He commanded them and forbade them in His book that He sent down upon Moses, and in fulfilling the covenant they made with Him in it. ***“And obey me”*** — in what he calls them to: attesting him in what his Lord and their Lord sent him with.

### **THE READING OF WHAT FOLLOWS**

The reciters differed over the opening particle of the next clause. The general reading of the cities reads it as a fresh statement. Some read it as dependent on the preceding, so that the sense becomes ***“I have come to you with a sign from your Lord — that God is my Lord and your Lord,”*** attaching it to the word for sign as a substitute.

The reading of the mass of reciters is preferred, on the principle stated: what they agree upon is a proof, and what an individual holds alone is an opinion, and an opinion is not set against a proof.

### **WHAT THE VERSE SETTLES**

This verse, though it has the form of a report, is described as containing the conclusive proof of God for His messenger against the delegation who argued with him from Najrān. By it, God informs that Jesus was quit of what was attributed to him by those who described him as other than he described himself: that he is a servant of God like the rest of His servants among the people of the earth, except for what God singled him out for of prophethood and of the proofs He gave him as an indication of his truthfulness — as He gave the rest of the messengers, other than him, marks and proofs of theirs — and as a proof of his prophethood.

Muḥammad ibn Jaʿfar states the closing words in that light: ***“be mindful of God and obey me; God is my Lord and your Lord”*** — a disowning of what they say about him and an argument for his Lord against them — and ***“worship Him; this is a straight path”*** — meaning this is what I have carried you upon and come to you with.
