# Surah 1: Al-Fātiḥah

## 1:1
> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

### Meaning
The opening formula is an invocation made before an action. To begin “in the name” of Allah is to name and remember Him at the threshold of what one is about to do; it is not to use God as an instrument or to say that an act is performed “by Allah” as though He were a tool. The action is left unspoken because the setting supplies it: before recitation, the sense is “I begin reading”; before sitting, eating, writing, or another task, the act changes while the invocation remains. Its purpose is to orient the beginning toward God before the deed proceeds. The mercy-names that follow make this opening an address of reverence and praise.

### What the grammar leaves implicit
The Arabic *bi-smi Allāh* is not the same construction as *bi-llāh*, “by Allah.” A listener can infer an omitted verb from the occasion, just as ordinary speech often leaves out a word that everyone already understands. Here the action might be reading, standing, sitting, or another act; the wording does not need to name each one. The evidence also treats *ism* in this formula as carrying the sense of naming or mentioning God. That keeps the phrase focused on invocation, not on the mechanics of how a human act is carried out.

This ellipsis helps explain why the formula is used at the start of many kinds of activity, not only at the opening of recitation. It is a short spoken threshold: the action is still the speaker’s action, but it begins with God named and remembered.

### Evidence from use and language
In the report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk], Gabriel instructs Muhammad to say the formula and explains it as, “Read in remembrance of your Lord; rise and sit in remembrance of Allah.” The account places recitation alongside ordinary movement, making remembrance part of how an act begins.

Arabic examples in the same evidence file connect the name *Allāh* with worship. At [Q 7:127], the reported reading *wa-ilāhataka* for Pharaoh’s phrase is glossed as “your worship” by Ibn ʿAbbās via ʿAmr b. Dīnār [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via ʿAmr b. Dīnār] and by Mujāhid via Ibn Jurayj [Report: Mujāhid via Ibn Jurayj]. A line attributed to Ruʾba [Poem: Ruʾba b. al-ʿAjjāj] uses *taʾalluhī* for worship and seeking God through devotion. These examples support the sense of God as the one worshipped, while the opening formula remains an invocation rather than a claim that God is being used as an instrument.

### A poetic farewell
Labīd’s farewell to his daughters [Poem: Labīd b. Rabīʿah] includes the line “then the name of al-Salām be upon you both.” The surrounding poem asks the daughters to lament him through a year; the line marks the farewell after that period. One interpretation takes it as urging them to keep God’s name and remembrance, while another hears a protective invocation for the two women. Either way, the name is invoked for the addressees. It does not make God an instrument in the speaker’s hands.

The formula therefore does more than introduce the next words. It models how to begin: remember God before speaking or acting, and let the mercy named in the formula set the tone of that beginning.

## 1:2
> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds

### Meaning
This verse begins the surah’s spoken praise. “All praise” is wider than one person’s private thanks: the wording gathers praise as such and directs it to Allah. It includes gratitude for gifts and recognition of the qualities for which God is praised. “Lord of all worlds” names the one praised as master and caretaker of creation, not merely the lord of one people or one age. The line moves from the invocation before recitation to a comprehensive acknowledgment of who receives praise and why.

### Praise, gratitude, and the worshipper’s voice
A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] explains *al-ḥamd* through “thanks, humility, and acknowledgment” of God’s favor, guidance, and initiating kindness. Other explanations distinguish praise of God’s names and qualities from thanks for His gifts, while also recognizing that the two can overlap in ordinary Arabic usage. The definite form *al-ḥamd* widens the statement beyond the amount of praise any one speaker can offer: it directs praise in its fullest sense to God.

One report from al-Aswad b. Surayʿ via al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī [Hadith: al-Aswad b. Surayʿ via al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī; Musnad Aḥmad 15650, 15654; al-Adab al-Mufrad 51] says that the Prophet affirmed, “Your Lord loves praise.” In the prayer dialogue attributed to Abū Hurayrah via Abū al-Sāʾib [Hadith: Abū Hurayrah via Abū al-Sāʾib; al-Muwaṭṭaʾ 84–85; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1:166], the reciter says this verse and God answers, “My servant has praised Me.” The wording is therefore both a statement about God and a line the worshipper is taught to say in a prayer. It is not limited to an inward feeling of gratitude.

The Qur’anic reminder that people cannot count God’s blessings [Q 14:34], repeated in [Q 16:18], adds scale to this praise. The point is not that a worshipper can enumerate every favor, but that praise does not run out once a few gifts have been named.

### What “Lord” conveys
The Arabic *rabb* can describe a master or chief, and it can describe one who tends, maintains, and sets something right. Labīd’s line [Poem: Labīd b. Rabīʿah] calls a tribal leader the “lord of Kinda”; al-Nābighah’s example [Poem: al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī] and al-Farazdaq’s line [Poem: al-Farazdaq] use the root for tending or repairing. In this verse the word gathers authority and sustaining care: the one praised holds lordship over creation and provides for it.

The blessings named in the explanation attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] are not only material. They include faculties that enable obedience, bodily capacity to perform duties, livelihood and sustenance, and guidance toward lasting good. Praise therefore embraces provision and direction as well as the qualities of the giver.

### The scope of “the worlds”
The explanations of *al-ʿālamīn*, “the worlds,” range from familiar classes of creation to the whole created order. Ibn ʿAbbās via ʿIkrimah [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via ʿIkrimah] and Saʿīd b. Jubayr via ʿAṭāʾ b. al-Sāʾib [Report: Saʿīd b. Jubayr via ʿAṭāʾ b. al-Sāʾib] name jinn and humans; another report from Saʿīd via ʿAṭāʾ b. Dīnār [Report: Saʿīd b. Jubayr via ʿAṭāʾ b. Dīnār] treats each people as a world of its time. Qatādah, through Saʿīd b. Abī ʿArūbah [Report: Qatādah via Saʿīd b. Abī ʿArūbah], says each kind is a world, and al-ʿAjjāj’s poem [Poem: al-ʿAjjāj] calls Khandif “the head of this world,” using the word for a people of its age. A report from Abū al-ʿĀliyah via Rabīʿ b. Anas [Report: Abū al-ʿĀliyah via Rabīʿ b. Anas] offers a much larger count of angelic worlds—fourteen or eighteen thousand—and preserves uncertainty about the number. That striking figure should not be mistaken for a settled inventory. Across these explanations, the phrase points beyond what a human observer can list: every world, known or unknown, falls under the same lordship.

The verse’s force comes from holding these dimensions together. Praise belongs to Allah because creation depends on Him, because His gifts cannot be counted, and because every world remains within His care.

## 1:3
> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

### Meaning
The two mercy-names deepen the praise of the preceding verse and prepare for the movement toward judgment in the next. Both are related to *raḥmah*, mercy, but the Arabic forms are not identical: *al-Raḥmān* has a more intensive pattern, while *al-Raḥīm* is another established form from the same root. The pair should not be flattened into empty repetition. Together they present mercy as central to the one praised, while leaving room for the following verse to speak of His authority on the Day of Judgment.

### Form, order, and emphasis
The names differ in form as well as sound: *Raḥmān* follows a pattern associated in Arabic with force or abundance, while *Raḥīm* is another form used to describe mercy. The difference does not by itself define two separate kinds of mercy, but it helps explain why both names can stand together without being redundant. Their order also directs the listener: the unique name Allah is followed by the specially divine title *al-Raḥmān*, then by *al-Raḥīm*, a word that can describe created beings as well. This explanation makes the sequence a way of identifying and praising God, not a reason to reduce the two names to exact synonyms.

### Different emphases in the reports
In Abū Hurayrah’s prayer-dialogue report via Abū al-Sāʾib [Hadith: Abū Hurayrah via Abū al-Sāʾib; al-Muwaṭṭaʾ 84–85; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1:166], when the reciter says these names, God answers, “My servant has extolled Me.” That response treats the naming of mercy as praise in its own right, not as a detour from the surah’s main movement.

The reports explain the relation between the names in different ways. Al-ʿArzamī, as heard by ʿUthmān b. Zafr [Report: al-ʿArzamī as heard by ʿUthmān b. Zafr], distinguishes their reach: *al-Raḥmān* is mercy toward all creation, while *al-Raḥīm* is mercy toward believers. A report attributed to the Prophet through ʿAṭiyyah al-ʿAwfī from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī [Hadith: Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī via ʿAṭiyyah al-ʿAwfī; parallel route attributed to Ibn Masʿūd] quotes Jesus distinguishing their temporal scope: mercy in this life and the next, and mercy in the next life. A gloss attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] describes tenderness toward those shown mercy and severity toward those treated harshly. These explanations ask different questions—who receives mercy, when it is received, and how it is experienced. They should be compared, not blended into one quotation or treated as though every report makes the same distinction.

### Mercy across the Qur’anic context
The Qur’anic passages keep broad and particular mercy in view: blessings that cannot be counted [Q 14:34] and [Q 16:18] are shared widely, while God is described as especially merciful to believers [Q 33:43]. The account of just judgment and multiplied good in [Q 4:40] adds another dimension. Together these passages let the two names be heard without reducing mercy either to material gifts alone or to a benefit reserved for one moment.

### The name and the verse boundary
A report from ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī via Abū al-Azhar Naṣr b. ʿAmr [Report: ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī via Abū al-Azhar Naṣr b. ʿAmr] connects the prominence of *al-Raḥmān* with Musaylima’s attempt to claim the name. An anonymous pre-Islamic line [Poem: anonymous pre-Islamic verse] and a poem by Salāmah b. Jandal [Poem: Salāmah b. Jandal] also use the name for God, offering evidence of Arabic usage. The Qur’anic command to call upon Allah or al-Raḥmān [Q 17:110], the recorded objection to the name [Q 25:60], and the passage about recognition without acceptance [Q 2:146] are distinct pieces of evidence; together they show why rejection of the name need not mean that the word was unknown.

A separate discussion in the evidence file debates how the opening formula relates to the repeated mercy-names and to verse divisions. Their written order alone does not settle that boundary question. Here the app’s alignment is followed: the formula is 1:1, and this repeated pair is 1:3. Its meaning in this sequence is clear without using word order as proof about where a verse begins or ends: praise names mercy before the prayer names judgment.

## 1:4
> Master of the Day of Judgment

### Meaning
The verse turns from mercy to final authority. “Master” points to the one who holds rule on the Day when deeds are accounted for and recompensed. The contrast is not between mercy and judgment as though one cancels the other: the prayer has just named mercy, and now it names the day on which no worldly power can rival God’s authority. “Day of Judgment” is therefore more than a future date; it is the day when actions meet their account and the limits of human rule become plain.

### Sovereignty and ownership in the readings
The Arabic recitation preserves *malik*, “king” or “sovereign,” and *mālik*, “owner” or “master.” The readings are related but not interchangeable: kingship emphasizes rule, while ownership emphasizes possession. The app’s “Master” can be read broadly enough to hold their shared point without erasing the distinction. A separate proposed accusative vocalization treats the phrase as direct address—“O Master of the Day of Judgment”—and raises a different grammatical question; it is not the form used in the app text.

In the report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk], no one on this day has judgment alongside God as people possess authority in this world. The account connects the day with restricted speech [Q 78:38], humbled voices [Q 20:108], and intercession only for those whom God approves [Q 21:28]. The question in [Q 40:16]—“To whom belongs the kingdom today?”—and its answer make this sovereignty vivid. These cross-references show that sovereignty affects more than a title: it governs who may speak, who may intercede, and whose decision stands. The two readings of *malik* and *mālik* thus meet in the lived meaning of the verse even while preserving their distinct emphases.

### What “the Day of Judgment” entails
The evidence explains *yawm al-dīn* as the day of reckoning and recompense. A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] describes people being repaid according to their deeds, with the matter ultimately belonging to God. Routes grouped under al-Suddī [Report: al-Suddī via Abū Mālik, Abū Ṣāliḥ, and routes from Ibn Masʿūd and other Companions] also call it the Day of Account; Qatādah via Maʿmar [Report: Qatādah via Maʿmar] describes the day God repays people for their deeds, and Ibn Jurayj via Ḥajjāj b. Muḥammad [Report: Ibn Jurayj via Ḥajjāj b. Muḥammad] glosses it as the day of reckoning. The Qur’an uses *al-dīn* in a context of denied accountability [Q 82:9–10], while [Q 56:86] asks how people could be beyond being repaid; both passages make reckoning central to the word.

A line by Kaʿb b. Juʿayl [Poem: Kaʿb b. Juʿayl] uses the same root for requiting someone as they have requited you: “As you requite, you will be requited.” An unattributed Arabic verse [Poem: unnamed Arabic verse] makes the same reciprocal point. These examples support the translation’s focus on judgment rather than a merely political transfer of power. They make *dīn* more than the name of a date: it signals an order in which deeds are accounted for and recompense answers to conduct.

### From praise to direct address
Placed after the Lord of all worlds and the mercy-names, this verse adds what neither phrase says by itself: all creation remains under God’s care, and all deeds finally come before His judgment. In the prayer-dialogue report from Abū Hurayrah via Abū al-Sāʾib [Hadith: Abū Hurayrah via Abū al-Sāʾib; al-Muwaṭṭaʾ 84–85; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1:166], God’s response at this point is, “My servant has glorified Me.” The report frames the line as the culmination of praise just before the prayer turns toward direct address and petition. The following “You” is not a change of subject but a change in how the worshippers speak to the one they have just praised.

## 1:5
> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help

### Meaning
The prayer now turns from speaking about God to speaking directly to Him. Its plural “we” makes the declaration communal, and the repeated “You alone” directs both worship and the request for help to the same addressee. Worship here is not only a private feeling: it includes humility, surrender, fear, hope, and obedience. Seeking help is not a second, unrelated request; it recognizes that worship and the rest of life depend on aid from God. The verse holds commitment and dependence together.

### Worship as humble service
In a report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk], Gabriel glosses the first clause as, “You alone we make one, fear, and hope in, our Lord.” The wording brings together the exclusive direction of worship and the trust expressed by turning to God alone. A related gloss for the second clause asks for help in obedience and in all affairs, so dependence is not limited to one narrow task.

The Arabic root of *ʿibādah* also carries the sense of being humbled and made compliant. Ṭarafah’s description of a well-trodden road [Poem: Ṭarafah b. al-ʿAbd] uses *muʿabbad* for a track worn smooth by travelers. That image helps explain why the language of worship includes submission and service, not just praise spoken aloud. It supports the verse’s posture without reducing worship to the image of a road.

### Assistance for what lies ahead
The request looks forward: help is sought for the remainder of life, for continuing in worship, carrying out what is commanded, and avoiding what is forbidden. Asking for help is meaningful precisely because the speaker has a responsibility to act and does not presume to be self-sufficient. The two clauses thus describe a pledge and the support needed to keep it.

One question raised by their order is whether help should be named first, since worship depends on it. The answer is that the wording does not set up two independent stages: one may say either “you met my need and did me a kindness” or “you did me a kindness and met my need.” In this prayer worship is named, then the aid needed for it is requested. The order does not claim that worship can happen without help.

### Why the object is repeated
The repeated Arabic *iyyāka* gives each verb its own direct address. The second clause does not drift into a general wish for assistance from anyone; the same exclusive orientation governs both. The repetition also lets each statement stand complete: the worshippers address God as the one worshipped, then address Him again as the one from whom help is sought. The verse’s meaning is therefore both devotional and practical: worship is directed to God, and continuing in it is also entrusted to Him. The next verse specifies the guidance for which that help is needed.

## 1:6
> Guide us along the Straight Path

### Meaning
This is a request for guidance as an ongoing course, not merely for information about a path the worshippers have never heard of. The preceding verse has declared worship and asked for help; now the community asks to be directed and kept on the right way. The plural “us” continues the shared voice. In this sequence, guidance includes recognizing what is right and being enabled to remain faithful to it through the life still ahead.

### What the request means
A gloss attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] renders *ihdinā* as “inspire us with the guiding path,” then identifies that path as God’s religion without crookedness. The Arabic verb can describe directing someone to a way and making them steady in following it. An alternative gloss in the evidence file asks for an increase in guidance; in the context of the verse, this can be understood as a request for continued right action and steadfastness, not only for new information.

An unattributed Arabic petition [Poem: unattributed Arabic line] asking God to “guide you to my request” means to help the listener meet it rightly, not simply to point out where it is. Abraham is described as guided to a straight path [Q 16:121], and another Qur’anic passage speaks of people guided to it [Q 7:43]; in these examples guidance includes being enabled to follow the way.

### What the straight path names
ʿAbd Allāh b. Masʿūd, through Abū Wāʾil [Report: ʿAbd Allāh b. Masʿūd via Abū Wāʾil; al-Mustadrak 2:258], calls the path the Book of God. Jābir, through ʿAbd Allāh b. Muḥammad b. ʿAqīl [Report: Jābir b. ʿAbd Allāh via ʿAbd Allāh b. Muḥammad b. ʿAqīl; al-Mustadrak 2:258–259], identifies it as Islam and describes it as “wider than what lies between heaven and earth.” Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyyah, through Ismāʿīl al-Azraq and Abū ʿUmar al-Bazzār [Report: Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyyah via Ismāʿīl al-Azraq and Abū ʿUmar al-Bazzār], calls it God’s religion, accepted from His servants. Reports grouped through al-Suddī [Report: al-Suddī via Abū Mālik, Abū Ṣāliḥ, and routes from Ibn Masʿūd and other Companions] also identify it as Islam. Abū al-ʿĀliyah, via ʿĀṣim al-Aḥwal [Report: Abū al-ʿĀliyah via ʿĀṣim al-Aḥwal], identifies the path with the Prophet and the two companions who followed him, Abū Bakr and ʿUmar. A Prophetic report from al-Nawwās b. Samʿān [Hadith: al-Nawwās b. Samʿān; Musnad Aḥmad 17711; al-Tirmidhī; al-Nasāʾī] presents a parable of a straight path and identifies it as Islam. Scripture, a lived religion, and exemplary guides can illuminate different dimensions of one request rather than cancel one another out.

The word *ṣirāṭ* names a road, and *mustaqīm* describes it as straight and without crookedness. Arabic also extends the image to any belief, statement, or action described as straight or crooked. The reports’ different identifications do not turn the path into a single narrow label: the Book guides, Islam is the path lived, and faithful examples show what that path looks like in practice.

### Not only a destination
The evidence also records an interpretation that asks God to lead the worshippers to Paradise in the hereafter. But the preceding request for help in worship makes a present course central: the prayer asks for a way to follow now and for steadiness along it. The references to Abraham being guided to a straight path [Q 16:121] and to people guided by their faith [Q 7:43] support this lived direction.

The petition therefore continues the previous verse’s dependence on God while making its purpose specific. It asks not only to see a correct course but to walk it, remain on it, and avoid turning away.

## 1:7
> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray

### Meaning
The final verse completes the request for guidance by describing the path through its people and its contrasts. It is the path of those whom God has blessed, not the path marked by divine displeasure or by wandering from the way. The focus is not on a route in the abstract, but on a life shaped by obedience and on the consequences of departing from it. The plural prayer asks to join a faithful company and to be protected from ways that lead away from right guidance.

### The blessed company
Qur’an 4:66–69 [Q 4:66–69] links obedience with being guided to a straight path, then names the blessed company as prophets, the truthful, martyrs, and the righteous. The report from Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] similarly describes those blessed through obedience and worship, including angels as well as those named in the Qur’anic passage. Al-Rabīʿ b. Anas via Abū Jaʿfar al-Rāzī [Report: al-Rabīʿ b. Anas via Abū Jaʿfar al-Rāzī] names prophets; reports through Ibn ʿAbbās via Ibn Jurayj [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via Ibn Jurayj] and Wakīʿ [Report: Wakīʿ] give the shorter answers “believers” and “Muslims.” ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Zayd via Ibn Wahb [Report: ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Zayd via Ibn Wahb] identifies the Prophet and those with him. These are different scopes of description—some give a broad company, others name examples or communities within it. The shared emphasis is that the path is recognized by faithful obedience, not by a label alone.

The wording “those You have blessed” also links the path to the earlier request for guidance. The blessed are not presented as people who found the way entirely on their own: their obedience and worship are described alongside the blessing that enabled them. The verse thus completes the previous petition by naming both the way to follow and the source of its guidance.

### The two warnings and their setting
In the answers attributed to the Prophet through ʿAdī b. Ḥātim [Hadith: ʿAdī b. Ḥātim; Musnad Aḥmad 4:378–379; al-Tirmidhī 4:67], “those under anger” are identified as Jews and “those who are astray” as Christians. A Wādī al-Qurā report transmitted through ʿAbd Allāh b. Shaqīq and Budayl al-ʿUqaylī [Hadith: Wādī al-Qurā report via ʿAbd Allāh b. Shaqīq and Budayl al-ʿUqaylī] makes them part of a brief exchange in which a man asks about the people being approached. Explanations attributed to Mujāhid via Mihrān from Sufyān [Report: Mujāhid via Mihrān from Sufyān], Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk], and routes grouped through al-Suddī [Report: al-Suddī via Abū Mālik, Abū Ṣāliḥ, and routes from Ibn Masʿūd and other Companions] repeat the pairing. The Qur’anic warning in [Q 5:77] about those who “went astray before, led many astray, and strayed from the even path” also connects misguidance with a course of action. These references explain the language used in this verse, but should not be collapsed into a complete description of every person in either community.

In the longer prayer attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk], he asks: “Keep us from the way of the Christians whom You led astray; do not be angry with us as You were angry with the Jews, and do not lead us astray as You led the Christians.” The Qur’anic descriptions in [Q 5:60] and [Q 5:77] accompany the same distinction. The discussion cautions against treating the labels as mutually exclusive traits: both communities can be described as astray and as under anger, while each is named by a characteristic. The verse’s contrast is a warning about paths and dispositions, not an exhaustive judgment about every individual.

### Grammar and the force of the contrast
The genitive form of *ghayri*, “other than,” can be read either as describing the blessed people or as repeating an implied “path”: the path of the blessed, the path other than those under anger. Both analyses preserve the contrast, even though they attach the phrase differently. A separate proposed accusative analysis is also discussed, but the app’s recitation keeps the genitive form.

A further disagreement asks whether *lā* before “those astray” merely completes the expression or is a genuine negation. The poem attributed to al-ʿAjjāj [Poem: al-ʿAjjāj] and the construction in [Q 7:12] are brought into the discussion of whether a negative can begin this second phrase; reading it as a real negative makes the final wording exclude both paths rather than leave the second warning without force.

### Anger, misguidance, and agency
The description of divine anger receives more than one explanation: one account connects it with punishment in this life or the next, as in the Qur’anic example in [Q 43:55] and the warning in [Q 5:60]; another connects it with condemnation of people and their deeds, while a third affirms the description and distinguishes it from the agitation or harm human beings experience. The verse names displeasure without making that word a full account of its nature.

The final phrase describes people as astray, while the Qur’an also explicitly speaks of God leading someone astray [Q 45:23]. The evidence notes that Arabic can attribute an action to its immediate actor or to its cause: the ships in [Q 10:22] are said to “run” though the wind moves them. The wording should not be used to erase either human action or divine agency.

The surah closes by making its central request concrete. Praise, mercy, and judgment lead to direct worship; worship asks for help; guidance asks for a way; and this final verse names the company to follow and the paths to avoid. The prayer ends not with an abstract definition of righteousness, but with a request to remain among those who walk the blessed path.
