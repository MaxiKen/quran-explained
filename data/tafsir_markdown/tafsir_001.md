# **SURAH 1: AL-FĀTIḤAH**

## **1:1**
> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

### **MEANING**
The opening words place Allah at the beginning of speech and action. Their sense is an invocation: the speaker names and remembers God before proceeding. The implied action depends on the occasion—beginning a recitation, writing, eating, or rising—so the wording can serve many ordinary beginnings without changing its central purpose. The mercy-names identify the One invoked and shape the reader’s first approach to the surah: praise begins with remembrance, and remembrance begins in hope of mercy. **In the Name of Allah** is therefore not a claim that God is an instrument used to perform an act.

### **A BEGINNING HELD IN REMEMBRANCE**
A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk relates that Gabriel taught Muhammad to say the formula and explained it as *“Read in remembrance of your Lord; rise and sit in remembrance of Allah.”* The report connects recitation with everyday movement. Its point is not that every activity needs a different sacred formula, but that the same invocation can accompany the changing acts of a person’s day.

Arabic leaves the verb unstated because listeners can infer it from what follows. When someone begins reciting, the implied wording is *“I read”*; when someone gets up, it is *“I rise.”* This familiar ellipsis makes the formula compact without making its meaning obscure. The phrase *bi-smi* directs attention to naming and remembering Allah. It is not equivalent to *bi-llāh*, *“by Allah,”* as though the speaker were describing God as a tool or substitute for the person acting. The worshipper remains the one who reads or rises, while consciously beginning under God’s name.

### **THE MERCY-NAMES AT THE THRESHOLD**
The opening invokes Allah as **the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful**. Their position matters to the listener’s first impression: the surah will speak not only of creation and authority, but also of the mercy through which God provides and guides. The formula itself does not promise that every human plan will succeed exactly as intended. It places a beginning under remembrance of the One whose mercy is named, so that the action starts with humility rather than self-sufficiency.

Another explanation attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk identifies Allah as the One whom all creation worships. This does not turn *bismi-llāh* into a complete declaration about the mechanics of divine aid. Rather, it clarifies whom the speaker remembers: the God worthy of worship, whose name is spoken before the act begins.

### **LABĪD’S FAREWELL TO HIS DAUGHTERS**
Labīd’s poem addresses his daughters after his death and asks them to mourn him through a full year. Its closing words are *“then the name of al-Salām be upon you both.”* One explanation hears an instruction to keep God’s name and remembrance when the year of mourning ends; another understands the line as invoking God’s name as protection for the two women. In either reading, the phrase is directed toward the daughters. It is a wish or exhortation for them, not a claim that the poet uses God as an instrument.

The poem makes the formula’s everyday force easier to hear. Naming God can mark a real act of remembrance and care within ordinary speech, just as the opening words set recitation under divine remembrance. This first verse thus teaches a manner of beginning: speak God’s name, recognize the One being invoked, and let the mercy named there frame what follows.

## **1:2**
> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds

### **MEANING**
The surah moves from invocation to a declaration about the One invoked. **All praise** belongs to Allah: the worshipper is not merely reporting personal gratitude for one benefit, but directing praise in its fullest sense to God. Calling Him **Lord of all worlds** widens the statement beyond one people or one visible part of creation. The verse gathers the reasons for praise into a single address—God’s sustaining care, His gifts, and the guidance by which creatures can know and worship Him. It is spoken before any request, so the prayer first recognizes the giver before asking for what is needed.

### **PRAISE THAT GATHERS THANKS**
A gloss attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk explains *al-ḥamd* through *“thanks to God, humility before Him, and acknowledgment of His favor, guidance, and beginning kindness.”* This account presents praise as more than polite speech. Gratitude for a gift, awareness of dependence, and recognition of the giver come together in the worshipper’s words. Another early explanation distinguishes praise of God’s names and qualities from thanks for particular favors, while Arabic also permits overlap between praise and thanks. The verse is broad enough to include both: God is praised for who He is and thanked for what He bestows.

The definite form of *al-ḥamd* helps express that breadth. The wording does not limit praise to what this reciter happens to remember or to a list that can be completed. Qur’an 14:34 and 16:18 say that people cannot count God’s blessings. These passages clarify why the opening declaration can be comprehensive even when each person’s knowledge is partial: **all praise** is directed to the giver of blessings too numerous to enumerate.

### **THE LORD WHO SUSTAINS CREATION**
The Arabic *rabb* can name a master or chief, and it can also describe one who tends, maintains, or puts something in order. Labīd uses it for a tribal leader; al-Farazdaq applies the root to something maintained and repaired. These uses illuminate the verse without reducing it to a dictionary list. Allah’s lordship combines authority with the continued care by which creation is sustained. The grounds for this praise include the faculties needed for obedience, the bodily capacity to fulfil duties, provision for life, and guidance toward lasting good.

The word *al-ʿālamīn* also reaches beyond a single class of beings. Reports attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through ʿIkrimah and to Saʿīd b. Jubayr through ʿAṭāʾ b. al-Sāʾib name jinn and human beings; Qatādah, through Saʿīd b. Abī ʿArūbah, describes each kind as a world. These explanations offer familiar examples and a wider principle rather than competing inventories. Whatever its kind or age, each created community remains within Allah’s lordship. The word **all** is therefore essential: no world stands outside the care and authority celebrated in this verse.

### **PRAISE AS THE WORSHIPPER’S SPEECH**
A report transmitted through al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī from al-Aswad b. Surayʿ quotes the Prophet: *“Nothing is more beloved to Allah than praise.”* It adds that God praised Himself by saying ***“All praise is for Allah.”*** Related versions appear in Musnad Aḥmad and al-Adab al-Mufrad. The prayer dialogue from Abū Hurayrah, transmitted through al-ʿAlāʾ b. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān from Abū al-Sāʾib and preserved in al-Muwaṭṭaʾ and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1:166, gives the reciter a direct response: after this verse, God says, *“My servant has praised Me.”* The line is thus both a statement about Allah and a sentence the worshipper is taught to utter. Its praise reaches beyond private feeling: the prayer voices a truth about the Lord of every world.

## **1:3**
> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

### **MEANING**
After naming Allah as Lord of all worlds, the surah names His mercy again. The repeated words are not an empty interruption: they keep mercy alongside universal lordship as the prayer approaches the Day of Judgment. **The Most Compassionate, Most Merciful** describes the One whose authority is already praised and whose mercy remains central as the worshipper continues. The pair should be heard together, while still allowing each name to contribute a shade of meaning. Their place beside one another invites reflection, not a forced choice between *“same”* and *“different.”*

### **TWO FORMS, ONE ROOT**
Both names come from the Arabic language of mercy, but their forms differ. *Al-Raḥmān* follows an intensive pattern; *al-Raḥīm* is another established form used to describe mercy. The grammar makes it reasonable to hear a difference in emphasis, yet it does not by itself define two separate acts or prove that the words are interchangeable. A report heard by ʿUthmān b. Zafr from al-ʿArzamī describes *“al-Raḥmān as mercy to all creation, and al-Raḥīm as mercy to the believers.”* This explanation distinguishes the reach of mercy: a care shared across creation and a particular care connected with faith.

A gloss attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk approaches the names through another contrast, describing God as *“gentle and kind toward those He wishes to treat mercifully, and severe toward those He wishes to treat harshly.”* These statements do not need to be collapsed into a single formula. One distinguishes the scope of mercy; the other sets kindness beside stern treatment. Together they show why the two names can stand together without making mercy and judgment unrelated subjects.

### **MERCY IN THE WIDER QUR’ANIC PICTURE**
Other passages bring out the breadth and particularity of mercy. Qur’an 14:34 and 16:18 speak of blessings beyond human counting, while Qur’an 33:43 describes God as merciful toward believers. These references echo the two emphases found in the reports: gifts spread throughout creation, and guidance and care especially associated with faith. The distinction does not mean that unbelievers receive no worldly mercy: provision, rain, health, and the growth of crops are shared blessings, while spiritual guidance and lasting reward are singled out as gifts to believers. Taken together, these points prevent mercy from being reduced to comfort alone; provision, direction, and just treatment all belong to the larger picture.

The names also differ in how they are used. *Al-Raḥmān* is presented as a name distinctively belonging to God, whereas *raḥīm* can describe created beings. Qur’an 17:110 says that people may call upon Allah or al-Raḥmān, identifying the two names with the same One. An explanation attached to a report from ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī, heard through Abū al-Azhar Naṣr b. ʿAmr, understands the paired names against Musaylima’s claim to al-Raḥmān: their combination distinguishes the divine name from a human claimant. Qur’an 25:60 records resistance to calling upon **the Most Compassionate**. The reports and passages give context to the name without making that episode a definition of mercy.

The phrase’s recurrence after **Lord of all worlds** returns the listener to the character of the One praised. Its position relative to the opening formula has prompted discussion of verse boundaries, but word order alone cannot settle where those boundaries fall. Whatever conclusion one reaches about verse boundaries, the sequence is clear: universal lordship is named, mercy is recalled, and the next verse turns to accountability.

## **1:4**
> Master of the Day of Judgment

### **MEANING**
The prayer now speaks of the day when every person answers for what they have done. **Master of the Day of Judgment** means that the authority to judge and repay belongs to Allah; no worldly ruler shares that final power. The verse does not cancel the mercy just named, nor does mercy erase accountability. It places both ideas together: the One whose mercy frames the prayer is also the One before whom every deed is brought to account. The **Day of Judgment** is therefore not only a future date but the setting in which human claims to power are shown to be limited.

### **SOVEREIGN AND MASTER**
The transmitted readings include *malik*, *“king”* or sovereign, and *mālik*, *“owner”* or master. The first stresses rule; the second stresses possession and authority. The rendering **Master of the Day of Judgment** can carry their shared force without making the two readings identical. Qur’an 40:16 asks, *“Whose is the kingdom today?”* and answers that it belongs to Allah, the One, the Overpowering. The passage makes visible what the title means: the power held by earthly kings does not accompany them into the final accounting.

A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk explains that no one has judgment alongside God on that day as people exercise authority in worldly life. It links this claim with Qur’an 78:38, where no one speaks except by permission; Qur’an 20:108, where voices are humbled; and Qur’an 21:28, which limits intercession to those God approves. The parallels describe different limits—speech and advocacy—but point to the same sovereignty. The title does not make the final judgment a negotiation between equal powers.

### **ACCOUNT AND RECOMPENSE**
The Arabic *yawm al-dīn* refers here to reckoning and repayment for deeds. Qatādah, in a report through Maʿmar, describes it as the day God repays His servants for their actions; Ibn Jurayj, through Ḥajjāj b. Muḥammad, calls it the day people are brought to account. A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk says that each person is repaid for good or evil *“unless He pardons; the decision belongs to Him.”* The phrase preserves accountability without treating recompense as a process independent of God’s will.

A poem by Kaʿb b. Juʿayl uses the same word for repayment: *“we repay them just as they repay us.”* Its everyday sense helps explain why the verse points beyond a day merely named on a calendar. What a person does has a consequence, but the measure and outcome are not controlled by human beings. The report about pardon makes that limit clear: judgment is real, and the final decision remains with the One who judges.

Qur’an 82:9–10 connects denial of the reckoning with the record of deeds, while Qur’an 56:86 challenges the thought that people cannot be repaid. The reminders deepen rather than displace mercy: hope does not make conduct irrelevant, and the mention of justice does not prevent pardon. In Abū Hurayrah’s prayer-dialogue report, transmitted through al-ʿAlāʾ b. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān from Abū al-Sāʾib and preserved in al-Muwaṭṭaʾ and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1:166, the response after this verse is *“My servant has glorified Me.”* Praise has named the sovereign over creation, mercy, and finally judgment before the worshippers turn to address God directly.

## **1:5**
> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help

### **MEANING**
The prayer changes from speaking about Allah to speaking to Him. Its plural voice makes the declaration communal: **You alone we worship** and **You alone we ask for help**. The two clauses belong together. Worship is exclusive devotion, while asking for help acknowledges that obedience and the rest of life depend on support beyond one’s own strength. The worshippers make a commitment, then ask for what will sustain it. This is neither a claim that they are already self-sufficient nor a request to be excused from action.

### **WORSHIP AS HUMBLE DEVOTION**
A gloss attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk explains the first clause as *“We make You one, fear You, and place our hope in You, our Lord.”* Another explanation puts the emphasis on humility: to worship is to submit and lower oneself before the One acknowledged as Lord. The Arabic language supports that association. The word *muʿabbad* can describe a road worn smooth by the passage of travelers; Ṭarafah uses it in a poem about camels moving over a road. The image helps clarify humility, but the verse is not merely a description of posture. The worshippers direct obedience, fear, hope, and reverence to Allah alone.

The repeated address matters. The pronoun *iyyāka* is brought forward before each verb, so the declaration’s exclusivity applies to both worship and seeking aid. Each clause is complete in itself: the worshippers do not serve one and seek decisive help from another. The wording therefore binds devotion and reliance without confusing them.

### **HELP FOR THE DEEDS AHEAD**
A report from Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk explains the second clause as seeking Allah’s help *“for Your obedience and for all our affairs.”* The request is not a substitute for effort. The worshippers commit to worship and ask for enabling help to fulfil that commitment, as well as to manage the rest of life. The earlier declaration of praise has named the giver of every world’s sustenance; this direct request now acknowledges dependence on that giver.

The prayer looks prospectively toward what remains to be done. Help is sought for continuing in obedience, meeting responsibilities, and avoiding what God has forbidden—not for rewriting actions already completed. The commitment is not treated as a finished accomplishment: strength and enabling for the next obligation remain gifts, so asking is neither redundant nor a claim to control what comes next. This perspective keeps both agency and dependence in view: worshippers act, but they seek the means to carry on. The next petitions make this direction more specific by asking for guidance along the right path. The sequence therefore moves from whom the community serves, to whom it depends on, to the course it needs help to follow.

In Abū Hurayrah’s prayer dialogue, transmitted through al-ʿAlāʾ b. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān from Abū al-Sāʾib and preserved in al-Muwaṭṭaʾ and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1:166, God’s response to the servant’s petition is *“that is for him.”* Its wording fits the transition: the worshipper has praised and glorified God, then asks for help and guidance. The verse holds pledge and petition together rather than allowing either to stand alone.

## **1:6**
> Guide us along the Straight Path

### **MEANING**
Having declared whom they worship and from whom they seek help, the worshippers ask to be led on the right course. **Guide us along the Straight Path** asks for more than information about a route. It asks for direction, sound judgment, and firmness in following the way of obedience as life continues. The plural *us* keeps the prayer shared: each speaker asks to be guided with the community, not merely to receive private insight. The request turns the preceding pledge into a practical need—worshippers need help to recognize and stay upon the course they profess.

### **GUIDANCE THAT STEADIES ACTION**
One gloss attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk renders *ihdinā* as *“inspire us with the guiding path,”* describing it as God’s religion without crookedness. In the surrounding prayer, guidance includes being enabled to hold to the path, not simply being told that it exists. Qur’an 7:43 depicts the people of Paradise praising God for guiding them to this; Qur’an 16:121 describes Abraham as guided to **a straight path**. The passages show both recognition and successful following, which explains why a worshipper can keep asking for guidance even after learning the prayer.

The request looks toward future acts. The worshipper asks for steadiness in obedience and sound choices in what remains of life, extending the plea for help in the previous verse. It does not suggest that earlier knowledge was useless, or that one must wait passively for direction. It expresses dependence on God for the ability to act on what is known and to keep choosing the right course. Repeating the prayer does not mean that a worshipper has never received guidance; it asks for renewed discernment as choices change and for steadiness when the right action becomes difficult. In this sense, knowing the route and being enabled to walk it meet in the same request.

### **WHAT THE STRAIGHT PATH NAMES**
The word *ṣirāṭ* describes a road and can also describe a way of belief or action; *mustaqīm* marks a course that is straight, clear, and without crookedness. A report from ʿAbd Allāh b. Masʿūd through Abū Wāʾil identifies the path with the Book of God. Jābir b. ʿAbd Allāh, in a report through ʿAbd Allāh b. Muḥammad b. ʿAqīl, calls it Islam and describes it as *“wider than what lies between heaven and earth.”* Abū al-ʿĀliyah, through ʿĀṣim al-Aḥwal, identifies the path with the Prophet and his companions Abū Bakr and ʿUmar. These descriptions focus on different aspects: revelation gives direction, Islam is the lived way, and exemplary followers show what that way looks like.

A Prophetic parable transmitted through ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Jubayr b. Nufayr, via his father Jubayr, from al-Nawwās b. Samʿān, also identifies the **straight path** with Islam. It is recorded in Musnad Aḥmad 17711, with related versions in al-Tirmidhī and al-Nasāʾī. The image reinforces the verse’s practical force: the request is to remain on a clear road, not to admire a description from a distance. A further interpretation takes the petition as asking to be led to Paradise in the hereafter. That hope fits the prayer, but it does not exhaust its present sense: the worshipper asks now for the faithful course that leads toward its final reward.

## **1:7**
> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray

### **MEANING**
The final petition describes the path by the people who walk it and by the ways the worshippers ask to avoid. They ask for **the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with**, rather than **those who are astray**. Guidance here is not an abstract map: it means joining a company whose lives answer God’s direction, while seeking protection from courses marked by disobedience and error. The verse completes the prayer by giving the Straight Path recognizable human examples and a serious contrast.

### **A BLESSED COMPANY**
Qur’an 4:66–69 links obedience with being guided to a straight path and names the blessed company as prophets, the truthful, martyrs, and the righteous. A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk likewise describes people blessed through obedience and worship, including angels as well as the named human groups. Other explanations narrow the reference in different ways: al-Rabīʿ b. Anas through Abū Jaʿfar al-Rāzī names prophets; Ibn ʿAbbās through Ibn Jurayj speaks of believers; Wakīʿ says Muslims; and ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Zayd through Ibn Wahb identifies the Prophet and those with him. These descriptions differ in scope rather than necessarily contradicting one another: some give a broad company, while others name a community or example within it.

The wording also joins divine favor with human response. The people follow the path in obedience, but their direction is described as something God has granted and enabled. The report through al-Ḍaḥḥāk makes that relationship explicit: guidance and worship are themselves part of the blessing. The prayer thus asks not only to recognize faithful examples but to receive help to follow them. Qur’an 4:66–69 gives the phrase a concrete shape by connecting obedience with fellowship among the prophets and the righteous.

### **THE TWO WARNINGS IN CONTEXT**
In one route through Ismāʿīl b. Abī Khālid and al-Shaʿbī from ʿAdī b. Ḥātim, the Prophet identifies those under anger with the Jews and those astray with the Christians; another, through Samāk b. Ḥarb and ʿAbbād b. Ḥubaysh from ʿAdī, gives the same pairing. Versions appear in Musnad Aḥmad 4:378–379 and al-Tirmidhī 4:67. A separate account from Wādī al-Qurā, transmitted through Budayl al-ʿUqaylī from ʿAbd Allāh b. Shaqīq, describes a man asking the Prophet whom he was besieging; the answer identifies the Jews as under anger. A related wording applies ***“those who are astray”*** to the Christians. The narrative helps explain how the phrases were applied, while the Qur’anic passages provide a wider frame: Qur’an 5:60 recalls people who incurred anger, and Qur’an 5:77 warns against those who *“went astray before, led many astray, and strayed from the even path.”*

The descriptions are characteristic, not exhaustive judgments about every individual. The two groups can share both descriptions: people may be astray and under anger, even though each label highlights a particular feature. The verse’s practical force remains a prayer about one’s own direction—ask to be among those who obey, and seek protection from the causes of misguidance and divine displeasure.

Arabic grammar also offers two close ways to connect **not those You are displeased with** to the earlier phrase: it can describe the blessed people, or it can imply another mention of *“the path.”* Either way, the worshippers ask for the same destination and exclude the same harmful routes. The surah ends by turning the reciter’s attention to a lived request: to remain faithful, to be joined with the righteous, and to be kept from wandering.
