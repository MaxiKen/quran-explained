# Surah 1: Al-Fatihah

## 1:1
> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

Al-Tabari begins with a grammatical question: what action is understood in *bismillāh* when no verb is spoken? He says the opening *bāʾ* calls for an action, while Arabic allows that action to remain unspoken when the situation makes it clear. Before Qur’an recitation, the sense is “I begin reading by naming Allah”; before standing, sitting, writing, or another task, the surrounding action supplies the omitted verb. He illustrates this with ordinary speech in which a short answer such as “food” is enough because the question has already supplied “what did you eat?” The formula is therefore not, in his account, an assertion that Allah is an instrument by which the reader performs an act. He explicitly rejects “I read by Allah” as the intended grammar and explains it instead as beginning with Allah’s name and remembrance. That distinction leaves room for the belief that every act depends on Allah’s aid, while keeping the sentence’s grammatical meaning focused on invocation. [al-Tabari, 1:1]

Two closely parallel reports attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās connect this interpretation with Gabriel’s instruction to Muhammad. In one, Gabriel says, “Recite in remembrance of your Lord, and rise and sit in remembrance of Allah”; the second preserves almost the same wording and explanation. These reports supply a transmitted setting for the formula, while al-Tabari’s argument about the omitted verb rests separately on how Arabic listeners recover an action from context. They also explain why he describes the practice as extending beyond recitation: the same opening remembrance is used before ordinary actions, letters, books, and requests. [al-Tabari, 1:1, report 138] [al-Tabari, 1:1, report 139]

Al-Tabari does not repeat every report as if each gloss must be accepted. One account gives a letter-by-letter interpretation of *bism* through a story about Jesus learning to write: “the *bāʾ* is Allah’s splendor, the *sīn* His radiance, and the *mīm* His dominion.” Al-Tabari says the transmitter may have joined letters that were being taught separately in a spelling lesson; he rejects the proposed meaning as unintelligible when the phrase is read as Arabic. This is a small but telling example of him testing a transmitted explanation against language rather than relying on its attribution alone. [al-Tabari, 1:1, report 140]

The name *Allah* is then explained through worship. A report from Ibn ʿAbbās calls Allah “the One to whom all creation owes worship.” Al-Tabari supports that gloss with Arabic usage: he connects *ilāh* with worship and adduces reports where Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid explain Pharaoh’s *ilāhatak* as “your worship,” as well as the poetic form *taʾalluhī*, which he glosses as worshipping and seeking Allah through one’s actions. The report, the Qur’anic usage, and the poem thus work together as evidence for the word’s sense; the name is not merely a label detached from what creatures owe their Lord. [al-Tabari, 1:1, report 141] [al-Tabari, 1:1, report 142] [al-Tabari, 1:1, report 143] [al-Tabari, 1:1, report 144]

The two mercy-names also come with competing explanations in the source. Al-ʿArzamī says, “The Compassionate is for all creation; the Merciful is for the believers.” Another report attributed to the Prophet, relating a saying of Jesus, distinguishes the names by time: “The Compassionate is merciful in this world and the next; the Merciful is merciful in the next life.” Ibn ʿAbbās offers a different gloss that emphasizes gentleness toward those shown mercy and severity toward those treated harshly. Al-Tabari compares these rather than treating them as interchangeable: he finds the first two explanations especially apt because one distinguishes the scope of mercy and the other its temporal reach, while the third still recognizes a difference between the names. He also argues from their Arabic forms that the pair is not empty repetition. The wider and more particular meanings are developed further under 1:3. [al-Tabari, 1:1, report 146] [al-Tabari, 1:1, report 147] [al-Tabari, 1:1, report 148]

## 1:2
> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds

Al-Tabari reads *al-ḥamdu lillāh* as praise and thanks belonging to Allah alone, grounded in gifts that cannot be counted. He names concrete evidence rather than leaving “blessing” abstract: the faculties that enable people to obey, the bodily capacities needed to fulfill obligations, provision and livelihood in this world, and guidance toward lasting reward. His explanation makes the verse both praise of Allah and recognition that religious and worldly goods come from Him. [al-Tabari, 1:2]

The reports he transmits show why “praise” and “thanks” overlap here. Ibn ʿAbbās explains the phrase as “thankfulness to Allah, submission to Allah, and acknowledgment of His blessing, guidance, and initiating favor.” Another report, attributed to the Prophet through al-Ḥakam ibn ʿUmayr, says, “When you say ‘Praise belongs to Allah, Lord of all worlds,’ you have thanked Allah, and He will increase you.” Kaʿb al-Aḥbār is also reported as calling *al-ḥamdu lillāh* praise of Allah, though al-Tabari notes that this report does not specify whether it means praise of Allah’s names and attributes or praise for His favors. A further report from al-Aswad ibn Surayʿ attributes to the Prophet the statement, “Nothing is more beloved to Allah than praise; that is why He praised Himself.” These reports approach the phrase from different directions: acknowledgment and gratitude, praise as a form of thanks, and praise taught by Allah Himself. [al-Tabari, 1:2, report 151] [al-Tabari, 1:2, report 152] [al-Tabari, 1:2, report 153] [al-Tabari, 1:2, report 154]

Al-Tabari’s language argument helps compare those explanations. He says Arabic speakers can say “Praise belongs to Allah, in thanks,” and that *ḥamd* can stand where *shukr* is intended; the terms are not sealed into separate boxes, even though one may emphasize commendation and the other gratitude for a favor. He also argues that the definite article in *al-ḥamd* gives the phrase comprehensive force: it means all praise and complete thanks belong to Allah, not only that this particular speaker offers a measure of praise. This grammatical point supports the scale of the reports’ meaning without reducing the whole verse to a single transmitted gloss. [al-Tabari, 1:2]

The word *rabb* gathers several related senses. Al-Tabari uses Arabic poetry as evidence: Labīd’s phrase “the *rabb* of Kinda” means Kinda’s chief, while other verses use the root for repairing, tending, or taking ownership of something. From these usages he draws three connected meanings—master, one who sets affairs right, and owner. They are not rival definitions that require choosing only one: together they describe authority, care, and possession. [al-Tabari, 1:2]

The reports on *al-ʿālamīn* range from narrower examples to an expansive account. In a report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās, the worlds include “the heavens and all within them, the earths and all within them, and what lies between—what is known and what is not known.” Other reports gloss the worlds as humans and jinn; Qatāda’s wording is broader again: “Every kind is a world.” Al-Tabari’s own lexical explanation treats each kind and generation of created beings as a world, which allows those narrower glosses to name prominent examples without limiting the phrase to them. Thus the evidence supports the translation’s universal scope: Allah is praised as master, caretaker, and owner of every world of creation. [al-Tabari, 1:2, report 155] [al-Tabari, 1:2, report 157] [al-Tabari, 1:2, report 158] [al-Tabari, 1:2, report 163] [al-Tabari, 1:2]

## 1:3
> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

Al-Tabari says he has already explained these two names in the opening formula, but their repetition lets him test how the transmitted explanations fit together. The report from al-ʿArzamī gives a distinction of scope: “The Compassionate is for all creation; the Merciful is for the believers.” A separate report attributed to the Prophet, quoting Jesus, gives a distinction of time: “The Compassionate is merciful in this world and the next; the Merciful is merciful in the next life.” These are not identical formulations. The first asks whom mercy reaches; the second asks when it is shown. Al-Tabari argues that both can be accommodated: the name *al-Raḥmān* describes mercy extended to all creation, while *al-Raḥīm* can describe a particular mercy for some of creation, whether in this life, the next, or both. [al-Tabari, 1:1, report 146] [al-Tabari, 1:1, report 147] [al-Tabari, 1:3]

He makes that comparison concrete. In worldly life, believers and non-believers share provision, rain, crops, bodily health, and sound understanding; these common goods illustrate the broad mercy associated with *al-Raḥmān*. Believers, however, receive additional help in faith and obedience, while those who reject faith do not share that particular guidance. For the next life, al-Tabari describes the broad mercy shown to all as Allah’s just judgment: no one is wronged, a good deed may be multiplied, and each soul receives what it earned. The particular mercy of *al-Raḥīm* is the lasting reward and honor prepared for believers. The distinction is therefore not “mercy for one group, none for everyone else”; it is shared care and just treatment alongside a further gift of guidance and reward. [al-Tabari, 1:3]

A third report, attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās, describes the names through gentleness toward those Allah wills to show mercy and severity toward those He wills to treat harshly. Al-Tabari says this still recognizes different meanings in the two names, but he finds the scope-and-time explanations more fitting to the question because they show precisely how the names differ. He also discusses ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī’s report that the name *al-Raḥmān* was taken by the false claimant Musaylima, so pairing it with *al-Raḥīm* distinguishes Allah’s name from that claim. A shorter report from al-Ḥasan calls *al-Raḥmān* “a name withheld”; al-Tabari adds that the community’s refusal to use it for people is sufficient evidence without relying on al-Ḥasan alone. These naming reports add a third comparison—exclusive naming—without displacing the reports about the scope and timing of mercy. [al-Tabari, 1:1, report 148] [al-Tabari, 1:1, report 149] [al-Tabari, 1:1, report 150]

The repeated words also become evidence in al-Tabari’s argument about verse division. He holds that the opening *Bismillāh* is not one of al-Fātiḥah’s verses. His reasoning is that counting it as a verse would repeat the complete phrase *al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm* again in the surah without an intervening passage of different meaning. He considers the objection that *al-ḥamdu lillāh rabb al-ʿālamīn* separates the repetitions, then reports an interpretation that reads the wording as advanced and delayed, placing the mercy-names before *rabb al-ʿālamīn*. On that reading, he argues, the apparent separator does not resolve the repetition. This is a distinct question from what the mercy-names mean: the reports address their semantic difference, while this grammatical argument is offered to support al-Tabari’s view of where the surah’s verses begin. The force of the verse-counting argument depends on the word-order analysis he invokes, a premise readers can assess separately from the mercy distinction. [al-Tabari, 1:3]

## 1:4
> Master of the Day of Judgment

The first word has more than one transmitted reading. Al-Tabari distinguishes *malik* (“King” or “Sovereign”) from *mālik* (“Owner” or “Master”), and also notes a vocalized form with the final consonant in the accusative. He treats the semantic difference between King and Owner seriously: the first declares sovereignty, while the second emphasizes possession. His preference is *malik*, “King,” because, as he reasons, every king is an owner, but an owner need not be a king. The title therefore includes possession while adding a distinct claim of rule. Since the preceding description has already called Allah *rabb al-ʿālamīn*—master and owner of all worlds—repeating ownership alone would add less; kingship adds the sovereignty that no creature can contest. Al-Tabari says he rejects the accusative reading as departing from the established recitation he accepts. [al-Tabari, 1:4]

The reports make the Day’s loss of rival authority more vivid. Ibn ʿAbbās explains *mālik yawm al-dīn* by saying, “No one will possess judgment with Him that day as they possessed it in this world,” and points to passages where speech and intercession occur only by permission. Another report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās calls it “the day the creatures are accounted for”; they are repaid for their deeds, good for good and evil for evil, except whoever Allah pardons, “for the command belongs to Him.” Reports from al-Suddī, Qatāda, and Ibn Jurayj likewise gloss the Day as accounting or recompense. Their wording varies, but each moves the phrase beyond a calendar date: earthly rulers and their claims have ended, and every creature answers to the sole judge. The reports therefore do more than define a term: they describe the Day’s procedure. Voices and intercession are subject to permission, while the second report allows pardon without removing accountability for deeds. Read together, these reports connect sovereignty with judgment: “King” names the authority who decides, and *dīn* names the reckoning carried out under that authority. [al-Tabari, 1:4, report 166] [al-Tabari, 1:4, report 167] [al-Tabari, 1:4, report 168] [al-Tabari, 1:4, report 169] [al-Tabari, 1:4, report 170]

Al-Tabari tests *yawm al-dīn* through both Qur’anic usage and Arabic poetry. He cites the declaration, “To whom belongs the sovereignty today? To Allah, the One, the Overpowering,” as a description of the Day when worldly kings have no competing rule. He also adduces the word *dīn* in lines about repaying an opponent “as they had lent to us” and the saying, “As you recompense, you will be recompensed.” Those examples support his gloss of *dīn* here as accounting and requital, rather than treating “judgment” as a vague future event. Comparing the reading variants with these reports and language examples shows why al-Tabari prefers “King”: the verse joins final accountability to exclusive sovereignty, while the preceding lordship phrase has already established ownership of creation. [Q 40:16, cited by al-Tabari at 1:4] [al-Tabari, 1:4]

## 1:5
> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help

The report al-Tabari places beside *iyyāka naʿbudu* attributes to Ibn ʿAbbās a gloss that includes unifying Allah, fearing Him, and hoping in Him: “We worship You, fear You, and hope in You, our Lord, and no other.” Al-Tabari does not simply repeat that list as his definition. He says he chooses the sense of humbling oneself, becoming low, and submitting before Allah as Lord. Fear and hope can accompany that posture, he notes, but the Arabic word *ʿibādah* itself points first to humility and submission. To support the lexical point he cites Arabic usage of *maʿbad* for a road worn low by repeated footsteps and Ṭarafa’s line about a camel moving over such a track. The comparison makes the word’s image concrete: worship is not merely an emotion but a yielded relation to the one worshipped. [al-Tabari, 1:5, report 171] [al-Tabari, 1:5]

That evidence also explains the exclusivity in the wording. Al-Tabari reads the repeated object as applying to each verb: the worship is directed to Allah, and the request for help is directed to Allah. The second report, again attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās, gives the scope of that request: “We ask You for help with obedience to You and with all our affairs.” The report’s broad wording and al-Tabari’s own explanation together prevent reducing *nastaʿīn* to help with one ritual alone. [al-Tabari, 1:5, report 172]

Al-Tabari then addresses an apparent difficulty: if people are already commanded to obey, why ask Allah to help them obey? His answer is temporal and theological. The worshipper asks for support in the portion of life still ahead, not for deeds already completed. The ability to carry out obligations and the divine enabling to remain obedient are a favor and kindness, not something the servant can presume to control. This makes the two clauses mutually dependent rather than contradictory: worship is the task, while seeking aid acknowledges the source of strength to continue it. He also contrasts the believer’s request to Allah with the way people may seek assistance from what they worship besides Him; in this prayer the servant directs reliance to the same Lord whom they worship. [al-Tabari, 1:5]

The order matters to the analysis. A reader might ask why worship is named before asking for help if worship itself requires help. Al-Tabari’s reasoning is that the two are inseparable: worship cannot be sustained without divine aid, and asking for aid has its proper aim in serving Allah. The repeated object prevents either verb from being detached from the exclusive address. Compared with the transmitted gloss that lists fear and hope, al-Tabari’s lexical evidence gives the central sense of worship, while the report’s emotions remain compatible consequences of humility. Together the report, the Arabic root evidence, and the argument about future obedience explain both halves of the verse without treating human responsibility and divine help as competing explanations. [al-Tabari, 1:5]

## 1:6
> Guide us along the Straight Path

Al-Tabari’s central reading of *ihdinā* is a request for *tawfīq*: enabling to remain steadfast on the straight path. A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās explains the instruction as “inspire us with the guiding way”; al-Tabari takes this inspiration to mean being granted success in continuing to obey, act rightly, and avoid what Allah forbids in the life still ahead. That future orientation connects the request to the preceding plea for help, but does not turn the two verses into duplicates: one asks for assistance, the other specifies the desired direction and steadfastness. [al-Tabari, 1:6, report 173] [al-Tabari, 1:6]

The reports identify the path at different levels. Reports attributed to ʿAlī and to ʿAbd Allāh call it “the Book of Allah”; another report from Jābir calls it Islam and describes it as “wider than what lies between heaven and earth.” Other transmitted glosses identify Islam, while Abū al-ʿĀliyah names the Messenger and the two companions who followed him, Abū Bakr and ʿUmar. These are not identical formulations: a scripture, a religion, and exemplary guides name different aspects of a way. Al-Tabari’s own interpretation does not need to erase that difference. The book gives guidance, Islam names the way of obedience, and the Prophet and his companions furnish examples of following it. Read together, the reports show why “the straight path” can be at once revealed teaching, lived religion, and faithful conduct. [al-Tabari, 1:6, report 176] [al-Tabari, 1:6, report 177] [al-Tabari, 1:6, report 178] [al-Tabari, 1:6, report 184] [al-Tabari, 1:6, report 186]

Al-Tabari’s linguistic evidence addresses what it means to ask for guidance when the path has already been made known. He cites Arabic petitions in which “May Allah guide you” means, in context, “May Allah grant you success in fulfilling my request” or in doing what is right. The examples show *hidāyah* can mean enabling success, not only pointing out a route. On this basis he rejects an interpretation that makes the prayer a request for information about obligations that Allah has not yet explained; he argues that people are shown what they are responsible for. He also rejects reducing the request to being brought into Paradise at the end, since the supplicant is asking to live rightly now. The reports help identify what the path is; the idiom and surrounding request for aid support al-Tabari’s reading of what the verb asks Allah to do: sustain the worshipper in following it. [al-Tabari, 1:6]

He also insists that “straight” describes correctness, not simply a route that happens to end at Paradise. The path is clear and without deviation; calling it straight says that it is right, not mistaken. The differences among the transmitted identifications therefore remain useful evidence rather than noise: they illustrate the path’s content, while the central petition seeks help to remain on it. This comparison keeps al-Tabari’s own preference visible without turning each report into the same statement or presenting his interpretation as though it were the only wording found in the source. [al-Tabari, 1:6]

## 1:7
> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray

The first phrase explains which straight path is being requested. In a report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās, it is “the way of those You favored through obedience and worship,” including angels, prophets, the truthful, martyrs, and righteous people. Other reports narrow the gloss: one says “the prophets,” another “the believers” or “the Muslims,” and another points to the Prophet and those with him. These statements differ in scope, but they need not contradict each other: the narrower groups can be examples within the broader company named in the fuller report. Al-Tabari connects the list to Q 4:69, which names prophets, the truthful, martyrs, and righteous as those whom Allah has favored. His explanation therefore moves from a label for the route to evidence about its followers and the qualities that mark it. [al-Tabari, 1:7, report 188] [al-Tabari, 1:7, report 189] [al-Tabari, 1:7, report 190] [al-Tabari, 1:7, report 191] [al-Tabari, 1:7, report 192] [Q 4:69, cited by al-Tabari at 1:7]

Al-Tabari draws a further conclusion from the wording “those You have favored”: their obedience is itself a divine favor. He argues that guidance and worship do not arise from human effort alone; the verse attributes the servants’ obedience to Allah’s gift of enabling. The prayer is therefore not simply “show us admirable people.” It asks Allah to grant the capacity to follow the way that those people represent. This interpretation connects the final verse back to the requests for help and steadfastness: the path is identifiable in scripture and example, but staying on it remains a gift. [al-Tabari, 1:7]

The grammar of *ghayri al-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim* also carries interpretive weight. Al-Tabari says the reciters agree on the genitive form of *ghayri*. He explains two ways to attach it: it can qualify “those You have favored,” or it can repeat the implied word *path*, yielding “the path of those You favored, the path of those not under anger.” He says these constructions converge in meaning. He considers an accusative reading grammatically possible but rejects it as departing from the widely transmitted recitation he follows. He also weighs an interpretation of the accusative as an exception against a grammatical objection: the following negative *wa-lā* does not fit the proposed exception construction in ordinary Arabic. The grammatical discussion is not decoration; it decides whether the ending is read as a connected exclusion or as an exception from the favored group. [al-Tabari, 1:7]

The reports identify “those under anger” with Jews and “those astray” with Christians. Several reports attributed to the Prophet through ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim give the first identification directly: “Those under anger are the Jews”; other accounts place a similar exchange at Wādī al-Qurā. Reports about the second phrase likewise say, “Those astray are the Christians.” Ibn ʿAbbās’s longer gloss connects the two warnings in a single prayer: “Keep us from the way of the Christians whom You have led astray … do not be angry with us as You were angry with the Jews, and do not lead us astray as You led the Christians.” The differences in the reports’ settings and wording do not alter their shared identifications, but al-Tabari does not make the groups mutually exclusive categories. He explicitly says both can be described as astray and under anger; each is named by a characteristic associated with it, not as though the other fault were impossible. [al-Tabari, 1:7, report 193] [al-Tabari, 1:7, report 194] [al-Tabari, 1:7, report 195] [al-Tabari, 1:7, report 196] [al-Tabari, 1:7, report 207] [al-Tabari, 1:7, report 208] [al-Tabari, 1:7, report 209] [al-Tabari, 1:7, report 215] [al-Tabari, 1:7]

At the end of the discussion al-Tabari answers why the surah gives more than a compressed statement of divine rule and human worship. Its praise teaches gratitude and magnifies Allah; its account of the favored clarifies the requested way; and its warnings make the consequences of disobedience visible. He also regards the Qur’an’s arrangement and language as part of its distinctive evidence, while the surah’s several clauses teach praise, reliance, guidance, and caution in ways a shorter formula would not. This closes the evidentiary arc of the chapter: the final request is defined through reports about exemplary people, grammatical analysis of the exclusion, and transmitted identifications of the paths to avoid. [al-Tabari, 1:7]
