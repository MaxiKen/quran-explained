# Surah 1: Al-Fatihah

## 1:1
> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

The opening *bi-smi* means “in the name of,” but the phrase leaves its action unstated. Arabic listeners can supply that action from what is happening: before reciting, “I begin reading while naming Allah”; before standing or sitting, “I stand” or “I sit” in remembrance of Him. A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās frames Gabriel’s instruction in just this practical way: “Read in remembrance of your Lord, and rise and sit in remembrance of Allah.” The report describes the invocation’s use; the grammar explains why no verb has to be spoken. Together, they make the opening a conscious naming before an act, not a claim that the reader is using God as a tool. [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk]

That distinction matters because *bi-smi Allāh* is not the same wording as *bi-llāh*, “by Allah.” The first names Allah and begins an action with His remembrance. The noun *ism* means “name”; the related *tasmiyah* means “naming.” Arabic can use a noun in a way that points to the act associated with it, as when a verbal noun stands where a form of “giving” or “striking” would be expected. Several lines of poetry are adduced for that kind of usage, so “in the name of Allah” can naturally mean “I begin by naming Allah” without replacing the phrase with “I act by Allah.” Labīd’s line “then the name of Peace upon you” is also discussed: it can be an invocation of God’s name or protection for the addressees, not proof that *ism*, “name,” simply means the person or thing named. [Poem: Arabic lines quoted for verbal-noun usage] [Poem: Labīd b. Rabīʿah]

The name *Allāh* is explained in a report from Ibn ʿAbbās as the one “to whom all creation owes worship.” The linguistic evidence links *ilāh*—a deity, or one worshipped—with Arabic forms of the verb *taʾallaha*, “to worship” or “to seek God through devotion.” A reading of Pharaoh’s complaint, *wa-ilāhataka*, is glossed as “your worship,” and the poet Ruʾba uses *taʾalluhī* for worship and seeking Allah through one’s deeds. These examples make worship the clearest meaning in this explanation. The proposed derivation is an inference from Arabic usage, not a verb form said to have been directly heard; its value here is that it connects the name with the exclusive devotion expressed elsewhere in the surah. [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via ʿAmr b. Dīnār] [Report: Mujāhid via Ibn Jurayj] [Q 7:127] [Poem: Ruʾba b. al-ʿAjjāj]

A different story, attributed to the Prophet through routes associated with Ibn Masʿūd and Abū Saʿīd, has Jesus explain the letters *bāʾ*, *sīn*, and *mīm* as splendor, radiance, and dominion. That is a report worth distinguishing from evidence about Arabic usage: the explanation works only if the letters are split apart, whereas *bism* is heard as one word in the formula. A second wording has Jesus answer a teacher’s question about writing “Allah” with “Allah is the deity of deities.” That connects the name with worship, but it does not establish the letter-by-letter reading. The narrative itself may preserve a children’s spelling lesson that became joined in transmission; as an interpretation of the phrase, its letter-by-letter meanings do not produce a coherent Arabic sentence. [Hadith: Abū Saʿīd via ʿAṭiyyah al-ʿAwfī; parallel route attributed to Ibn Masʿūd]

The final two names are built from *raḥmah*, “mercy.” Their different Arabic forms invite a comparison rather than treating one as a needless echo; the next two verses develop that distinction. The opening thus combines an act of naming, a claim about who is worshipped, and the mention of mercy before the prayer turns to praise and petition. [Q 17:110]

## 1:2
> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds

*Al-ḥamd* means “praise,” and the reports also place it close to *shukr*, “thanks.” Ibn ʿAbbās explains the phrase through gratitude, humility, and acknowledgment of Allah’s favor, guidance, and initiating kindness. A report attributed to the Prophet through al-Ḥakam b. ʿUmayr makes the connection practical: “When you say ‘Praise belongs to Allah, Lord of all worlds,’ you have thanked Allah, and He will increase you.” Kaʿb al-Aḥbār likewise calls it praise, although that brief report does not specify whether the praise concerns divine names and qualities or gifts received. These statements overlap without becoming identical: praise can honor what Allah is, while thanks names the good received from Him. [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] [Hadith: attributed to the Prophet by al-Ḥakam b. ʿUmayr; route through Bāqiya b. al-Walīd, ʿĪsā b. Ibrāhīm, and Mūsā b. Abī Ḥabīb] [Report: Kaʿb al-Aḥbār via al-Salūlī]

The wording also reaches beyond one person’s act of praise. The definite article in *al-ḥamd* supports the broad sense “all praise,” while Arabic speakers can use *ḥamd* where *shukr* is intended. One Prophetic report says that nothing is more beloved to Allah than praise, and that He therefore praised Himself. A longer report from Abū Hurayrah supplies a response to the recitation: when the worshipper says *al-ḥamdu lillāh*, Allah answers, “My servant has praised Me.” The first report links praise with what God loves; the second stages it as a dialogue between worshipper and Lord. A parallel report preserves the same response sequence. [Hadith: al-Aswad b. Surayʿ; Musnad Aḥmad 15650, 15654; al-Adab al-Mufrad 51; parallel from Ibn Masʿūd in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim] [Hadith: Abū Hurayrah; al-Muwaṭṭaʾ 84–85; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1:166]

The word *rabb* can mean “lord” or “master,” and its Arabic uses also carry the sense of tending, setting affairs right, and exercising ownership. In Labīd’s line, “the lord of Kinda” means Kinda’s chief; another poetic use calls an unrepaired hide *ghayr marbūb*, “not tended or made right.” These senses meet in *rabb al-ʿālamīn*: the one praised is not only powerful over the worlds but also their caretaker and master. The line therefore joins sovereignty with an ongoing relation to creation. [Poem: Labīd b. Rabīʿah] [Poem: al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī] [Poem: al-Farazdaq]

*Al-ʿālamīn* means “the worlds.” Reports attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās describe the heavens, the earths, their inhabitants, and what lies between them, both what people know and what they do not. Other reports name humans and jinn; one says each kind of being is a world, and another says each nation is a world in its own right. Those narrower examples can name familiar groups without setting a limit on the broader phrase. A line by al-ʿAjjāj also uses *ʿālam* for a world or age, showing the word’s reach beyond a single human community. [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via ʿIkrimah] [Report: Saʿīd b. Jubayr] [Report: Qatādah] [Poem: al-ʿAjjāj]

One report from Abū al-ʿĀliyah offers an unusually precise picture: beyond humans and jinn, it counts either fourteen or eighteen thousand angelic worlds, with the transmitter explicitly unsure which number. This stands apart from the reports that define *al-ʿālamīn* by kinds of creatures rather than a count. The uncertainty is part of the evidence, not a number the verse itself requires readers to settle. Across the range of reports, the stable point is scope: Allah is praised as *rabb* of every created world, known or unknown. [Report: Abū al-ʿĀliyah via Rabīʿ b. Anas]

## 1:3
> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

The two names share the root *raḥmah*, “mercy,” but their forms are not identical. *Al-Raḥmān* follows the pattern *faʿlān*; *al-Raḥīm* follows *faʿīl*. The Arabic argument preserved with these verses treats the first form as a more emphatic expression of praise, while the second can name mercy directed particularly toward some recipients. That difference gives a linguistic reason not to flatten the pair into exact synonyms. A report attributed to al-ʿArzamī makes the distinction one of reach: *al-Raḥmān* is “for all creation,” while *al-Raḥīm* is “for the believers.” Another report, which quotes Jesus in a saying attributed to the Prophet, distinguishes time: the Compassionate is merciful in this life and the next, while the Merciful is merciful in the next life. [Report: al-ʿArzamī as heard by ʿUthmān b. Zafr] [Hadith: saying attributed to Jesus through routes via Ibn Abī Mulaykah/Ibn Masʿūd and ʿAṭiyyah al-ʿAwfī/Abū Saʿīd]

The sequence of names can be read in a similar movement from identification to description. An Arabic argument places *Allāh* first as the name most closely tied to exclusive worship, then *al-Raḥmān* as a specially divine title, and then *al-Raḥīm* as a word that can also describe people. The order does not make human mercy equal to God’s; it shows how a distinctive name can be followed by names and qualities that describe the manner of divine care. [Q 17:110]

Those are different contrasts, not necessarily competing definitions. “All creation” versus “believers” asks who receives mercy; “this life and the next” versus “the next” asks when. The two can be brought together if general mercy means the provisions shared across human life—rain, food, health, and other gifts—and particular mercy means guidance to faith and the promised reward of believers. The cited Qur’anic language supports both registers: blessings cannot be counted, while Allah is also described as merciful to believers. A report from Ibn ʿAbbās uses another distinction, describing tenderness toward those shown mercy and severity toward those treated harshly. That wording emphasizes contrasting relations rather than contrasting periods or groups. [Q 14:34] [Q 16:18] [Q 33:43] [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk]

A further report links the distinctiveness of *al-Raḥmān* with the claim that Musaylima had taken the name for himself; al-Ḥasan is also reported as calling the name restricted. This speaks to exclusive divine naming, but it does not show that Arabic speakers had never encountered the word. Two attributed poems use *al-Raḥmān* as a name for God, and the Qur’an itself answers the question “What is the Compassionate?” by directing worship to Allah or al-Raḥmān. The example of people knowing a truth while denying it is also invoked in the same discussion. The evidence therefore separates knowledge of a word from accepting the claim it makes. [Report: ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī via Abū al-Azhar Naṣr b. ʿAmr] [Report: al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī via ʿAwf] [Poem: anonymous pre-Islamic verse] [Poem: Salāmah b. Jandal] [Q 25:60] [Q 2:146] [Q 17:110]

The names also do work in the surah’s sequence. A report from Abū Hurayrah says that when the worshipper recites “the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful,” Allah answers, “My servant has extolled Me.” Mercy is thus not merely a description inserted between praise and judgment: it is itself a reason for praise and part of the worshipper’s exchange with God. [Hadith: Abū Hurayrah; al-Muwaṭṭaʾ 84–85; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1:166]

The repeated phrase raises a separate question about the opening’s structure: why mention the names in the invocation and again after the praise? One grammatical proposal takes “the Compassionate, the Merciful” as descriptions of Allah that belong semantically before *rabb al-ʿālamīn*, even though they appear after it in the written sequence. Arabic can place a description later than the noun it describes, as the wording of Q 18:1 is used to illustrate. That proposal concerns word order and verse boundaries, not the meaning of mercy itself; it does not by itself settle how the opening phrase is counted. In the verse divisions represented by the app, the two placements can also be heard with different functions: the first names God as recitation begins, and the later phrase explains whom the praise addresses. [Q 18:1]

## 1:4
> Master of the Day of Judgment

The Arabic word behind “Master” is read in two closely related ways: *mālik*, “owner,” and *malik*, “king” or “sovereign.” The first emphasizes possession; the second emphasizes rule. They are not interchangeable in every sentence—an owner need not be a king—but the two meanings converge in the day named here, when no earthly ruler can claim independent authority. The familiar app rendering “Master” leaves room for both aspects without turning the verse into two separate statements. An accusative vocalization is also mentioned and raises a separate question about how the phrase attaches to the preceding praise; the main contrast concerns the owner and the king. [Q 40:16]

The verse also prepares a change in how the prayer speaks. Up to this point, Allah is described in the third person as Lord and Judge; in the next verse the worshippers address Him directly as “You.” Arabic poetry can shift from speaking about someone to addressing that person, as in lines by Abū Kabīr al-Hudhalī and Labīd. Q 10:22 likewise moves from “you were in the ships” to “they sailed,” showing that a change of person need not break a passage’s meaning. Here the movement from naming the Judge to speaking before Him makes the prayer more immediate. [Poem: Abū Kabīr al-Hudhalī] [Poem: Labīd b. Rabīʿah] [Q 10:22]

A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās says that on that day “no one will possess authority alongside Him as they possessed it in this world.” It pairs the claim with descriptions of restricted speech, humbled voices, and intercession only for those whom God permits. Other reports call it the day of reckoning; Qatādah describes it as the day God requites people for their deeds, and Ibn Jurayj as the day they are brought to account. The emphasis is not merely that God owns the future: worldly power is exposed as temporary, while judgment belongs to Him without rival. [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] [Q 78:38] [Q 20:108] [Q 21:28] [Report: al-Suddī via Abū Mālik, Abū Ṣāliḥ, and routes from Ibn Masʿūd and other Companions] [Report: Qatādah] [Report: Ibn Jurayj]

The word *dīn* here is explained as “reckoning” and “requital.” Arabic poetry uses the verb *dāna* for repaying someone as they have repaid you; another line says, in effect, “as you requite, you will be requited.” Qur’anic usage likewise pairs denial of *al-dīn* with the rejection of accountability, and asks how people could escape being repaid for what they have done. These examples make “Day of Judgment” a fitting English rendering: it is the day when deeds are brought to account and answered. [Poem: Kaʿb b. Juʿayl] [Poem: unnamed Arabic verse] [Q 82:9–10] [Q 56:86]

The two readings are heard together in another report about recitation: after “Master of the Day of Judgment,” Allah answers, “My servant has glorified Me.” The report does not explain the reading difference, but its response fits the verse’s double emphasis: God’s ownership and sovereignty are both reasons to glorify Him. Q 40:16 gives the scene in a compressed question and answer—“To whom belongs the kingdom today?” “To Allah, the One, the Overpowering.” The phrase therefore moves from praising the Lord of all worlds to recognizing who alone judges them. [Hadith: Abū Hurayrah; al-Muwaṭṭaʾ 84–85; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1:166] [Q 40:16]

## 1:5
> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help

The repeated *iyyāka* places “You” before each verb: *iyyāka naʿbudu*, “You alone we worship,” and *iyyāka nastaʿīn*, “You alone we seek help from.” Repeating the object makes the two declarations distinct while directing both to the same addressee. The plural “we” also matters: the worshipper speaks as part of a community, not only as an isolated individual. A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās expands the first clause as “we affirm Your oneness, fear You, and hope in You—our Lord, not another.” Its emotional language shows what worship entails, while the Arabic root supplies a more basic sense. [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk]

Repeating *iyyāka* is not simply padding. The fronted pronoun is the object of each verb, so it can be repeated to give both statements their own complete address: “You we worship, and You we seek help from.” Arabic can leave a shared object understood, but the repeated form makes the exclusivity audible in each clause. It is unlike a relation such as “between,” which requires two terms to complete its meaning; each verb here can stand as a full statement on its own.

*ʿIbādah* means worship or devoted service and is related to *ʿabd*, “servant.” The linguistic evidence connects the root with being humbled or brought under another’s authority. A road worn smooth under travelers’ feet is called *muʿabbad*, “trodden and made level”; a poem by Ṭarafah uses that word for a well-traveled path. The comparison does not reduce worship to humiliation. It shows the posture carried by the word: the worshipper yields, serves, and recognizes the Lord’s authority. Fear and hope can accompany that posture, as the report says, without exhausting its meaning. [Poem: Ṭarafah b. al-ʿAbd]

The second verb, *nastaʿīn*, means “we seek help.” Ibn ʿAbbās glosses it as asking Allah for help “with obedience to You and with all our affairs.” This is wider than help with one ritual, yet the order of the two clauses keeps the request directed toward worship: devotion is named, then the worshipper admits dependence in carrying it out. The placement does not make the two ideas rivals. Someone can be responsible for worship and still ask for the strength to continue it. [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk]

That request also raises a natural question: why ask for help with obedience if one is already obeying? A prospective reading resolves the apparent tension: a worshipper asks for support in the life still ahead, not for deeds already completed. On that reading, the prayer neither claims that the person has done nothing nor assumes that past success guarantees future steadiness. It places intention and action alongside dependence: the servant acts, while asking God to enable what remains. This is a more specific request than a general wish for assistance, and it makes “we worship” and “we ask for help” mutually illuminating.

A report about the whole recitation gives the transition a dialogic shape. After “You alone we worship and You alone we ask for help,” the response says, “This is between Me and My servant, and My servant shall have what he asks.” The preceding words praise and glorify; here the reciter turns from describing God to addressing Him and asking. The repeated “You” makes that turn unmistakable: service and reliance are not sent to different powers. [Hadith: Abū Hurayrah; al-Muwaṭṭaʾ 84–85; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1:166]

## 1:6
> Guide us along the Straight Path

*Ihdinā* means “guide us,” and *al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm* means “the straight path.” A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās paraphrases the request as “inspire us with the guiding way.” The word “inspire” emphasizes direction within the person; the noun “path” gives that direction a concrete course to follow. Arabic petitions such as “May God guide you to my request” can mean “May God enable you to fulfill what is right,” not simply “May God show you where the request is.” That usage supports hearing the prayer as a plea for help in following what is right as well as learning it. [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] [Poem: unattributed Arabic lines illustrating *hadāka Allāh*]

An alternative gloss asks God to “increase us in guidance.” That wording can include deeper understanding, continued direction, or success in acting on what is known. The surrounding prayer favors a meaning broader than receiving new information alone: the worshippers have already declared their devotion and asked for help, yet still ask to be guided. Nor does the request point only to a final arrival in Paradise; the reports name scripture, Islam, a road, and exemplary conduct as the path to be followed.

The reports identify the path from several angles. ʿAlī and ʿAbd Allāh b. Masʿūd call it “the Book of God”; other reports call it Islam, the religion with no crookedness, or simply “the road.” Jābir’s report describes Islam as wider than the distance between heaven and earth. Abū al-ʿĀliyah gives a more personal identification: the Messenger and his two companions Abū Bakr and ʿUmar. These are not the same kind of answer. A scripture names guidance’s source, Islam its lived way, and a model of the Prophet and his companions a way of seeing conduct embodied. Their difference helps show why the singular “path” can encompass teaching, practice, and example. [Report: ʿAlī via al-Ḥārith al-Aʿwar] [Report: ʿAbd Allāh b. Masʿūd via Abū Wāʾil] [Report: Jābir b. ʿAbd Allāh via ʿAbd Allāh b. Muḥammad b. ʿAqīl] [Report: Abū al-ʿĀliyah, affirmed by al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī]

Other attributions reinforce the identification with Islam: Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn al-Ḥanafiyyah, al-Suddī with several Companion routes, and Ibn Zayd all give that sense, while another Ibn ʿAbbās gloss is simply “the road.” A Prophetic report from al-Nawwās b. Samʿān begins a parable of a straight path and explicitly identifies that path as Islam. These reports converge on a way of life without erasing the distinct “Book,” “religion,” and “example” formulations. “Straight” marks the way as right rather than crooked; one Qur’anic cross-reference likewise describes Abraham as guided to a straight path. [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via Maymūn b. Mihrān] [Report: Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyyah via Abū ʿUmar al-Bazzār] [Report: al-Suddī via Abū Mālik, Abū Ṣāliḥ, and routes from Ibn Masʿūd and other Companions] [Report: ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Zayd] [Hadith: al-Nawwās b. Samʿān; Musnad Aḥmad 17711; al-Tirmidhī; al-Nasāʾī] [Q 16:121]

The context prevents “guide us” from being only a request for first information. The people who have just declared worship and dependence still ask to be guided; knowledge of a route does not make continued adherence automatic. The prayer asks for direction and steadiness in what remains, and it is voiced in the plural so that guidance is sought as a shared course rather than a private possession. The distinction between knowing the path and being helped to remain on it also links this verse back to the plea for help in 1:5 without making the two requests identical.

The evidence therefore gives the phrase both a clear object and an ongoing force. The Book, Islam, the road, and exemplary followers describe what the way is; the petition asks God to lead the worshippers into it and keep them on it. The phrase “Straight Path” does not name a destination alone. In the language of these reports, it is the right course of belief and action to be followed now. [Q 7:43] [Q 16:121]

## 1:7
> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray

The final verse identifies the requested path by its followers and by two ways from which the worshippers seek protection. *Anʿamta ʿalayhim* means “those You have favored.” A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās describes them as people favored through obedience and worship, listing angels, prophets, the truthful, martyrs, and righteous people. Other transmitted glosses name prophets, believers, Muslims, or the Prophet and those with him. The shorter answers can be read as examples within the wider list rather than as rival lists of unrelated groups. The Qur’anic cross-reference at Q 4:69 names prophets, the truthful, martyrs, and righteous people as those whom God has favored; the next verse links obedience to being guided to a straight path. [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] [Report: al-Rabīʿ b. Anas] [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via Ibn Jurayj] [Report: Wakīʿ] [Report: ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Zayd] [Q 4:66–69]

The two warnings are explained through familiar collective identifications: “those You are displeased with” are identified as Jews, and “those who are astray” as Christians. Several reports attributed to ʿAdī b. Ḥātim place these answers in a direct exchange with the Prophet; another group of reports recalls a question asked at Wādī al-Qurā. Reports from Mujāhid, Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Suddī, al-Rabīʿ, and Ibn Zayd repeat the same pair of identifications. Ibn ʿAbbās also gives a longer prayer: “Keep us from the way of the Christians whom You have led astray; do not be angry with us as You were angry with the Jews, and do not lead us astray as You led the Christians.” The wording makes the objects of avoidance ways or paths, not simply names detached from conduct. [Hadith: ʿAdī b. Ḥātim; Musnad Aḥmad 4:378–379; al-Tirmidhī 4:67] [Hadith: the Wādī al-Qurā exchange reported by ʿAbd Allāh b. Shaqīq] [Report: Mujāhid] [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] [Report: al-Suddī via Abū Mālik, Abū Ṣāliḥ, and routes from Ibn Masʿūd and other Companions] [Report: al-Rabīʿ b. Anas] [Report: ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Zayd]

The collective labels do not make the two faults mutually exclusive. Both groups can be described as astray and as under anger; each is named by a characteristic, not presented as the only fault its members could have. The verse’s contrast is therefore meaningful without becoming a complete description of every person in either community. It warns the worshipper away from a path marked by rejection, error, or disobedience and asks for a different company and course. [Q 5:60] [Q 5:77]

There is also more than one explanation of divine anger. One describes its consequence as punishment in this life or the next; another describes it as censure of people and their actions. These accounts emphasize result and evaluation respectively. The warning in this verse does not require the reader to settle every question about divine emotion; its immediate force is that the path incurs serious displeasure and is to be avoided. [Q 43:55] [Q 5:60]

The grammar of *ghayri al-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim* helps connect the warning to the path. In the genitive form, *ghayri* (“not those of” or “other than”) can qualify “those You have favored.” It can also be understood as repeating an implied noun: “the path of those You favored—the path other than that of those under anger.” Both analyses preserve the exclusion in the app’s translation. An accusative parse is also discussed as an exception, but the following *wa-lā* (“and not”) makes that construction harder to sustain: the negative phrase reads more naturally as a further exclusion than as part of an exception. The grammar shapes how the ending attaches to the request, but neither parsing changes the need to distinguish the way sought from the ways refused. A related dispute asks whether *wa-lā* before “those astray” carries its straightforward negative force or is a completing particle in the construction. Arabic poetry and the phrase *mā manaʿaka allā tasjud* (“what prevented you from prostrating?”) in Q 7:12 are brought into that discussion. The choice affects the clause’s syntax, but the app’s ordinary reading preserves the two warnings as parallel exclusions. [Poem: al-ʿAjjāj] [Q 7:12]

The words “under anger” and “astray” also do not, by themselves, settle every question about agency. A person may be named for an action while another cause is also involved; Arabic speech can say that a tree moved even when wind moved it. The Qur’anic image of ships sailing with a favorable wind gives the same kind of attribution, while another passage explicitly speaks of God leading someone astray. The examples caution against using one grammatical label to prove that no other agency or cause is present. In the prayer, the worshippers ask both to avoid a misdirected path and to be protected from divine displeasure. [Q 10:22] [Q 45:23]

The ending also completes the surah’s movement rather than merely repeating it. Praise names the Lord and the scope of His care; mercy and judgment give reasons for glorifying Him; worship and dependence turn praise into direct address; guidance asks for a course to follow; and the final verse describes that course through exemplary people and cautions. A report from Abū Hurayrah presents the surah’s first statements as a dialogue—praise, extolling mercy, glorification of the Judge—before the worshipper asks for what remains. The concluding verse thus clarifies the request rather than replacing it with a second summary: it shows whose way is being sought and which departures are being refused. [Hadith: Abū Hurayrah; al-Muwaṭṭaʾ 84–85; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1:166]
