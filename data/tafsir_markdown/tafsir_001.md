# Surah 1: Al-Fatihah

## 1:1
> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

Al-Tabari understands *bismillāh* as beginning an action by naming and remembering Allah. The action is left unspoken because the context supplies it: before reciting, the sense is “I begin reciting in Allah’s name”; before standing, sitting, writing, or another task, that action is understood. He rejects taking the phrase to mean “I do this by Allah,” as if Allah were the instrument of the act. The point is to begin by invoking the Lord’s name and remembrance.

The two names in the opening phrase both express mercy. Al-Tabari connects them with *raḥmah* and distinguishes their scope more fully in his explanation of 1:3: mercy broadly extended to creation and a particular mercy for believers.

## 1:2
> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds

Al-Tabari reads *al-ḥamdu lillāh* as praise and thanks that belong to Allah alone. The opening *al-* makes the praise comprehensive: the verse acknowledges not just one speaker’s gratitude but all praise due to the Lord. He connects that praise with Allah’s blessings—sound faculties that enable obedience, bodily capacities to carry out duties, provision in worldly life, and guidance toward lasting reward. The wording gathers gratitude for gifts of both life and religion.

He explains *rabb* through connected Arabic senses: **master**, **owner**, and one who sets affairs right. Together these describe Allah’s authority over creation: He owns and governs it and brings its affairs to order. *Al-ʿālamīn* refers to the kinds and generations of created beings. Reports he cites name human beings and jinn, while his broader explanation includes every kind of creation, known and unknown. Allah is thus praised as Lord of all worlds, not only one community or class of beings.

## 1:3
> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

Both names are related to *raḥmah* (mercy), but al-Tabari does not treat them as mere duplication. He explains *al-Raḥmān* as mercy reaching all creatures, while *al-Raḥīm* points to a particular mercy shown to believers—especially guidance to faith and obedience, and the reward prepared for them. He presents these scopes as complementary: creation receives Allah’s broad care and provision, while believers receive this further spiritual favor.

Al-Tabari also uses the repetition of *al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm* in a discussion of verse division. He holds that the opening *Bismillāh* is not itself a verse of al-Fātiḥah. He argues that counting it as one would repeat a complete verse with the same wording and meaning, without an intervening phrase of a different meaning. In response to the objection that *al-ḥamdu lillāh rabb al-ʿālamīn* comes between the two occurrences, he cites an interpretation that reads the wording as reordered, placing *al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm* before *rabb al-ʿālamīn*. He treats this as evidence for his position on where the surah’s verses begin.

## 1:4
> Master of the Day of Judgment

Al-Tabari discusses two principal readings for the first word: *malik* (“King” or “Sovereign”) and *mālik* (“Owner” or “Master”). Both affirm complete authority, but he prefers *malik*, “King.” His reason is that kingship entails ownership, while ownership alone does not necessarily entail kingship; “King” therefore adds a distinct sense after Allah has been named Lord of all worlds. On the Day of Judgment, rulers who claimed power in worldly life will have no share in sovereignty.

*Yawm al-dīn* is the Day of account and recompense, when people meet the outcome of what they did. Al-Tabari emphasizes that Allah’s kingship then stands alone: no earthly ruler can contest it, and those who once ruled will stand humbled before Him.

## 1:5
> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help

For al-Tabari, *iyyāka naʿbudu* is a confession of exclusive worship expressed as humility, lowliness, and submission before Allah as Lord. He considers fear and hope relevant to worship, but prefers the sense of humble service: the Arabic root of *ʿibādah* conveys being brought low or subdued, like a well-trodden path. The fronted and repeated object makes the exclusivity explicit for each act: worship and the plea for help are directed to Allah alone.

With *iyyāka nastaʿīn*, worshippers ask Allah alone for help with worship, obedience, and all their affairs. They ask for support to keep obeying through what remains of life. Al-Tabari presents this capacity as divine favor: the worshipper asks to be sustained in acts of obedience still ahead. The clauses depend on one another: a person needs Allah’s aid in order to worship, and seeking aid expresses reliance on Him. He also explains the repeated object as applying the request for help explicitly to Allah, just as the preceding verb makes worship exclusive to Him.

## 1:6
> Guide us along the Straight Path

Al-Tabari takes *ihdinā* here chiefly as a request for Allah to grant *tawfīq*—the enabling to remain firm on the straight path, obeying what He commands and avoiding what He forbids. The prayer looks ahead to the part of life still to come: as in the preceding request for help, the worshipper seeks the ability to continue in right belief and action. It is not merely a request for information about a path already made clear, nor simply a request to be brought into Paradise at the end.

He describes *aṣ-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm* as a clear, straight way without error or deviation. The explanations he gathers—including Islam, adherence to Allah’s scripture, and following the Prophet and those who obey Allah—describe this same way from different angles. In his central reading, the request is to be enabled to follow the truth consistently and to remain on the way of those whom Allah has favored.

## 1:7
> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray

The final phrase specifies which straight path is meant: the way of those whom Allah has favored with obedience and worship. Al-Tabari relates this to the Qur’anic description of the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous. He presents obedience itself as a blessing from Allah and a result of His enabling; the prayer asks for steadfast guidance along the way of those servants.

For *ghayri al-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim wa-lā al-ḍāllīn*, al-Tabari favors the genitive reading *ghayri* and treats the phrase as an exclusion: not the path of those under anger or those who have gone astray. Interpretations he transmits identify the first group as Jews and the second as Christians. He does not make these descriptions exclusive: both groups may be described as astray and under anger, though each is named by a characteristic associated with it in the reports. The prayer therefore asks to be kept from disobedient and misguided paths, not simply to recite labels.

Al-Tabari also explains why the surah’s praise and petition unfold at length. Its praise teaches gratitude and magnifies Allah; its account of those favored clarifies the path being requested; and its warnings show the danger of disobedience. He further points to the Qur’an’s distinctive composition and language. In his explanation, the surah’s praise, gratitude, request for guidance, and warning each serve a purpose rather than repeat the same point.
