# **SURAH 1: AL-FĀTIḤAH**

## **1:1**
> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

### **MEANING**
The opening formula teaches the worshipper how to begin an act: by naming and remembering Allah before speaking or doing it. Its verb is left unstated because the occasion makes it clear. Before reciting, the implied sense is *“I begin reading”*; before another task, that task supplies the action. The phrase is an invocation at the threshold, not a claim that God is an instrument or that a person acts *“by Allah”* as though using Him as a tool. The two mercy-names, **the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful**, describe the One whose name is invoked and set a tone of reverence and hope before the surah’s praise begins.

### **BEGINNING WITH GOD’S NAME**
A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk says that Gabriel taught Muhammad to begin with the formula and explained it as *“Read in remembrance of your Lord; rise and sit in remembrance of Allah.”* The instruction links recitation with ordinary movement. Remembrance is not reserved for a single ceremony; the same invocation can open a reading, a letter, a meal, or another undertaking. The words **In the Name of Allah** make the beginning explicit while leaving the action itself to be understood from the moment.

The Arabic *bi-smi* is not the same expression as saying *“by Allah.”* The former names and remembers God as one begins; the latter would describe God as the means by which the action is done. This difference matters because the formula does not deny that human beings act, nor does it claim that God is being handled like an instrument. It teaches the speaker to place the act under God’s remembrance before it proceeds. The omitted verb changes with the task, but the orientation of the beginning stays the same.

This explanation also clarifies why the formula appears before many kinds of speech and action. The listener can infer what is being begun from the setting, just as ordinary conversation leaves out a word when the context has already supplied it. The formula therefore joins a concrete act to a prior act of remembrance without turning into a complete sentence about the mechanics of divine assistance. Its force is simple and practical: mention God first, then continue with the work at hand.

### **A FAREWELL POEM AND A PRAYER FOR PROTECTION**
Labīd’s farewell poem addresses his two daughters and asks them to mourn him through a year. Its closing line says *“then the name of al-Salām be upon you both”* after that period. One explanation hears an exhortation to keep God’s name and remembrance when the mourning is over. Another understands the words as a protective invocation, like wishing that God’s name be upon the two women against harm. In either reading the line is directed toward the daughters: God’s name and protection are invoked for them, not treated as an instrument in the poet’s hands.

The poem helps show that naming God can carry a real purpose in ordinary speech, not merely introduce a subject. Here remembrance and protection belong together, just as the surah’s opening joins the act of beginning with the names of mercy. The formula does not promise that every undertaking will unfold exactly as the speaker wishes. It instead frames the beginning as an act of reverence, dependence, and hope in the One whose name is spoken.

## **1:2**
> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds

### **MEANING**
The verse turns the opening invocation into praise. **All praise** belongs to Allah, not only the thanks a particular person happens to express. The phrase **Lord of all worlds** widens the declaration to every kind of creation and every age. Allah is praised both for who He is and for the gifts and guidance on which creation depends. This is a communal sentence of worship: the prayer teaches its speakers to recognize the source of their blessings before asking for anything.

### **PRAISE, GRATITUDE, AND THE WORSHIPPER’S VOICE**
A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk explains *al-ḥamd* as *“thanks to God, humility before Him, and acknowledgment of His favor, guidance, and beginning kindness.”* The explanation gathers gratitude and recognition rather than reducing praise to a passing feeling. A worshipper may thank God for a particular gift, but the verse directs praise more broadly to the One whose kindness, guidance, and sustaining care make life possible.

An account transmitted by al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī from al-Aswad b. Surayʿ recounts the Prophet saying that *“your Lord loves praise.”* Related wording appears in Musnad Aḥmad, including reports 15650 and 15654, and in al-Adab al-Mufrad 51. In the prayer dialogue narrated from Abū Hurayrah through Abū al-Sāʾib, preserved in al-Muwaṭṭaʾ and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1:166, God answers the worshipper’s praise with *“My servant has praised Me.”* The verse is therefore both a statement about God and words the worshipper is taught to say; praise becomes the voice of prayer rather than an abstract description.

Qur’an 14:34 and 16:18 remind their readers that God’s blessings cannot be counted. That limit is not a weakness in the praise; it explains why the declaration is so wide. The worshipper cannot list every benefit, but can direct praise to the One from whom provision, capacity, and guidance come. Gratitude for particular gifts thus belongs within a larger acknowledgment of God’s generosity. The definite wording also reaches beyond one speaker’s personal thanks: the worshipper voices a sentence that assigns praise as a whole to Allah. A single recitation cannot enumerate every gift, but it can acknowledge the One to whom every genuine praise returns.

### **THE LORD AND THE WORLDS**
The Arabic word *rabb* can describe a master or chief, and it can also describe one who tends and maintains what is under his care. Labīd uses it for a tribal chief, while examples from al-Nābighah and al-Farazdaq use the root in connection with tending or setting something right. These usages help convey why the verse’s lordship includes both authority and care: Allah is not merely named as ruler of creation but as the One who sustains it.

Reports attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through ʿIkrimah and to Saʿīd b. Jubayr through ʿAṭāʾ b. al-Sāʾib explain the worlds as jinn and humankind. Qatādah, through Saʿīd b. Abī ʿArūbah, gives a broader expression, saying that each kind is a world. These descriptions need not be forced into competition. Jinn and humans are familiar examples; the wording itself reaches further, to every realm of created life, whether known to people or not. The phrase **Lord of all worlds** thus grounds praise in universal dependence: no community and no world lies outside God’s care.

## **1:3**
> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

### **MEANING**
The verse repeats the two mercy-names after praise of the Lord of all worlds. This repetition deepens, rather than interrupts, the opening movement: the One praised as Creator and Sustainer is also named through mercy before the prayer turns to the Day of Judgment. The forms *al-Raḥmān* and *al-Raḥīm* come from the language of mercy but are not identical in form or emphasis. Their grammatical shapes suggest different shades within a shared root, so the pair can intensify praise without requiring the reader to assign a separate event to each name. The Qur’anic context, rather than a one-word gloss, shows the range of that mercy. Together they make mercy central to how the worshipper addresses Allah. The words **the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful** should be heard as a pair, not collapsed into one empty repetition.

### **TWO NAMES, RELATED EMPHASES**
The forms differ in Arabic: *al-Raḥmān* carries an intensive pattern, while *al-Raḥīm* is another established description from the same root. That difference does not by itself define two unrelated kinds of mercy, but it helps explain why both names can stand together. A report heard by ʿUthmān b. Zafr from al-ʿArzamī gives one distinction: *“al-Raḥmān is mercy to all creation, and al-Raḥīm is mercy to the believers.”* The report directs attention first to mercy’s breadth and then to its special relation to those who believe.

A separate explanation attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk describes God as *“gentle toward those shown mercy and severe toward those treated harshly.”* These explanations approach the pair from different angles. One distinguishes the reach of mercy; the other contrasts gentleness with stern treatment. Neither requires the reader to imagine two competing qualities. Both preserve the central point that the names disclose aspects of divine mercy while leaving judgment, named in the next verse, within the same surah.

### **MERCY IN THE QUR’ANIC CONTEXT**
The Qur’an places mercy alongside both shared blessing and particular care. Qur’an 14:34 and 16:18 say that people cannot enumerate God’s favors, giving context for mercy that reaches across creation. Qur’an 33:43 describes God as merciful toward the believers, which fits the report that speaks of a special relation to them. The passages do not make mercy a synonym for comfort alone: care, guidance, provision, and just treatment all belong to the wider picture.

In this pairing, *al-Raḥmān* is used as a name distinctively associated with God, while *raḥīm* can also describe created beings. The Qur’anic command to call upon Allah or al-Raḥmān in Qur’an 17:110 makes clear that these are names of the same One, not competing deities. A report from ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī, heard through Abū al-Azhar Naṣr b. ʿAmr, connects the prominence of *al-Raḥmān* with Musaylima’s attempt to claim the name. Qur’an 25:60 records an objection to calling upon **the Most Compassionate**; the episode concerns resistance to the name, not a new definition of mercy. Read in sequence, the pair extends the praise of verse 2: the Lord over every world is merciful, and His mercy remains present as the prayer proceeds toward accountability. The order of the names helps the listener follow that movement, but word order alone does not settle separate questions about verse boundaries.

## **1:4**
> Master of the Day of Judgment

### **MEANING**
The verse brings the opening praise to the day when human power and responsibility are seen in their true limits. **Master of the Day of Judgment** means that judgment and recompense belong to Allah alone. This does not set mercy against justice: the prayer has named mercy, and now it names the One before whom every deed is answered. The phrase is more than a date on a calendar; it points to the final accounting and to the authority that no worldly ruler can share. This makes the verse both an affirmation of divine sovereignty and a reminder of each worshipper’s accountability.

### **KINGSHIP AND OWNERSHIP**
The Arabic readings *malik* and *mālik* carry related but distinguishable senses: king or sovereign, and owner or master. Kingship emphasizes rule; ownership emphasizes possession and authority over what belongs to one. The translation **Master of the Day of Judgment** can be heard broadly enough to gather their shared force without erasing the difference. Qur’an 40:16 makes the sovereignty of that day vivid by asking who owns the kingdom then and answering that it belongs to Allah, the One who prevails.

A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk says that no one has judgment alongside God on that day as people exercise authority in worldly life. The report connects this sovereignty with restricted speech in Qur’an 78:38, humbled voices in Qur’an 20:108, and intercession only for those God approves in Qur’an 21:28. It describes a day when speech and advocacy are not independent powers. These passages clarify what the title means in practice: rule belongs to God, and every permission to speak or intercede remains under His authority.

### **THE DAY OF ACCOUNT AND RECOMPENSE**
The expression *yawm al-dīn* points to the day of reckoning and repayment for deeds. Another report from Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk describes it as the day of account, when people are repaid for what they did and the final decision remains God’s. Qatādah, in a report through Maʿmar, calls it the day God repays His servants for their deeds; Ibn Jurayj, through Ḥajjāj b. Muḥammad, likewise explains it as the day of reckoning. Their brief explanations agree on accountability even as they phrase it differently. An account grouped under al-Suddī, transmitted through Abū Mālik and Abū Ṣāliḥ from Ibn ʿAbbās and through other Companions, also calls it the Day of Account. Ibn ʿAbbās’s wording leaves room for pardon—*“unless He forgives; the decision is His”*—so repayment is not pictured as a process independent of God’s will.

Qur’an 82:9–10 connects denial of final reckoning with the record of deeds, while Qur’an 56:86 challenges the idea that people are beyond being repaid. Together they show why this verse follows the names of mercy: hope does not erase responsibility, and judgment does not erase mercy. In the prayer dialogue narrated from Abū Hurayrah through Abū al-Sāʾib and preserved in al-Muwaṭṭaʾ and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1:166, the response after this verse is *“My servant has glorified Me.”* The praise has now named creation, mercy, and final authority before the worshippers turn directly to God.

## **1:5**
> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help

### **MEANING**
The prayer now shifts from speaking about Allah to addressing Him directly. Its plural voice is shared: the worshippers say **You alone we worship** and **You alone we ask for help**. These clauses belong together. Worship is exclusive devotion, expressed through humility, obedience, hope, and reverence; asking for help admits that neither worship nor the rest of life is sustained by human strength alone. The verse is both a pledge and a request for the means to remain faithful.

### **EXCLUSIVE WORSHIP**
A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk gives the first clause this sense: *“We make You one, fear You, and place our hope in You, our Lord.”* The report brings worship together with the attitudes that shape it. To worship God alone is not simply to pronounce His name; it is to direct devotion to Him and not to rivals. The Arabic language of worship also carries humility and submission. A line in Ṭarafah’s description of travel uses *muʿabbad* for a road worn smooth by footsteps, an image that helps explain the idea of being made low or compliant without reducing worship to a word study.

The second clause makes the same exclusive address in seeking assistance. Ibn ʿAbbās’s explanation through al-Ḍaḥḥāk says that help is sought for obedience and for all affairs. It is not a request to be excused from acting. The worshippers commit themselves to service and ask Allah to enable them to carry it out. In the sequence of the prayer, this request looks forward to what remains of life: help is sought for continuing in obedience, fulfilling responsibilities, and avoiding what has been forbidden.

### **THE PRAYER’S RESPONSE AND ITS ORDER**
The plural voice includes each worshipper among others, but the direct address remains personal: each speaker is saying whom they serve and from whom they seek support. Worship is not presented as an achievement already secured; the prayer asks for aid on the path ahead. The pledge and request answer different questions—whom do we serve, and from whom do we seek support?—and together they turn devotion into practical dependence. The explanation attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk extends help to obedience and all affairs, so the same prayer can guide daily choices as well as formal worship.

In the prayer dialogue narrated from Abū Hurayrah through Abū al-Sāʾib, preserved in al-Muwaṭṭaʾ and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1:166, God’s response after the worshipper completes the recitation is *“that is for him.”* The response places the petition within the prayer’s address to God. The worshippers affirm whom they serve and then ask for the help they need.

Some readers might expect assistance to be named first because worship depends on it. The order does not deny that dependence. The two acts are inseparable in practice: a person cannot continue worship without help, and the request for help is directed toward worship and every other affair. The verse first states the commitment and then asks for the aid that will sustain it. Its repeated address to Allah keeps both clauses under the same exclusive devotion, and the next verse makes the needed guidance explicit.

## **1:6**
> Guide us along the Straight Path

### **MEANING**
The worshippers now ask for the guidance needed to live out their pledge. **Guide us along the Straight Path** is not only a request to learn that a path exists; it asks for direction and firmness in following it. The plural *us* continues the communal voice of the previous verse. Guidance includes recognizing the way of obedience and receiving help to remain on it in what lies ahead. The petition therefore joins knowledge with action and asks that neither be left to human effort alone.

### **WHAT GUIDANCE ASKS FOR**
A gloss attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk renders *ihdinā* as *“inspire us with the guiding path”* and describes that path as God’s religion without crookedness. The verb can describe directing someone to a way and helping that person follow it. Qur’an 7:43 recalls the people of Paradise praising God for guiding them to this, while Qur’an 16:121 describes Abraham as guided to a **straight path**. These passages show that guidance is not merely information; it is also being led to the way and enabled to walk it.

The request also looks ahead. A worshipper does not ask only about what has already been understood or done, but for steadfastness in the deeds still to come. The connection with the previous plea for help is direct: the community has declared worship, asked for aid, and now names the path along which that worship should continue. The repeated prayer does not assume that guidance can be stored once and for all; it asks for renewal and firmness as life proceeds.

### **THE STRAIGHT PATH IN THE REPORTS**
The explanations name different aspects of the path. ʿAbd Allāh b. Masʿūd, through Abū Wāʾil, identifies it with the Book of God. Jābir b. ʿAbd Allāh, in a report through ʿAbd Allāh b. Muḥammad b. ʿAqīl, calls it Islam and describes it as *“wider than what lies between heaven and earth.”* Abū al-ʿĀliyah, through ʿĀṣim al-Aḥwal, identifies the path with the Prophet and the companions who followed him, Abū Bakr and ʿUmar. These reports do not have to be treated as rival definitions: scripture guides, Islam is the religion lived, and faithful examples show the path in action.

A Prophetic report from al-Nawwās b. Samʿān presents a parable of a **straight path** and identifies it with Islam. It appears in Musnad Aḥmad 17711, and related versions are also found in al-Tirmidhī and al-Nasāʾī. That image reinforces the prayer’s central request: not simply to know a correct description, but to remain on a clear way. The word *ṣirāṭ* can describe a road and can also be used for a course of belief or action; *mustaqīm* stresses that it is straight, with no crookedness in its direction.

### **A WAY TO FOLLOW NOW**
One explanation takes the request as asking to be led to Paradise in the hereafter. Yet its place after the declaration of worship and the request for help makes the present course essential: the worshippers ask for a way to live now as well as for its final destination. Qur’an 16:121 and 7:43 connect guidance with a path followed by Abraham and by the people who reach their reward. The petition is therefore not a choice between earthly obedience and a future home. It asks for the faithful course that leads from the present life toward that home.

## **1:7**
> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray

### **MEANING**
The final verse explains the Straight Path through the people who follow it and the paths from which the worshippers seek protection. They ask for the way of **those You have blessed**, rather than **those You are displeased with** or **those who are astray**. The prayer is not describing a road in the abstract; it asks to join a company formed by obedience and to avoid ways marked by anger and misguidance. It completes the request by naming both the goal and the warning.

### **THE BLESSED COMPANY**
Qur’an 4:66–69 links obedience with guidance to a straight path and names the blessed company as prophets, the truthful, martyrs, and the righteous. A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk describes those blessed through obedience and worship, while a report from al-Rabīʿ b. Anas through Abū Jaʿfar al-Rāzī names the prophets. Other early explanations use shorter descriptions: Ibn ʿAbbās through Ibn Jurayj speaks of believers, Wakīʿ says Muslims, and ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Zayd through Ibn Wahb points to the Prophet and those with him. These answers emphasize different scopes, but all direct attention to faithful obedience rather than a name detached from conduct.

The verse also connects divine blessing with human response. Those on the path act in obedience, yet the prayer describes their course as something God has blessed them to follow. The worshippers therefore ask not only to recognize a good example but to receive guidance and help to follow it. The company named in Qur’an 4:66–69 gives the request a concrete shape: the straight path is lived among people who answer God’s guidance with faithfulness.

### **THE WARNINGS AND THEIR CONTEXT**
In reports from the Prophet transmitted through ʿAdī b. Ḥātim, the people described as under anger are identified as Jews, and those described as astray are identified as Christians. Versions of the report appear in Musnad Aḥmad 4:378–379 and al-Tirmidhī 4:67. A separate account from Wādī al-Qurā, carried through ʿAbd Allāh b. Shaqīq and Budayl al-ʿUqaylī, places the Prophet on campaign when a man asks about the people before them; the answer identifies the group under anger, and a related wording identifies those astray. The narrative setting shows how the phrases were explained in a concrete encounter.

The Qur’an gives related context in different passages. Qur’an 5:60 recalls people who incurred anger and were punished, while Qur’an 5:77 warns against those who *“went astray before, led many astray, and strayed from the even path.”* The report explanations distinguish characteristic errors, but they should not be turned into a complete description of every person in either community. The verse’s wider request remains active for every worshipper: seek the way of obedience, avoid deliberate departure from it, and ask God for protection from error.

The closing contrast thus gathers the surah’s themes into one request. Praise has led to worship; worship has asked for help; help has been specified as guidance; and guidance has been tied to the company of those who receive and follow it. The final words do not invite the worshipper to judge others from a distance. They teach a prayer for one’s own direction, steadiness, and protection from the paths that lead away from God.
