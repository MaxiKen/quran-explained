# Surah 1: Al-Fatihah

## 1:1
> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

Two reports from Ibn ʿAbbās connect the opening formula to Gabriel’s instruction to Muhammad: “Read in remembrance of your Lord,” and “rise and sit in remembrance of Allah.” They present *bismillāh* not as a special phrase confined to recitation, but as an invocation before action. The surrounding explanation extends that practice to ordinary beginnings, correspondence, requests, meals, and other acts. Its role is to turn the beginning of an act toward God before the act proceeds. [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk]

The wording itself leaves the action unspoken. Before a recitation, the listener understands “I begin reading in the name of Allah”; before standing or sitting, the act supplies the missing verb. Arabic permits that ellipsis when the situation makes the action clear. The phrase *bi-smi Allāh* is also distinct from *bi-llāh*, “by Allah”: it names God at the start of an act rather than grammatically making Him an instrument used by the speaker. A report explains the name *Allāh* as “the one to whom all creation owes worship.” The same formula is used before slaughter, hunting, meals, speech, and writing, confirming that it frames actions through invocation and remembrance. [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk]

A story attributed to the Prophet through routes associated with Ibn Masʿūd and Abū Saʿīd tells of Jesus learning to write. When asked to write *bism*, he assigns meanings to its separate letters: the *bāʾ* is divine splendor, the *sīn* radiance, and the *mīm* dominion. A second wording has him answer that Allah is “the deity of deities.” These tellings connect the letters and the name with God, but they are not the same kind of evidence as ordinary Arabic usage. The letter-by-letter explanation depends on separating sounds that form one word in *bismillāh*; the account itself suggests the letters may have been taught separately before being joined in transmission. The phrase’s meaning is better tested as a phrase than as an acrostic. [Hadith: Abū Saʿīd via ʿAṭiyyah al-ʿAwfī; parallel route attributed to Ibn Masʿūd]

The connection between Allah’s name and worship is also illustrated by the Qur’anic phrase *wa-ilāhataka*, which Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid explain as Pharaoh’s “worship”; a line by Ruʾba uses *taʾalluhī* for worship and seeking God through devotion. This linguistic evidence supports the gloss “the one worshipped,” while the derivation of the name from related Arabic forms is presented as an inference from usage rather than a directly attested verb form. [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via ʿAmr b. Dīnār] [Report: Mujāhid via Ibn Jurayj] [Q 7:127] [Poem: Ruʾba b. al-ʿAjjāj]

The two mercy-names in the formula prepare the praise that follows. Elsewhere the Qur’an joins the names Allah and al-Raḥmān when directing people to call upon God; here the invocation places mercy before the surah turns to God’s gifts, judgment, and the worshipper’s request. [Q 17:110]

## 1:2
> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds

Reports from Abū Hurayrah present the opening of al-Fātiḥah as a dialogue. When the worshipper says, “Praise belongs to Allah, Lord of all worlds,” God answers, “My servant has praised Me.” One transmission stops at Abū Hurayrah’s own wording, while other routes attribute the response to the Prophet; the same sequence is cited in al-Muwaṭṭaʾ and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. This exchange helps explain why the verse is more than a private statement of gratitude: the worshipper voices praise, and the report describes that praise as received and answered. [Hadith: Abū Hurayrah; al-Muwaṭṭaʾ 84–85; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1:166]

Other reports bring out different aspects of the same praise. Ibn ʿAbbās links it to thanks, humility, and acknowledging Allah’s favor, guidance, and initiating kindness. A report attributed to the Prophet through al-Ḥakam b. ʿUmayr says, “When you say ‘Praise belongs to Allah, Lord of all worlds,’ you have thanked Allah, and He will increase you.” Kaʿb al-Aḥbār calls the phrase praise but does not specify whether the praise is for God’s names and qualities or for His gifts. A report from al-Aswad b. Surayʿ says that praise is especially beloved to Allah, who praised Himself; its wording is also preserved in cited collections and has a parallel about God loving praise from Ibn Masʿūd. Taken together, the reports do not force a choice between praising who God is and thanking Him for what He gives. The Qur’anic reminders that people cannot count their blessings give that gratitude an expansive scope: praise is not exhausted by one person’s words. [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] [Hadith: attributed to the Prophet by al-Ḥakam b. ʿUmayr; route through Bāqiya b. al-Walīd, ʿĪsā b. Ibrāhīm, and Mūsā b. Abī Ḥabīb] [Report: Kaʿb al-Aḥbār via al-Salūlī] [Hadith: al-Aswad b. Surayʿ; Musnad Aḥmad 15650, 15654; al-Adab al-Mufrad 51; parallel from Ibn Masʿūd in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim] [Q 14:34] [Q 16:18]

The phrase’s scope is broad. The definite form *al-ḥamd* supports “all praise,” rather than only the amount one speaker can offer; Arabic usage also allows *ḥamd*, “praise,” and *shukr*, “thanks,” to overlap. Thus the line gathers praise for divine qualities and gratitude for blessings without reducing one to the other. The wording of a report from Ibn ʿAbbās about the next phrase expands that scope to “all creation—the heavens and those within them, the earths and those within them, and what lies between, what is known and what is not known.” [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk]

The word *rabb*—“lord,” “master,” or one who tends and sets affairs right—adds care to authority. Poetry uses it for a chief: Labīd’s “lord of Kinda” means the tribe’s leader. Other lines use the root for tending or repairing something. In *rabb al-ʿālamīn*, the one praised is therefore not only powerful over creation but its master and caretaker. [Poem: Labīd b. Rabīʿah] [Poem: al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī] [Poem: al-Farazdaq]

The reports about *al-ʿālamīn*, “the worlds,” range from familiar examples to the entire created order. Some name humans and jinn; another says every kind is a world, and another treats each nation as a world in its own time. A line by al-ʿAjjāj calls the tribe of Khandif “the head of this world,” using *ʿālam* for a people of a particular age. One report from Abū al-ʿĀliyah gives an uncertain count of fourteen or eighteen thousand angelic worlds. That striking number differs from the reports defining worlds by kinds and generations, and the transmitter signals uncertainty. The shared point is not a fixed inventory but the breadth of the praise: Allah is *rabb* of every world, known and unknown. [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via ʿIkrimah] [Report: Saʿīd b. Jubayr] [Report: Qatādah] [Poem: al-ʿAjjāj] [Report: Abū al-ʿĀliyah via Rabīʿ b. Anas]

## 1:3
> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

The dialogue reported from Abū Hurayrah continues: when the reciter says “the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful,” God answers, “My servant has extolled Me.” The names therefore have a place in the surah’s movement, not just in its vocabulary. They explain why the praise is addressed to God, and the report treats naming His mercy as itself an act of praise. [Hadith: Abū Hurayrah; al-Muwaṭṭaʾ 84–85; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1:166]

Several transmitted explanations distinguish the two names. Al-ʿArzamī says that *al-Raḥmān* is “for all creation,” while *al-Raḥīm* is “for the believers.” A report that quotes Jesus in a saying attributed to the Prophet instead distinguishes time: the Compassionate is merciful in this life and the next, while the Merciful is merciful in the next life. Ibn ʿAbbās describes tenderness toward those shown mercy and severity toward those treated harshly. These explanations focus on different questions—who receives mercy, when it is received, and how it is experienced—so they should not be flattened into a single quotation. [Report: al-ʿArzamī as heard by ʿUthmān b. Zafr] [Hadith: saying attributed to Jesus through routes via Ibn Abī Mulaykah/Ibn Masʿūd and ʿAṭiyyah al-ʿAwfī/Abū Saʿīd] [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk]

The contrast between general and particular mercy can make sense alongside the Qur’anic passages that speak of uncountable blessings and of God’s mercy toward believers. Shared provisions—rain, crops, health, and sustenance—reach people broadly; guidance and promised reward are described as particular gifts. The distinction can also extend to the final judgment: general mercy is expressed in just treatment, with no one wronged and good multiplied, while believers receive a further promised honor. Another report explains mercy through gentleness toward some and harshness toward others. Each account illuminates a different dimension, while the repeated names hold them together: mercy is both a relation to creation and a distinctive gift to those who believe. [Q 14:34] [Q 16:18] [Q 33:43] [Q 4:40]

A further report associates the name *al-Raḥmān* with Musaylima’s attempt to claim it; al-Ḥasan is also reported as calling the name restricted. Yet poems attributed to an anonymous pre-Islamic poet and to Salāmah b. Jandal use *al-Raḥmān* for God. The question “What is the Compassionate?” in Q 25:60 and the command to call upon Allah or al-Raḥmān in Q 17:110 show that a disputed name and denial of its claim are not the same as a word being unknown. The cited example of people recognizing a truth while rejecting it makes the same distinction. [Report: ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī via Abū al-Azhar Naṣr b. ʿAmr] [Report: al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī via ʿAwf] [Poem: anonymous pre-Islamic verse] [Poem: Salāmah b. Jandal] [Q 25:60] [Q 17:110] [Q 2:146]

The names’ Arabic forms also suggest that the pair is not empty repetition: *al-Raḥmān* follows a more emphatic pattern than *al-Raḥīm*, while both are derived from *raḥmah*, “mercy.” The order moves from the unique name Allah, through the specially divine title al-Raḥmān, to al-Raḥīm, a word that can describe people as well. A separate grammatical debate asks how the repeated names relate to the opening invocation and whether that affects the verse divisions. One proposal takes them semantically before *rabb al-ʿālamīn*, despite appearing after it in the written sequence, and uses Q 18:1 as an example of a description placed later than the word it describes. That argument concerns structure, not the meaning of mercy; in the app’s verse division, the first placement opens the recitation and the second follows the praise. [Q 18:1] [Q 17:110]

## 1:4
> Master of the Day of Judgment

Qur’anic descriptions of the final day emphasize that authority is no longer shared with earthly rulers: “To whom belongs the kingdom today?”—“To Allah, the One, the Overpowering.” A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās says that no one will possess judgment alongside God as people possess authority in this world. It connects the verse with the restriction of speech, humbled voices, and intercession only by permission. Other reports simply call it the day of reckoning; Qatādah describes it as the day God requites people for their deeds. The cross-references make the loss of worldly power concrete: speech is permitted only to those whom the Merciful allows and who speak rightly; voices are humbled; and intercession is limited to those approved. Together they show why “Master” means more than possession of a date on the calendar: judgment, speech, and mediation all fall under divine authority. [Q 40:16] [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] [Q 78:38] [Q 20:108] [Q 21:28] [Report: al-Suddī via Abū Mālik, Abū Ṣāliḥ, and routes from Ibn Masʿūd and other Companions] [Report: Qatādah] [Report: Ibn Jurayj]

The app’s “Master” stands over two related readings in Arabic: *mālik*, “owner,” and *malik*, “king” or “sovereign.” The first emphasizes possession, the second rule. They are not identical—an owner need not be a king—but on the Day of Judgment both lead to the same central contrast: worldly authority is temporary, whereas God’s judgment has no rival. The short- and long-vowel readings therefore bring together two dimensions of authority rather than offering unrelated meanings. An accusative vocalization is also mentioned; it raises a separate question about how the phrase attaches to the preceding praise, while the main interpretive contrast remains ownership and kingship. [Q 40:16]

The phrase *yawm al-dīn* is explained as the day of accounting and requital. Poetry uses *dāna* for repaying someone as they have repaid you; another line says, “As you requite, you will be requited.” The Qur’an also uses *al-dīn* in contexts of denying accountability and asks how people could escape being repaid. These parallels support “Day of Judgment” as a day when deeds are brought to account, not merely a day on which rule changes hands. [Poem: Kaʿb b. Juʿayl] [Poem: unnamed Arabic verse] [Q 82:9–10] [Q 56:86]

The recitation report from Abū Hurayrah gives the verse its place in the prayer’s exchange: after “Master of the Day of Judgment,” God answers, “My servant has glorified Me.” A report from Jābir likewise describes the prayer as divided between God and the worshipper: the praise and glorification belong to God, while what follows is the worshipper’s request. This makes the next turn—from describing God as Judge to addressing Him as “You”—a natural movement from praise to direct appeal. [Hadith: Abū Hurayrah; al-Muwaṭṭaʾ 84–85; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1:166] [Hadith: Jābir b. ʿAbd Allāh via Saʿd b. Isḥāq b. Kaʿb b. ʿUjrah]

Arabic poetry can shift from speaking about someone to addressing that person, and Q 10:22 moves from “you were in the ships” to “they sailed.” The transition into the next verse is therefore not a break in the subject: the one praised as sovereign is now addressed directly by the worshippers. [Poem: Abū Kabīr al-Hudhalī] [Poem: Labīd b. Rabīʿah] [Q 10:22]

## 1:5
> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help

A report from Abū Hurayrah describes the turn in the prayer after the opening praise: when the servant says, “You alone we worship and You alone we ask for help,” God’s answer is, “That is for him.” In Jābir’s version, God says, “I divided the prayer between Me and My servant in two halves, and My servant shall have what he asks.” It then marks the opening praise, mercy, and judgment as praise of God before the prayer turns toward what the servant asks. Together, the reports make the movement from praise to direct appeal explicit. [Hadith: Abū Hurayrah; al-Muwaṭṭaʾ 84–85; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1:166] [Hadith: Jābir b. ʿAbd Allāh via Saʿd b. Isḥāq b. Kaʿb b. ʿUjrah]

Ibn ʿAbbās explains the first declaration as “we affirm Your oneness, fear You, and hope in You—our Lord, not another.” The wording brings together belief, feeling, and devotion. Other reports expand the second declaration: “We ask You for help with obedience to You and with all our affairs.” This is not assistance for a single ritual alone. The two clauses reinforce one another: the worshippers commit themselves to serve God and acknowledge that they need His help in that service and in their wider lives. [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk]

The repeated *iyyāka*—“You”—makes the exclusive address audible before each verb: “You we worship, and You we ask for help.” Arabic can leave a shared object understood, but repeating it gives each clause its own complete address. That repetition is not ornamental; it prevents the second request from drifting away from the one just named as the object of worship. The plural “we” also makes the prayer communal: the worshipper speaks as part of a body of servants, not only for an isolated self.

The root of *ʿibādah*, “worship,” carries the sense of devoted service and submission. The Arabic word *muʿabbad* describes a road made level by being trodden; Ṭarafah uses it for a path worn by travelers. That poetic usage helps explain the posture in the report: worship is not only fear and hope, but a yielding of one’s direction and conduct. [Poem: Ṭarafah b. al-ʿAbd]

A question arises from the sequence: if obedience requires God’s help, why is worship stated before asking for help? A forward-looking reading resolves the difficulty. The worshipper asks for help in the life still ahead, not for deeds already completed. Present responsibility and dependence are thus held together: people act, but do not presume they can sustain obedience without help. The order is relational rather than a timeline; worship is not a finished stage before dependence begins. The worshipper names the commitment and, in the same breath, asks for help to maintain it. The next verse makes the request more specific by asking for guidance along the right path.

## 1:6
> Guide us along the Straight Path

The reports do not define the Straight Path in only one way. ʿAlī and ʿAbd Allāh b. Masʿūd identify it as “the Book of God.” Several reports call it Islam: Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn al-Ḥanafiyyah, al-Suddī with several Companion routes, and Ibn Zayd all give that answer. Jābir describes Islam as “wider than what lies between heaven and earth.” Abū al-ʿĀliyah identifies the path with the Messenger and his two companions Abū Bakr and ʿUmar. These answers name different dimensions—a revealed source, a lived religion, and an embodied example—rather than necessarily contradicting one another. [Report: ʿAlī via al-Ḥārith al-Aʿwar] [Report: ʿAbd Allāh b. Masʿūd via Abū Wāʾil] [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via Maymūn b. Mihrān] [Report: Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyyah via Abū ʿUmar al-Bazzār] [Report: Jābir b. ʿAbd Allāh via ʿAbd Allāh b. Muḥammad b. ʿAqīl] [Report: al-Suddī via Abū Mālik, Abū Ṣāliḥ, and routes from Ibn Masʿūd and other Companions] [Report: ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Zayd] [Report: Abū al-ʿĀliyah, affirmed by al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī]

A Prophetic report from al-Nawwās b. Samʿān introduces a parable of a straight path and explicitly identifies the path as Islam. Its placement alongside the other explanations adds transmitted support for treating the phrase as a way of life, not merely a road to a final destination. Q 16:121 likewise describes Abraham as guided to a straight path; Q 7:43 speaks of people guided by their faith. Those passages connect the path with lived belief and divine guidance. [Hadith: al-Nawwās b. Samʿān; Musnad Aḥmad 17711; al-Tirmidhī; al-Nasāʾī] [Q 16:121] [Q 7:43]

The phrase *ihdinā* means “guide us,” but the reports and Arabic examples give the request a continuing force. Ibn ʿAbbās glosses it as “inspire us with the guiding way” and identifies that way as God’s religion, without crookedness. In Arabic petitions, “May God guide you to my request” can mean “May God enable you to fulfill what is right,” not simply “show you where it is.” This helps explain why worshippers who have just declared their faith still ask for guidance: knowing a teaching is not the same as remaining steady in following it. [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] [Poem: unattributed Arabic lines illustrating *hadāka Allāh*]

An alternative gloss asks for an increase in guidance. That can mean deeper understanding or continued success in acting on what is known. The immediate context—worship, dependence, then guidance—allows both knowledge and steadfastness to matter, while making a request for first information alone too narrow. The reports likewise resist reducing the “path” to Paradise at the end: they describe scripture, Islam, a road, and faithful examples to be followed in this life. The word *mustaqīm*, “straight,” then marks the path as right and free of deviation. One formulation makes the time frame explicit: ask for steadfastness and right action in the life still ahead, not for deeds already completed. This echoes the previous request for help but gives that help its destination. [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] [Q 40:55]

The verse thus asks for more than directions. It gathers the scripture, religion, and example named in the reports, and asks God to lead “us” into that course and keep the community on it. The plural form makes guidance a shared request, and the link with the prior plea for help makes continued adherence—not an isolated insight—the focus of the petition.

## 1:7
> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray

Q 4:66–69 supplies an explanation for “those You have blessed.” It links obedience to being guided to a straight path, then names the favored company as prophets, the truthful, martyrs, and righteous people. A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās similarly describes those favored through obedience and worship, listing angels, prophets, the truthful, martyrs, and righteous people. Other reports shorten the answer to prophets, believers, Muslims, or the Prophet and those with him. These are different scopes of answer: the shorter labels can identify examples within the fuller company, while the Qur’anic passage connects the company to obedience. [Q 4:66–69] [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] [Report: al-Rabīʿ b. Anas] [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via Ibn Jurayj] [Report: Wakīʿ] [Report: ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Zayd]

The transmitted explanations then identify “those You are displeased with” as Jews and “those who are astray” as Christians. In the short answers attributed to the Prophet through ʿAdī b. Ḥātim, the wording is direct: “The Jews” and “the Christians.” Another cluster recalls a question at Wādī al-Qurā, where a man asks who is being approached and receives the same answer. Reports from Mujāhid, Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Suddī, al-Rabīʿ, and Ibn Zayd repeat these identifications. They preserve both a brief formula and a narrative setting, but converge on the same pairing. The Qur’anic warning in Q 5:77 also describes people who “went astray before, led many astray, and strayed from the even path,” connecting the group label to a path and conduct. [Hadith: ʿAdī b. Ḥātim; Musnad Aḥmad 4:378–379; al-Tirmidhī 4:67] [Hadith: the Wādī al-Qurā exchange reported by ʿAbd Allāh b. Shaqīq] [Report: Mujāhid] [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] [Report: al-Suddī via Abū Mālik, Abū Ṣāliḥ, and routes from Ibn Masʿūd and other Companions] [Report: al-Rabīʿ b. Anas] [Report: ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Zayd] [Q 5:77]

Ibn ʿAbbās also gives a longer prayer that brings both warnings together: “Keep us from the way of the Christians whom You have led astray; do not be angry with us as You were angry with the Jews, and do not lead us astray as You led the Christians.” Another transmitted explanation says both groups can be described as astray and as under anger; each is named by a characteristic, not as though the other fault were impossible. The labels therefore function here as collective descriptions of paths and dispositions, not as a claim that every individual can be reduced to one word. [Report: Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ḍaḥḥāk] [Q 5:60] [Q 5:77]

The Arabic grammar adds a question about how the two refusals attach to the path. In the genitive, *ghayri* can qualify “those You have favored,” or it can repeat an implied word “path”: “the path of those You favored—the path other than that of those under anger.” An accusative parse is also discussed as an exception, but the following *wa-lā*, “and not,” makes a separate negative clause a more natural reading. A further grammatical dispute asks whether *lā* before “those astray” is a real negation or a completing particle. Poetry and Q 7:12 are brought to bear on how Arabic can use such a construction. These questions affect the parsing, but the recited wording still sets the favored path against both warnings. [Poem: al-ʿAjjāj] [Q 7:12]

There are also different explanations of divine anger: one describes its consequence as punishment in this life or the next, another describes censure of people and their actions, and a third affirms the description while distinguishing divine anger from the agitation or harm experienced by people. The Qur’anic passages cited in that discussion associate anger with punishment, while the phrase itself remains a warning to avoid a path that incurs displeasure. Questions of agency are not resolved by grammar alone: Arabic can attribute a ship’s sailing to the ship even when wind moves it, and another verse explicitly speaks of God leading someone astray. These examples allow the action and its cause to be discussed without making the two attributions mutually exclusive. [Q 43:55] [Q 10:22] [Q 45:23]

The surah closes by specifying the request rather than repeating it. Praise and mercy name the one addressed; judgment explains His authority; worship and reliance turn praise into direct speech; guidance asks for a course; and this verse describes that course through a favored company and the paths to avoid. The report from Abū Hurayrah about the prayer’s dialogue follows the same movement: God receives praise and glorification, then the servant is promised what is asked. The final verse therefore returns to the central plea—guide us—now clarified by examples and warnings. Compressing the surah to a statement of sovereignty and service would lose the distinct moves from praise to address, from address to guidance, and from a general path to recognizable models and cautions. [Hadith: Abū Hurayrah; al-Muwaṭṭaʾ 84–85; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1:166]
