=61=

Having contrasted the fleeting nature of this world with the lasting reward that is with Allah, the surah now poses a rhetorical question that demands an honest answer from every listener. The verse sets up two categories of people and asks whether they can ever be equal.

The first group consists of those to whom Allah has made “a fine promise,” a promise described as beautiful, excellent, and completely trustworthy because it comes from the Most Truthful. This promise encompasses Paradise, forgiveness, divine pleasure, and eternal peace.

The verse adds that they “will see it fulfilled,” meaning their certainty about the promise is not based on wishful thinking but on the firm assurance that Allah never breaks His word. Their hearts are at rest because they know that death is not an end but a doorway to the realisation of that promise.

The second group consists of those whom Allah has allowed to enjoy the pleasures of this worldly life. Notice the verb “allowed” indicates that their enjoyment is not a sign of Allah’s approval but a temporary permission within the divine plan. They indulge in food, drink, wealth, status, and sensual pleasures, thinking they have achieved success. Then the verse reveals their true end: on the Day of Judgment, they will be brought forward, not as honoured guests, but as the accused who are summoned for punishment. The contrast is staggering. One group is approaching an eternal, perfect reward; the other is being herded towards a terrible punishment. The rhetorical question, “Can they be like…?” expects a resounding no. A moment’s honest reflection reveals that the two outcomes are incommensurable. The verse is crafted to jolt the heedless out of their complacency. It is not enough to simply know this distinction intellectually; one must live as though the promise is real, prioritising actions that lead to the fine promise over those that merely increase the fleeting enjoyment.

The verse also serves as a comfort to believers who see the disbelievers enjoying prosperity while they themselves struggle. The outward inequality of this life will be completely reversed in the next.

=62=

The surah now pulls back the curtain on a specific scene from the Day of Judgment, prefacing it with the word “Watch for,” a call to the Prophet and all believers to anticipate this moment with the certainty of its occurrence. On that Day, Allah Himself will address the polytheists with a question that exposes the emptiness of their lifelong beliefs.

The question, “Where are those you claimed were My associate-gods?” is not a request for information, for Allah knows all things. It is a question of rebuke, confrontation, and exposure. During their earthly lives, these people had insisted that various beings—idols, angels, prophets, saints, or natural forces—were partners with Allah, sharing in His divinity, worthy of worship, or capable of interceding on their behalf without His permission. They had invested their devotion, fear, and hope in these associates. Now, in the courtroom of the Almighty, they are asked to produce them.

The question highlights the utter absence of those so-called partners. Where are they? They are not present to defend their worshippers. They are not standing alongside to share in the judgment. They have vanished, because they were never real in the first place. The idols have crumbled, the false gods have disappeared, and even those righteous beings like Jesus or the angels, who were wrongly deified, will disown their worshippers. This verse resonates with the Quranic theme that on that Day, every false object of worship will desert its follower, and the bonds of shirk will dissolve into bitter regret. The polytheists of Mecca, who associated idols like Lat, Uzza, and Manat with Allah, and who also worshipped angels and jinn, are the immediate audience.

Yet the verse extends to all forms of shirk, whether the worship of human tyrants, ideologies, wealth, or the ego. Everything that a person sets up as a rival to Allah in their love, obedience, or fear will be summoned, only to be found utterly incapable of providing any assistance. The call itself is terrifying, for it signals the beginning of a reckoning from which there is no escape.

The question leaves the polytheist speechless, with no valid answer to offer, setting the stage for the misleaders to attempt a desperate disassociation that the next verse describes.

=63=

This verse reveals the reaction of the misleaders, the leaders of disbelief and corruption, when they hear the divine question and see the punishment approaching. The decree of torment has already been justified against them because of their own persistent disbelief and their role in leading others astray. Faced with the prospect of being blamed by their followers, they desperately try to shift responsibility. They address Allah as “Our Lord,” an appeal that comes too late, and they point to the followers, saying, “These are the ones we caused to deviate. ” The admission is striking: they confess to having misled others.

However, they immediately attempt to mitigate this by claiming that they did not force anyone; they simply led them into deviance because “we ourselves were deviant.” Their reasoning is that since they themselves were already astray, their influence was merely an expression of their own condition, and the followers chose to follow. They then make a dramatic declaration of dissociation: “We disassociate ourselves from them before You. ” They try to sever all ties, hoping that the blame and punishment will fall solely on the followers and not on them.

This is a scene of utter selfishness and betrayal. The relationships that were built on shared rebellion against Allah collapse instantly under the weight of the judgment.

The final statement, “It was not us that they used to worship,” is a theological defence. They argue that the followers did not technically worship them as God; they merely obeyed them, followed their orders, and imitated their lifestyle. True worship, they imply, was directed to idols or desires, not to them personally, so they should not be held accountable as false gods. The Quran elsewhere confirms that the followers will retort, blaming the leaders for their misguidance, and Allah will declare that both parties share the torment. This verse paints a picture of the complete breakdown of evil alliances on the Day of Judgment. It serves as a severe warning to those who influence others towards sin—whether through media, politics, scholarship, or social pressure—that their followers will not be a shield but a source of accusation, and their attempts to dissociate will fail. The only safe path is to sever ties with deviance before death and to lead others to truth.

=64=

After the misleaders have attempted to disassociate themselves, the disbelievers as a whole are addressed with a command that is both a mockery of their past beliefs and a practical demonstration of their helplessness. The divine voice, or the angels at Allah’s command, tells them to call upon the associate-gods they used to invoke in the world.

This is a taunt, similar to the way they were told to enjoy their life, but now it carries the weight of irreversible consequence. The disbelievers, in their desperation, will actually call out to those false gods, hoping against hope that somehow they might respond and save them from the horrifying punishment that now stands before their eyes. But the call will be met with absolute silence. “They will receive no response.” The idols cannot hear, the false gods cannot act, the angels and prophets they falsely worshipped will not intercede without Allah’s leave, and any jinn they served will be powerless. The silence is deafening. It is the collapse of a lifetime’s worth of false hopes.

The verse then describes their state: “they will face the punishment,” seeing it directly, experiencing its initial waves, and knowing that there is no averting it. In that moment, they will be filled with an overwhelming regret, “wishing they had been rightly guided.” This wish is the essence of the remorse of the losers. They will wish they had listened to the messengers, used their intellect, and followed the guidance that was made so clear to them. But on that Day, wishes are worthless; the time for choice has ended, and only the reality of recompense remains. The verse serves as a chilling warning to the living. Every time a person calls upon something other than Allah for ultimate help, they are rehearsing a call that will be utterly ignored on the Day they need help most. The only call that will never go unanswered is the sincere call to Allah alone.

The verse also emphasises that guidance is a priceless treasure whose value is fully realised only when it is too late. The believer, therefore, clings to guidance now, grateful for having been spared the regret that will consume those who turned away.

=65=

The surah presents another scene from the Day of Judgment, again introduced with the attention-grabbing “Watch for.” This time, the divine question directed at the disbelievers concerns their response to the messengers who were sent to them. Throughout human history, Allah sent prophets to every nation, calling them to worship Him alone and to live righteously. The messengers delivered the message clearly, endured hardships, and demonstrated exemplary character. Now, on the Day of Reckoning, the recipients of those messages are asked to give an account.

The question, “What response did you give to the messengers?” is comprehensive and personal. It is not merely asking whether they intellectually accepted the message; it encompasses their entire reaction—their words, their actions, their attitudes of belief or rejection, their obedience or defiance. Did they believe and follow? Did they mock and persecute? Did they ignore and turn away?

The question implies that the messengers fulfilled their duty by conveying the truth, so the focus is now entirely on the response of the people. This verse highlights the central role of the messengers in the divine plan. Rejection of the messenger is rejection of the One who sent him. The Meccans who rejected Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, are directly in view, but the principle applies to all people in relation to the prophets sent to them. The Quran elsewhere states that on that Day, the messengers will be brought as witnesses, and the disbelievers will be unable to deny that the message reached them.

The question serves to establish the just basis for the judgment that follows. No one will be punished without having had a clear warning.

The verse also prompts believers to reflect on their own response. When we read the Quran, which is the message of the final messenger, what is our answer? Are we responding with sincere faith, obedience, and gratitude, or with selective acceptance, procrastination, and neglect?

The question will be asked of every human being who lived after the advent of the Prophet. Preparing a good answer now, by aligning one’s life with the guidance, is the only way to face that questioning without terror.

=66=

This verse depicts the psychological state of the disbelievers when confronted with the question about their response to the messengers. The Arabic word describing them conveys a sense of being struck dumb, having all news and information blotted out from their minds. It is a state of extreme confusion, shock, and mental paralysis. In worldly life, when people are faced with a difficult question, they might look to one another for help, consult their companions, or fabricate a collective story. But on the Day of Judgment, the sheer terror and the weight of the divine presence will render all such strategies useless. The verse specifies that they will not even be able to “ask one another” for answers. Communication and collaboration, which were so easy in the world, will be impossible. The impact of the scene will isolate every individual. Each soul will be consumed by its own predicament, too overwhelmed to think about coordinating a defence with others.

This description also implicitly contrasts with the believers, who will be greeted with peace and whose faces will be bright with joy as they receive their records. The dumbstruck state of the disbelievers is the result of a life spent ignoring the truth. Because they did not use their faculties of speech, hearing, and reason to respond correctly to the messengers in the world, those faculties will fail them when they need them most. Some commentators explain that the news will be “blotted out” in the sense that all the false arguments, excuses, and justifications they used to rely upon will suddenly vanish. They will see the truth with stark clarity and realise that none of their old rationalisations hold any weight. In that moment of blinding truth, they will have nothing to say. This verse underscores the importance of using the time in this world to prepare a clear and truthful answer. The believer is one who constantly engages with the message, asking himself the question now: “What response am I giving to the messenger?” By doing so in the safety of this life, he secures the ability to answer on that Day, while those who postponed their response will find themselves in a silence from which there is no rescue.

=67=

After the chilling descriptions of the disbelievers’ state, the surah offers a verse of hope and a clear path to salvation. It describes three sequential qualities that mark the successful.

The first is repentance, which involves turning away from sin and from all forms of associating partners with Allah, returning to the Lord with a sincere heart. Repentance is the gateway; without it, the soul remains stained and distant.

The second is belief, which is the firm, internal conviction in Allah, His messengers, His books, His angels, the Last Day, and the divine decree. This belief is not a passive mental assent but a living faith that reshapes one’s worldview and priorities.

The third is doing good, which translates that faith into righteous actions—prayer, charity, honesty, kindness, and all forms of obedience to Allah and service to His creation. These three are interconnected: true repentance leads to faith, and genuine faith produces good deeds.

The verse then states that for such people, “it is right to hope that they will be among the successful.” The phrasing is beautiful and balanced. The hope mentioned is not an uncertain wish but an expectation grounded in Allah’s promise. Allah has pledged that those who meet these conditions will succeed, so their hope is legitimate and well-founded. At the same time, the verse guards against complacency. The believer should never become arrogantly certain of his salvation, forgetting that it is ultimately Allah’s mercy that admits one into Paradise.

Thus, he combines hope with reverent fear, doing good while constantly seeking Allah’s forgiveness. The word “successful” refers to those who achieve the ultimate success: being saved from the Fire and admitted into Paradise, where they will attain all that their hearts desire and more. This verse is a beacon for the sinner who feels the weight of the descriptions of punishment. No matter how far one has strayed, the door of repentance, coupled with belief and righteous action, remains open. It answers the question of verse 65: the correct response to the messengers is precisely to repent, believe, and do good. The verse thus provides the cure for the regret depicted in the previous scenes.

=68=

The surah now turns to the absolute sovereignty of Allah, addressing a core error of the polytheists. They had presumed to dictate the terms of prophethood, claiming that the messenger should have been an angel, or that a different person from a more prestigious tribe should have been chosen. This verse cuts down all such objections. It declares that the Lord, meaning the nurturing, sustaining Master, creates whatever He wills from nothing, and chooses from His creation whatever He wills for specific roles and honours. The act of choice—whether in selecting a prophet, assigning a message, determining the form of worship, or distributing worldly provisions—belongs exclusively to Allah.

The phrase “the choice is not theirs” is emphatic. Human beings, no matter their status or intelligence, have no authority to second-guess Allah’s choices or to demand that divine decisions conform to their desires. The One who fashioned the universe and knows every soul’s true capacity is uniquely qualified to choose whom He entrusts with His message.

The verse then glorifies Allah, declaring Him far above the partners they associate with Him. Their false gods have no power to create or to choose; they are themselves created and chosen for nothing. The act of associating partners with Allah in any aspect of choice or authority is therefore an insult to His exclusive sovereignty. This verse applies not only to the specific issue of prophethood but to all matters of divine decree. When a person objects to the circumstances of their birth, their appearance, their family, or the tests they face, they are, in effect, questioning the choices of their Lord.

The verse reminds the believer to submit to divine wisdom in all things. Allah creates what He wills—including the believer’s own life situation—and He chooses what is best, even if the human mind cannot fully grasp it. The acceptance of this truth brings deep peace, because one trusts that the choices of the All-Wise are always superior to the choices of the creature. It also protects against arrogance, for any talent, knowledge, or status a person possesses is not of their own choosing but a gift from the One who chooses.

=69=

This short but powerful verse continues the theme of Allah’s absolute knowledge, which underpins His right to choose and His perfect justice. It states that the Lord knows two categories of human inner reality. First, “what their hearts conceal,” referring to the secret thoughts, hidden intentions, suppressed emotions, unspoken doubts, and private schemes that no other human being can access. A person may smile and speak politely while harbouring jealousy, hatred, or a plot. Another may perform religious acts outwardly while inwardly harbouring hypocrisy. The deepest recesses of the heart, with all its complexities and contradictions, are an open book to Allah. Second, “what they reveal” refers to the words they speak, the actions they perform, and the expressions they display. These are known to other humans, but Allah’s knowledge of them is not dependent on observation; He knows them with the same immediate, perfect knowledge with which He knows the hidden. The verse follows the discussion of Allah’s sole right to choose and is particularly relevant to the rejection of the Prophet by the Quraysh. They publicly claimed that their rejection was based on a rational demand for a different kind of messenger, but Allah knew what their hearts concealed: the real reasons of tribal pride, fear of losing status, and stubborn adherence to ancestral ways. The verse is both a comfort to the Prophet and a warning to the rejecters. The Prophet is assured that the Lord is fully aware of the sincerity in his heart and the malice in the hearts of his opponents, and He will deal with both justly. The rejecters are warned that their hidden motives, no matter how cleverly disguised, are fully known and will be judged.

For the believer, this verse cultivates a constant awareness of Allah’s scrutiny, which is the essence of ihsan—to worship Allah as if you see Him. Knowing that Allah knows what the heart conceals encourages sincerity, because the value of an act is determined by the intention behind it, and no secret good deed is ever lost. It also deters secret sins, because the privacy of the act does not hide it from the Knower of the unseen.

=70=

This majestic verse is a compact creedal statement, gathering fundamental attributes of Allah that form the core of Islamic monotheism. It begins with the declaration “He is Allah,” the personal name of the one true God, encompassing all perfect attributes. Then follows the fundamental negation and affirmation: “There is no god worthy of worship except Him.

” This is the testimony of faith, denying divinity to all else and affirming it for Allah alone. The verse then moves to praise: “All praise belongs to Him in this life and the next.” Praise for every beauty, every blessing, every act of wisdom, and every answered prayer ultimately returns to Allah. In this world, the believers praise Him, and even the natural world glorifies Him.

In the Hereafter, the inhabitants of Paradise will praise Him eternally, and the judgment itself will reveal His perfect justice, eliciting praise from all creation. Next, “All authority is His.

” The Arabic word for authority here encompasses absolute sovereignty, dominion, and the power to command. No ruler, government, or force has any independent authority; all authority is borrowed and will be reclaimed.

This statement dethrones every tyrant and false god in the heart of the believer. Finally, “And to Him you will all be returned.

” This is the ultimate destination. Every human being, from the first to the last, will be resurrected and brought back to their Lord for judgment. This final clause ties the entire verse together: because Allah is the only true God, because He alone deserves all praise, and because He holds absolute authority, the return to Him is both inevitable and just. The verse serves as a powerful summary of the message that Moses, Muhammad, and all prophets conveyed. It refutes the pharaonic claims of self-deification, the polytheistic worship of idols, and the materialistic denial of the Hereafter.

For the believer, reciting or reflecting on this verse renews the core of faith, re-centres the heart on Allah, and puts the fleeting concerns of the world into the perspective of the eternal return. It is a verse of immense theological density, delivered with a rhythm and clarity that make it suitable for deep meditation and frequent repetition.

=71=

The surah now engages the polytheists with a series of rational arguments based on the most ordinary yet profound blessings: the alternation of night and day. The Prophet is instructed to present a hypothetical scenario. He is to ask them to imagine that Allah were to make the night permanent, an unending darkness stretching all the way to the Day of Judgment. The sun would never rise again. The world would be plunged into continuous blackness. What would be the consequence? Life as we know it would become impossible. Plants would die without sunlight, temperatures would drop catastrophically, navigation and daily activities would halt, and human psychology would crumble under the weight of perpetual gloom.

The question then follows: “which god other than Allah could bring you sunlight?” The word “sunlight” here refers to the light and illumination of day, the very thing that would end the endless night. The polytheists, who believed in numerous deities, are challenged to name even one of those gods who could restore the sun and overcome the perpetual night decreed by Allah.

The answer is obvious: none of them could. The idols are powerless, the angels cannot alter cosmic laws, and no human or jinn can command the sun. Only the One who created the sun and ordained the cycle of night and day can do that.

The verse ends with “Will you not then listen?” This is a call to pay attention to the argument and to let the obvious truth penetrate their hearts. The ear that hears this reasoning should lead the mind to the necessary conclusion: the God who controls the cosmic order is the only one worthy of worship. This verse uses the familiar experience of night and day to awaken gratitude and monotheism. The believer, upon reading it, is reminded that the simple sunrise each morning is not an accident but a mercy from Allah that no other power could replicate. It turns an everyday occurrence into a daily sign, renewing faith with every dawn.

=72=

This verse is the complementary counterpart to the previous one, completing the argument about the alternation of night and day. Now the Prophet is told to ask the disbelievers to imagine the opposite scenario: what if Allah were to make the day permanent, with the sun never setting, blazing continuously until the Day of Judgment? At first, one might think perpetual day is more desirable than perpetual night, but reflection reveals it would be equally catastrophic. The unrelenting heat and light would scorch the earth, destroy crops, make sleep nearly impossible, and drive both humans and animals to exhaustion. The night is not just the absence of light; it is a covering, a coolness, a time of rest and renewal that is essential for life.

The question then comes: “which god other than Allah could bring you night to rest in?” The polytheists are challenged to produce any of their idols or intercessors who could create the night, with its darkness that facilitates sleep, its calmness that soothes the nerves, and its coolness that restores the body. The verse specifically mentions the function of the night as a time “to rest in,” highlighting the mercy embedded in this cosmic design. If the day were perpetual, rest would be nearly impossible, and life would collapse from sheer physical exhaustion. The concluding question, “Will you not then see?” shifts from the previous verse’s call to “listen” to a call to “see.

” This is appropriate because the argument deals with light and vision. They are asked to see with their eyes the obvious benefit of the night, and then to see with their minds the necessary conclusion that the Lord who provides this night is the only one deserving of worship. Together, these two verses form a powerful, self-contained proof of monotheism drawn from the daily experience of every human being. The alternation of night and day is a universal sign that no one can miss, yet the polytheists fail to extract its meaning. The verses train the believer’s eye to see the fingerprints of divine mercy and power in the ordinary rhythms of life, transforming the sunrise and sunset into moments of spiritual reflection.

=73=

After posing the two hypothetical scenarios, the verse now directly explains the wisdom and mercy behind the creation of night and day. It begins by anchoring this cosmic arrangement in the attribute of mercy. The day and night are not arbitrary cycles or mechanical results of planetary motion devoid of purpose; they are deliberate gifts from a Merciful Lord who designed them precisely to suit human needs.

The verse then spells out the two primary functions. During the night, the function is rest. The darkness, the quiet, and the natural drop in human activity provide the conditions for sleep, which is a miraculous state of physical repair, mental reset, and spiritual detachment from the world’s noise. Sleep is described in the Quran as a form of death, a daily reminder of our dependence and the coming of the greater sleep of death, from which Allah wakes us each morning as a mini-resurrection. During the day, the function is to “seek His bounty. ” The daylight enables movement, work, trade, agriculture, study, and all the activities through which a person earns a livelihood and seeks the provisions that Allah has spread throughout the earth.

The phrase “His bounty” reminds the believer that the income and success achieved during the day are not solely the fruits of personal effort but are ultimately provisions from Allah, who facilitates the work and blesses it. The verse concludes with the purpose of this entire arrangement: “and perhaps you will be grateful.” The alternation of night and day is meant to produce gratitude. When a person lies down to rest after a long day, the comfort of the bed and the release of sleep should stir gratitude. When the sun rises and a new day of opportunities dawns, the energy and ability to strive should stir gratitude. Gratitude is the heart’s recognition of the Giver, leading to worship, obedience, and a life lived in appreciation. The “perhaps” indicates the goal that Allah intends for humanity, though many fail to reach it. This verse encapsulates the Islamic perspective on time and daily life: the night is for rest and worship in the quiet hours, and the day is for constructive effort, and both are arenas for expressing gratitude to the One who alternates them in perfect measure.

=74=

The surah returns to the scenes of the Day of Judgment, repeating the question first introduced in verse 62. The repetition is not redundant; it serves to reinforce the gravity of this divine interrogation and to frame the new elements that will follow in the next verse. The call “Watch for” again alerts the Prophet and the believers to be ever-conscious of this inevitable encounter.

The question will be asked a second time, or perhaps it is the same question being highlighted from a different angle, because the context has now included the rational arguments about Allah’s exclusive power over creation and the signs in night and day. After establishing that only Allah can bring sunlight and night, the question “Where are those you claimed were My associate-gods?” becomes even more piercing. The disbelievers have been shown that their false gods have no share in the most basic cosmic operations. If those gods cannot create light or bring darkness, what can they possibly do on the Day of Judgment?

The repetition of the question in the surah is also a stylistic feature of the Quran, intended to engrave the scene in the memory and to remind the reader that this confrontation is a central pillar of the prophetic warning. The polytheists lived their lives as if these associates were real and reliable, but on that Day, the reality will be laid bare. This verse serves as a moment of pause and reflection before the next verse, which adds the element of the witness from every community.

The question will be asked, and the demanded proof will be impossible to produce. The verse also functions as a spiritual check for the believer. By regularly reading and hearing this question, the believer examines his own heart: is there any hidden attachment, any reliance, any love or fear that rivals the devotion owed to Allah alone?

The question purifies the faith, stripping away layers of subtle shirk that may cling to the soul without conscious awareness. The believer joins the Prophet in “watching for” that Day, living with the consciousness that a day will come when all false objects of attachment will vanish, and only the relationship with Allah will remain.

=75=

This verse adds a new and devastating dimension to the judgment scene. Not only will the polytheists be asked about their associates, but a witness will be brought forth from every nation or faith-community. These witnesses are the prophets who were sent to each community. Each prophet will testify about his people, confirming that he delivered the message clearly and that their response was one of belief or rejection. The presence of these witnesses establishes the complete fairness of the judgment. No community can claim that the message was unclear or that the messenger was negligent. After the witnesses have given their testimony, the polytheists will be directly challenged: “Show Us your proof.” They had spent their lives arguing, debating, and justifying their idolatry with various rationalisations and inherited arguments. Now they are asked to produce the evidence that their shirk had any basis in truth. This demand is, of course, impossible to meet. There is no proof that idols are divine, no evidence that angels are daughters of God, no rational basis for worshipping anything besides the Creator. The challenge exposes the utter intellectual bankruptcy of polytheism.

The verse then states the outcome: “Then they will come to know that the truth is with Allah alone.” The word “know” indicates a knowledge that is experiential and undeniable. In the world, they had the opportunity to know this through faith and reflection, but they rejected it. Now, in the Hereafter, this knowledge is forced upon them by the undeniable reality of the situation. The truth—that there is no deity but Allah, that He alone deserves worship, and that His promise of resurrection and judgment was absolutely real—is now manifest with crystal clarity.

The final clause, “And whatever gods they fabricated will fail them,” seals their fate. All the beings they invoked, the idols they carved, the concepts they deified, will vanish from their sight and be unable to offer even the slightest help. This verse is a powerful conclusion to the argument against shirk, showing that what people rely on besides Allah will inevitably fail when it matters most.

=76=

The surah now introduces a new historical example that illustrates the themes of arrogance, wealth, and divine punishment, complementing the story of Pharaoh. Korah, known as Qarun in Arabic, was an Israelite, a member of the same community as Moses and Aaron.

This fact makes his story particularly striking: he was not an outsider or a member of Pharaoh’s regime; he was from the chosen people who had been saved from bondage. Yet he “behaved arrogantly towards them,” meaning he acted with superiority, oppression, and contempt towards his own people, forgetting his origins and the favours Allah had bestowed upon their community.

The verse then describes the staggering extent of his wealth. Allah had granted him treasures so immense that the very keys to his treasure chambers would burden a group of strong men. Some commentators explain that the keys were numerous, perhaps carried by dozens of men, indicating an extraordinary accumulation of riches.

This detail emphasises that his wealth was a gift from Allah, not a product of his independent genius. The test of wealth is even more subtle and dangerous than the test of poverty. In response to his arrogance, some righteous members of his community offered him sincere advice. They told him, “Do not be prideful!

” This is a direct command to abandon the attitude that was destroying him. Pride here means exulting in one’s wealth, looking down on others, and attributing success solely to oneself. The advisors backed their advice with a divine principle: “Surely Allah does not like the prideful.” The dislike of Allah is a severe warning, for if Allah dislikes a person, who will love and protect him? The advice was given with compassion and concern, reflecting the duty of believers to counsel one another. Korah’s wealth had become a veil that blinded him to his own mortality and his dependence on Allah. The story that follows serves as a timeless warning to every wealthy or talented person who might be tempted to credit only themselves and to treat others with disdain.

=77=

The advice of the righteous people continues, and it is one of the most balanced and comprehensive statements of the Islamic economic and social ethic. They told Korah not to abandon his wealth, but to redirect its purpose. “Seek the reward of the Hereafter by means of what Allah has granted you.” This means he should use his vast resources as a tool for earning Paradise, through charity, supporting the needy, funding righteous causes, and using his influence for justice. Wealth, in this view, is not evil in itself; it is a means that can be invested for eternal profit.

The next phrase, “without forgetting your share of this world,” adds a crucial balance. Islam does not demand a complete rejection of worldly pleasures or a monastic withdrawal. A person is permitted to enjoy the lawful comforts of life, to eat well, dress well, and provide for their family, as long as it is done in a permissible manner and does not lead to heedlessness or extravagance.

This is the “share” of the world, which is a gift to be used, not an idol to be worshipped. The third piece of advice is “Be good to others as Allah has been good to you.

” This is the principle of reflecting divine goodness. Just as Allah had blessed Korah with incredible wealth, Korah should in turn be a channel of goodness to Allah’s creation—through generosity, kindness, and support.

The final piece is a warning: “Do not seek to spread corruption in the land.” Wealth and power can easily be misused to spread corruption—through bribery, exploitation, oppression, and fostering moral decay. Such behaviour attracts Allah’s severe displeasure, as “Allah certainly does not like the corruptors.” This verse is a complete ethical charter. It teaches the believer to invest for the Hereafter without ignoring worldly responsibilities, to use blessings for the benefit of others, and to avoid the corruption that often accompanies great wealth. The advisors did not tell Korah to give up his riches but to transform his relationship with them from a source of pride into a means of divine nearness.

=78=

Korah’s arrogant response is recorded here. He dismissed the sincere advice of his people and attributed his immense wealth not to Allah’s favour and test, but to his own merit. “I have been granted all this because of some knowledge I have,” he claimed. The exact nature of the knowledge he referred to is debated. Some commentators say he meant his business acumen, his expertise in trade, or his intelligence in acquiring riches. Others say he claimed a special religious knowledge, perhaps of the Torah, which he thought made him deserving of wealth as a sign of divine approval. In either case, his error was fundamental: he saw the blessing as a direct consequence of his own qualities, thereby denying that it was a gift and a test from Allah.

This is the classic trap of wealth—the successful person begins to believe that their success is self-generated, forgetting the countless factors beyond their control, from their natural talents and health to the market conditions and the divine will that ultimately grants or withholds. The Quran refutes Korah’s claim with a devastating historical question: “Did he not know that Allah had already destroyed some from the generations before him who were far superior to him in power and greater in accumulating wealth?” If wealth and power were proof of Allah’s pleasure or of one’s own independent ability, then those earlier tyrants and wealthy nations, who were much mightier than Korah, would not have been annihilated. Their destruction is evidence that material success is not a shield against divine punishment, and that it can vanish in an instant.

The verse concludes with a puzzling statement: “There will be no need for the wicked to be asked about their sins. ” This means that their sins are so obvious and numerous that they will not be interrogated in a detailed manner for the record; they will be seized and punished based on what is already known. Alternatively, it means they will not be asked in a way that seeks an excuse, because no excuse will be accepted. It underscores the severity of Korah’s arrogance and the certainty of his doom.

The story continues with a vivid scene that depicts the psychological effect of Korah’s display of wealth on the masses. Korah, after receiving the advice and rejecting it, decided to make a public appearance. He went out before his people “in all his glamour.

” The Arabic word used for glamour refers to full adornment, splendour, and the trappings of luxury. He was accompanied by his servants, riding magnificent animals, wearing the finest clothes, and surrounded by the visible signs of his immense riches. It was a deliberate show of power and status, designed to impress and to assert his superiority over those who had advised him to be humble. The reaction of a segment of the people illustrates the seductive power of worldly success. Those who “desired the life of this world,” meaning those whose hearts were attached to material gain and whose values were shaped by worldly measures of success, looked upon Korah with envy and admiration. They expressed a wish: “If only we could have something like what Korah has been given.” They saw his wealth as the ultimate good, a fortune that guaranteed happiness and fulfilment. Their statement, “He is truly a man of great fortune,” was an acknowledgment of what they perceived as his immense luck and share of worldly blessings. They did not see the spiritual danger he was in. They did not consider that his wealth was a test that he was failing. They evaluated his situation purely through the lens of immediate, material enjoyment. This scene is timeless. In every age, there are figures like Korah who flaunt their wealth and become objects of public admiration. The media glorifies them, the young aspire to be like them, and the worldly-hearted wish for a similar fortune. The verse captures the tension between the value system of faith and the value system of materialism. The believers, as we will see in the next verse, have a completely different assessment. The scene serves as a warning to believers to examine their own reactions to displays of wealth. Does the heart lean towards admiration and envy, or does it recognise the hidden peril and remember that the real fortune is the fortune of faith and righteousness?

=80=

This verse presents the contrasting reaction of the spiritually mature, those who had been gifted with true knowledge. This knowledge is not merely academic information; it is the deep understanding of the purpose of life, the reality of the Hereafter, and the relative worth of worldly possessions compared to eternal bliss. When these knowledgeable people heard the envious wishes of the worldly-minded crowd, they responded with a sharp rebuke: “Shame on you!

” This is an expression of strong disapproval, aimed at waking the people from their delusion. They were essentially saying: How can you desire what Korah has, knowing that his wealth is a trial that is leading him to destruction, and that what is with Allah is incomparably greater? Their statement, “Allah’s reward is far better for those who believe and do good,” redirects the focus from the temporary to the eternal. They were not denying that Korah’s wealth was impressive in worldly terms, but they were asserting a hierarchy of value. The reward that Allah has prepared in the Hereafter for the believers who combine faith with righteous deeds is infinitely superior in quality, duration, and the peace it brings. A single moment in Paradise outweighs all the treasures of the earth combined. The knowledgeable ones then added a crucial qualifier: “But none will attain this except the steadfast.” This means that the wisdom to see through the glitter of this world and to prioritise Allah’s reward requires a quality of patience and self-restraint. The steadfast are those who hold firm to their faith despite the temptations of immediate gratification. They are patient in obeying Allah, patient in avoiding sin, and patient with the trials of life, including the trial of seeing the wicked prosper.

This verse teaches the believer to cultivate a knowledge-based, faith-grounded perspective that immunises against the envy of the worldly successful. It is a call to develop steadfastness as the essential virtue that enables one to delay gratification for the sake of a far greater, lasting reward. The contrast between the two reactions to Korah’s glamour is a perpetual test for every community, and the side one takes reveals the true orientation of one’s heart.
