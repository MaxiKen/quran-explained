=1=

The surah opens with three separate Arabic letters: Ṭâ, Sĩn, and Mĩm. These are among the disjointed letters that appear at the beginning of twenty-nine chapters of the Quran. Their precise meaning is known only to Allah, and the early generations of Muslims approached them with a combination of faithful acceptance and reverent silence. The companion Abu Bakr reportedly said that every book has a secret, and the secret of the Quran is found in these opening letters. Scholars have offered a range of interpretive insights without claiming absolute certainty. One widely held view is that these letters serve as a direct challenge to the eloquent Arabs of the time. The Quran is composed of the very same letters they used in their daily speech and poetry, yet they cannot produce anything comparable to its divinely inspired message. The fact that a simple Ṭâ-Sĩn-Mĩm precedes the proclamation that “these are the verses of the clear Book” immediately draws the listener’s attention. It is as if the speaker places three building blocks before you and then declares that from such ordinary pieces a miraculous structure has been built. This shows that the source of the Quran is not a human being manipulating language, but the Lord of all creation who uses humble letters to convey eternal guidance.

Another dimension is that these letters often occur before a mention of the Book or revelation, as we see in the very next verse, thereby preparing the heart to receive what follows with seriousness and humility. By beginning with Ṭâ-Sĩn-Mĩm, the surah reminds us that not everything has to be fully grasped by the intellect for it to be true and beneficial. The believer hears these letters, recognises that they come from the All-Wise, and feels a sense of awe. For a person coming to the Quran with a sincere heart, even the disjointed letters become a doorway to contemplation, signalling that what follows is not ordinary speech but a communication from the unseen realm that demands careful, repeated reflection. This opening thus establishes the spiritual atmosphere required for absorbing the detailed stories of Moses, Pharaoh, and other events that will unfold in the surah.

=2=

After the three letters, Allah immediately draws attention to the nature of what is being recited. The expression “the clear Book” uses the Arabic word mubîn, which carries the sense of something that is manifest, clarifying, and leaving no ambiguity in its guidance. The verse identifies the Quranic revelations as verses—signs that point towards a greater truth.

The word “verses” itself hints that every passage, every instruction, and every story carries a signpost directing the heart to its Creator. Unlike obscure writings that confuse the reader, this Book makes the path of righteousness distinct from the paths of error. When the Meccan polytheists heard these early revelations, they could not honestly claim that the message was unclear; they recognised its eloquence and moral force, yet their pride and worldly interests prevented them from submitting. The clarity spoken of here is not merely linguistic but spiritual and intellectual. The Quran explains the nature of faith, the purpose of life, the reality of the afterlife, and the stories of previous nations in a way that appeals to sound reason and uncorrupted human nature. It does not rely on blind acceptance but invites reflection and observation. By pairing this statement with the story of Moses that follows, the surah demonstrates how clarity operates through historical narrative. The events of Pharaoh’s tyranny, the rescue of Moses, and the ultimate destruction of oppression are not recounted as myth but as fact that illuminates eternal principles. For the believers in Mecca who were facing persecution, this verse reassured them that the revelation they held onto was not vague speculation but solid truth from their Lord, capable of guiding them through hardship just as it had guided earlier communities. Today, the same clarity remains available, but the heart must approach the Book without the veils of arrogance or heedlessness to truly see the light it offers. The description “clear” also implies that the Quran does not hide behind riddles that mislead the seeker; rather, it openly proclaims the oneness of Allah, the necessity of accountability, and the beauty of righteous conduct, all of which are central to the story about to be narrated.

=3=

Here Allah tells the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, that the account about to be relayed is not a product of human research or legend, but a narration directly from the All-Knowing. The phrase “We narrate to you” emphasises that this is revelation being communicated to a man who was unlettered and had no access to the scriptures of the Jews or Christians. The Quraysh knew that the Prophet had not studied under any scholar, so the detailed knowledge of Moses’ life constituted a powerful sign of the Quran’s divine origin. The expression “part of the story” signals that the Quran does not aim to give a complete chronological biography but selects the episodes most relevant to the spiritual and moral lessons being taught.

In this surah, the focus is on Moses’ vulnerability as an infant, his flight from Egypt after the unintentional killing, his time in Midian, and his eventual prophethood, with particular attention to Pharaoh’s arrogance. The words “in truth” assure that the narration is free from the distortions that had crept into earlier accounts. It presents the actual events as they occurred, with their correct theological meaning.

Finally, “for people who believe” defines the primary audience that truly benefits. While anyone can hear the story, only those with faith or a readiness to believe will allow it to penetrate their hearts, strengthen their conviction, and transform their behaviour. This sets the stage for a surah that repeatedly calls its listeners to reflect, believe, and remain steadfast. For the early Muslims in Mecca, hearing that Moses too faced a mighty tyrant who slaughtered infants and yet was saved by Allah’s plan served as a direct source of consolation. The narrative was not just history; it was a mirror in which they saw their own struggle, and a promise that the oppressed who hold onto faith will ultimately be given victory and succession in the land, a theme explicitly mentioned in the coming verses.

=4=

The story begins with a portrait of tyranny. Pharaoh did not merely rule; he elevated himself to the point of claiming divinity, as will be mentioned later in the surah.

The Arabic wording conveys that he exalted himself with haughtiness, crossing every limit of justice and decency. His method of control was to divide the population into factions, setting one group against another to prevent unity and maintain his grip. Within this system, the Children of Israel were singled out for the harshest treatment. They were systematically weakened: their newborn sons were slaughtered, while their women were kept alive for servitude and humiliation. This was not a random outburst of cruelty but a calculated policy. Pharaoh feared the birth of a male child among the Israelites who, according to some interpretations of ancient knowledge, would end his kingdom. So he resorted to mass infanticide to preempt that divine decree, not realising that his very attempt to avert fate would become the means by which the promised child was saved and raised in his own palace.

The verse then seals this description by declaring Pharaoh “truly one of the corruptors.” Corruption here is not limited to personal vice; it describes a person who spreads systemic injustice, ecological and social ruin, and moral decay on a grand scale.

The surah presents Pharaoh as an archetype of the arrogant ruler who thinks his power places him beyond accountability. For the believers in Mecca who were suffering at the hands of the Quraysh chiefs, this depiction exposed the nature of all oppressors across history. The same arrogance, the same division of people into ranks, and the same targeting of the weak mark the conduct of those who rebel against divine guidance. The verse thus serves both as historical record and as a warning that such behaviour inevitably invites destruction from which no army or fortress can protect.

=5=

Directly contrasting Pharaoh’s corruption, Allah announces His own will. The phrase “it was Our Will” expresses a settled divine decree that no tyrant can overturn. The oppressed ones, the very people Pharaoh sought to annihilate, are the object of this favour. Favour here means not only rescue from suffering but a comprehensive blessing that includes spiritual leadership and political authority. The verse says they would be made “models of faith,” which in Arabic uses a word that can mean leaders, guides, and exemplary figures whom others follow. The Children of Israel, once enslaved and broken, were destined to become a nation to whom prophets would be sent and through whom the light of monotheism would shine for centuries. They were also to become “successors,” meaning they would inherit the land and the power that Pharaoh had abused.

This promise fulfilled itself when Moses led his people out of Egypt and later when they were established in the Holy Land. On a deeper level, this verse establishes a principle that runs through human history: when a community holds onto faith and patience under oppression, Allah’s help arrives and often transforms the downtrodden into the very inheritors of the civilisation their oppressors built. The early Muslims in Mecca took immense hope from these words. They were a small, persecuted minority, yet the Quran was telling them that the same Lord who destroyed Pharaoh and elevated the enslaved Israelites would grant them succession if they remained sincere. The promise, however, is not a blank cheque for any group that suffers. The oppressed are favoured when their suffering results from their adherence to truth, and they must embody the faith that qualifies them to be models.

The verse also contains a subtle correction: the favour is entirely from Allah, not the result of any strength the oppressed possessed on their own. This guards against arrogance when success does come. The lesson is timeless, offering perspective to every believer facing systemic injustice that the final outcome belongs to the righteous who rely on their Lord.

=6=

This verse continues the statement of divine will. “To establish them in the land” means granting the oppressed not just fleeting victory but a secure, rooted presence and the ability to govern and shape society. It is a promise of full empowerment.

The second part introduces a profound irony. Pharaoh and his inner circle, including his chief minister Hamân, lived in constant dread of the prophesied downfall at the hands of a child from the Israelites. Their entire policy of slaughtering male infants was designed to prevent that feared outcome.

Yet Allah’s plan ensured that the very thing they dreaded would come to pass through the child they unknowingly saved and raised, Moses, and through the people they brutalised. The phrase “what they feared” encompasses the loss of power, the collapse of their oppressive system, and their own destruction. The statement also highlights that Allah’s punishment is not arbitrary; it is perfectly suited to the crime. Because Pharaoh’s fear drove him to commit atrocities, his punishment would be seeing his greatest fear materialise before his eyes as the sea closed over him. Hamân is mentioned by name alongside Pharaoh, and in later verses, he will reappear building a tower of clay. This shows that the henchmen of tyranny are not forgotten; they share both the guilt and the doom of the regime they prop up. For the Prophet, peace be upon him, and his companions, this was a direct message that the mockery and oppression they faced from the leaders of Quraysh, including their own arrogant figures, would end in a similarly decisive fashion if they remained on the path of guidance. The verse thus completes the frame of the story: tyranny appears powerful for a moment, but its fear is actually a premonition of its own divinely ordained end. When believers read this verse, they are reminded to never envy the apparent stability of unjust systems, for the Lord is quietly advancing His plan to uproot them through the very people they despise.

=7=

From the grand narrative of tyranny and divine will, the surah zooms into the intimate story of a mother and her newborn. The verse uses the word “inspired,” which in this context refers to a form of intuitive, divinely placed guidance rather than the formal prophetic revelation given to messengers. Allah communicated to the heart of Moses’ mother what she needed to do in a situation of extreme danger. The instruction is remarkably detailed: she is to continue nursing her son as long as she can do so safely, but the moment she genuinely fears that Pharaoh’s agents will discover and kill him, she must place him into the river.

This is a command that goes against every maternal instinct. A mother’s natural impulse is to hold her child closer when danger approaches, not to cast him into the water.

Yet she is told not to fear or grieve. The reassurance is backed by a clear, twofold promise: the child will be returned to her, and he will be made one of the messengers. The combination of these two promises is what made the otherwise impossible act feasible. The mother’s heart was steadied by the knowledge that the separation was temporary and that her son was destined for a purpose far greater than she could imagine. This verse illustrates that when Allah takes something away from a believer, it is only to return it in a better state or to open a path that could never have been opened otherwise. The river, which seemed like a grave, would become the very means of Moses’ rescue and his entry into Pharaoh’s palace. The mother’s obedience under such pressure shows the highest level of trust in divine wisdom. Her story is a timeless lesson for parents who fear for their children’s future and for all believers who are asked to take steps that seem counterintuitive. When Allah inspires a direction in the heart and confirms it with His promise, the only appropriate response is trust-filled action, leaving the outcome in His hands without allowing fear or sorrow to paralyse the soul.

=8=

The divine plan now unfolds through what appears to be a tragic accident from the tyrant’s perspective. The household of Pharaoh, perhaps his servants or his wife’s attendants, found the basket carrying the infant Moses and brought it into the palace. The phrasing “it so happened that” indicates that while the events seemed like ordinary occurrences to human eyes, they were orchestrated by Allah’s precise decree. The family of the tyrant who had ordered the killing of Israelite boys unknowingly took into their very home the child destined to destroy their kingdom.

The phrase “only to become their enemy and source of grief” is a devastating summary of the outcome. Every kindness they showed the child, every resource they spent on his upbringing, and every moment they felt affection for him was, in the divine scheme, contributing to their eventual ruin.

The verse then connects this to their moral state: they were “sinful.” Their sinfulness was not a single act but a pattern of transgression that included massacring innocents and claiming divinity for Pharaoh. Because of their persistent wrongdoing, Allah made their own actions the instrument of their downfall. There is a frightening principle here: when people are entrenched in sin, the very blessings they enjoy can become a trap. The child they rejoiced over would grow up to challenge their beliefs, expose their falsehood, and lead the oppressed out of bondage before the final military defeat.

The mention of Hamân again emphasises that those who enforce tyranny share in its curse. The early Meccan Muslims, witnessing the pride of their opponents, drew strength from this. It reassured them that the power structures that seemed unshakeable were actually nurturing the seeds of their own collapse, and that the believers’ apparent weakness was a phase within a larger divine plan that would end with truth prevailing and falsehood vanishing.

=9=

This verse introduces a figure of remarkable grace in the midst of the oppressive palace: Pharaoh’s wife. Some scholars identify her as Âsiyah, who is later mentioned in the Quran as an example of perfect faith alongside Mary. When the basket was brought before Pharaoh, his immediate instinct, shaped by his policy of killing Israelite males, was likely to order the child’s execution.

Yet his wife intervened with words that Allah placed on her tongue to protect the infant. She described the baby as “a source of joy,” using a term that suggests deep comfort and delight for the heart. Notice that she did not make a religious or moral argument about the wrongness of killing children; she appealed to Pharaoh’s emotions and self-interest. She presented the possibility that the child might benefit them in the future, perhaps through service or by becoming an heir since the couple had no son. The alternative she offered was adoption, a loving, permanent bond that would bring them happiness. Her speech moved Pharaoh, and the child was spared.

The final phrase, “they were unaware,” refers to Pharaoh and his household’s complete ignorance of the divine reality they were dealing with. They thought they were saving a cute orphan who might become a loyal servant; they had no idea he would grow up to shatter their system of falsehood and drown their army. This highlights the vast gap between human calculation and divine knowledge. A tyrant thinks he is exercising his power freely, but Allah uses his own household to shelter the very person who will be his undoing.

For believers, the verse demonstrates that Allah can place allies for the truth in the most unlikely settings. It also gives a profound lesson about not judging situations by their surface appearance. What appeared to be an innocent adoption was, in the divine plan, a crucial step toward liberation and prophetic mission. The faith of Pharaoh’s wife herself stands as an eternal reminder that a person’s environment does not have to determine their destiny; she believed in Moses’ Lord and disbelieved in her husband’s tyranny, earning her a place among the most honoured women in paradise.

=10=

After placing her infant in the river, Moses’ mother experienced an agony that only a mother can understand. The verse describes her heart as being empty or utterly drained of everything except the burning thought of her lost child. The pain was so intense that she came dangerously close to exposing the secret. She might have cried out in grief, asked suspicious questions about the missing baby, or given some indication that the child in the palace was hers.

The Arabic expression conveys that she would have revealed the matter had Allah not strengthened her heart. The phrase “reassured her heart” means that Allah bound up her breaking heart with firmness, patience, and a deep-seated tranquility that only He can bestow. The purpose is explicitly stated: “in order for her to have faith in Allah’s promise.” This shows that faith is not merely intellectual assent; it is a stabilising force that calms the emotions, controls the tongue, and guides behaviour in moments of extreme crisis. The promise she was asked to believe in was the earlier revelation that her son would be returned and become a messenger. The test was whether she would cling to that promise when every maternal fibre screamed that her child was lost to the palace of a murderous tyrant. Her near-slip is not recorded as a criticism but as a window into the intensity of the trial and the sufficiency of divine support. This verse has consoled millions of believers facing loss and anxiety. It acknowledges the rawness of human emotion while simultaneously directing the sufferer to the anchor of divine reassurance. A heart may ache until it feels it will burst, but the Lord who knows its weakness can pour into it a peace that defies circumstances. The mother’s experience also teaches that believers are not expected to be emotionless blocks; they feel deeply, but they are sustained by a faith that prevents them from saying or doing what would jeopardise the divine plan unfolding through their trial.

=11=

With her own heart now steadied, Moses’ mother acts wisely. She instructs her daughter, Moses’ older sister, to follow the path of the basket and see where it goes and what becomes of the child. The instruction “Keep track of him” shows that the mother, having placed her trust in Allah, did not passively sit back but used whatever lawful means were available to stay informed. The sister’s role was to be an unseen observer, gathering intelligence without drawing attention to herself.

The phrase “she watched him from a distance” paints a picture of a young girl walking along the bank, her eyes fixed on a basket floating among the reeds until it was picked up by the palace servants. She watched as her baby brother was carried inside the most guarded residence in Egypt, and she remained there, observing developments without the guards suspecting anything. The last part, “while they were unaware,” highlights once again that the entire sequence of events was hidden from the eyes of Pharaoh’s people. They had no idea that a young Israelite girl was tracking their movements and that this girl would soon play a pivotal role in reuniting the child with his mother. This verse underscores the importance of subtle, intelligent planning in the life of a believer. Reliance on Allah does not mean abandoning practical measures. The sister’s watchfulness, carried out with discretion, became the human means through which the divine promise would begin its fulfilment. It also shows how Allah uses apparently weak members of society—a young girl from an enslaved people—to outmanoeuvre the security apparatus of a superpower. For anyone who feels that their small, quiet efforts go unnoticed or cannot possibly affect a great outcome, this verse offers encouragement. When those efforts are aligned with the commands and promises of Allah, they become threads in a tapestry of deliverance that no tyrant can unravel. The sister’s discreet surveillance is a model of faithful agency blended with prudent caution.

=12=

Once the infant Moses was inside the palace, the natural need for a wet-nurse arose. The household sought women who could breastfeed the child, but the baby refused to accept any of them. The verse makes clear that this was not a coincidence; “We had caused him to refuse” indicates a direct divine intervention. Allah created an instinctive aversion in the infant so that he would not latch onto any of the palace wet-nurses. This seemingly small detail was the pivot on which the entire reunion would turn. The distressed palace staff were now looking for anyone who could solve their problem. At that precise moment, Moses’ sister, who had been watching from a distance, stepped forward with a suggestion. She had no official status, no authority, and was from the very people being oppressed, yet her words were crafted with perfect wisdom. She did not give away her relationship to the child but simply asked, in a helpful and innocent manner, whether she should direct them to a family who could nurse the baby and care for him. The phrasing “bring him up for you and take good care of him” appealed to their desire for a reliable, nurturing arrangement. The palace officials, desperate and unaware of the trap they were walking into, agreed. The sister’s conduct here is a masterclass in wise speech and timely action. She had been patient, observing until the right opening appeared, and then she presented a solution that served their perceived needs while perfectly fulfilling her own family’s hope. This verse demonstrates how Allah arranges even the physiological reflexes of an infant to achieve His purpose. It also illustrates the power of a well-chosen word spoken at the right moment. Believers are taught not to force matters prematurely but to wait for the opportunities that Allah creates, and then to act with intelligence and discretion, couching the truth in a manner that the listener finds acceptable without compromising their integrity. The sister became the unseen link that restored the broken family.

=13=

The verse draws together the threads of the entire episode. The phrase “This is how” points back to the remarkable chain of events: the inspiration to place the child in the river, the palace picking him up, the intervention of Pharaoh’s wife, the sister’s tracking, the divine blockage of all other wet-nurses, and the sister’s timely suggestion. All these seemingly disconnected occurrences were orchestrated so that the baby would be returned to the arms of his mother in the most unexpected way—not as a fugitive in hiding, but as a royal child being nursed openly in the palace with Pharaoh’s own permission and salary. This outcome achieved two purposes for the mother. First, her heart was put at ease and her grief removed; she could hold her son, nurse him, and watch him grow without fear. Second, she would know with experiential certainty that Allah’s promise is always true. She had been promised both the child’s return and his prophethood, and the first part of that promise was now literally in her arms. The concluding remark, “but most people do not know,” is a profound reflection on the human condition. Most people do not grasp how Allah’s promises operate, how His plan unfolds through apparent setbacks, or how His mercy penetrates the darkest circumstances. They see the river, the tyrant, and the basket and think only of loss and tragedy. They do not see the hidden hand that transforms the river into a safe passage, the tyrant’s palace into a nursery, and the bereaved mother into a paid royal employee nursing the very child she thought she had lost forever.

For the believer, this verse is a call to deepen trust. When Allah makes a promise, it will be fulfilled, but the route it takes may pass through valleys that seem to contradict the promised destination. Wisdom lies in holding onto the promise rather than being swayed by the terrain. The early Muslim community, separated from their homes and facing persecution, clung to verses like this, knowing that the same Lord who returned Moses to his mother would restore honour and security to the believers who remained patient.

=14=

The narrative now jumps forward many years, leaving the period of Moses’ childhood and youth in the palace unmentioned here, as the Quran focuses on the events most relevant to its message. The verse marks the transition of Moses into adulthood by noting he attained his “full strength and maturity.” This refers to the prime of his physical and mental powers. At this stage, Allah blessed him with two things: wisdom and knowledge. Wisdom is the soundness of judgement that enables a person to speak and act appropriately, to discern right from wrong beyond mere information, and to apply knowledge in a way that benefits oneself and others. Knowledge includes the understanding of divine truths, the traditions of his people, and the realities of the world that would later be necessary for his prophetic mission. Though Moses had been raised in a pagan environment, surrounded by Pharaoh’s claim to divinity and the idolatrous culture of Egypt, his heart remained unpolluted because of Allah’s protection and the early nurturing he received from his own mother. The verse states clearly that this bestowal of wisdom and knowledge was a reward. “This is how We reward the good-doers.

” The term “good-doers” refers to those who excel in doing good, whose goodness is not merely occasional but describes their character and conduct. This includes Moses’ mother, who demonstrated profound excellence in her trust and obedience, Moses’ sister, who acted with intelligence and discretion, and Moses himself, who grew up displaying righteousness even before his formal prophethood. It establishes a principle: when a person strives to do good with sincerity, Allah grants them an increase in wisdom, understanding, and the ability to navigate life’s complexities. The reward is not delayed until the hereafter alone; it begins in this world as spiritual insight and practical competence.

For every believer, this verse serves as encouragement that the most valuable capital in life is not wealth or status but the wisdom and knowledge that Allah places in the heart of the sincere.

=15=

This verse describes a pivotal incident that changed the course of Moses’ life. The phrase “unnoticed by its people” suggests he entered during a time of rest, such as midday when the streets were empty, allowing him to move through the city without being recognised as the prince of the palace. His heart, however, was not disconnected from the suffering of his people. He came across a violent altercation: an Israelite, one of his own oppressed community, and an Egyptian, who was among the oppressors. When the Israelite saw Moses, he called out for help, and Moses’ sense of justice was immediately stirred. He intended to intervene to stop the aggression, but the situation escalated and he struck the Egyptian with a punch that unexpectedly caused his death. The Quranic language is careful to indicate that Moses did not plan to kill the man; the blow was meant as a defensive or corrective measure but had a fatal outcome. As soon as he realised what had happened, Moses’ prophetic nature became evident. He did not try to justify himself or cover up the act, but immediately turned to Allah, declaring that what had occurred was from Satan’s handiwork. He understood that the excessive force, the unintended fatal result, and the entire chain of provocation were part of Satan’s effort to ensnare him. His statement, “He is certainly a sworn, misleading enemy,” is both an insight and a warning. Satan’s ultimate goal is not just to make a person commit a single sin, but to use that sin to lead them into despair, further transgression, or a path that takes them away from Allah’s mercy. Moses, by immediately naming the source and turning to Allah, shut that door.

This verse teaches believers that even the best of people can make serious mistakes under pressure. What distinguishes the righteous is not sinlessness but the immediate recognition of the error, the attribution of the deviation to the enemy’s promptings, and the swift return to the Lord in repentance. It also shows that a person who has been granted strength must be extremely careful in its use, for a moment of anger can lead to irreversible consequences.

=16=

Following his immediate recognition of the satanic hand in the mistake, Moses turned directly to his Lord in a deeply personal plea. His words are a model of honest repentance: he acknowledged without any excuse that he had wronged his own soul. The admission “I have definitely wronged my soul” contains profound spiritual insight. Sin is ultimately an act of self-harm. It may involve harming others, as the killing of the Egyptian obviously did, but at its core, every sin injures the sinner’s own relationship with Allah, darkens the heart, and invites negative consequences in this life and the next. Moses did not say that the Egyptian had forced him, or that the circumstances were to blame. He took full personal responsibility while simultaneously turning to the only source of forgiveness. His request was simple: “so forgive me. ” He did not negotiate terms or list his good deeds as a counterbalance; he threw himself entirely on the divine mercy.

The verse then records the immediate response: “So He forgave him.” The speed and certainty of this forgiveness is emphasised by the subsequent description of Allah as “the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” These two names appear together frequently in the Quran and indicate that Allah’s forgiveness is both vast in scope and motivated by a mercy that seeks the restoration of the servant. The constant repetition of this divine attribute assures every sinner that no wrong is too great to be forgiven if the repentance is sincere. For the believers in Mecca, and for all subsequent generations, this short exchange between Moses and his Lord became a powerful source of hope. The man who would become one of the greatest messengers committed a grave error in his pre-prophethood days, yet his immediate repentance brought him complete forgiveness. This demolishes the despair that Satan wants to plant in the heart after a fall. The correct response to sin is not to run further from Allah out of shame, but to run towards Him with the words of Moses: “My Lord, I have wronged my soul, so forgive me. ” The verse also subtly prepares the way for the next stage of Moses’ story, where even though he is forgiven by Allah, the worldly consequences of his action will force him to leave Egypt, showing that divine forgiveness and earthly repercussions can coexist under Allah’s wise decree.

=17=

Moses’ experience of being forgiven after such a grave mistake produced in him not complacency but a strengthened resolve. He makes a solemn pledge to Allah, couched in deep gratitude. He begins by acknowledging the favours his Lord has showered upon him: saving him from slaughter as an infant, returning him to his mother, raising him in the palace with all its privileges, granting him wisdom and knowledge, and now forgiving him for a fatal error. The expression “for all Your favours upon me” shows that Moses connected the dots of his entire life story and saw it all as a continuous stream of divine grace. This comprehensive awareness of past blessings became the fuel for his future commitment. The content of his pledge is significant: he would never be a supporter or an ally to the criminals.

The term “wicked” or “criminals” here points to those who persist in wrongdoing, injustice, and rebellion against Allah’s commands. Moses had just witnessed where passion and impulsive intervention could lead. He realised that being drawn into the conflicts of the wrongdoers, even with good initial intentions, could entangle a person in a web of unintended consequences. His vow was therefore to completely dissociate himself from the camp of evildoers and to never again place himself in a situation where he would be acting as their aid or associate. This pledge reflects the proper response to divine forgiveness: not a casual return to carelessness, but a firm decision to change one’s course and align oneself unambiguously with the side of righteousness.

The verse teaches believers that when Allah forgives them, they should use the relief and gratitude they feel as motivation to make clear boundaries between themselves and the paths that led to the sin. Moses’ words are a declaration of loyalty: because You have been so good to me, my life will be dedicated to Your cause, and I will not allow myself to drift into the company or the ways of those who defy You. It is a resolution that every believer can adopt upon receiving any blessing or forgiveness.

=18=

The consequences of the previous day’s event now surface. The killing had not gone unnoticed; the news had spread, and the Egyptian authorities were investigating. Moses spent the following day on high alert, moving through the city with a sense of fear and vigilance.

The phrase “watching out” suggests he was scanning his surroundings, anticipating possible danger. In the midst of this anxiety, he encountered the same Israelite man he had helped the day before. This man was again embroiled in a fight, and upon seeing Moses, he cried out for assistance once more.

However, this time Moses’ reaction was different. He did not rush to intervene as he had before. Instead, he rebuked the man sharply, calling him “clearly a trouble-maker.

” The Arabic term indicates someone who is openly and habitually straying from the right path. Moses had gained a painful lesson: the man he had defended was not an innocent victim in every situation. His constant involvement in quarrels and fights suggested a pattern of provocation and conflict. The verse subtly highlights that not everyone who shares one’s ethnic or religious identity is necessarily in the right. The Israelite may have been oppressed in general, but in this specific instance, his own misconduct was the cause of the commotion. Moses’ rebuke shows the clarity of vision that wisdom grants. He could distinguish between the legitimate struggle of an oppressed community and the reckless behaviour of an individual who uses that communal identity as a cover for his own troublemaking.

This is a crucial lesson for any movement or community. Allegiance must ultimately be to truth and justice, not to group identity divorced from moral conduct.

The verse also captures the emotional state of Moses, a mixture of justified caution and frustration. His fear was not merely for his own life, but perhaps also a fear of being drawn into further chaos that would derail the larger purpose Allah had for him. It marks a turning point where Moses realises that his presence in Egypt has become untenable, not only because of the regime’s enmity but also because the environment itself is pulling him into conflicts that threaten his mission.

The scene intensifies quickly. Despite his rebuke of the Israelite troublemaker, Moses saw that a fight was still underway and was about to physically intervene against the Egyptian opponent to stop the aggression.

The phrase “about to lay his hands on their foe” indicates he was ready to use force again, though this time with the clear intention of restraining, not killing. Before he could act, the Egyptian man, recognising Moses and having apparently heard about the previous killing, cried out in alarm. His words were calculated to both save himself and to expose Moses publicly: “Do you intend to kill me as you killed a man yesterday?

” This is the moment the secret killing became public knowledge. The Egyptian’s next accusation was even sharper: “You only want to be a tyrant in the land. You do not intend to make peace! ” The irony is heavy. The man calls Moses a tyrant, using the same kind of language that truly described Pharaoh. He frames Moses’ intervention not as an act of justice but as a power grab, painting him as a violent bully who does not seek reform but dominance. This accusation, coming from the mouth of a member of the actual oppressor class, was a complete distortion of Moses’ character and intentions.

Yet it was effective. It forced Moses to realise that his actions were now being interpreted through a lens that made him look indistinguishable from the tyrants he despised. The verse illustrates how a single error, even one that has been forgiven by Allah, can be used by adversaries to smear a person’s entire reputation and undermine their moral standing. Moses could not effectively argue his case at that moment, and his continued presence in Egypt might lead to further provocations that would confirm the false narrative. The Egyptian’s words, though malicious, served as a signal that the stage of discreet protection was over and that a new chapter of open confrontation or flight had to begin. The accusation “you do not intend to make peace” also reveals a deep truth about the perception of reform: those who challenge entrenched systems of corruption will often be labelled as the very source of disorder, even when their ultimate goal is to establish genuine peace based on justice.

At the critical moment when the situation in the city had become extremely dangerous, Allah’s help arrived through an unexpected source. A man came hurrying from the remote outskirts of the city. The description “rushing from the farthest end” indicates that this person had to travel a long distance quickly, which suggests both the urgency of the situation and the fact that news of the conspiracy had spread to those not normally in the circles of power. This man was not an Israelite; according to some commentators, he was a member of Pharaoh’s own family or court who had kept his faith hidden. The Quran here does not name him, but later in Surah Ghafir, a similar believing man from Pharaoh’s family is mentioned as defending Moses. Whether the same or different, what matters is that he brought a precise and verified piece of intelligence: the chiefs of the state had held a council and decided to execute Moses.

The phrase “conspiring against you” shows that the decision was not a public legal proceeding but a secret plot. The advice given was straightforward and unequivocal: “leave the city. ” He did not suggest negotiation, hiding, or mounting a defence; flight was the only wise option. The man then added, “I really advise you to do so,” emphasising his sincerity and concern. He took a great personal risk to bring this warning, for if his actions had been discovered, he would have been executed as a traitor. His courage demonstrates that even in the most oppressive regimes, Allah raises individuals of conscience who will assist the righteous in their time of need. For Moses, this warning was the clear signal that his time in Egypt had come to an end and that the next phase of his life, leading to Midian and eventually to prophethood, was about to begin.

The verse teaches believers that accepting sincere advice, especially when it calls for a difficult change of plans, is a mark of wisdom. Moses did not argue or delay; he recognised the hand of Allah in this timely warning.

The verse also shows that fleeing from danger when staying serves no religious or moral purpose is not cowardice but prudence, and it was precisely this departure that set him on the path to Mount Sinai where he would receive his commission.
