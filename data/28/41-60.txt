=41=

The destruction of Pharaoh and his soldiers was not the end of their evil influence. This verse reveals a terrifying dimension of their legacy. After drowning them, Allah declares that He made them “leaders inviting to the Fire.

” The word for leaders here is the same used for imams, guides whom others follow. Pharaoh and his inner circle, through their words, actions, and the system they built, became permanent models of misguidance. Their lifestyle, their philosophy of self-deification, and their oppression of the weak continue to inspire tyrants and corrupters throughout history. Every ruler who follows the pharaonic pattern of arrogance and injustice is, in effect, a student of Pharaoh’s school, and his invitation leads his followers straight to Hell.

This is a profound warning: leadership is a massive responsibility. A leader who guides to righteousness earns a continuous reward, but a leader who guides to evil earns a continuous burden of sin for every soul he misleads, without diminishing their own guilt.

The verse then states that on the Day of Judgment, these leaders “will not be helped.” In this world, they enjoyed armies, bodyguards, and a network of supporters who rushed to their aid. On that Day, none of that will exist. There will be no ally, no intercessor, no lawyer to plead their case. They will stand utterly alone before the Judge.

The phrase also implies that they will not be granted any assistance to avoid or lighten the punishment. This contrasts starkly with the earlier promise that Moses and his followers would prevail with Allah’s help. The help that matters is the help of the Day of Judgment, and the oppressors will find none of it. The verse thus completes the arc: Pharaoh’s power, which seemed so formidable, was erased from the earth, and his spiritual legacy became a cursed one that leads to the Fire. Believers are reminded to scrutinise the leaders they follow and the examples they admire. Do those leaders invite to the Fire or to Paradise?

The answer determines one’s eternal company.

=42=

This verse adds another layer to the punishment of Pharaoh and his people. A curse was made to follow them in this world. The curse means being expelled from Allah’s mercy, being spoken of with condemnation, and having one’s name associated with disgrace. In the immediate historical context, the curse manifested in the way subsequent generations remembered Pharaoh: not as a glorious monarch but as the epitome of tyranny, whose drowned body was preserved as a sign for later nations. The Quran itself perpetuates this curse by recording his evil deeds and his terrible end, ensuring that believers across the ages will recite his story and invoke Allah’s displeasure upon him. This worldly curse is a foretaste of the greater disgrace that awaits in the Hereafter.

The verse then moves to the Day of Judgment and describes their state with the word “outcasts.” The Arabic term carries the sense of being disfigured, expelled, and held in abhorrence. On that Day, they will be driven away from any good, refused entry to mercy, and made repulsive in the sight of all creation.

This is the opposite of the honour and nearness to Allah that the righteous will experience. The pairing of a curse in this life and the status of outcasts in the next demonstrates the comprehensive nature of divine justice. The arrogant wanted to be elevated; they are brought low. They wanted to be revered; they become objects of contempt. For the believers facing persecution in Mecca, this verse was a powerful consolation. The Quraysh leaders who mocked the Prophet and tortured the weak Muslims might have appeared honoured in society, but if they continued on their path, a curse would attach to their names, and their final abode would be among the outcasts.

The verse also serves as a broader principle: those who wage war against Allah’s messengers and oppress the believers are never truly successful, even if their biographies in history books are written by sycophants. The divine record, which is the only record that ultimately matters, brands them with a curse that no human praise can wash away.

=43=

After narrating the destruction of Pharaoh, the surah now transitions to the blessing of revelation. Allah gave Moses the Scripture, meaning the Torah, which contained detailed laws, moral guidance, and the clear affirmation of monotheism. The timing is significant: it was given “after destroying earlier nations.” This refers to the people of Noah, ‘Ad, Thamad, and the Egyptians themselves. The destruction of those previous civilisations served as both a historical backdrop and a warning. The Torah came to a people who had witnessed or knew of these events, so the Scripture was not a theoretical treatise but a practical guide for a nation that had just been delivered from bondage and was being formed into a community under divine law. The Torah is described with three qualities. First, it was “an insight for the people. ” The word “insight” refers to deep spiritual perception, a light that enables one to see the truth of things clearly. The Torah illuminated the nature of Allah, the purpose of life, and the path to righteousness. Second, it was “a guide,” meaning it provided specific directions for conduct, worship, and social organisation. Guidance implies both showing the way and leading along it. Third, it was “a mercy.” The Torah was not a burden; it was a compassionate gift that, if followed, would bring peace, justice, and divine favour upon the Israelites.

The phrase “so perhaps they would be mindful” states the ultimate purpose: the Torah was meant to produce a constant God-consciousness, a state of heart where the remembrance of Allah shapes every action. The verse connects to the Quran, which similarly came as insight, guidance, and mercy to a humanity that had witnessed the rise and fall of nations and was in dire need of a reminder. The continuity between the Torah and the Quran is a central theme in this surah, as later verses will address the responses of Jews and Christians to the new revelation. The destruction of earlier nations is not merely historical trivia; it is a recurring lesson that the Quran uses to urge mindfulness before the opportunity for repentance passes.

=44=

The surah now shifts to a direct address to the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, to establish the divine origin of the detailed knowledge he is conveying. The verse begins by negating any human means by which he could have acquired the story. He was not physically present on the western side of Mount Sinai when Allah gave Moses the tablets containing the Commandments. The western side of the mountain is specified with a geographical detail that would be known only to an eyewitness or through revelation, as the exact location where the Torah was delivered is not something the Arabs of Mecca had any knowledge of.

The phrase “nor were you present in his time” broadens the negation to include the entire era of Moses. The Prophet could not have learned these narratives from direct observation, from attending a school of scripture, or from sitting with the rabbis of the Jews. He was an unlettered man born centuries later in a land far removed from the Israelite tradition. This verse is part of a powerful argument for the authenticity of the Quran. The detailed, accurate, and theologically corrected accounts of Moses’ life that fill this surah could only have come from the One who was there, the One who inspired Moses’ mother, who called from the bush, and who gave the Scripture. The Meccans knew that Muhammad, peace be upon him, had never travelled to study with scholars or read the previous scriptures.

Therefore, the information he was providing could not be explained by natural means. This argument also serves a spiritual purpose: it builds the confidence of the believers that their Prophet is truly receiving revelation, and it challenges the disbelievers to account for the source of this knowledge.

The mention of the western side of the mountain adds a vivid, concrete detail that underscores the realism of the revelation. This is not myth; it is history that occurred at a specific geographical location, known to the Lord who created that mountain. The verse thus prepares the listener for the following verses, which continue to emphasise that the Prophet’s role is to convey a mercy he received from his Lord, not a story he crafted.

=45=

The argument for the divine source of the Prophet’s knowledge continues. After Moses, many generations rose and passed away, and over the centuries the original teachings became obscured, forgotten, or corrupted.

The phrase “the ages took their toll on them” indicates that the passage of time eroded the memory of precise events, mixed truth with legend, and allowed distortions to creep into the religious traditions. This means that by the time of Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, the accurate and complete story of Moses was not readily available in its pure form. Anyone trying to piece together these events from the oral traditions or the altered scriptures of the time would inevitably get details wrong.

Yet the Quran presents the story with complete internal consistency, profound moral clarity, and theological precision. This could not be the work of a human compiler.

The verse then specifically addresses the Midian phase of Moses’ life. The Prophet was not living among the people of Midian, reciting or studying revelations about them. Midian was a remote area, and the events of Moses’ sojourn there, his encounter with the two women, his marriage, and his service as a shepherd, were not matters of common knowledge in Mecca. There was no school in Midian where the Prophet could have enrolled; the very idea is almost humorous in its impossibility. The conclusion is stated emphatically: “But it is We Who have sent this revelation to you. ” The “We” refers to Allah, and the revelation refers to the Quran as a whole, including this surah. The entire narrative is not a product of research, conversation with travellers, or reading of old scrolls. It is a direct, divine disclosure. This verse reinforces the concept of the Quran as a miraculous sign in itself. Its knowledge of the unseen past, presented by an unlettered prophet, is evidence of its heavenly origin.

For the believer, this deepens trust that every word of the Quran is true and that the Prophet is faithfully delivering what he received, without any human interpolation. It also counters any orientalist or sceptical claim that the Quran borrowed its stories from the Bible or Jewish folklore. The Quran claims its source is the One who authored history.

=46=

The negation of the Prophet’s presence at the key revelatory events reaches its climax with this verse. He was not at the side of Mount Ṭûr when Allah called out to Moses. This refers to the seminal moment of divine speech, the giving of the Torah, and the covenant with the Children of Israel. Mount Ṭûr, or Sinai, is one of the most sacred locations in prophetic history, and the Prophet Muhammad had no physical connection to it. The purpose of repeating these negations is to drive home the point beyond any doubt: the source of the Quran is revelation, not human acquisition.

However, the verse then pivots to a magnificent statement of the Prophet’s own mission. The “but” marks a transition from what he was not to what he is. He has been sent “as a mercy from your Lord. ” The entire prophetic mission, including the warnings, the laws, the stories, and the guidance, is an expression of divine mercy. The Prophet himself is described as a mercy to the worlds in another surah, and here his message is characterised as a mercy. The purpose of this mercy is “to warn a people to whom no warner has come before you.” The Arabs of the Prophet’s time were descendants of Ishmael, and although there had been prophets in the distant past, such as Ishmael himself, no messenger had been sent specifically to them for a long interval. They were in a period of spiritual darkness, a gap in the prophetic chain, with no living memory of a warner. The Quran came to fill that void, to revive the awareness of Allah, and to call them back to the pure monotheism of their ancestor Abraham.

The final clause, “so perhaps they may be mindful,” echoes the purpose of earlier revelations. The mercy is not forced; it invites a response of mindfulness, a conscious turning to the truth. This verse is deeply comforting for the Prophet himself. Amidst the rejection and hardship he faced in Mecca, he is reminded that he is not just a warner but a mercy from the Lord of the worlds, and his people are blessed to receive a guidance that had been absent for centuries. It also implies that if they reject this mercy, their ingratitude is all the more blameworthy.

=47=

The verse explains another reason for sending the Prophet and the revelation: to block a foreseeable excuse on the Day of Judgment. Human beings have a tendency to deflect blame and make excuses for their disobedience. Allah, in His perfect justice, removes any legitimate ground for such excuses. The scenario described is that if a punishment were to descend upon a people for their sins without a prior messenger having been sent, they would cry out in protest, claiming that they had not been given a fair chance. They would say, in effect, “We were not warned. We had no guide. If only a messenger had come with clear revelations, we would have believed and been among the righteous. ” This verse shows that Allah’s sending of messengers is an act of establishing a clear argument against humanity. Once a messenger has come with the truth, no one can plead ignorance or claim they were left without guidance. The early Meccans were now in precisely that position: the Prophet was among them, reciting Allah’s revelations, and they had no excuse for their idolatry and immorality. The principle extends to all people. The Quran serves as the final, preserved message, and access to its teachings, whether through direct reading or through the call of those who convey it, establishes the proof.

This verse also answers a common theological question: what about those who never heard of a prophet? The Quran establishes that Allah does not punish a people until He has sent a messenger to them. Those who genuinely had no access to the message are judged by a different standard of divine mercy and justice, known fully only to Allah. But for those to whom the message comes clearly, the excuse is invalid. The verse deepens the sense of urgency in receiving the Quran. It is not just a collection of wisdom; it is the document that will be used as the criterion on the Day of Judgment. Either it will testify for a person who believed and acted upon it, or it will testify against the one who turned away after it reached him. This removes any sense of complacency and encourages the believer to truly be mindful, as the previous verse urged.

=48=

The scene shifts to the Meccan disbelievers’ reaction to the Quran. The truth came to them from Allah in the form of the Quran and the prophethood of Muhammad, yet they did not receive it with gratitude. Instead, they raised an objection: why was the Prophet not given the like of what Moses was given? The “like of what Moses had been given” refers to the dramatic, public, and tangible miracles that accompanied Moses, such as the staff turning into a serpent, the radiant hand, the parting of the sea, and the giving of the Torah on tablets in a single, awe-inspiring event. The Meccans, influenced by Jewish critics, argued that if Muhammad were truly a prophet, he should bring a book all at once from heaven or perform miracles of that spectacular scale. Their demand was not a sincere search for truth but a pretext to justify their rejection. The verse immediately exposes the hypocrisy of this demand by asking a rhetorical question: “Did they not deny what had been given to Moses earlier?” The very same disbelievers, or the human species across history, did not accept Moses’ signs when they came. Pharaoh and his people called them magic.

Furthermore, the contemporary Jews of Medina and their allies who prompted this question had themselves tampered with the Torah and failed to fully believe in it. The Quran then quotes their own contradictory statement: they claimed that both the Torah and the Quran are works of magic that support each other. This accusation reveals the absurdity of their position. Moses’ book and Muhammad’s book, they said, are two sorceries that back each other up. But if Moses’ book was true, then its prophecies about the coming of a final prophet would authenticate the Quran. If they claimed both were magic, they were rejecting Moses as well, which even the Jews nominally did not do. Their final declaration, “We truly deny both,” is a sweeping rejection of all divine revelation. This verse captures the tragic state of a heart that, when confronted with truth, resorts to shifting, self-contradictory arguments rather than submitting. It warns against the mindset that treats religion as a debate to be won rather than a truth to be followed.

=49=

In the face of the disbelievers’ rejection of both the Torah and the Quran, Allah instructs the Prophet to issue a bold and rational challenge. He is told to say to them: if you are truthful in your claim that the Torah and the Quran are merely two works of magic, and you are not just making excuses to avoid the truth, then produce a scripture from Allah that is a better guide than these two. The challenge is brilliant in its simplicity and fairness. The disbelievers had criticised the Quran for not being like the Torah, and they had denigrated both. The Prophet does not argue about which is superior; he simply asks them to bring an alternative that surpasses either of them in guidance.

The word “guide” is the core criterion. The measure of a scripture is not the spectacular nature of the miracles accompanying it but the quality of the guidance it provides for humanity. Does it teach the pure oneness of God? Does it command justice, mercy, and moral excellence? Does it provide a coherent explanation of creation, life, and the afterlife? Does it transform individuals and societies towards righteousness? If the Torah and the Quran fail these criteria in their view, then they should produce something that does better. Of course, they cannot. No human being can compose a text that matches, let alone surpasses, the divine scriptures in their comprehensive and perfect guidance. The challenge remains open for all time.

The verse also implies that if such a better scripture were to be brought, the Prophet himself, as a sincere seeker of truth, would follow it. This demonstrates the humility and objectivity of the prophetic stance. He is not following a family tradition or a personal whim; he is following the best guidance available. Since no one can meet the challenge, the disbelievers’ rejection is exposed as empty and their claim of seeking better guidance as false.

This verse teaches believers how to engage with those who criticise Islam: not with anger or compulsion, but with a calm, logical invitation to produce the evidence for their alternative. It also affirms the sufficiency and excellence of the Quranic guidance.

=50=

The verse now states the inevitable outcome of the challenge. The disbelievers will not and cannot bring any scripture better than the Torah and the Quran. Their failure to respond is not due to a lack of time or opportunity; it is because no such alternative exists. The Prophet is told to “know” with certainty, on the basis of this failed challenge, that these people are not following reason, evidence, or a sincere search for truth. They are following their own desires.

The word “desires” here refers to the whims, lusts, and preferences of the ego that have no grounding in divine guidance. Their objection to the Quran was never really about the lack of a spectacular miracle; it was a smokescreen for their unwillingness to abandon the idolatrous traditions that gave them status, wealth, and a license to indulge.

The verse then asks a rhetorical question that serves as a severe warning: “Who could be more astray than those who follow their desires with no guidance from Allah? ” The answer is that no one can be more astray. When a person cuts himself off from the divine light and makes his own inclinations his compass, he inevitably wanders into error, confusion, and ultimate destruction. Guidance from Allah is the only reliable map for the human soul. Without it, the desires of the ego are like a ship’s captain who is drunk, constantly changing direction, leading the vessel onto the rocks.

The verse concludes with a definitive statement: “Surely Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people.” The term “wrongdoing people” describes those who have chosen the path of oppression, injustice, and rejection of truth. Their hearts have become sealed by their persistent sins, and the guidance of Allah, in the sense of the enabling grace that leads to faith, is withheld from them.

This is not because Allah is ungenerous, but because they have made themselves unworthy receivers. A person who closes his eyes cannot blame the sun for not providing light. This verse is a profound analysis of the psychology of disbelief and a call to self-examination. Believers must ask themselves whether their own religious choices are based on sincere seeking of truth or on following the comfortable desires of the ego.

=51=

From the discussion of those who follow their desires, the surah turns back to the mercy and wisdom of Allah in revealing the Quran. The phrase “steadily delivered” conveys the sense of continuity, connection, and careful arrangement. The revelation did not come down all at once as a single book but was sent down in stages, verse by verse, surah by surah, over a period of twenty-three years. This gradual descent served multiple purposes. It allowed the hearts of the believers to absorb the message deeply and without being overwhelmed. It provided timely guidance in response to unfolding events, strengthening the Prophet and his community at critical moments. It answered questions from both believers and skeptics as they arose.

The Word was not delivered haphazardly; it was woven together with a deliberate, sustained coherence that made it easy to memorize, recite, and reflect upon. The purpose of this steady delivery is stated clearly: “so they may be mindful.

” The Arabic term for mindfulness, dhikr, implies remembrance, reflection, and a state of constant awareness of Allah. The Quran was sent to be a living source of remembrance. When a person engages with it regularly, their heart is repeatedly struck with the truths of faith, the consequences of sin, the mercy of Allah, and the reality of the Hereafter. This frequent exposure chips away at the hardness of the heart and replaces forgetfulness with mindfulness. The gradual revelation also reflects Allah’s intimate knowledge of human psychology. A massive, instantaneous delivery might have been difficult to sustain; the piecemeal approach kept the recipients engaged, eager, and attentive. The verse serves as a reminder to believers that the arrangement of the Quran is not random. The surahs and verses are placed in an order that facilitates continuous reflection. It also encourages a relationship with the Quran that is not a one-time reading but a steady, daily engagement, allowing the Word to work its transformative effect over a lifetime. The continuity of revelation, now complete, continues to deliver its benefits whenever a person commits to its steady recitation and study, leading them towards the mindfulness that is the goal of all worship.

=52=

This verse refers to a specific group among the People of the Book, the Jews and Christians, who were sincere in their faith. Before the coming of the Quran, they had been given the Torah and the Gospel. They were not mere nominal adherents; they were genuinely “faithful” to those scriptures, studying them, living by their true teachings, and awaiting the advent of the final prophet as foretold in their books. The verse states that when these sincere people heard the Quran, they believed in it. Their belief in the previous scriptures did not become a barrier to accepting the new revelation; on the contrary, it was their very familiarity with the divine promises and descriptions of the coming prophet that allowed them to recognise the truth of the Quran immediately. This refers to individuals like Abdullah ibn Salam, a learned Jewish rabbi in Medina who embraced Islam upon seeing the signs of prophethood in Muhammad, peace be upon him, or the Christian delegation from Abyssinia who, when the Quran was recited to them, wept and declared their faith. The verse serves as both a praise of these sincere believers and a rebuttal to the claim of the disbelievers that the Quran contradicts previous scriptures. The sincere People of the Book saw the Quran as the continuation and culmination of the divine message they already believed in. Their faith was not a betrayal of Moses or Jesus but a completion of it.

This verse also provides a criterion for distinguishing between those who merely inherit a religious identity and those who truly seek God. The former cling to their community’s rejection of the truth out of pride or fear of change, while the latter follow the truth wherever it leads, even if it means crossing communal boundaries. The verse reassures the Muslim community that their scripture is not alien to the earlier revelations but is its confirmed, perfected form, and that the best of the earlier communities are their brothers and sisters in faith.

=53=

This verse continues the portrayal of the sincere People of the Book. Their response to hearing the Quran recited is not hesitation, skepticism, or a demand for more proof; it is an immediate and emphatic declaration of faith. “We believe in it,” they say, without reservation. They do not treat it as a foreign book but recognise it instantly as the truth from the same Lord who sent the earlier scriptures.

The phrase “this is definitely the truth from our Lord” carries a sense of joyful recognition. They were waiting for this truth, they found its description in their own books, and now that it has arrived, it is unmistakably divine.

The final part of their statement is profound: “We had already submitted even before this.” They are saying that their submission to Allah did not begin with the Quran; they were already Muslims, in the universal sense of the word, having submitted to Allah through the true teachings of Moses and Jesus. The Quran is therefore not asking them to abandon their Lord but to deepen and complete their submission by accepting the final messenger. This verse describes an ideal spiritual disposition: a heart that is already oriented towards God, already living in a state of surrender, and therefore ready to embrace any further truth that comes from Him. It contrasts sharply with those whose faith is merely a cultural identity that resists any call to renewal or completion.

The verse also validates the concept that true religion across the ages is one: Islam, in its broadest sense of submission to the one God. The sincere believers among the previous nations were already part of this spiritual community, and the Quran invited them to update their practice to the final, most comprehensive form of that same submission.

For the Muslim reader, this verse cultivates respect for the sincere seekers of truth in all traditions and encourages a confident, welcoming attitude towards those who come to Islam with a pre-existing devotion to God.

=54=

After praising the faith and submission of the sincere People of the Book, Allah announces a unique honour for them: a double reward. The double reward is explained as a recognition of their spiritual journey. They believed in their original scripture and lived by it faithfully in an environment that may have been hostile, and then when the final revelation came, they believed in that as well, often facing rejection and persecution from their own communities. Their perseverance through these multiple tests of faith earned them multiplied blessings.

The verse then lists some of the specific qualities that contributed to this double reward. First, “their perseverance” – they endured the difficulty of maintaining their faith in the earlier period, and then they endured the backlash that came when they accepted Islam. Second, “responding to evil with good” – they did not meet the insults or hostility of their opponents with similar negativity but with goodness, patience, and dignified conduct. This quality is a hallmark of mature faith: the ability to absorb harm and return it with kindness, thereby diffusing conflict and often winning over enemies. Third, “donating from what We have provided for them” – their faith was not merely intellectual or emotional; it translated into generous spending on the needy and on the support of the truth. These three qualities — perseverance, responding to evil with good, and charitable giving — are a concise summary of holistic righteousness, covering the personal, social, and financial dimensions of life. The double reward is not a mechanical formula but a reflection of Allah’s generous appreciation for those whose journey to Him was long and costly.

The verse also serves as an encouragement to all believers, especially converts who have left their former communities and face isolation or hostility. Their sacrifices are seen, valued, and will be rewarded manifold. It affirms that God is not stingy; He multiplies the recompense for those who show extraordinary faithfulness in the face of extraordinary challenge.

=55=

Another praiseworthy trait of these believers is their reaction to the offensive speech they encounter. The verse describes a situation where they hear “slanderous talk,” which includes mockery of their faith, insults directed at the Prophet or the Quran, and crude, argumentative language designed to provoke them. Instead of being drawn into the mudslinging, they choose to disengage with dignity. They turn away, physically or intellectually, refusing to participate in the hostile exchange. Their verbal response, when a response is necessary, is a calm and principled statement. First, “We are accountable for our deeds and you for yours.” This asserts the principle of personal responsibility. They are not judges over the others; each person will answer for their own conduct. This removes the need for a heated defence of ego. Second, they say, “Peace is our only response to you!

” This is the greeting of salam, but here it functions as a declaration of non-engagement and a desire for a safe, peaceful distance. It does not mean they approve of the slander; it means they will not fight fire with fire.

The word “peace” implies that the conversation ends there; no further argument, no retaliation, no escalation. Third, they declare, “We want nothing to do with those who act ignorantly.

” This is a statement of dissociation from ignorance and its practitioners. They recognise that the argument is not based on a sincere quest for truth but on ignorant prejudice, and they refuse to be companions to such behaviour.

This verse teaches a profound lesson in conflict management and spiritual self-preservation. Engaging with every hostile comment leads to exhaustion, anger, and potential sin. The believer is to know when to walk away, leave the judgment to Allah, and protect the purity of his or her own soul.

The verse also sets a high standard for interfaith and interpersonal relations: maintain clarity about one’s own principles, refuse to be provoked, and part with a word of peace rather than a curse.

=56=

This verse serves as a source of comfort and direction for the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, and through him for all who call others to the truth. The Prophet’s deep compassion and concern for his people, including his close relatives and beloved uncle Abu Talib, made him intensely eager that they accept Islam. This eagerness sometimes weighed heavily upon him when they persisted in disbelief. Allah addresses this directly: “You surely cannot guide whoever you like. ” Guidance, meaning the inner transformation that leads a person to accept faith and live by it, is not in the hands of the Prophet, no matter how much he loves the person or how hard he tries. It is a matter of the heart, and only the Creator of hearts can open them.

The verse then states the positive fact: “but it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills.” This is not arbitrary; it is rooted in Allah’s perfect wisdom and knowledge.

The next phrase clarifies this: “He knows best who are fit to be guided.” Allah’s will in guidance is not random; it is based on His knowledge of who has the sincerity, humility, and receptivity to receive the truth. Those who are genuinely seeking, who are not arrogant, and who are willing to submit when the truth becomes clear, are the ones whom Allah guides. Those who stubbornly resist, not because of intellectual confusion but because of pride, worldly interests, or blind following of tradition, are left to their own choices. This verse does not teach fatalism or discourage preaching. The Prophet and the believers are commanded to convey the message, argue in the best way, and show the beauty of Islam. Their efforts are the outward means that Allah may use. But the outcome is not their burden.

This is a liberating truth for anyone involved in da‘wah, teaching, or even parenting. You do your best, you make the message clear and attractive, and then you entrust the hearts to the Lord who alone can guide. It prevents burnout, guilt, and despair, and it places ultimate reliance where it belongs. The Prophet is reminded that his role is a mercy and a clear warner, not a guarantor of conversion, a theme that resonates throughout the surah.

=57=

The disbelievers of Mecca present yet another excuse for their rejection. They claim that if they were to follow the Prophet’s guidance, they would be “snatched away” from their land. This reflects their fear of losing the political and economic stability of Mecca. As the custodians of the Ka‘bah and the hosts of the annual pilgrimage, the Quraysh enjoyed a privileged position in Arabia. They feared that embracing Islam would provoke the hostility of other pagan tribes, disrupt the pilgrimage trade, and lead to war and their eventual expulsion or destruction. This was a worldly calculation dressed as a rational concern. The Quran responds by pointing to an obvious fact that should have reframed their entire thinking. Allah asks, through the Prophet, whether He has not already established for them a “safe haven. ” The Arabic word “haram” refers to the sacred precinct of Mecca, where bloodshed was forbidden and where even a criminal found refuge. This sanctuary was not a human achievement; it was a divine institution going back to the prayer of Abraham, who asked Allah to make Mecca a place of security and to provide its people with fruits. The verse notes that “fruits of all kinds are brought” to Mecca, a barren valley with no agriculture, from surrounding fertile regions. This economic miracle, the influx of provisions due to the pilgrimage, was a direct result of Allah’s favour, not Quraysh’s power. The verse implies a simple argument: if Allah has provided such security and provision for them while they were still polytheists, why would He not provide even greater security and provision if they embraced His guidance? Their fear was baseless, rooted in a lack of trust in the Lord who had already sustained them. The concluding phrase, “but most of them do not know,” indicates that their perception was clouded by their worldly obsessions. They failed to connect their current blessings to the Giver of blessings, and so they misjudged the consequences of obeying Him.

This verse teaches believers to examine their own fears about following religion fully. Any perceived worldly loss that might come from obedience is dwarfed by the proven provision of the One who has already sustained us.

=58=

The surah now turns to a powerful historical argument. The Quraysh feared losing their comfortable life in Mecca. Allah reminds them of the many communities before them that were even more spoiled by luxurious living.

The phrase “spoiled by their comfortable living” describes a condition where abundant wealth, ease, and pleasure led to arrogance, forgetfulness of God, moral decay, and eventual destruction. These societies, like those of ‘Ad, Thamad, and the people of Lot, felt secure in their prosperity and thought it would last forever. Instead, divine punishment suddenly descended upon them, wiping them out.

The verse then invites the Meccans to look at the physical evidence: “Those are their residences, never inhabited after them except passingly.” The ruined dwellings of these destroyed nations were along the trade routes that the Quraysh travelled. They could see with their own eyes the empty, crumbling remains of once-powerful civilisations, now silent and desolate, occasionally visited by travellers who paused briefly before moving on. The ruins were a testimony to the transience of worldly power and the reality of divine judgment.

The verse then declares, “And We alone were the Successor.” When those people perished, Allah inherited their lands and their possessions, for there was no one else left to claim them. This emphasises that the true owner of everything is Allah, and He gives and takes away dominion as He wills. The message to the Quraysh was stark: do not be deceived by your current prosperity and security. Many before you enjoyed even more and were destroyed for their rejection of truth. Your comfortable homes and your thriving trade will not protect you from Allah’s decree if you persist in disbelief and oppression. This verse is a timeless warning to all nations and individuals who measure success by material comfort and feel immune from accountability. The empty ruins of history are the silent preachers of this truth, and the Quran calls humanity to hear their message before it is too late.

=59=

This verse states two fundamental principles of divine justice regarding the destruction of nations. The first principle is that Allah never punishes a community with annihilation until He has sent a messenger to its central city, reciting His revelations and making the truth undeniably clear. The “capital” or main city is specified because it was usually the centre of power, wealth, and culture, from which the message would radiate to surrounding areas. The messenger’s job was to convey the revelation, warn of consequences, and call the people to righteousness. Once this warning is delivered with clarity, the proof is established. No one can claim ignorance or lack of opportunity.

The second principle is that destruction does not occur simply because of unbelief alone, but because the people “persisted in wrongdoing.” The term “wrongdoing” encompasses injustice, oppression, corruption, and the violation of the fundamental rights of others. Persistence means they continued in their evil ways, refusing to repent or reform, despite the repeated warnings of the messenger. The verse thus clarifies that divine punishment is not arbitrary or impulsive. It comes after a period of patient warning, after the messenger has exhausted every effort, and after the society has demonstrated that it will not change.

This principle was important for the Prophet and the early Muslims to understand. The fact that the Quraysh had not yet been destroyed, despite their mockery and persecution, was not because they were right or because Allah was unaware. It was because the messenger was still among them, conveying the message, and the period of respite was still ongoing. The opportunity for individual repentance remained open.

The verse also serves to reassure the believers that divine justice is measured, deliberate, and perfectly timed. It counters the misconception that a merciful God would never destroy a people; the destruction occurs only when the conditions of persistent, warned-against oppression are fully met. It also places a sobering responsibility on the reader: the message has now reached us through the Quran, and we are accountable for our response.

=60=

The surah now transitions to a direct reflection on the nature of worldly life, building on the previous warnings about destroyed civilisations that were intoxicated by their comforts. Allah addresses humanity, reminding them that all the pleasures, possessions, and beauties they enjoy are merely the “enjoyment and adornment of this worldly life.

” The word “enjoyment” in Arabic refers to a temporary, utilitarian benefit, like the food one eats or the journey one takes, which serves its purpose and then passes. “Adornment” refers to the decorative, aesthetic aspects of life, the things that beautify one’s surroundings and appearance. Both are described as “fleeting” or ephemeral. The verse does not forbid these pleasures; it labels them accurately so that people do not mistake them for the ultimate goal. They are a short stop on a long journey, not the destination.

The verse then contrasts this with what lies with Allah: “far better and more lasting. ” The rewards of the Hereafter, including the gardens of Paradise, the pleasure of Allah, and the peace and security that never end, are infinitely superior in quality and eternal in duration. The phrase “far better” addresses quality, indicating that the least of Paradise’s joys surpasses the greatest of earthly pleasures. “More lasting” addresses duration, because even a billion years of earthly life would end, while the Hereafter never ends. The rhetorical question, “Will you not then understand?” is a gentle but piercing call to reason. A rational person, when faced with a choice between a limited, inferior good and an unlimited, superior good, should choose the latter.

Yet human beings, in their heedlessness, often chase the temporary at the expense of the eternal. This verse is a cornerstone of the Quranic worldview on materialism. It does not promote asceticism or the rejection of lawful worldly enjoyments; it puts them in their proper place. They are to be used as provisions for the journey, not worshipped as the goal. The believer works for this world but invests for the next, always keeping the divine perspective: the best trade is to exchange the fleeting for the lasting.
