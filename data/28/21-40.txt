=21=

The warning from the sincere advisor at the farthest end of the city now translated into immediate action. Moses did not delay, debate, or try to gather belongings; he departed from Egypt, the land of his upbringing, in a condition that combined two intense emotions: fear and vigilant caution.

The Arabic wording paints the picture of a man constantly looking over his shoulder, acutely aware that at any moment the authorities might catch up with him. His fear was not the panic of a coward but the prudent apprehension of someone who knows the enemy is powerful, ruthless, and actively seeking his life.

Yet this fear did not paralyse him; it was accompanied by the practical caution of choosing the right route and remaining watchful. Most importantly, his heart was connected to Allah in supplication. His words, “My Lord! Deliver me from the wrongdoing people,” are a profound expression of reliance. He did not say “deliver me from death” or “give me a comfortable journey,” but specifically sought rescue from the company and the clutches of those who are unjust.

The term “wrongdoing people” identifies the Egyptian regime not merely as a political adversary but as a moral category, people whose very character is defined by oppression. In praying this way, Moses aligned his escape with a spiritual request: he wanted to be saved not just physically but to be separated from an environment of evil. This supplication teaches the believer that in moments of danger and flight, the first resort is not to one’s own cleverness but to the Lord who controls all circumstances. The prayer also contained an implicit confession that deliverance is entirely in Allah’s hands; Moses had no power to secure his own safety against the Egyptian state. Many scholars note that Moses fled without any provision, mount, or companion, placing his complete trust in the One he called upon. This moment mirrors the experience of the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, during his own emigration from Mecca, when he and Abu Bakr hid in the cave and the pursuers were at the entrance, yet the Prophet calmed his companion by saying that Allah was with them. Both journeys began in fear and outward vulnerability but were carried forward by an inner certainty that the Lord hears the call of the oppressed and opens a path where none seems to exist. The verse thus marks the transition from palace life to wilderness journey, from the false security of Pharaoh’s Egypt to the real security of divine protection that manifests through a road to Midian.

=22=

Having left Egypt behind, Moses now faced the vast and unfamiliar terrain between Egypt and Midian. He had no map, no guide, and no previous experience of the route. The geographical distance was considerable; Midian is generally located in the northwestern part of the Arabian Peninsula, across the Sinai. In this state of complete dependence, Moses spoke words that reveal the essence of his faith: “I trust my Lord will guide me to the right way. ” The word translated as “I trust” conveys a deep sense of hope combined with confident expectation. Moses did not say he was certain of the exact path or that he had figured out the directions. He entrusted the matter to Allah, believing that the One who commanded him to leave would also show him the way.

The phrase “the right way” can be understood on two levels. On the immediate physical level, it referred to the road to Midian, a place where he would find safety, hospitality, and ultimately a new life. On a deeper spiritual level, it expressed his hope for guidance in the broader sense—the path of righteousness, the plan that Allah had for his life, and the eventual fulfilment of his destiny. These two levels are united because the road to Midian was not a random escape; it was the divinely chosen path leading to his marriage, his years of shepherding, and his eventual encounter with Allah at the burning bush. Moses’ statement is a model for the believer who finds himself at a crossroads, not knowing what to do. Instead of being overwhelmed by uncertainty, one can vocalise trust and hope in divine guidance. This does not mean sitting idle; Moses was actively walking, making his best judgement about the direction. But his heart was not leaning on his own intelligence. He knew that true guidance, which leads to safety and success, comes from Allah alone.

This verse also subtly hints at a larger Quranic theme: those who emigrate for the sake of Allah and place their trust in Him are guided to provisions and paths they never anticipated. For the Meccan Muslims who would later migrate to Abyssinia and Medina, Moses’ journey to Midian was a prophetic mirror, showing them that leaving one’s homeland in fear is followed by divine direction and eventual establishment, if the heart remains anchored in trust.

=23=

After a long and exhausting journey through the desert, Moses finally reached the watering place of Midian. Wells in such regions were central gathering points for shepherds and their flocks, often scenes of bustling activity and competition for water. The verse depicts the scene with vivid detail. Moses saw a crowd of men actively watering their animals, but at a distance he noticed two women who were not pushing forward. They were holding back their herd, an indication that they were in a vulnerable position. Despite his own exhaustion, hunger, and the fact that he was a fugitive in a foreign land, Moses’ sense of justice and his natural inclination to help the weak were immediately stirred. He approached the women and asked a simple question: “What is the problem?” His question was direct and compassionate, seeking to understand why they were not participating. Their reply was telling: they could not water their animals until the male shepherds had finished. This suggests that the well area was dominated by stronger shepherds who did not readily make space for two lone women, and these women, out of modesty or an inability to compete physically, waited patiently for the crowd to disperse. The deeper reason they gave was the age of their father. “Our father is a very old man” explains their presence at the well to begin with. In that society, tending flocks was typically men’s work, but their father’s frailty had forced his daughters to take up this task, exposing them to a difficult and potentially uncomfortable situation. This brief statement reveals the poverty and vulnerability of the family, but also the dignity and responsibility of the daughters who cared for their elderly parent without complaint. The scene sets the stage for Moses’ intervention, which will demonstrate his strength, his chivalry, and his instinctive care for the oppressed, all qualities that mark him as a future leader and prophet.

The verse also teaches that a believer, even when burdened by personal hardships, does not become blind to the difficulties of others. Moses could have ignored the women and focused on his own needs, but his moral compass would not allow it, and that act of kindness became the key that unlocked the next chapter of his life.

=24=

Without hesitation, Moses stepped forward and performed the task of watering the women’s flock. The verse does not elaborate on how he managed it, but the implication is that his strength, honed by years of palace living and perhaps training, enabled him to push through the crowd of shepherds and draw enough water for the animals. This was an act of pure altruism. He did not know these women, expected no reward, and was himself a weary traveller in need of rest. Having completed the task, he did not linger to receive thanks or strike up a conversation. Instead, he withdrew to the shade of a tree or a wall, seeking relief from the desert sun. Then he turned to his Lord with a prayer that is among the most moving in the Quran: “My Lord! I am truly in desperate need of whatever provision You may have in store for me. ” The Arabic word translated as “desperate need” is often rendered as “poor” or “needy,” but in this context it carries an intensity that goes beyond material lack. Moses was physically destitute: no food, no shelter, no employment, no family, and a price on his head back in Egypt.

Yet his prayer did not specify a particular request. He did not say, “Give me food” or “Give me a wife. ” He opened his entire condition of poverty before Allah and asked for any good that Allah might send down.

This is the ultimate form of reliance: acknowledging total dependence and leaving the form of the provision entirely to the wisdom of the Giver. The prayer reflects a heart that trusts Allah’s choice more than its own wishes. It was answered almost immediately in ways Moses could not have imagined: an invitation to a home, a secure job offer, a wife, and a period of peaceful preparation for prophethood.

The sequence teaches that when a believer sincerely places their need before Allah after having done what is right, the response can exceed all expectations. It also shows the beautiful combination of active service and private supplication. Moses exhausted his physical ability to help, then retreated into the shade of solitude to connect with his Lord, teaching that the most effective helpers are those whose outward actions are fuelled by inward poverty before Allah.

=25=

The immediate fruit of Moses’ selfless act and his sincere prayer now unfolds. One of the two sisters returned to the well, approaching Moses. The Quran describes her manner of walking as “bashfully,” a term that conveys modesty, shyness, and dignity. In a culture where women did not typically interact with strange men, her approach was necessary but carefully conducted with the utmost propriety. She delivered her father’s message succinctly and professionally: the invitation was extended so that the father could properly compensate Moses for his service. There is a beautiful balance here: Moses had helped without expectation of reward, but Allah arranged for him to receive hospitality and honour precisely because of that help. The young woman’s bashfulness and her direct, business-like speech reflect the character of a family rooted in righteous values. Moses accepted the invitation and accompanied her, and upon meeting the elderly man, identified by some scholars as the Prophet Shu‘ayb, though this identification is not unanimous, he recounted his entire story. Moses held nothing back; he explained his flight from Egypt, the unintentional killing, and the conspiracy against his life. The old man listened and then responded with words of reassurance: “Have no fear! You are now safe from the wrongdoing people.” This declaration of safety was a profound relief to Moses. The elderly father recognised that Midian lay outside Pharaoh’s effective jurisdiction, and he extended his personal protection.

The phrase “wrongdoing people” echoes Moses’ own prayer in verse 21, showing how Allah answered him by placing him under the wing of a righteous household that explicitly acknowledged the injustice of the regime he had fled. The hospitality of this Midianite family was not just a meal and a bed; it was the provision of security, acceptance, and a new community. This encounter also demonstrates the sunnah of rewarding kindness with kindness and the barakah that descends upon a home where an elderly father, unable to work himself, instilled such modesty and work ethic in his daughters that they became the means of attracting a prophet into their lives.

=26=

After Moses had been welcomed and his story heard, one of the two daughters, likely the same one who had approached him at the well, made a suggestion to her father. Her words are recorded with precision. She addressed him with tenderness, saying “O my dear father,” indicating a close, respectful relationship. Her proposal was that her father should hire Moses as a worker. The reasoning she gave has become proverbial in Islamic ethics: “The best man for employment is definitely the strong and trustworthy one.” She had observed two qualities in Moses that qualified him. Strength was evident from his ability to singlehandedly water their flock despite the crowd of shepherds. Trustworthiness was deduced from his conduct: he did not try to take advantage of the two women when they were alone at the well, he looked away with modesty, he did the work without demanding payment, and when she came to summon him, he walked behind her or averted his gaze, showing complete integrity. These two qualities—competence and honesty—are the pillars of any successful employment or leadership. The daughter’s statement is a timeless piece of wisdom that believers are encouraged to apply in their personal and professional lives. When hiring someone for any task, religious or secular, the primary criteria should be the person’s ability to perform the work and their moral reliability. Physical strength without trustworthiness leads to exploitation and betrayal; trustworthiness without strength leads to incompetence and failure to deliver.

The verse also highlights the intelligence and perceptiveness of the young woman. She was not shy to the point of being unable to offer sound advice to her father. Her family valued her opinion, and her father, as we see, acted upon it. This corrects cultural misconceptions that a pious woman’s voice has no place in practical affairs. Her suggestion was entirely proper and led to a blessed outcome.

Moreover, she recommended employment, not charity, which preserved Moses’ dignity by offering him a means of earning through his own effort. The whole exchange is a model of how families should discuss important decisions with mutual respect, frankness, and a clear moral framework.

=27=

The father’s response to his daughter’s suggestion went beyond mere employment. He recognised in Moses a man of exceptional character, and he saw an opportunity to integrate him fully into the family. His proposal combined marriage with a work contract. He offered Moses one of his two daughters in marriage, with the mahr, or bridal gift, being a period of service. The specified term was eight years, which would fulfil the obligation. He then added that if Moses chose to complete ten years, that would be an act of voluntary goodness from Moses’ side, not a requirement. The father immediately clarified his own intention: “I do not wish to make it difficult for you.” He was not trying to drive a hard bargain or extract maximum labour. The ten-year option was mentioned as an open, flexible possibility, not a pressure. Then he sealed the offer with the beautiful phrase, “Allah willing, you will find me an agreeable man. ” This statement reveals several layers of his character. He acknowledged that his own claim of good conduct was subject to Allah’s will and would be proven through experience. He did not boast but humbly expressed the hope that Moses would find him fair and just.

The mention of “Allah willing” also shows his habit of referring every future matter to divine decree. The entire proposal is a masterclass in ethical contracting. It is transparent, balanced, and free of any hidden traps. The mahr is clearly defined, the length of service is specific, and the terms are framed in a spirit of goodwill. The father did not demand the maximum term; he left the door open for Moses to choose the longer period as a gesture of generosity. This reflects the Quranic ideal of contracts that are mutually beneficial and not exploitative. The scene also subtly continues the theme of divine provision: the man who arrived in Midian with nothing is now offered a wife, a home, and secure employment in a single meeting. Allah’s answer to the prayer under the shade exceeded Moses’ unspoken hopes, a pattern that reminds believers that the Lord’s generosity is not limited by the meagreness of the supplicant’s specific requests.

=28=

Moses’ reply is immediate, clear, and dignified. He accepts the proposal and formalises the agreement with words that establish a binding contract. “It is settled between you and I” indicates that the negotiation phase is over and mutual consent has been reached. He then clarifies his understanding of the terms, emphasising that whichever of the two periods he fulfils—the eight or the ten years—his obligation will be completely discharged. There will be no lingering claims, no additional demands, and no ongoing liability. This insistence on clarity is crucial in any agreement, especially one involving marriage and long-term service. By stating it explicitly, Moses prevented any future misunderstanding or resentment. He then invokes the highest witness: “And Allah is a Witness to what we say. ” This statement elevates the agreement from a mere social arrangement to a solemn covenant observed by the Creator. Both parties now knew that breaking the terms would not only be a breach of human trust but an act for which they would be accountable before Allah. This invocation of divine witness was a common practice of the prophets and the righteous, serving both as a deterrent against dishonesty and as a source of comfort that justice would prevail even if one party later denied the terms. The verse highlights the Islamic principle that verbal agreements, when made with clear terms and sincerity, are binding and should be honoured. Written contracts are recommended as a practical measure, but the moral and spiritual weight lies in the consciousness that Allah is watching and hearing. Moses’ conduct here also shows that he was not a man who shied away from commitment. He had been a fugitive, but now he was ready to settle down, work hard, and fulfil a long-term responsibility. This period of shepherding and family life was, in Allah’s plan, a seminary for the soul. It would teach him patience, leadership, and the management of flocks before he would be called to shepherd a nation. The agreement concluded with complete satisfaction on both sides, and the chapter of Midian began, a chapter marked by peace, honest work, and preparation for the world-changing mission that lay ahead.

=29=

Years passed. Moses faithfully served the full term, and most scholars agree that he completed the longer period of ten years, as that was more befitting of his character. Now the contract was fulfilled, and he was travelling with his family, leaving Midian, perhaps heading towards Egypt to visit his mother and relatives or simply moving to a new location. The journey took them through the wilderness near Mount Ṭûr, also known as Mount Sinai. It was a cold desert night, and the family was in need of both direction and warmth. Moses saw a fire flickering on the side of the mountain. In the darkness, a fire is a beacon of hope and a source of practical help. He instructed his family to remain where they were while he went to investigate. His words to them reveal his role as a caring, responsible husband and father, providing for his family’s needs even in the middle of a difficult journey. He stated two possible benefits of the fire: he might bring them some directions, meaning information about the route from any people camped there, or at least a burning torch so they could warm themselves. The verse is deliberately ordinary in its description because the extraordinary was about to occur, and the contrast heightens the impact. Moses thought he was approaching a simple campfire or a natural flame that would provide practical help. He had no idea that this fire was the veil of divine light and that the mountain contained the sacred valley where he would hear the speech of his Lord.

The verse shows that major spiritual turning points often arrive when a person is simply going about their daily duties. Moses was not in a state of ritual retreat or formal worship; he was a traveller seeking comfort for his family. It was in that mundane moment of need that Allah chose to honour him with prophethood. This teaches believers to remain spiritually alert during the ordinary routines of life, for one never knows when a “fire” spotted on the horizon will become the site of the most profound encounter with the divine.

The mention of Mount Ṭûr connects this event to the sacred geography that will later be the site of the giving of the Torah.

=30=

Moses approached the fire, expecting to find an ordinary source of light. Instead, he encountered something entirely beyond his experience. The fire appeared within or around a bush that was not being consumed, a phenomenon that signalled that the natural laws were suspended in that place. As he drew near, a voice called out to him. The call came not from the fire itself but from the direction of the bush, located in the sacred ground to the right side of the valley. The precise geographic description emphasises the historicity and specificity of the event; this was not a vision or a dream but a real occurrence at a real place.

The first words spoken were the announcement of divine identity: “O Moses! It is truly I. I am Allah—the Lord of all worlds.” The direct address by name would have been startling. Moses was being spoken to personally by the Creator of the universe. The double emphasis, “It is truly I. I am Allah,” eliminates any possibility that this was an angelic messenger or a deceptive spirit. The voice identified itself clearly as the One God, using the name Allah, and then added the title “Lord of all worlds” to leave no ambiguity about His absolute sovereignty over every realm of existence, including the realm of Pharaoh who claimed to be a lord himself. This moment marks Moses’ official commissioning. The encounter is unique in its directness and the clarity of the self-revelation. Moses was not left to guess or infer; he was plainly told who was addressing him.

For the believer, this verse underscores the majesty of Allah’s communication with His chosen servants. While ordinary people receive guidance through the revealed Book and through inspiration, the prophets were privileged to hear the speech of Allah directly or through an angel. The location being “sacred ground” also highlights the concept that certain places, by Allah’s designation, carry a special blessed status. The valley of Ṭuwâ, as mentioned elsewhere, became hallowed because Allah spoke there. The voice that once guided Moses’ mother to place her child in the river was now speaking to the grown man, connecting his entire life story to this defining moment.

=31=

After establishing His identity, Allah gave Moses a command that would serve as both a sign and a test. “Throw down your staff.” The staff was an ordinary object that Moses had used for years during his shepherding: for walking, for guiding his flock, for beating down leaves for his sheep. He was instructed to cast it to the ground. As soon as he did, the staff transformed into a living snake, writhing and moving rapidly. The sight was terrifying. Despite being a strong and courageous man, Moses could not control his natural human instinct, and he fled without looking back. The wording captures the suddenness of his reaction. He was not paralysed by fear but ran, which is itself a sign of the snake’s frightening appearance and movement. Immediately, Allah called out again with words of reassurance: “O Moses! Draw near, and have no fear. You are perfectly secure. ” The command to draw near is powerful. Allah was telling Moses to overcome his fear by returning to the very source of the fright. The assurance of perfect security meant that in this sacred valley, under direct divine protection, nothing could harm him. This moment illustrates a profound spiritual principle: the initial encounter with divine signs can be overwhelming to human sensibilities. The ordinary staff becoming a serpent was a small demonstration of the creative power that Allah holds, but it also shattered Moses’ previous understanding of reality. Fear is a natural human response to the supernatural, yet Allah does not leave His servant in that state. The command to return and the promise of safety are characteristic of how the Lord trains His prophets, moving them from natural fear to confident trust. This exact sequence—command, miracle, fear, divine reassurance—would later repeat itself when Moses faced Pharaoh and the magicians. The staff was not just a miracle for Pharaoh; it was first a personal lesson for Moses, teaching him that what appears deadly becomes harmless when Allah wills, and that safety is found in obedience to the command “draw near” rather than in running away.

=32=

After the staff, Allah provided Moses with a second sign, one that was deeply personal and involved his own body. He was told to put his hand inside his collar, placing it near his chest, and then draw it out. When he did so, the hand emerged shining with a brilliant white light, yet without any disease or blemish. This was not leprosy but a radiant, supernatural whiteness that served as a visible proof of divine power. Alongside this, Allah instructed him to draw his arm close to his side, a gesture that would help calm the natural tremor of fear that had seized him. This physical instruction is a beautiful acknowledgment of human psychology. Fear has physiological effects, and Allah addressed both the spiritual and physical dimensions of Moses’ anxiety. He was being prepared and steadied for the monumental task ahead: confronting the most powerful tyrant on earth. The two signs—the staff turning into a serpent and the radiant hand—are described as “two proofs from your Lord to Pharaoh and his chiefs.” They were not merely magic tricks; they were evidences that the sender of Moses was the Lord of creation, who could alter the very nature of objects and bodies at will. The description of Pharaoh’s people as “a rebellious people” explains why such powerful signs were necessary. They were not a community inclined to listen to reasoned argument alone; they had strayed so far into arrogance and defiance that only direct divine intervention would shake their foundations.

This verse also shows that Allah does not send His messengers empty-handed. The proofs given to Moses corresponded to the context of Egypt, a land renowned for magic and sorcery. The signs would challenge the magicians on their own ground and demonstrate that what Moses brought was not an advanced form of their art but a completely different category of power: the creative command of the Almighty.

For believers, the two signs symbolise that Allah equips those He charges with a mission with exactly what they need, even if the task appears humanly impossible.

=33=

Despite having just witnessed two staggering miracles and hearing the direct reassurance of his Lord, Moses remained acutely aware of his human situation. His response was not a refusal of the mission but an honest presentation of a legitimate concern. He laid before Allah the reality of his personal history: he had killed an Egyptian, and that incident was still on the record in Egypt. The legal and political consequences had not disappeared.

The phrase “from them” shows that Moses considered the Egyptians as a collective, and he knew that the slain man’s case would be used to justify his execution. His fear was not a lack of trust in Allah; it was a factual assessment of the danger, similar to his earlier flight from the city. Prophets are human, and their emotions are real. They feel fear, yet they proceed after receiving divine assurance. Moses’ statement also contains an implicit request for some kind of solution to this problem. He was not telling Allah something He did not know; he was placing his vulnerability before his Lord in a spirit of humble dependence.

This is the essence of prophetic supplication: full acknowledgment of one’s weakness, combined with full confidence that the Lord can address it. The verse demonstrates that bringing one’s practical worries to Allah is not a sign of weak faith but of honest relationship. Moses did not pretend to be superhuman. He admitted the very real possibility that as soon as he returned to Egypt and started calling Pharaoh to the truth, the old murder charge would be revived and used against him. His concern also had a strategic dimension. If he were killed immediately, the mission would appear to end before it began.

Therefore, he needed some form of protection or a change in the circumstances. Allah’s answer, which follows, would not remove the historical fact of the killing but would provide the support that made the threat ineffective. This teaches believers that past mistakes, even after forgiveness, can have ongoing worldly consequences that must be wisely managed, and that one should bring those concerns to Allah rather than letting them become obstacles to fulfilling His commands.

=34=

Moses added a second request. He acknowledged a personal limitation and proposed a solution. He stated that his brother Aaron was more fluent and articulate in speech. There are several explanations for Moses’ concern about his own speech. Some scholars mention that Moses had a slight speech impediment, perhaps a lisp or difficulty in clear expression, which he felt would hinder his communication with Pharaoh and his court. Others emphasise that Aaron possessed a natural eloquence and persuasive ability that Moses felt he lacked. In either case, Moses was not making excuses; he was identifying a practical need for the mission. He asked Allah to send Aaron with him as a helper, someone who would support and back up what he said.

The word “helper” in Arabic carries the connotation of a minister, an aide, or someone who strengthens another’s position. Moses understood that the rejection he feared was not only on the basis of the old murder charge but also because his message might be dismissed if it was not delivered with maximum clarity and force. By asking for Aaron, he was seeking to optimise the effectiveness of the prophetic call. The request demonstrates an important principle in Islamic work: one should not hesitate to involve others who possess complementary skills. Humility means acknowledging that someone else may be better at a particular task, and teamwork is a divinely sanctioned method for accomplishing great objectives. The brotherhood of Moses and Aaron became one of the most beautiful partnerships in prophetic history. Aaron, who had remained in Egypt, would be the perfect partner, familiar with the people and able to articulate the message. Moses’ prayer also shows that the fear of rejection is a real burden on a messenger. He deeply desired that the truth be accepted, and he knew that the quality of presentation could affect the outcome, even though guidance ultimately belongs to Allah.

Thus, he asked for the tools that would give the message the best possible hearing. Allah’s response, which follows, not only granted this request but expanded it into a comprehensive promise of support and authority.

=35=

Allah answered Moses’ requests with a magnificent promise that addressed both his fears. The response contained four elements. First, “We will assist you with your brother.” Aaron was granted as a prophet alongside Moses, exactly as requested.

The word for “assist” implies strengthening, backing, and empowering, so Aaron would be more than a mere translator; he would be a co-prophet sharing in the mission. Second, “and grant you both authority.

” This is a crucial addition. Allah did not just send them as two individuals with a message; He gave them an authority that would manifest in their confrontation with Pharaoh. This authority included the compelling power of the signs, a spiritual dominance that would make their words carry weight, and a protective shield that would frustrate Pharaoh’s ability to simply arrest and execute them.

The word translated as “authority” can also mean a clear proof or an overpowering argument, indicating that they would have the upper hand in the debate despite Pharaoh’s might. Third, “so they cannot harm you.” This specifically addressed Moses’ fear of being killed for the old crime. Allah assured that the Egyptians would be prevented from causing them physical harm. The promise of protection was not an unconditional guarantee that no one would ever try to hurt them; it meant that their attempts would fail, and the mission would proceed safely until its completion. Fourth, “With Our signs, you and those who follow you will certainly prevail.” The ultimate victory was guaranteed. Not only would Moses and Aaron be safe, but the community of believers who followed them would achieve triumph over Pharaoh’s regime.

The word “prevail” indicates upper hand, supremacy, and success. This promise was fulfilled when the Israelites were delivered from bondage and Pharaoh’s army was drowned. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, and his followers, this divine response served as a powerful precedent. The Lord who strengthened Moses and Aaron, gave them authority, protected them from harm, and granted their community ultimate victory is the same Lord who oversees the mission of every prophet. The verse encapsulates the journey from fear to full confidence, showing that when a believer places their concerns before Allah, the answer comes in a form that far exceeds the original request.

=36=

The scene now shifts from the sacred valley to the court of Pharaoh. Moses, equipped with the staff and the radiant hand, along with the support of his brother Aaron, presented the “clear signs” to Pharaoh and his chiefs. The description “clear” emphasises that the miracles left no room for honest doubt. The staff devouring the magicians’ serpents and the hand shining with light were not ambiguous phenomena; they were unmistakable demonstrations of supernatural power.

Yet the response of Pharaoh’s court was not belief but denial dressed in a specific accusation: magic. The phrase “conjured magic tricks” is an attempt to categorise divine miracles as a form of advanced sorcery, something that could be learned and performed by skilled magicians. This was a convenient explanation because Egypt was a centre of magic and occult arts, and by labelling Moses’ signs as magic, Pharaoh could call his own magicians to replicate them.

The second part of their response reveals a deeper reason for their rejection: “We have never heard of this in the history of our forefathers. ” The argument from ancestral tradition has been the refuge of rejectors throughout history. They did not evaluate the signs on their own merit but filtered them through the lens of what their forefathers had believed and practiced. Since monotheism, resurrection, and accountability were absent from their inherited polytheistic culture, they dismissed the message without genuine consideration. This verse exposes two common barriers to truth: the tendency to explain away the miraculous by mislabelling it, and the blind adherence to the religion of one’s ancestors. The Meccan polytheists would later use exactly the same argument against the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, claiming that the Quran was magic and that it contradicted the ways of their forefathers.

Thus the verse serves as both historical narrative and a mirror for every generation. It warns believers that when they present the clear signs of revelation and reason, they should not be surprised if the response is not logical refutation but name-calling and appeals to tradition. The strength of the message does not guarantee acceptance by hearts that have been hardened by arrogance and the comfort of inherited falsehood.

=37=

Moses did not engage in a prolonged argument trying to prove his signs were not magic. Instead, he delivered a reply that shifted the focus from the present confrontation to the ultimate outcome, grounding his confidence in divine knowledge. His statement has three interconnected parts. First, “My Lord knows best who has come with true guidance from Him. ” This is a profound expression of reliance on Allah’s perfect knowledge. Moses was not asking Pharaoh to take his word for it; he was entrusting the judgment to the One who knows the reality of every claim. Since Allah knows who is truly guided and who is a deceiver, the truth will eventually be made manifest. This response also contains a dignified refusal to descend into a shouting match. Moses stated his position clearly and left the verification to the course of events that would prove which side was divinely supported. Second, “and will fare best in the end.

” The Arabic phrase points to the final outcome, the ultimate destination in the Hereafter. Moses was reminding his audience that worldly power and apparent victory do not determine who is right. The true measure is who succeeds in the eternal life, who attains safety and bliss when this short life is over. Third, “Indeed, the wrongdoers will never succeed.

” This is a definitive declaration of a divine principle. Those who engage in oppression, who deny clear truth out of arrogance, and who persist in their rejection will not achieve real success, regardless of their temporary dominance. Pharaoh seemed to be at the height of success, but Moses pronounced his failure based on the unchanging laws of Allah. The statement is both a warning and a prophecy.

For the believer, this verse provides a powerful response to situations where truth seems overmatched by falsehood. One does not need to win every argument in the moment. Faith says: my Lord knows the truth of my claim, and the end will reveal who was right.

The verse also fortifies patience, teaching that success is defined by Allah’s standards, not by the applause of the crowd or the approval of the powerful. Moses’ calm, rational reply stands as a model for how to maintain prophetic dignity in the face of arrogant dismissal.

=38=

Moses’ words enraged Pharaoh, and his response marks the absolute peak of arrogance recorded in the Quran. Addressing his inner circle of advisors and nobles, Pharaoh made a claim that moved him from tyranny to open blasphemy: “I know of no other god for you but myself.” This was a direct declaration of self-deification. Pharaoh, who had built a system that presented himself as the divine ruler, now stated it explicitly. He denied any higher power over himself, and he expected his people to accept him as their only object of worship. This single sentence captures the essence of the pharaonic mindset that recurs across history: a ruler who recognises no authority above his own will and demands absolute allegiance. Immediately after this claim, Pharaoh issued a command to Hamân, his chief minister or master builder, which was both a practical project and a public mockery of Moses. He ordered Hamân to bake bricks out of clay, implying the construction of a massive, fired-brick tower, a technology that symbolised power and permanence. The purpose of the tower was stated sarcastically: “so I may look at the God of Moses.” Pharaoh pretended to take Moses’ message seriously enough to investigate, but his real intention was to ridicule. He knew no tower could reach heaven, but by commissioning this project, he aimed to impress his subjects and dismiss Moses’ God as inaccessible and therefore irrelevant.

The final phrase, “although I am sure he is a liar,” reveals that beneath the sarcasm lay a hardened heart. Pharaoh was not genuinely searching; he had already concluded that Moses was a liar, and the tower was a political stunt. This verse exposes how power corrupts the human soul to the point of utter delusion. A mortal being made of flesh, who needed food, drink, and sleep, and who could be felled by a single illness, declared himself god. The command to build the tower also demonstrates the futility of human arrogance when it challenges the divine. Pharaoh’s tower is long gone, his body drowned, yet the name of Moses and the worship of the one true God continue to fill the earth. The verse is an eternal warning against the seduction of power that leads a person to forget their own mortality.

=39=

This verse summarises the moral condition and the false assumption that sealed Pharaoh’s fate. The behaviour of Pharaoh and his military forces was marked by arrogance.

The Arabic word for arrogance conveys a sense of haughty superiority, unjust oppression, and a refusal to submit to truth. They acted as if they were the absolute masters of the land, accountable to no one.

The phrase “with no right” clarifies that their claim to authority was utterly baseless when it transgressed the limits set by the Creator. All legitimate authority is a trust from Allah; Pharaoh had turned it into a tool of self-worship. Their arrogance manifested in the enslavement and slaughter of the Israelites, in the mockery of Moses, and in the rejection of all signs.

The second part of the verse pinpoints the ideological root of this arrogance: “thinking they would never be returned to Us. ” This was their fatal miscalculation. They lived as if life was confined to this world, as if there would be no resurrection, no judgment, and no accountability. Their denial of the Hereafter freed them, in their own minds, to enjoy absolute power without consequences.

This is a recurring theme in the Quran: the rejection of the afterlife is not merely a theological error; it is the primary driver of moral corruption. If a person believes that this life is all there is, then the pursuit of power and pleasure becomes the logical goal, and any moral restraint appears as an unwelcome limitation. Pharaoh’s regime is the archetype of a materialistic, atheistic power structure. For the believers, the verse serves as a diagnosis of tyranny. Whenever a government or an individual behaves with oppressive arrogance and acts as if they are beyond judgment, the underlying disease is a functional denial of returning to the Creator.

The verse also carries a warning: this delusion, no matter how powerful the army that backs it, will be shattered the moment death arrives. Pharaoh thought he would never return to Allah; moments later, as the sea closed over him, his desperate, last-minute declaration of faith would come too late. The arrogance that refused to bow in life was humbled by a force as simple as water.

=40=

The long story of Pharaoh’s tyranny, Moses’ mission, and the stubborn rejection reaches its climactic conclusion in this verse. The divine punishment is described with a powerful brevity. “We seized him” uses a word that implies a sudden, firm, inescapable grip. It was not a gradual decline or a military defeat but an instantaneous destruction that came from Allah directly. The method was drowning: they were cast into the sea. The historical context is well-known: Moses led the Children of Israel out of Egypt, and when Pharaoh and his army pursued them, Allah parted the sea for Moses and his followers to cross safely. When Pharaoh’s forces entered the dry path, the waters returned upon them, and they drowned. The verse does not mention these details here because the focus is on the moral lesson, not the narrative sequence. The phrasing “casting them into the sea” turns the sea itself into an agent of divine wrath. The army that had terrorised the land became food for the waves.

The final sentence shifts to a direct address to the Prophet, and through him to all who hear the Quran: “See then what was the end of the wrongdoers!” This is a call to reflect and observe the pattern of history.

The word “see” is not merely visual but intellectual; it means to contemplate and understand. The “end” refers to the final outcome, the ultimate fate.

The term “wrongdoers” links back to the many earlier descriptions of Pharaoh and his people as oppressors, corruptors, and arrogant rebels. The verse invites the listener to draw a conclusion: this is not an isolated historical incident but an illustration of a universal law. All who follow the path of Pharaoh in their arrogance and oppression will meet a similarly disastrous end, if not in this world then certainly in the Hereafter. For the early Muslims, this verse contained a direct warning to the Quraysh leaders who were oppressing the believers. Their power, wealth, and mockery were not signs of impunity; they were following the same trajectory that had led Pharaoh to the bottom of the sea. The verse is a reassurance that divine justice is not slow in the sense of being absent; it arrives at the precisely appointed time and sweeps away the tyrants, leaving behind only their story as a lesson for all.
