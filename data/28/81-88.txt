=81=

The story of Korah reaches its sudden and terrifying conclusion. The same earth that had yielded its treasures for him, that had been the foundation of his palaces and the source of his agricultural wealth, now became his grave. Allah did not send an army or a storm; He simply commanded the earth to swallow Korah and his dwelling.

The Arabic word used for “swallow” conveys a complete, engulfing seizure. In an instant, the man who had strutted before his people in all his glamour, who had dismissed good advice with arrogant self-confidence, was pulled down into the ground along with his home and all its luxuries. The house that was meant to be a monument to his success became the instrument of his burial. The verse emphasises two layers of his helplessness. First, “there was no one to help him against Allah. ” The entourage of servants, the network of business partners, the admirers who envied his fortune—none of them could lift a finger to prevent the divine decree. All his wealth could not buy a single moment’s delay. The earthly means on which he relied so heavily proved utterly worthless when the command of Allah came. Second, “nor could he even help himself. ” He could not save his own soul. Despite all his boasted knowledge, expertise, and capability, he was completely powerless. His self-attributed intelligence, which he had claimed was the source of his wealth, could not devise an escape. This double negation—no external helper, no internal capacity—highlights the absolute nature of divine power and the utter vulnerability of the arrogant human being. The punishment was perfectly fitting for his crime. Korah’s arrogance was a form of earthly exaltation, a desire to rise above others and to claim a status reserved for the divine. So he was buried in the earth, the lowest of elements, swallowed into humiliation. The story links back to the earlier themes in the surah: Pharaoh, who also claimed divinity, was drowned in the sea; Korah, who claimed self-sufficient knowledge and wealth, was swallowed by the earth. The methods differ, but the principle is the same: those who arrogantly reject divine guidance and oppress others with their blessings face a ruin that neither their power nor their riches can avert. For the believers, the story is a source of both warning and comfort: warning against the love of wealth that leads to pride, and comfort that the ostentatious rich who mock the faith are not beyond the reach of divine justice. Their end may be as sudden and as complete as Korah’s, while the humble who trust in Allah remain secure under His protection.

=82=

The dramatic punishment of Korah produced an immediate transformation in the hearts of those who, just the day before, had envied him. The verse captures their changed perspective with precision. They who had said “If only we could have something like what Korah has been given” now witnessed the terrifying end of that very fortune.

The word “Ah!” expresses a deep realisation, a sudden awakening from the spell of materialism. The sight of Korah and his mansion being swallowed into the earth jolted them into a completely different understanding of wealth and success. They made a confession that is essentially a creedal statement. First, they acknowledged that “it is certainly Allah Who gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills of His servants.

” This is a fundamental Islamic teaching: all provision, whether vast or meager, is in the hand of Allah alone. He grants abundance to some as a test, and He restricts the provision of others as a test. Neither condition is inherently a sign of divine pleasure or wrath; both are trials. This realisation corrected their earlier assumption that Korah’s wealth indicated his special status or fortune. Second, they expressed gratitude and fear: “Had it not been for the grace of Allah, He could have surely caused the earth to swallow us up! ” They recognised that they were not inherently better than Korah; they had simply been spared by Allah’s grace. Their envy had been a form of spiritual sickness, and had Allah not been merciful, they might have shared Korah’s fate.

This is profound humility and repentance. They no longer saw themselves as entitled to criticize divine distribution; instead, they saw their own survival as an undeserved gift.

Finally, they concluded with a general principle: “Oh, indeed! The disbelievers will never succeed.” They now understood that real success is not measured by bank balances or public glamour but by safety from divine punishment and attainment of eternal bliss. Korah’s end was the definitive proof that the disbeliever, no matter how wealthy, is ultimately a failure. The verse is a beautiful depiction of the educative effect of divine punishment on the observers. The believers who might have momentarily faltered in their perspective are brought back to truth not by argument but by witnessing the reality of Allah’s power. It teaches that the believer should never envy the worldly prosperity of those who lack faith, for that prosperity is a fleeting illusion that can turn into a curse at any moment. The only secure position is faith, gratitude, and humility before the Giver.

=83=

After the destruction of Korah and the lessons drawn by the onlookers, the surah now states a clear principle about who will inherit the eternal home of Paradise. The verse begins with “That eternal Home in the Hereafter,” emphasising that Paradise is the real, lasting abode, in contrast to the temporary homes of this world, whether they be Pharaoh’s palaces, Korah’s mansions, or the simple dwellings of the righteous.

The word “eternal” highlights the permanence that makes its value incalculable. Allah declares that He reserves this Home for a specific category of people: “those who seek neither tyranny nor corruption on the earth. ” The Arabic word for tyranny here is ‘uluww, which literally means elevation or highness, and refers to the arrogance, self-exaltation, and desire to dominate and oppress others that characterised Pharaoh and Korah. Those who seek to elevate themselves above others, to impose their will unjustly, and to subjugate the weak are disqualified from Paradise.

The second excluded trait is corruption, which encompasses all forms of spreading mischief—moral decay, economic exploitation, environmental destruction, and social discord. The phrasing “seek neither” indicates that the disqualification is not only for those who commit these acts but also for those who actively desire and strive for them.

The verse then concludes: “The ultimate outcome belongs only to the righteous.” The word “righteous” refers to those who have taqwa, a comprehensive God-consciousness that leads to obeying Allah and avoiding sin. They are the ones who, instead of seeking self-elevation and corruption, humble themselves before Allah, serve His creation, and uphold justice. The ultimate outcome—meaning the final, decisive victory and the eternal reward—is theirs. This verse directly counters the logic of materialism and power-worship. Pharaoh and Korah appeared to have the best outcomes in worldly terms, but their ends were destruction and disgrace. The real, ultimate outcome is reserved for the humble, righteous believers, even if they are poor and oppressed in this life. The verse is a powerful comfort to the persecuted early Muslims and to all believers who suffer at the hands of arrogant oppressors. It assures them that the current state of affairs is not the final verdict.

The final verdict will be pronounced in the Hereafter, and it will be entirely in favour of those who maintained their righteousness, humility, and refusal to engage in the tyranny and corruption that mark the disbelievers.

=84=

This verse elaborates on the principle of divine justice that underpins the distribution of reward and punishment in the Hereafter. It begins with a promise of abundant mercy: “Whoever comes with a good deed will be rewarded with what is better.

” The phrase “comes with” refers to the Day of Judgment, when every person will appear before Allah carrying the record of their deeds. A “good deed” is any act of faith, worship, or righteousness done sincerely for Allah’s sake. The reward is not a mere one-to-one compensation; it is “what is better,” meaning a reward multiplied in quantity and elevated in quality. A single good deed can earn ten times its like, or up to seven hundred times, or even more, according to Allah’s boundless generosity. The reward in Paradise is not just a payment but a gift that far exceeds the merit of the action.

This is the manifestation of Allah’s attribute of grace. The verse then addresses the other side: “And whoever comes with an evil deed, then the evildoers will only be rewarded for what they used to do.” An evil deed includes disbelief, shirk, oppression, and sin.

The word “only” is crucial: the punishment for evil will be strictly equivalent to the deed, with no multiplication or increase. The evildoer will receive exactly what his actions deserve, not more.

This is the manifestation of Allah’s attribute of justice. The verse does not say “whoever comes with an evil deed will be rewarded with worse,” because Allah does not multiply the punishment as He multiplies the reward. This asymmetry between mercy and justice is a fundamental aspect of the Islamic understanding of Allah. His mercy outstrips His wrath, and His generosity exceeds His punishment.

The verse also notes that the recompense is for “what they used to do,” emphasising that the punishment is a direct consequence of their own persistent actions, not an arbitrary infliction. The verse serves as both an encouragement to do good and a warning against evil. It assures the believer that no good deed, however small, will be wasted; it will return multiplied in ways unimaginable. At the same time, it warns that sins have consequences, and those consequences, while justly measured, are severe. The connection to the previous verse is clear: the righteous who inherit the eternal Home are those who bring good deeds, and their reward will be far better than anything they sacrificed in the world.

=85=

This verse is understood by many commentators to be a prophecy and a personal reassurance to the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, during a time of great difficulty. It was revealed during the Meccan period, possibly around the time of the emigration to Medina, or in anticipation of it. The Prophet was being driven out of his beloved hometown, the city where he was born and where the Ka‘bah stood.

The verse begins with a powerful emphasis: “Most certainly, the One Who has ordained the Quran for you... ” The word “ordained” can mean He who imposed the Quran as a duty, or He who revealed it in portions. This reminds the Prophet that the same Lord who gave him the weighty responsibility of conveying the Quran, with all the hardship that entailed, has also decreed a specific outcome. That outcome is expressed as “will ultimately bring you back home to Mecca.” This was a promise that after the exile, the Prophet would return victoriously to Mecca. The prophecy was fulfilled spectacularly when, years later, the Prophet entered Mecca with an army of believers in the peaceful conquest of the city, and he stood once again in the sacred precincts from which he had fled.

This promise served as a deep source of comfort and motivation during the difficult years of exile. It assured the Prophet that his departure was not a permanent defeat but a phase in a divine plan that would culminate in return and triumph.

The verse then instructs the Prophet to say: “My Lord knows best who has come with true guidance and who is clearly astray. ” This echoes Moses’ response to Pharaoh earlier in the surah, creating a deliberate parallel. The Prophet is not to argue endlessly or despair over the rejection of his people. He is to leave the ultimate judgment to Allah, who knows perfectly who is following truth and who is lost.

This statement also functions as a dignified, non-confrontational response to the disbelievers’ accusations. Instead of a direct curse or harsh word, the matter is entrusted to the divine knowledge that will be vindicated on the Day of Judgment. The verse is a timeless lesson for all believers who are forced to leave their homes or face displacement for the sake of their faith. The Lord who called them to faith is also the Lord who will, in His own way and time, restore them to what is good, either in this life or in the eternal home. The return may be physical, as with the Prophet, or it may be the ultimate return to the true home of Paradise, but the promise stands firm.

=86=

This verse addresses the Prophet directly, reminding him of his own humanity and the entirely gratuitous nature of prophethood. He had not been anticipating, seeking, or preparing for the revelation of a scripture. Before the first verses were revealed in the cave of Hira, he was a man living a quiet, reflective life, known for his honesty, but not expecting to become the recipient of a divine book that would change the course of history.

The phrase “You never expected” serves to humble the Prophet and to affirm to any who might doubt his sincerity that he was not a self-appointed prophet pursuing a personal ambition. The Quran was something that came to him unsought, a divine surprise.

The verse then clarifies the source and nature of this unexpected gift: “but it came only as a mercy from your Lord.” The entire Quran, with its guidance, warnings, stories, and laws, is an act of mercy. It was not given because the Prophet or his people inherently deserved it, but because Allah, in His infinite compassion, chose to bestow this final revelation on humanity.

The word “mercy” encapsulates the purpose of the Quran as described throughout the surah: to guide, to warn, to give insight, and to bring people from darkness into light. Following this reminder of the undeserved grace of prophethood, the verse issues a command: “So never side with the disbelievers in their disbelief.” This does not mean to be unkind or unjust to them, but to never support, approve, or compromise on the core matters of faith that they reject. The Prophet’s mission was to stand firmly on the truth, even if the whole world opposed him. Since the revelation came purely from Allah’s mercy, it would be a betrayal of that mercy to water down the message or to align with the disbelievers’ position to gain their approval. The verse is a call to uncompromising integrity in faith. It also provides a psychological defence against the temptation to soften the message when faced with rejection. The believer remembers that the guidance is a mercy from the Lord, not a human product that can be edited for popularity.

This command was especially relevant in Mecca, where the Quraysh offered various compromises—some would worship the Prophet’s God for a year if he would worship their gods for a year. The verse firmly shuts the door on any such negotiation.

For every believer, it is a reminder that faith is a trust of mercy that must be preserved and conveyed with fidelity, without diluting its truths to accommodate the prevailing disbelief.

=87=

The verse continues the theme of steadfastness, presenting it from both a negative and a positive angle. First, the negative instruction: “Do not let them turn you away from the revelations of Allah after they have been sent down to you.” The “them” refers to the disbelievers, who employed various tactics—mockery, argumentation, temptation, and threats—to deter the Prophet from reciting and practicing the Quran.

The phrase “turn you away” implies a persistent, active effort to obstruct. The danger was not that the Prophet would apostatise, which is unthinkable for a prophet, but that the constant pressure might hinder the full proclamation of the message or cause hesitation. The verse commands him to resist all such efforts. The revelations are a precious trust, having been “sent down” from the highest heaven, and no earthly pressure should diminish one’s commitment to them. The positive instruction follows: “Rather, invite all to the Way of your Lord. ” This is the core mission. Instead of being turned away, the Prophet is to actively invite, to call all people to the path that leads to Allah.

The use of “all” emphasises the universal scope of the invitation, transcending tribe, class, and ethnicity. The phrase “the Way of your Lord” refers to Islam, the path of submission to the One God. The command to invite is a clear directive to engage, not to withdraw, despite opposition.

Finally, the verse ends with a strict personal warning: “and never be one of the polytheists.” The Prophet, who was the purest monotheist, is told not to be among those who associate partners with Allah. This instruction, while addressed to the Prophet, is also a warning to the entire community. It indicates that the danger of polytheism is real, and that even the most devout must remain vigilant. The slightest compromise on the core principle of monotheism, whether out of fear or a desire for social harmony, is absolutely forbidden. This verse connects back to the story of Moses: the greatest crime of Pharaoh was his claim to divinity, and the core message of all prophets was the exclusive worship of Allah. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, is to uphold that same message with unwavering determination.

For the believer today, this verse is a call to hold fast to the Quran, to actively share its message in the best manner, and to guard the purity of one’s faith against any form of shirk, whether obvious idolatry or the subtle idolatry of placing other loyalties, desires, or fears above the command of Allah.

=88=

The surah concludes with a powerful and majestic finale that reiterates the fundamental themes of the entire chapter. It begins with a command: “Do not invoke any other god with Allah.” This prohibits directing any form of worship—prayer, supplication, sacrifice, or ultimate reliance—towards anything else. The phrasing “with Allah” indicates that the prohibition includes even mixing other deities with the worship of the One God; it is not enough to worship Allah while also worshipping others. Monotheism must be absolute and exclusive. The command is followed immediately by the foundational creedal declaration: “There is no god worthy of worship except Him.

” This is the heart of Islam, the testimony that negates all false deities and affirms divinity solely for Allah. It is the same truth that Moses, Aaron, and all the prophets proclaimed.

The verse then asserts a profound truth about the nature of existence: “Everything is bound to perish except He Himself.” The Arabic word used for “perish” indicates utter annihilation and disappearance. Every creature, every object, every star, every power, every pleasure, every tyrant, and every treasure—all of it will cease to exist. Pharaoh perished, Korah perished, their wealth and kingdoms vanished. Only Allah, the Eternal, the Ever-Living, will remain. His being is necessary and everlasting, while all else is contingent and transient. This contemplation should instantly humble the human soul. Why would anyone worship or ultimately fear that which is doomed to perish? The only logical recipient of worship is the One who never dies. Next, “All authority belongs to Him.

” The word for authority encompasses judgment, sovereignty, and the final say. In this world, tyrants and kings claim authority, but on the Day their claims perish, the sole authority of Allah will be manifest.

This statement both consoles the oppressed and warns the oppressors. Finally, “And to Him you will all be returned.” This closing phrase ties the surah back to the many scenes of the Day of Judgment described throughout. The return is inevitable, and it is the moment when each soul will face the consequences of whether they lived by the truth declared in this verse. The surah opened with the story of Moses and Pharaoh, illustrating these truths in history, and it concludes by distilling the lessons into this timeless proclamation. It leaves the reader with a clear, unequivocal message: worship Allah alone, recognise your own mortality and His eternity, live in awareness of His authority, and prepare for the return to Him. The entire tafsir of the surah is encapsulated in this final, luminous verse.
