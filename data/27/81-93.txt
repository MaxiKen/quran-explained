=81=

This verse completes a powerful trilogy of metaphors that began with the dead and the deaf, now moving to the blind. The Prophet is told, "Nor can you lead the blind out of their misguidance.

" The image is of a person who has lost their sight and is wandering aimlessly in a state of error, and the guide, no matter how skilled, cannot make them see the path. The blindness here is not physical but spiritual, a blindness of the heart and insight. The disbelievers, immersed in their polytheistic traditions and blinded by arrogance, cannot perceive the light of truth that is shining right in front of them. A blind person might trip over an obstacle, and similarly, these people stumble through life, mistaking idols for gods and worldly gain for ultimate purpose. The Prophet's role is to hold the lamp of the Quran and to call out the directions, but he cannot implant the faculty of sight. That is solely the domain of the Creator.

The verse then makes a decisive and hopeful clarification, stating the only group who can truly benefit: "You can make none hear the truth except those who believe in Our revelations, fully submitting to Allah." The ability to "hear" here means to listen with understanding, acceptance, and obedience. The condition for this hearing is twofold. First, they must "believe in Our revelations," meaning they approach the signs of God with an open heart, ready to accept their divine origin. Second, they are described as "fully submitting to Allah.

" The Arabic word is *muslimīn*, those who surrender their entire being to God. This indicates that the true hearing is not a mere intellectual acknowledgment but a complete, willful submission that shapes one's life. This was a source of immense comfort to the Prophet, as it clarified that the rejection he faced was not a failure of the message's clarity, but a result of the recipients' lack of the essential quality of *īmān* and *islām*. Only the fertile soil of a submitted heart can receive the seed of divine guidance and allow it to grow.

=82=

From the metaphors of spiritual blindness, the surah plunges directly into one of the most striking apocalyptic signs, a concrete event that will occur as the world approaches its end. The verse opens by setting the stage: "And when the decree of the Hour comes to pass against them." This refers to the time when the Day of Judgment is imminent, when the door of repentance is about to be closed, and the divine decision for the end of the world has been made. It is a point of no return. At this critical juncture, Allah will bring forth a "beast from the earth.

" The Arabic word is *dābbah*, a creature that moves and crawls. Its emergence is one of the major signs of the Hour, an extraordinary event that shatters the natural order. The purpose of this beast is to "tell them that the people had no sure faith in Our revelations. " It will speak to humanity in a clear, intelligible language, delivering a damning diagnosis. The beast acts as a divine witness and a marker, publicly declaring that the root cause of humanity's destruction is their lack of *yaqīn*, or certain faith. They had been given signs in the horizons and in their own souls, they had received clear Books and messengers, yet they remained in doubt and conjecture, treating the revelations as fables. When this creature speaks, it will be too late for those who have not already believed. Faith at that moment will not be accepted, because it is no longer belief in the unseen but a forced acknowledgment of a visible, undeniable reality. The beast's testimony is the final, undeniable proof of their rejection. This verse serves as a sobering reminder to the Meccans, who were arrogantly demanding to see a sign before they would believe, that a sign would indeed come, but a sign of closure and judgment, not one of a new opportunity.

=83=

The apocalyptic scene intensifies as the Quran paints a vivid picture of the gathering on the Day of Judgment, a scene meant to strike awe and terror into the hearts of the mockers. The command to "Watch for" or "be mindful of" the Day is an urgent warning. On that Day, Allah will "gather from every faith-community a group of those who denied Our revelations.

" The phrase "faith-community" translates *ummah*, referring to every nation or group to whom a prophet was sent. The selection of "a group" from each community suggests the leaders, the figureheads of disbelief, and the most obstinate deniers who spearheaded the opposition against the prophets. They are the ones who set the trend for their societies, and their punishment will be exemplary. The scene is one of utter subjugation: "and they will be driven in ranks.

" The Arabic word *yūzaʿūn*, also used earlier to describe the perfect organization of Solomon's army, is now used in a context of fearful humiliation. They will not be allowed to disperse or delay; they will be marshaled, pushed, and herded in orderly rows like cattle towards the plain of judgment. The disbelievers who once roamed the earth with arrogance and considered themselves a powerful, free elite are now completely stripped of their autonomy. The disciplined ranks that once signified the glory of Solomon’s divine kingdom now signify the terrifying, inescapable control of divine justice. This verse connects directly back to the earlier stories of Thamūd and the people of Lot. The Quraysh are being told that they will not be exempt from this gathering; they too will be rounded up with the deniers of other nations, their chiefs driven together in disgrace to face the Lord they mocked.

=84=

The journey of the driven ranks culminates in the most terrifying and humiliating of moments: standing in the presence of their Lord. The verse says, "When they finally come before their Lord," indicating the end of their march and the beginning of their direct, personal reckoning. The moment of judgment is not a silent one; it is initiated by a direct question from the Divine. Allah, the All-Knowing, will ask them, though His question is not for His own information but to expose the reality of their state and to render their guilt undeniable before all of creation.

The question has two piercing parts. The first is, "Did you deny My revelations without even comprehending them?" This exposes the intellectual dishonesty and arrogance at the heart of their disbelief. They did not reject the truth after a deep, systematic investigation that found it wanting. They rejected it without understanding, driven by blind prejudice, ancestral tradition, and pride. They mocked what they did not know, passing a verdict on a Book they refused to read with an open heart.

The second part of the question closes off any other excuse: "Or what exactly did you do?" This is an open-ended demand for a justification. If they were not ignorantly rejecting, then what was their actual occupation in life? What did they do with their intellect, their time, and their youth? Did they dedicate their lives to a noble pursuit of truth, or were they simply lost in the pursuit of pleasures and worldly gains? This question leaves them with no escape route. They cannot claim they were sincere seekers, and their lives of heedlessness and sin stand as a testimony against them.

The question is a devastating exposure of a wasted life and an arrogant, baseless denial.

=85=

The divine interrogation of the previous verse leads directly to its inevitable and terrifying conclusion. With their intellectual dishonesty and wasted lives exposed by Allah’s question, the verdict is pronounced. "And the decree of torment will be justified against them.

" The Arabic word for decree, *al-qawl* (literally "the word"), refers to the pre-eternal divine pronouncement that wrongdoers will be punished. It is now actualized and enforced upon them in a specific, personal way. The torment is not an arbitrary infliction but a perfectly "justified" consequence, a legal sentence based on overwhelming evidence. The reason for this justification is explicitly stated: "for their wrongdoing." Their *ẓulm* was the comprehensive injustice they committed, first and foremost against their own souls by denying the truth, and then against the prophets, the believers, and the entire moral order. They placed creation where the Creator should be, and they lived lives of oppression and corruption.

The final, pathetic state of these once-arrogant deniers is captured in the last phrase of the verse: "leaving them speechless." The Arabic word *lā yanṭiqūn* means they are rendered mute, unable to articulate a single word in their defense. Every excuse they had prepared, every sophistry they relied on, every mocking argument they wielded in the world, evaporates in the overwhelming presence of undeniable reality. Their speechlessness is the ultimate form of humiliation and admission of guilt. The tongues that were so fluent in mocking the truth, slandering the messengers, and constructing elaborate justifications for their evil deeds are now completely paralyzed.

This is the end state of the arrogant plotter and the blind follower alike: a total, crushing silence before the majesty and justice of the Lord of all worlds.

=86=

After the terrifying sequence of apocalyptic scenes, the Quran pivots back to the gentle, ever-present signs in the natural world, as if to offer one last, merciful invitation to reflection before it is too late. The verse begins with a rhetorical question filled with wonder and rebuke: "Do they not see that We made the night for them to rest in and the day bright?" The alternation of night and day is the most repetitive, reliable, and undeniable miracle of human existence. The night, with its all-encompassing darkness, is specifically designed as a garment of "rest" (*sukūn*). It is a time when the body's metabolism slows, the mind is calmed, and the soul can retreat from the noise of the world. It is a forced period of daily death and rejuvenation, a mercy that no human can manufacture. Conversely, the day is made "bright" (*mubṣirā*), a light that allows for sight, work, movement, and the pursuit of livelihood.

The word implies not just brightness itself, but a state that enables seeing. This perfect, continuous cycle is not a random cosmic accident; it is a deliberate creation calibrated with profound wisdom for human benefit.

The verse concludes, "Surely in this are signs for those who believe." The word for signs, *āyāt*, connects the physical phenomenon back to the spiritual truths of a Creator, a Planner, and a Resurrector. The One who can bring the brilliant day out of the dark death of night can just as easily bring life out of the darkness of the grave.

However, only "those who believe" can truly see these signs for what they are. A disbeliever sees a sunrise as a mere physical event; a believer sees it as a daily invitation to gratitude and a proof of the resurrection. This verse is a call to escape the spiritual blindness described earlier and to join the ranks of those who can perceive the light.

=87=

The gentle call to reflect on the day and night is immediately followed by another seismic shift back to the terror of the Hour, reminding the listener that the gentle, orderly world they know will be violently disrupted. The command is to "beware of" that Day. The central event is the blowing of "the Trumpet.

" The Arabic word is *ṣūr*, a horn or trumpet into which the angel Isrāfīl will blow. This is the second or third blast, the blast of the resurrection and the gathering. The effect of this cosmic sound is absolute and universal: "all those in the heavens and all those on the earth will be horrified to the point of death.

" The phrase "to the point of death" conveys a terror so extreme and overwhelming that creatures would die of sheer fright if they were not sustained by Allah for the reckoning. The angels, the jinn, and all of humanity, high and low, will be gripped by an indescribable, paralyzing terror.

Yet, even in this moment of universal horror, divine mercy manifests. The verse states, "except those Allah wills to spare." These are the chosen servants of God—according to many commentators, the prophets, the martyrs, and the most righteous believers—who will be granted a state of security and tranquility amidst the panic. Their hearts will be at peace because of their firm connection to their Lord. The scene then shifts to the arrival: "And all will come before Him, fully humbled." The arrogant kings, the mocking chieftains, the wealthy oppressors—all will approach the court of the Almighty in a state of complete, abject humility. The posture that they refused to adopt in the world, the submission of the heart and body to the One God, will be forced upon them by the sheer, undeniable reality of the Day. There will be no proud strides, only the bowed, fearful steps of the humbled.

=88=

From the terror of the Trumpet, the Quran zooms in on one of the most solid and permanent symbols of the earth: the mountains. The verse describes a scene of total cosmic upheaval that will occur on the Day of Judgment. It begins, "Now you see the mountains, thinking they are firmly fixed." In this world, mountains are the ultimate symbol of stability, rooted deep into the earth's crust, immovable and ancient. Human psychology relies on their permanence as a metaphor for strength and endurance.

However, on that Day, this perception will be shattered. The verse reveals their true state on the Day of Judgment: "but they are travelling just like clouds." The massive, seemingly eternal mountains will be uprooted from their foundations and will fly through the air, moving swiftly and lightly like clouds driven by the wind.

This is the moment when the earth is shaken to its very core, pulverized, and transformed into a flat plain. The awe-inspiring image completely dismantles the human illusion of a permanent, material world.

The verse then immediately anchors this terrifying vision in divine wisdom, stating, "That is the design of Allah, Who has perfected everything." Even this cataclysm is not chaos; it is the precise, perfect, and purposeful act of the same Creator who perfected the formation of the mountains in the first place. The destruction of the old world is the necessary, perfectly designed prelude to the creation of the new, eternal world of the Hereafter.

The verse concludes with a sobering reminder of divine accountability: "Surely He is All-Aware of what you do." The perfection of His creation and His destruction is mirrored by the perfection of His knowledge and His records, guaranteeing that every deed done in the shadow of those once-steady mountains is accounted for.

=89=

After the description of the apocalyptic terror, the Quran immediately provides the criteria for salvation and the nature of divine justice, offering a clear path to safety. The verse begins with the principle of generous reward: "Whoever comes with a good deed will be rewarded with what is better." The "good deed" (*ḥasanah*) is comprehensive, encompassing the sincere testimony of faith in the oneness of Allah, as well as all righteous actions, pure intentions, and acts of worship. The core of this good deed is monotheism and the obedience that flows from it. The reward for this deed is described not as an equal exchange, but as something "better" (*khayrun minhā*). This reflects the infinite generosity of Allah. A single good deed is multiplied by ten, or seven hundred, or many times more, and it is refined and purified in the Hereafter into a bliss that far surpasses any worldly reward or even the mere moral satisfaction of having done good. This multiplication is an act of pure divine grace, as no human act can truly merit the eternal rewards of Paradise.

The second part of the verse describes the specific, immediate benefit on the Day of Judgment: "and they will be secure from the horror on that Day." The word "horror" (*fazaʿ*) refers to the overwhelming terror and panic that grips all of creation as described in the previous verses. The one who comes with sincere faith and good deeds will be placed in a state of divine protection, a zone of spiritual tranquility. While others are drowning in fear, these believers will be greeted with peace by the angels.

This promise of security provides immense comfort and motivation; it transforms the Day of Judgment from a day of pure dread for the believer into a day of hope, where the mercy they trusted in will become their tangible shield.

=90=

The balance of divine justice requires that the standard of generous reward be matched with the standard of just retribution. This verse presents the terrifying fate of the opposite group. It states, "And whoever comes with an evil deed will be hurled face-first into the Fire." The "evil deed" (*sayyiʾah*) is primarily the sin of *shirk*, associating partners with Allah, and it also encompasses the major sins and acts of oppression for which a person has not repented. The punishment is described with a vivid, humiliating detail: they will be "hurled face-first" (*kubbat wujūhuhum*). Being thrown onto one's face is a gesture of utter contempt, disgrace, and helplessness. In the world, these were the arrogant ones who walked with their heads held high in pride against God. Now, the noblest part of their body, their face, is the first to enter the scorching fire, a direct punishment for their refusal to bow their faces in prostration to Allah in their earthly life.

The verse then concludes with a powerful rhetorical question that summarizes the entire principle of divine justice: "Are you rewarded except for what you used to do?" This is a statement of absolute, undeniable fairness. No soul will be punished for a sin it did not commit, nor will any good deed be overlooked. The punishment is the direct, causal consequence of one's own actions. This question silences any potential protest from the wrongdoers. They cannot claim to be victims of an arbitrary destiny; they are simply reaping the harvest of the seeds they themselves planted. This verse, paired with the previous one, lays out the two destinations with stark clarity, leaving the listener with the freedom and the responsibility to choose their path.

=91=

The surah now moves toward its powerful conclusion, addressing the Prophet directly and, through him, defining the pure essence of his mission in the face of a polytheistic society. The command is "Say," a declaration meant to be public and unequivocal.

The first part of his proclamation is, "I have only been commanded to worship the Lord of this city of Mecca, Who has made it sacred." This is a direct challenge to the Quraysh. They considered themselves the custodians of the Sacred House, the protectors of the sanctuary.

Yet, while they honored the city, they corrupted its purpose by filling it with idols. The Prophet, an insider from their own tribe, declares that his worship is exclusively for the true Lord of that very city, the One who established its sanctity long before their idols were erected. By calling it "this city," he personalizes the message, grounding the universal call to monotheism in their immediate, local reality. He invokes the sacredness of Mecca, a status they all acknowledged, and traces it back to its rightful, original source—Allah. He then expands his declaration of monotheism to encompass all of creation: "and to Him belongs everything." The Lord of Mecca is not a local deity; He is the Owner and Sovereign of the entire universe.

Finally, he defines his entire life's orientation: "And I am commanded to be one of those who fully submit to Him. " The word *muslimīn* is the core identity of a follower of all prophets. His mission is not to create a new, tribal religion, but to restore the pure, primordial state of submission to the One God, a state that is the logical consequence of recognizing His ownership and His sanctity.

=92=

The Prophet’s declaration of his divine mandate continues and is now linked directly to the revelation he is delivering. The command is given: "and to recite the Quran." His role is not merely to have a private faith, but to publicly transmit the words of God. The recitation of the Quran is his active duty, the tool by which he clarifies the truth and calls humanity to their Lord.

The verse then immediately establishes the consequences of this recitation by laying out the principle of personal responsibility with absolute clarity. "Then whoever chooses to be guided, it is only for their own good." The Arabic word for "chooses to be guided" (*ihtadā*) implies a deliberate, willful action of seeking and accepting guidance. The benefit of this choice is entirely self-contained; it does not add anything to the Prophet or to Allah. It is the soul's own liberation and its ticket to eternal bliss. Conversely, "whoever chooses to stray, say, O Prophet, 'I am only a warner.'" The act of straying (*ḍalla*) is also a choice, and its harm is confined to the one who strays. The Prophet is instructed to respond to their misguidance not with frustration or a sense of failure, but with a clear, dignified statement of his limited role.

The term "warner" (*nadhīr*) defines the job description of a prophet: to deliver the warning, to make the truth crystal clear, and to alert people to the danger of a path that leads to destruction. The success or failure of the message in terms of people's conversion is not his burden. This verse is a profound lesson in emotional detachment for anyone who calls others to the truth. We deliver the message to the best of our ability, and then we entrust the hearts of the listeners to Allah, knowing that each soul is ultimately responsible for its own choice.

=93=

The surah concludes with a final, comprehensive instruction to the Prophet, a statement that blends gratitude, a powerful prophecy, and a concluding warning. The command is: "And say, 'All praise is for Allah!'" This is the ultimate declaration of *ḥamd*, a praise that acknowledges that all perfection, beauty, and goodness belong to God, and that He is worthy of this praise regardless of whether humanity acknowledges it or not. This praise is the believer's shield and his final word, an expression of contentment with divine decree. Then follows a statement that is both a promise and a threat: "He will show you His signs, and you will recognize them. " The signs (*āyāt*) here can be understood on multiple levels.

For the disbelievers, it is a prophecy that they will see the truth of the Quran's message manifest in the world—through the victory of the believers, the unfolding of historical events, the discovery of scientific facts, and ultimately, through the unmistakable signs of their own death and the approaching Hour. The phrase "and you will recognize them" is a chilling assurance that the truth they denied will become so undeniable that they will remember having been warned about it, but that recognition will be of no avail when it comes too late. For the believers, it is a promise that they will see the fulfillment of Allah’s promises and the proofs of His truth, which will increase them in faith and certainty.

The surah then closes with the perfect attribute: "And your Lord is never unaware of what you do." The Arabic *bi-ghāfilin* negates any notion of divine forgetfulness or neglect. Every action, public and secret, is within His constant, perfect awareness. This final sentence leaves the listener with a profound sense of accountability and comfort, a fitting conclusion to a surah that has woven together stories, arguments, and warnings into a single, magnificent tapestry of divine guidance.
