=41=

With the Queen’s throne having been miraculously transported to his palace in the blink of an eye, Solomon now orchestrates a further, more subtle test of her intellect and spiritual readiness. He instructs his attendants, “Disguise her throne for her.

” The Arabic verb *nakkirū* means to alter, transform, or make something unrecognizable. The magnificent throne, the very symbol of her earthly power and identity, was to be modified—perhaps by changing some of its ornamental features, covering certain parts, or placing it in an unfamiliar context—so that its appearance would not be immediately obvious. This was not a childish game, but a profound prophetic exercise. Solomon’s stated purpose reveals a careful and wise methodology: “so we may see whether she will recognize it or she will not be able to.” He was testing the sharpness of her perception and the depth of her intuition. A person of keen intelligence and fitrah, a pure primordial nature, would be able to see through superficial disguises and identify the essence of the object. More importantly, this test had a spiritual dimension. The throne had just been moved by a miracle tied to divine knowledge. Her ability to recognize it despite the disguise would signal an openness to perceiving the signs of God, a mind not easily fooled by outward alterations, but one that could grasp deeper truths. It was a way to gauge whether her heart was truly ready to move beyond the outward appearances of her old religion and recognize the reality of divine power. This test also served as a gentle introduction to the greater marvel she was about to witness in the crystal palace. Solomon was, in his wisdom, leading her step by step, from one sign to a greater one, each encounter dismantling a layer of her worldly attachment and preparing her for the ultimate recognition of her Lord.

=42=

The Queen of Sheba arrives at Solomon’s court, and the test of the disguised throne is immediately put into motion. The question is posed to her, likely by Solomon or his courtiers: “Is your throne like this?” The phrasing is ambiguous and clever; it does not ask “Is this your throne?” but rather whether the object before her resembles her own. Her response is a masterpiece of diplomatic and spiritual intelligence. She replies, “It looks to be the same.

” The Arabic expression *kaʾannahu huwa* conveys a strong resemblance, an almost certain identification, but it leaves a sliver of wonder. She recognized her throne despite the alterations, proving the sharpness of her perception that Solomon was testing.

However, her next words are far more significant. She declares, “We have already received knowledge before this miracle, and have submitted to Allah.

” This is a crucial clarification. She is stating that her submission to the truth was not a sudden conversion triggered solely by this astonishing display of her throne’s teleportation. Rather, her journey of faith had already begun. The “knowledge” refers to the accumulated signs: the noble letter beginning with the name of the Most Compassionate, the report of her envoys who witnessed Solomon’s rejection of worldly gifts and his unshakeable prophetic confidence, and the rational realization that his power was not that of an ordinary king. These earlier evidences had already convinced her mind and touched her heart, leading her to an internal state of Islam. The miracle of the throne, therefore, served as a confirmation and a public manifestation of what her soul had already accepted. Her statement demonstrates that true guidance often begins internally, with an honest recognition of the truth, before it is fully declared and lived outwardly.

=43=

This verse provides a crucial theological commentary on the Queen’s previous spiritual condition, explaining the barrier that had kept her from the truth for so long. The phrase “But she had been hindered” translates the Arabic *wa ṣaddahā*, which means she was turned away, blocked, or prevented. The agent of this hindrance was “what she used to worship instead of Allah”—the sun, and the entire religious and cultural system built around it.

This is a profound insight into the spiritual psychology of false religion. The object of worship itself, when it is a created thing, becomes a veil over the heart. A person’s deep emotional, social, and ancestral attachment to their inherited deities acts as a powerful obstruction, making it immensely difficult to accept pure monotheism even when the evidence for it is clear. The sun-worship of her people was not just a set of rituals; it was the foundation of their identity, their kingdom’s mythology, and their justification for power.

The verse emphasizes that this hindrance was not due to a lack of intelligence on her part, but due to the deeply rooted nature of her polytheistic upbringing: “for she was indeed from a disbelieving people. ” This statement shows the powerful influence of environment and society. She was not an isolated individual; she was a product and a leader of a disbelieving civilization. Her earlier statement that she had “already received knowledge” and submitted indicates that she eventually broke free from this hindrance. The verse, therefore, describes her past state, highlighting the immense spiritual struggle she overcame. It serves as an encouragement: even one deeply entrenched in a false tradition, surrounded by the pressures of a disbelieving elite, can, with Allah’s guidance, recognize the truth and transcend the barriers of her old life. The stage is now perfectly set for the final, climatic test that will externalize her internal break from that past.

=44=

The final and most profound sign for the Queen of Sheba is now presented, one that directly addresses her, leads to a moment of physical exposure, and culminates in her complete, public declaration of faith. She is invited to “Enter the palace,” a grand structure whose floor, or sarḥ, was made of perfectly clear, smooth crystal or glass, beneath which water flowed. The architectural marvel created a perfect illusion. When she saw the hall, “she thought it was a body of water,” and reacting instinctively to avoid wetting her garments, “she bared her legs” by lifting her dress. This moment is deeply symbolic. The transparent floor represents the reality of Solomon’s kingdom and the truth of his prophethood: what appeared to be an obstacle or a different element was, in fact, a solid path of clarity and light. Her act of uncovering her legs, though done in practical self-preservation, was a physical exposure that mirrored a deeper spiritual unveiling. All pretenses of royal decorum were momentarily suspended, reflecting the stripping away of her old identity. Solomon immediately clarifies the illusion with gentle dignity: “It is just a palace paved with crystal.” There is no mockery, only a revelation of the truth behind the appearance. This final sign shatters any remaining veil. Her response is immediate, comprehensive, and deeply personal. She declares, “My Lord! I have certainly wronged my soul.

” This is the essence of sincere repentance: an admission of personal injustice, not an excuse about her upbringing or environment. Her use of “My Lord” is intimate and direct. She then completes her submission: “Now I fully submit myself along with Solomon to Allah, the Lord of all worlds.” Her Islam is not a political capitulation to Solomon, but a joint submission alongside him to the same universal Lord. Her journey from a sun-worshipping queen to a believer in the Lord of the worlds is now complete, a testament to the power of divine signs when met with a sincere and intelligent heart.

=45=

From the inspiring story of a queen who embraced faith, the surah now turns to cautionary tales of nations who rejected it, beginning with the ancient people of Thamūd. This serves as a direct warning to the Meccan pagans who were repeating the same patterns of denial.

The verse begins with the familiar Quranic pattern of mercy: “And We certainly sent to the people of Thamûd their brother Ṣâliḥ.” The use of “their brother” is profoundly important. It emphasizes that Prophet Ṣâliḥ, peace be upon him, was not a foreigner or a conqueror, but one of their own kin, from their own tribe and lineage. They knew his character, his honesty, and his lineage from before his prophethood. This established trust, yet it also became a source of their arrogance, as they could not accept that an ordinary man from among them could be a chosen messenger of God. His core message was identical to all prophets: “Worship Allah. ” It was a pure call to monotheism, to abandon their false deities and direct all worship to the One Creator. The reaction of Thamūd was not one of passive disbelief, but a sharp and active polarization. The verse states, “but they suddenly split into two opposing groups.

” The Arabic word *fārijūn* or the context indicates they became two hostile factions. One group consisted of the believers, those who accepted Ṣâliḥ’s call, often the weak and downtrodden of society. The other was the dominant, arrogant majority of disbelievers who rejected him vehemently. This split represents a universal social phenomenon that occurs when truth challenges a corrupt society: it forces a clear division. The truth cannot coexist with falsehood in peaceful ambiguity; it draws a line, and people must choose which side they are on. This polarization became the seed for the intense conflict and the eventual plot against the prophet and his followers, mirroring the division the Prophet Muhammad was facing in Mecca.

=46=

Having witnessed his people split into two hostile factions, Prophet Ṣâliḥ directs his compassionate and urgent plea specifically toward the disbelieving group. His address, “O my people!” is filled with a painful sense of belonging and concern. He is still their brother, and he desperately wants their salvation. His question is a sharp, logical challenge to their self-destructive behavior: “Why do you seek to hasten the torment rather than grace? ” The disbelievers of Thamūd, like the Meccan pagans, used to mockingly challenge their prophet to bring down the punishment he kept warning them about. They would say, “If you are truthful, then bring us the torment!” This was an arrogant and foolish way of demanding the very thing that would destroy them. Ṣâliḥ reframes their entire perspective. They were actively chasing punishment with their mockery and disbelief, when the path of “grace”—Allah’s mercy, forgiveness, and the blessings of a righteous life—was wide open before them. He presents them with the clear, alternative course of action that could avert the disaster: “If only you sought Allah’s forgiveness so you may be shown mercy!” The connection between seeking forgiveness (*istighfār*) and receiving mercy is a fundamental Quranic principle. Their problem was sin and transgression; the direct, logical, and divinely-promised solution was to turn to the Forgiver. Had they humbled themselves, admitted their error, and sincerely sought pardon, the threatened punishment would have been lifted and replaced with the encompassing, nurturing mercy of Allah. The verse captures the essence of prophetic warning: it is not a desire for people to suffer, but an impassioned effort to redirect them from the path of destruction to the path of mercy. The tragedy is that their arrogance prevented them from uttering the simple words of repentance that would have saved them.

=47=

Faced with Ṣâliḥ’s compassionate call to forgiveness and mercy, the disbelievers of Thamūd respond not with logical argument, but with a deeply ingrained pagan superstition. They say, “You and your followers are a bad omen for us.

” The Arabic phrase *iṭṭayyarnā bika* comes from the ancient practice of *ṭiyarah*, or augury, where people would interpret the flight of birds or other random events as signs of good or bad fortune. Here, they personalize this superstition, blaming Ṣâliḥ and the believers for any misfortune, drought, or hardship that befell their society.

This is a classic psychological defense mechanism of the arrogant wrongdoer. Instead of seeing their own sins and moral corruption as the cause of divine displeasure and worldly difficulties, they scapegoat the righteous minority. They inverted reality, considering the callers to purity and reformation as a source of pollution and bad luck. Ṣâliḥ’s response dismantles their superstition with a powerful theological correction. He says, “Your omens are destined by Allah. ” Everything that occurs—good or bad, fortune or misfortune—comes from the decree of Allah, not from the presence of a human being. There is no such thing as a contagious bad omen. The hardships they faced were tests and consequences from their Lord, not a magical affliction caused by him. He then delivers a piercing diagnosis of their actual situation: “In fact, you are only a people being tested. ” Their wealth, their power, their very lives, and now the arrival of a prophet with a clear message—all of it was a divine trial to see if they would be grateful, humble, and believing, or arrogant, superstitious, and rejecting. By clinging to their omens, they were failing the test spectacularly, refusing to read the signs of God correctly and instead inventing a false narrative of bad luck that justified their hostility toward the truth.

=48=

The Quran now zooms in on the specific human agents of evil within the Thamūd society, revealing the organized, internal source of their moral decay. “And there were in the city nine elite men.

” The Arabic word *raht* signifies a group of individuals, usually fewer than ten, bound by a common bond. These were not street criminals, but the social, political, and economic elite of Thamūd, the influential chieftains whose word was law. Their defining characteristic is explicitly stated: they “spread corruption in the land, never doing what is right.” The corruption (*fasād*) they spread was comprehensive—moral, spiritual, social, and economic. As the leaders, they set the tone for the entire society, institutionalizing oppression, idolatry, and injustice.

The phrase “never doing what is right” (*lā yuṣliḥūn*) signifies an absolute and consistent refusal to engage in any act of reform or righteousness. Their entire mode of operation was to oppose good and promote evil. This verse exposes a critical principle: the corruption of a nation often stems from a small, organized, and powerful clique at the top. Their influence poisons the entire body of society. The specific number “nine” is mentioned, and their story serves as a timeless warning. When the elite of a society abandon the worship of God and dedicate themselves to spreading corruption, they inevitably see the messengers of truth as a direct threat to their power structure, leading them to plot the most heinous of crimes. These nine men are the human antithesis of the nine clear signs given to Moses. While Moses’ nine signs were revelations of divine truth and mercy, the nine men of Thamūd represent the concentration of human wickedness conspiring to extinguish the light of God’s message and murder the righteous.

=49=

The corruption of the nine elite men reaches its climax as they conspire to carry out the ultimate crime: the assassination of the Prophet of God and his entire household. Their plot is described with chilling detail, revealing the depths of their depravity and the perversion of their religious sense. They begin their pact with a solemn oath: “Let us swear by Allah.

” This is staggering in its hypocrisy. These men were idolaters and corruptors, yet they invoke the name of Allah—the Supreme Being they acknowledged as the Creator—to sanctify their most heinous act. This demonstrates that a corrupt society does not necessarily descend into atheism; rather, it manipulates the vestiges of religion to justify its evil. Their specific plan was to “take him and his family down by night,” meaning to launch a surprise, coordinated night attack to murder Ṣâliḥ, peace be upon him, and all members of his household, leaving no survivors. Night-time was chosen for its cover of secrecy. They then prepared a meticulous alibi to shield themselves from justice and blood-money claims: “Then we will certainly say to his closest heirs, ‘We did not witness the murder of his family. We are definitely telling the truth.’” They pre-emptively planned a public denial of any knowledge, swearing to their innocence with emphatic language. The plan appears watertight from a worldly perspective: a secret killing and a unified front of denial from the most powerful men in the city. This conspiracy mirrors the plot of the Quraysh in Mecca, who once gathered to assassinate the Prophet Muhammad in his sleep. The verse exposes the dark reality that when a people’s hearts are sealed, they do not merely passively disbelieve; they actively conspire to silence the voice of truth, thinking they can outmaneuver the divine plan.

=50=

This short but immensely powerful verse delivers the divine response to the intricate conspiracy of the nine elite men. The phrase “And so they made a plan” summarizes their entire scheme—the secret oath, the choice of night, the prepared alibi. From a worldly perspective, their plot was cunning, comprehensive, and seemingly foolproof.

However, the verse then introduces the overriding reality with a majestic and terrifying parallelism: “but We too made a plan. ” Allah, the Creator and Master of all, attributes the term *makr*, or planning, to Himself in a way that befits His majesty. This divine “plan” is the counter-scheme, the hidden, inescapable response that nullifies the evil plots of the wrongdoers and turns their own schemes against them. While their plan was limited by human ignorance and a narrow timeframe, Allah’s plan was based on absolute knowledge, perfect wisdom, and complete power. The crucial and chilling detail is the phrase “while they were unaware. ” The nine men, in their arrogance, had no perception whatsoever of the divine trap that was closing in on them. They thought they were the masters of the situation, working in the shadows, but the light of divine knowledge was illuminating their every move. They were unaware that their very swearing by Allah was being recorded, that their night-time plan was seen, and that their destruction was already decreed. This verse is a profound source of comfort for the believers of all times. It teaches that the apparent power and intricate conspiracies of the corrupt elite are not beyond the control of the Almighty. He allows them to weave their plots, giving them the rope of respite, only for His counter-plan to manifest at the precise, decreed moment, shattering their might in a way they could never have anticipated.

=51=

After building the suspense of the divine counter-plan, this verse delivers the shocking and decisive outcome. The command “See then” (*fa-nẓur*) is a powerful rhetorical device, inviting the Prophet, his followers, and every subsequent listener to reflect and take a profound lesson from this historical event.

The question is posed: “what the consequences of their plan were.” The Arabic word for consequence is *ʿāqibah*, which refers to the final end, the ultimate outcome of a course of action. The nine men had plotted to secretly murder Ṣâliḥ, peace be upon him, and his family, expecting a clean and successful operation. But the consequence of their plot was not what they had planned. Instead, Allah reveals: “We utterly destroyed them and their people all together.” The divine punishment did not just target the nine conspirators individually; it engulfed the entire disbelieving community.

The word “utterly destroyed” (*dammarnā*) implies a total and comprehensive annihilation, leaving nothing standing. The phrase “and their people all together” shows that the destruction was collective, because the society as a whole was complicit in its rejection of the truth and in its support—actively or passively—for its corrupt elite. The meticulous plan of the powerful nine, designed to eliminate the prophet and preserve their society, instead became the direct catalyst for the community’s complete eradication.

This is the perfect manifestation of the divine plan. Their scheme to kill God’s messenger was the final act of transgression that sealed their collective fate. For the Meccans, this was an unmistakable warning. The ruins of the Thamūd dwellings lay along their caravan route to Syria, a visible, physical testimony to the consequences of plotting against a prophet and rejecting the call to worship Allah alone.

=52=

Following the declaration of their total destruction, the Quran draws the listener’s attention to the physical, enduring evidence of that divine punishment. The verse begins, “So their homes are there,” indicating that the dwellings of the Thamūd—carved spectacularly into the mountains of the region of Al-Ḥijr in northwestern Arabia—still stood as visible ruins. These were not mere huts but magnificent, advanced architectural achievements that spoke of their civilization’s power and skill.

Yet, they were now “completely ruined,” utterly empty and desolate, haunted by the memory of a divine cataclysm. The Arabic word *khāwiyah* conveys an image of something hollowed out, empty, and collapsed. The verse explicitly states the cause of this ruin: “because of their wrongdoing.” Their physical annihilation was not a random natural disaster; it was a direct and just consequence of their moral and spiritual corruption, their oppression, and their rejection of their prophet. The grand houses, in which they felt so secure and for which they were so arrogant, became their tombs.

The verse then highlights a crucial point: “Surely in this is a lesson for people of knowledge.” The Arabic word for lesson is *āyah*, the same word for a divine sign or a miraculous verse. The ruins themselves are a sign, a tangible piece of evidence of a divine law in action. The lesson, however, is only truly perceived by “people of knowledge”—those who possess a knowledge that connects the physical reality to its spiritual and moral cause. A superficial observer might see mere archaeological ruins, but a person of knowledge sees the fingerprints of divine justice, the inevitable end of a society that defies its Creator. This verse invites believers to travel, observe, and engage their intellects to decode the signs of God in history, transforming the landscape of the earth into a vast, open-air lesson book of spiritual truths.

=53=

The picture of the ruined homes is immediately followed by a message of hope and a clear statement of divine justice, ensuring that the listener does not imagine a blanket, indiscriminate punishment. The verse states, “And We delivered those who were faithful and were mindful of Allah.” Amidst the total destruction that engulfed the disbelieving people of Thamūd, Prophet Ṣâliḥ, peace be upon him, and the believers who followed him were saved.

The Arabic word for delivered is *anjaynā*, which carries the meaning of rescuing from the midst of peril, pulling someone out to safety. This deliverance was physical and immediate, as the divine punishment meticulously avoided the righteous while annihilating the wicked. The two qualities that marked the saved are profound and interconnected.

The first is that they “were faithful” (*āmanū*). They had genuine, internal faith, accepting the truth of Ṣâliḥ’s message and believing in the oneness and power of Allah.

The second is that they “were mindful of Allah” (*kānū yattaqūn*). The concept of *taqwā* goes beyond mere belief; it is a constant, active consciousness of God that manifests in righteous deeds and a careful avoidance of His displeasure. Their faith was not a passive intellectual assent but a living, breathing state of God-consciousness that governed their actions.

This verse establishes the immutable divine law: when a collective punishment descends, it spares those who have maintained their faith and taqwā. It is a direct refutation of the superstitious notion held by the disbelievers of Thamūd that Ṣâliḥ and his followers were a bad omen. On the contrary, their very presence was a source of deliverance. The believer is never collateral damage in the divine plan. This provides immense comfort to every minority of believers living in a corrupt society, assuring them that their commitment to faith and God-consciousness is their ultimate guarantee of safety and divine protection, both in this world and the next.

=54=

The surah continues its series of prophetic warnings by recounting the story of Lot (Lūṭ), peace be upon him, a nephew of Abraham who was sent to the people of Sodom. The audience is instructed to “remember” Lot, drawing another parallel to the Prophet Muhammad’s situation in Mecca and a lesson for all humanity. The verse immediately highlights the direct, confrontational nature of his prophetic rebuke. Lot did not speak in whispers or hint at the sin; he openly and courageously challenged his people: “Do you commit that shameful deed? ” The Arabic word for “shameful deed” is *al-fāḥishah*, a term that denotes an obscenity of the highest order, a sin so vile that its repulsiveness is recognized by sound human nature. The specific “shameful deed” was the act of men engaging in sexual relations with men, a practice that had become endemic in their society. What made their sin even more heinous is captured in the clarifying phrase “while you can see one another.” This means they committed this obscenity openly, in public gatherings, with no sense of shame, privacy, or moral restraint. It was not a hidden vice but a brazenly celebrated part of their public culture. This open display of corruption represented the complete inversion of the natural moral order, a society that had lost all sense of decency. Lot’s question is a powerful blend of astonishment and condemnation. He appeals to their innate human conscience, to the basic sense of shame that even the most corrupt person retains deep within. By highlighting that they do this while seeing each other, he underscores that the sin is compounded by its flagrancy, a collective participation in evil that erodes the very foundation of a healthy society. This verse stands as a timeless warning against the normalization and public celebration of what the Creator has decreed to be an abomination.

=55=

Lot, peace be upon him, continues his direct and uncompromising confrontation with his people, moving from the general description of their “shameful deed” to its precise nature and its root cause. His question, “Do you really lust after men instead of women?” strips away any euphemism or cultural justification they might have invented for their behavior.

The Arabic word *shahwah* denotes intense, passionate desire and lust. The construction of his question is a powerful moral argument from design and natural disposition (*fiṭrah*). He is pointing out that they have abandoned the natural, divinely-ordained relationship designed for intimacy, procreation, and the continuation of the human species, turning instead to a desire that is a deviation from the purpose for which their own nature was created.

The phrase “instead of women” (*min dūni an-nisāʾ*) emphasizes the act of bypassing and abandoning what is natural and pure. It’s an act of deliberate transgression, not a mere alternative. Lot then delivers the ultimate diagnosis of their condition, which is a profound insight into the psychology of sin: “In fact, you are only a people acting ignorantly. ” The word for “acting ignorantly” is *tajhalūn*, from *jahl*, which in the Quranic context often refers not to a lack of intelligence, but to a state of reckless foolishness, an absence of moral and spiritual wisdom, and a willful disregard for the consequences of one’s actions. It is the ignorance of a person who knows deep down that something is harmful but does it anyway. Their problem wasn’t a lack of information; it was that they were acting against their own sound nature. This profound judgment explains that all sin is, at its core, a form of ignorance. A person sins because their reason has been clouded by passion and their moral compass has been broken. Lot’s words stand as an eternal reminder that the call to righteousness is a call to true knowledge and wisdom, while deviance, in all its forms, is an act of self-destructive foolishness.

=56=

After the clear, logical, and powerful rebuke from their prophet Lot, the response of the people of Sodom reveals the depth of their moral bankruptcy. They did not offer a single argument to defend their actions, nor did they engage with his reasoning. The Quran states, “But his people’s only response was to say...” This highlights their inability to counter the truth intellectually. Their only recourse was raw force and social ostracism. Their statement, “Expel Lot’s followers from your land!” is a direct incitement to persecution. They called for the expulsion of the entire righteous minority from their towns and territories. The charge they brought against the believers is one of the greatest ironies in the Quran. They said, “They are a people who wish to remain chaste!

” The Arabic word is *yataṭahharūn*, which comes from *ṭahārah*, meaning purity, cleanliness, and chastity. The believers’ very virtue—their refusal to participate in the obscene practices of the society—was, in the twisted eyes of the corrupt majority, a crime worthy of exile.

This is the complete inversion of moral values that occurs when a society fully gives itself over to vice. Good is seen as evil, and evil is seen as good. The pure are mocked as being impure or backward, and the corrupt are celebrated as progressive and liberated. This verse describes a chilling psychological phenomenon: the collective conscience of a decadent society becomes so offended by the silent witness of the righteous that it cannot tolerate their presence. The believers, by their very existence and their adherence to purity, were a living condemnation of the lifestyle of the majority.

Therefore, the solution in the eyes of the corrupt was not to reform themselves, but to silence and banish the voice of purity. This response echoes the Quraysh’s pressure on the Prophet Muhammad’s early followers, whom they persecuted and drove from their homes simply for their belief in one God and their adherence to moral decency.

=57=

The divine narrative now reaches the moment of decisive judgment and deliverance, echoing the pattern already established with the people of Thamūd. The verse begins, “So We delivered him and his family,” referring to Prophet Lot, peace be upon him, and the believing members of his household. The angels who had come to Abraham with the news of the impending punishment of Sodom were then sent to Lot, and they instructed him to leave the city by night with his family and not to look back. Lot’s deliverance was a direct, miraculous act of divine mercy, plucking the righteous out of the midst of a society that was about to be obliterated.

However, this deliverance is immediately qualified by a stark and tragic exception: “except his wife.” Lot’s wife is a figure of immense warning in the Quran. Though she was physically in the household of a prophet, lived under his roof, and witnessed his character, her heart was not with him. She had secretly sympathized with the sinful people of Sodom, shared their corrupt values, and acted as an informant for them, revealing the arrival of the angelic guests. Her outward association with a prophet was of no avail because her inner being was aligned with disbelief and betrayal. The verse explains this exception with the divine decree: “We had destined her to be one of the doomed.

” The Arabic *qaddarnā* signifies a prior divine determination based on Allah’s all-encompassing knowledge. It does not mean she was forced to be disbelieving against her will. Rather, Allah knew from eternity the choice she would freely make, and thus her fate was decreed in accordance with His knowledge. This verse shatters any illusion of spiritual privilege based on lineage or marriage alone. The bond of faith is the only bond that matters in the divine scale. A prophet cannot save his own wife from the consequences of her own betrayal, teaching that on the Day of Judgment, every soul will stand alone, accountable only for its own faith and deeds.

=58=

The divine judgment upon the people of Sodom is described in language that is both terrifyingly precise and poignantly figurative. The punishment was a catastrophic overturning and a "rain of brimstone.

" The phrase “We poured upon them a rain” uses the word *amṭarnā*, which is typically used for the beneficial, life-giving rain sent down from the sky. Here, however, the “rain” is not water but a shower of baked clay stones, brimstone, and fire, each marked for a specific sinner. This reversal of a normal blessing into a tool of destruction is a common Quranic theme: the elements that a people think are under their control become the very instruments of their doom when they defy the Lord of nature.

The use of the word “rain” (*maṭar*) for punishment also carries a tone of deep irony and contempt. They had been accustomed to praying for rain for their crops and their life; now a very different kind of rain descends upon them, bringing not life but total annihilation.

The verse then concludes with a powerful, sorrowful rhetorical exclamation: “How evil was the rain of those who had been warned! ” This statement contains multiple layers of meaning. First, it emphasizes the severity and the horrific nature of the punishment; it was an "evil rain" indeed. Second, and most crucially, it highlights that they were not an ignorant people who were caught unawares. They had been "warned" (*mundharīn*) again and again by Prophet Lot. They had been given every chance to repent and change their ways. The fact that the warning was clear and continuous, and yet was met with mockery and persecution, made the punishment, when it came, a particularly humiliating and terrible one. It serves as a stark reminder that divine punishment is not arbitrary; it is the final, horrific consequence of a persistent, willful refusal to heed the merciful warnings sent by God.

=59=

Having concluded the narratives of Ṣâliḥ and Lot, the surah now shifts directly to a series of powerful, logical arguments meant to be posed to the disbelieving Quraysh. The verse begins by instructing the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, to open his address with a declaration: “Praise be to Allah, and peace be upon the servants He has chosen.” This preamble is significant. It is a declaration of faith and an announcement of independence from the false gods of the pagans. The Prophet is to express his absolute gratitude and praise to Allah alone, and to send salutations of safety, peace, and honor specifically upon the chosen servants—the prophets and the righteous believers. This opening statement, by its very nature, excludes the idols and those who worship them from any share of divine praise or peace. It establishes a clear boundary. Then follows the direct challenge to the idolaters, a rhetorical question that cuts to the heart of their polytheism: “Ask the disbelievers, ‘Which is better: Allah or whatever gods they associate with Him?’” The Arabic phrasing is striking, using “whatever” (*mā*) rather than “whomever,” to emphasize the unreality and the inanimate nature of their false deities.

The question forces a mental comparison. The disbelievers, even in their polytheism, acknowledged that Allah was the Supreme Creator, the Lord of the heavens and the earth. He was inherently “better” in their own understanding. By juxtaposing this acknowledged Supreme Being with the idols of wood and stone that could neither create, provide, nor harm, the question exposes the absurdity of their worship. It invites them to use their reason: if Allah is self-evidently greater and better, then why do they worship that which is inferior? The stage is thus set for a series of detailed questions in the following verses that will systematically dismantle the foundations of polytheism using the evidence of creation and providence that they themselves witnessed daily.

=60=

The series of compelling, reason-based questions continues, probing the pagan Arabs about the most fundamental and observable aspects of their existence. The question is structured as a multi-part demonstration of divine power and benevolence, beginning with the cosmos and moving to their immediate sustenance.

The first and most comprehensive question is, “Who created the heavens and the earth?” The vast, perfect, and intricate universe is a fact. The polytheists never attributed its creation to their idols of stone and wood; they acknowledged it was the work of the Supreme Creator, Allah.

The question then narrows down to a life-sustaining miracle they witnessed repeatedly: “and sends down rain for you from the sky.” Rain, the source of all fresh water, descends from clouds formed by a precise, recurring system no idol has any control over. The result of this rain is then described with a personal, divine touch: “by which We cause delightful gardens to grow. ” The shift to the majestic “We” emphasizes the direct involvement of divine power in producing gardens that are beautiful and pleasing to the eye and soul, places of delight.

The next statement is a direct challenge to human capability and, by extension, to the power of any false god: “You could never cause their trees to grow.” A human being can plant a seed and water it, but the miraculous process of germination, growth, and the unfurling of a complex tree from a tiny seed is beyond human power. No idol can do it. This leads to the inescapable, repeated question: “Was it another god besides Allah?” The implied answer for any honest, rational person is a resounding no.

Yet, despite this logical clarity, the disbelievers still associated partners. The verse concludes by diagnosing their state: “Absolutely not! But they are a people who set up equals to Allah!

” The word “equals” (*yaʿdilūn*) means they deviate from pure monotheism and place their idols on par with the Creator, not because of evidence, but because of a deep-rooted deviation from truth and sound reason.
