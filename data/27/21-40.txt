=21=

Solomon’s tone shifts from gentle curiosity to the language of a firm and decisive ruler. After noting the hoopoe’s absence, he immediately pronounces a harsh judgment upon it, demonstrating that his leadership is balanced with accountability.

The first part of the threat is “I will surely subject him to a severe punishment.” The Arabic intensifies this by using an oath-like emphasis, showing the certainty of the consequence. The nature of this severe punishment is not specified, but it indicates that absence without permission in such a highly organized kingdom, where every creature had its assigned role, was treated as a serious breach of discipline. Solomon’s justice required that no one, however small, could neglect their duty without facing the consequences.

The second part escalates the matter dramatically: “or even slaughter him.” This shows that the punishment could be capital. To modern ears, the idea of executing a bird for being absent may seem harsh, but it must be understood within the context of Solomon’s unique, divinely-ordained kingdom. The hoopoe was not a mere wild bird; it was a soldier in a miraculous, disciplined army. Desertion in times of duty, or negligence of a vital state function, could warrant such a sentence.

Moreover, this statement sets the stage to highlight the immense importance of the bird’s subsequent news, showing that what was at stake was not a trivial matter of attendance but affairs of state tied to the worship of the one true God. Yet, Solomon’s justice is immediately tempered with due process. He adds the crucial exception: “unless he brings me a compelling excuse.

” The phrase “compelling excuse” translates sulṭān mubīn, which literally means a clear, manifest authority or proof. Solomon was not ruling by mere whim; he was open to hearing a justifiable reason. This teaches a profound leadership lesson: even a pre-announced severe punishment is suspended the moment a valid justification is presented. It shows that Islamic governance and even prophetic rule operate on the principle of examining evidence before execution, rejecting a culture of arbitrary punishment.

=22=

The narrative moves swiftly, resolving the suspense created by Solomon’s threat. The phrase “It was not long before” indicates that the hoopoe’s absence was brief, which adds to the credibility of its mission; it had not been idling away but had been engaged in an urgent and distant reconnaissance. The hoopoe’s immediate address to the mighty king is bold and confident. It does not start by begging for mercy or fearfully explaining its absence. Instead, it offers a direct and powerful justification by stating, “I have found out something you do not know.” This opening line is masterful, as it immediately captures Solomon’s attention and establishes that the bird’s absence was not negligence but an act of initiative that yielded intelligence beyond the king’s current knowledge. It implies a service so valuable that it instantly overrides the cause for punishment. The bird then contextualizes its information: “I have just come to you from Sheba with sure news. ” Sheba, known in Arabic as Sabaʾ, was a wealthy and powerful kingdom in southern Arabia, in the region of modern-day Yemen. The distance from Palestine, where Solomon’s court likely was, to Sheba was immense for a small bird, making the journey a marvel and the news all the more significant. The description of the news as “sure” (yaqīn) indicates it was not a rumor or speculation, but a firsthand, verified observation.

This is a crucial principle: intelligence and information that form the basis of important decisions must be certain and not based on conjecture. The hoopoe presents itself as a reliable informant with certain knowledge, which dignifies its role and introduces the pivotal story of the Queen of Sheba, a narrative that would test Solomon’s kingdom and ultimately lead a nation to faith.

=23=

The hoopoe now delivers its “sure news,” and it immediately highlights three striking and interconnected details about the kingdom of Sheba, each one calculated to be of immense interest to Solomon. First, the bird states, “Indeed, I found a woman ruling over them.” This was a matter of great significance. In the ancient Near East, political leadership was overwhelmingly patriarchal, and a nation being ruled by a queen was a notable anomaly that signified a unique political and social structure. For a prophet-king like Solomon, this would immediately raise questions about the spiritual state of the land, as the Quran would later clarify the potential pitfalls and the background that led to her rule.

The second detail is that she “has been given everything she needs.” This is a description of immense worldly prosperity and a fully equipped kingdom. She lacked no material resource, military might, or earthly splendor. The hoopoe is essentially telling Solomon that this is a powerful and wealthy sovereign, not a minor tribal chieftain, making the news all the more important.

The third detail is a specific, visual marvel: “who has a magnificent throne.” The Arabic word for throne is ʿarsh, and it is described as ʿaẓīm, meaning magnificent, mighty, and grand. In the ancient world, a throne was the ultimate symbol of a monarch’s authority, power, and majesty. A magnificent throne signified a glorious, established, and proud kingdom. By mentioning these three elements—gender, prosperity, and the symbol of power—the hoopoe succinctly summarizes the Queen of Sheba’s worldly status.

Yet, for a prophet who submits only to the Lord of the Mighty Throne, this description sets the stage for a profound spiritual contrast. The earthly throne, no matter how magnificent, is a fleeting shadow compared to the divine one, and the real poverty of this prosperous kingdom is about to be exposed.

=24=

The hoopoe’s report moves from political and material observations to the spiritual heart of the matter, which is the true reason this news is so critical for Solomon, a prophet of God. The bird declares, “I found her and her people prostrating to the sun instead of Allah.

” This is the crucial piece of intelligence. The act of prostration (*sujūd*) is the highest form of physical submission and worship. Their prostration was not to the Creator of the sun, but to the sun itself—a created object, a great sign of God turned into a false god. This was the ultimate injustice (*ẓulm*) of *shirk*. In a single sentence, the hoopoe has identified a nation drowning in the most fundamental error, despite their worldly advancement. The bird then demonstrates a theological insight that is nothing short of prophetic, diagnosing the root cause of their misguidance: “For Satan has made their deeds appealing to them. ” This connects beautifully to the description of disbelievers in verse 4 of this very surah, where Allah says He makes evil deeds appear fair to those who reject the Hereafter. The hoopoe understands the spiritual psychology of sin. Satan beautified their sun-worship for them, so they saw it not as an error but as a noble and beautiful tradition. The result of this satanic beautification is two-fold: “hindering them from the Right Way and leaving them unguided. ” Their own perception, corrupted by Satan, acts as a barrier, obstructing them from the straight path of pure monotheism. They are thus left wandering, unable to find guidance because the very tool of perception—their heart and mind—has been tampered with. The hoopoe’s theological diagnosis proves it is not just a scout but a believer whose heart is aligned with divine truth, and its report becomes a call to prophetic action.

=25=

The hoopoe’s inspired speech continues, elaborating on the precise nature of the misguidance and contrasting it with the awe-inspiring attributes of the true God. The sentence begins with “so they do not prostrate to Allah,” explaining the tragic consequence of Satan’s deception. Instead of submitting to the One who alone deserves worship, their act of prostration, an innate human impulse to revere something greater, is misdirected to the sun. The hoopoe then provides the compelling reason why Allah alone is worthy of that prostration by highlighting two of His unique and exclusive divine actions. First, He is the One “Who brings forth what is hidden in the heavens and the earth.

” The Arabic word *al-khabʾ* refers to all that is hidden and concealed—the treasures of the earth like water, minerals, and plants, the secrets of the heavens like the coming of rain, and the unseen realities known only to God. The sun, to which they prostrate, cannot do this. It is a powerful but unthinking creation. The sun does not know what is hidden in the earth; it does not cause the seeds to germinate by its own will. Only the Creator can bring forth life from dead matter and disclose hidden truths. Second, He “knows what you all conceal and what you reveal.” This shifts the focus to Allah’s comprehensive knowledge of human actions and inner states. The sun is blind and unaware of the worship offered to it. It does not hear their prayers or know their secrets. But Allah knows the open prostrations and the hidden thoughts, the public deeds and the secret plots. The hoopoe’s argument is a masterpiece of natural theology, using reason to demonstrate the absurdity of prostrating to a created object that itself is under divine command. This theological declaration from a small bird would have resonated powerfully with Solomon, affirming the very core of his prophetic mission: the call to worship Allah, the Knower of the unseen and the manifest.

=26=

The hoopoe’s report culminates in a pure and powerful declaration of faith, which serves as the doctrinal climax of its entire speech. It cries out, “He is Allah!

” This is the ultimate statement of truth, a simple grammatical shift that profoundly redirects all worship. After describing the attributes of the true God—bringing forth the hidden and knowing the concealed and revealed—the bird now names the Possessor of these attributes with the Supreme Name, Allah.

This is a direct refutation of the Queen of Sheba’s sun-worship. It is as if the hoopoe is saying: The One who does all these things, and whose knowledge is perfect, is none other than Allah. The declaration then moves to the fundamental creed of all prophets: “There is no god worthy of worship except Him.” This phrase of *tawḥīd*, the absolute negation of any deity followed by the affirmation of Allah’s exclusive divinity, is the central message of the Quran. The hoopoe is not only a scout but a fully-fledged caller to monotheism, uttering the very testimony of faith. It is a stunning moment where a bird teaches humanity the essence of belief.

The verse concludes with a majestic attribute that directly confronts the report’s earlier detail: “the Lord of the Mighty Throne.” Previously, the hoopoe had mentioned that the Queen of Sheba had a “magnificent throne. ” Now, it proclaims that Allah is the Lord of the true, ultimate, and mighty Throne (*al-ʿArsh al-ʿAẓīm*).

This is a brilliant rhetorical and theological contrast. An earthly throne, no matter how magnificent, has a Lord over it, a Creator and Owner. Allah’s Throne, on the other hand, is the symbol of His absolute sovereignty and dominion over all creation. The Queen’s throne is a fading object in a kingdom that will fall, but Allah’s Throne represents His eternal, unshakeable majesty. This small bird thus proves itself to be a profound theologian, distinguishing between the Lord and what is merely lorded over, and its proclamation sets the entire stage for Solomon’s subsequent mission to Sheba.

=27=

Having listened to the hoopoe’s extraordinary report, Solomon’s response is immediate, measured, and entirely befitting a wise and just judge. He does not become swept up in the excitement of the theological declaration, nor does he dismiss it outright as a tale too fantastic to believe. Instead, his words embody the Quranic principle of verifying information before acting upon it. He says, “We will see whether you are telling the truth or lying. ” The use of the plural “We” reflects the royal “we” of a sovereign, speaking on behalf of his court and his state’s process. This simple statement contains profound legal and ethical principles. First, it upholds the presumption of neither guilt nor innocence. The hoopoe’s previous record, or even the eloquence of its monotheistic speech, does not exempt it from an objective investigation of its factual claims. A beautiful sermon about God does not automatically validate a news report. Second, the statement demonstrates that in matters of state and intelligence that could lead to war or diplomatic missions, due diligence is a sacred duty. Solomon would not mobilize an army or send a letter to a foreign monarch based on the uncorroborated word of a single bird, regardless of how pious it seemed. Third, this teaches a vital lesson in leadership: a leader must be discerning and must not act on emotion. Solomon’s question was not an insult to the hoopoe but a necessary step in establishing the truth. He was about to act on the report, but only after a methodical verification. This approach contrasts sharply with the impulsiveness of tyrants and highlights that the prophetic model of governance is rooted in certainty and clear evidence. The stage is thus set for the next step, where the bird’s report will be tested, and the greatness of Solomon’s wisdom will unfold in the diplomatic mission that follows.

=28=

Solomon immediately devises a plan to verify the hoopoe’s intelligence through a direct diplomatic engagement. His method is brilliant in its simplicity and effectiveness. The command, “Go with this letter of mine and deliver it to them,” transforms the hoopoe from a mere informant into an official state envoy.

This is a remarkable elevation of a small bird to the highest level of international diplomacy, carrying a message from one of the world’s most powerful kings to the court of a distant queen. The fact that Solomon entrusted the hoopoe with this task is itself a partial confirmation of his belief in the bird’s truthfulness, but the letter’s content and the response it would generate would provide the final, objective verification. The letter is a tangible, physical piece of evidence that would either be acknowledged by the Queen’s court as authentic or dismissed as a forgery, revealing the reality of her kingdom.

The second part of the command reveals Solomon’s strategic acumen: “then stand aside and see how they will respond.” The hoopoe is instructed not to engage in debate or to preach, but simply to deliver the letter and then withdraw to an observation point, acting as an unseen intelligence-gatherer on the reaction.

The Arabic suggests a sense of stepping back, turning away from them, and watching closely. This serves a dual purpose: it ensures the hoopoe’s safety from a potentially hostile initial reaction, and it provides Solomon with invaluable, unfiltered information about the internal dynamics of Sheba’s ruling class. Solomon wanted to know how they would receive the message, what their private discussions would be, and whether they would show arrogance, fear, consultation, or aggression.

This is the method of a master strategist, combining a clear, bold message with a covert means of gathering the response, all while maintaining the dignity and safety of his envoy. The truthfulness of the hoopoe’s claim about the sun-worshipping kingdom would now be objectively proven by the reality of their reaction to a call to submit to Allah.

=29=

The scene shifts seamlessly to the court of Sheba, where the hoopoe has successfully delivered Solomon’s letter. The narrative now uses the Queen’s own voice to confirm the bird’s report and to demonstrate her political acumen. The Queen calls her advisory council together and announces, “O chiefs! Indeed, a noble letter has been delivered to me. ” The Arabic word she uses for the letter is *kitāb*, which can mean a formal written communication, and she immediately describes it as *karīm*, meaning noble, honorable, and dignified. This initial, instinctive description is highly significant. The Queen, an experienced monarch, is a master of statecraft and symbolism. By recognizing the letter as “noble,” she is assessing its origin, its seal, its material, and the grandeur of its style, all of which spoke of a sovereign of immense stature. A letter written on precious material, sealed with a powerful king’s signet, and delivered in an extraordinary manner (by a bird) would naturally evoke such an assessment. More importantly, her decision to immediately convene her chiefs, her inner circle of advisors, demonstrates that she was not an autocratic tyrant. Her first action upon receiving this unprecedented communication was to seek counsel. This reflects a positive leadership quality mentioned in the surah, showing that her rule was consultative even before encountering Solomon’s monotheistic message.

The use of “O chiefs” also confirms to the reader the existence of a structured, powerful state, aligning with the hoopoe’s description of a kingdom of material wealth and might. The stage is now set for the revelation of the letter’s startling content and the collaborative decision-making process that will follow.

=30=

The Queen, having gathered her chiefs, now identifies the sender and begins to recite the content of the noble letter. She simply states, “It is from Solomon,” a name that would have been known across the ancient world as a king of immense power, wisdom, and a unique prophetic legacy. This brief statement confirms the source and begins to frame the gravity of the message. The letter does not open with boasts of Solomon’s military might or a list of his own royal titles. Instead, it begins with the most profound and revolutionary phrase, the *basmalah*: “In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful.

” This is the very first written communication from a prophet of God to a foreign, polytheistic power recorded in the Quran. The choice to begin with this phrase is a masterclass in prophetic *daʿwah*, or invitation to faith. First, it establishes the ultimate source of the message’s authority: it is not merely a letter from Solomon, a powerful man, but a message sent in the Name of the Lord of all worlds. Second, the two chosen attributes, “the Most Compassionate” (*ar-Raḥmān*) and “Most Merciful” (*ar-Raḥīm*), are of critical importance. For a people who worship the sun and likely fear their gods, Solomon introduces a God whose predominant attribute is not wrath or distant power, but all-encompassing, nurturing, and continuous mercy. This was a direct invitation to a God of love and grace, completely reframing their concept of the divine. It was a psychological and spiritual overture before any command or threat. The contrast between the sun, a blazing, unmerciful object, and the Most Compassionate Lord is implicit but powerful. The letter thus begins by planting a seed of hope and curiosity, preparing the heart to receive the command that follows.

=31=

After the merciful and majestic opening, the letter delivers its core message, which is a clear, concise, and uncompromising call. Solomon’s words are two direct commands.

The first is a prohibition: “Do not be arrogant with me.” The Arabic word for arrogance here is *taʿlū*, which literally means to be or act superior, to elevate oneself above another. Solomon, as a prophet-king, is not commanding them to submit to his personal ego. The “arrogance” he forbids is the arrogance that would prevent them from hearing and accepting the truth of God. Political pride, attachment to a false religion, and the refusal to humble oneself before the divine call are all forms of this arrogance. It is a warning against letting their worldly power become a barrier to their spiritual submission.

The second command is the positive call to action: “but come to me, fully submitting to Allah.” The Arabic phrase is *muslimīn*, which is the plural of *muslim*.

This is the heart of the entire mission. Solomon does not invite them to worship him, nor does he ask them to merely sign a political treaty. He invites them to the core of the prophetic faith: Islam, the complete, peaceful submission of one’s entire being to the will of Allah. The letter’s brevity is its genius. It is free of complex theological arguments, relying on the power of the *basmalah* to introduce the divine and then presenting a stark, clear choice: abandon the arrogance of false worship and come into the fold of true submission. This directness leaves no room for misinterpretation. The Queen’s recitation of this letter to her chiefs demonstrates the letter’s startling and provocative content. It is a call that would challenge the very foundations of their society, their religion, and their national identity. The stage is now perfectly set for the Queen’s political response and the test of her wisdom.

=32=

Faced with a direct, powerful, and non-negotiable call to submit from a superior king, the Queen of Sheba once again demonstrates a defining quality of her rule: consultative leadership. She does not panic, nor does she issue a rash, solitary decree. Instead, she turns to her chiefs and says, “O chiefs! Advise me in this matter of mine. ” Her request for *aftūnī*, meaning to give a formal legal or advisory opinion, shows she respects the institution of counsel and values their experience and perspective. She frames it as “this matter of mine,” acknowledging the issue as a personal and state crisis that belongs to her but requires collective wisdom. She then explains her principle: “for I would never make any decision without you.

” This is not a sign of weakness or indecisiveness; it is a mark of political sagacity and strength. A ruler who understands the weight of decisions that affect an entire kingdom knows that sustainable and wise governance requires buy-in and the consideration of multiple viewpoints. Her statement also serves to politically empower her chiefs and secure their loyalty. By involving them in such a critical decision, she ensures that whatever the outcome, they share in the responsibility and the consequences. This verse implicitly teaches that *shūrā*, or mutual consultation, is a praiseworthy principle of governance, even when practiced by a non-Muslim ruler. Her approach stands in stark contrast to the solitary, arrogant decisions of Pharaoh earlier in the surah. The Queen’s style of leadership, while still operating within a polytheistic framework, is presented as a noble, intelligent, and pragmatic quality that prepares her, and her people, to eventually accept the truth. Her request sets the stage for the chiefs to voice their position, which will reveal the nation’s raw military confidence.

=33=

The chiefs of Sheba deliver their counsel, and their reply reveals the immediate, instinctive posture of a proud and powerful military nation. The opening statement is a declaration of their capabilities: “We are a people of strength and great military might.

” The Arabic words used are *ulū quwwatin* (possessors of strength) and *ulū baʾsin shadīd* (possessors of severe might in war). This is not an empty boast; it is an assessment based on their formidable army, weaponry, and history of successful defense. It is the language of a warrior aristocracy that sees every external challenge first through the lens of armed force. Their first thought is not diplomacy or submission, but war.

However, the second half of their reply is remarkably mature and reveals a profound, disciplined loyalty that balances their martial fervor. They immediately concede, “but the decision is yours, so decide what you will command. ” Despite their military confidence and their own inclination toward a forceful response, they place the final authority entirely in the hands of their Queen. This shows a remarkable political structure. The chiefs are not a rebellious council that overthrows a weak monarch; they are loyal to the institution and willing to follow her lead, even if it potentially contradicts their own preference for battle. Their statement is effectively a transfer of the burden of the final decision back to her, while simultaneously informing her that the military option is viable. It reveals that her consultation was genuine and that they provided their honest assessment—that they have the power to fight. The Queen now has the full picture: a formidable threat in Solomon’s letter, an assurance of her army’s strength, and the total loyalty of her command to follow whatever path she chooses. Her wisdom in the next verse must navigate between the arrogance of war and the humiliation of immediate surrender.

=34=

The Queen of Sheba, having heard the warlike confidence of her chiefs, now demonstrates why she is the ruler. She does not give in to the emotional pull of military pride. Instead, she offers a brilliant and timeless piece of political analysis based on historical precedent and the observable laws of power. She begins, “Indeed, when kings invade a land, they ruin it. ” Her use of the word “kings” (*mulūk*) is specific; she is speaking of worldly conquerors motivated by power and glory. She understands the sociology of conquest: a conquering army, once it breaches the defenses, does not simply change the flag. It “ruins” the land, corrupting its infrastructure, plundering its resources, and disrupting the social fabric.

The Arabic word *afsadū* denotes a deep and comprehensive corruption. She then identifies a targeted, specific outcome of this corruption: “and debase its nobles.” Conquerors systematically humiliate the existing elite to break the will of the people and establish an unchallengeable new order. The upper class is imprisoned, executed, or stripped of all honor and status. Her observation is not just theoretical; it’s an ancient, grim reality. Her concluding emphasis, “They really do so!”, reinforces this as an ironclad historical rule, not a possibility.

This is a lesson in pragmatic statecraft. A proud nation, if it chooses to fight and loses, will face total annihilation not just of its army, but of its entire society, its honor, and its future. She is effectively de-escalating the war-mongering of her chiefs by painting a vivid and catastrophic picture of potential defeat. Her reasoning immediately reveals that her earlier consultation was not just for show; she was seeking counsel, but she already possessed the superior wisdom to weigh the option and its horrific consequences. Her mind is already moving toward a path that avoids this ruinous outcome, leading to her next strategic proposal.

=35=

Having wisely argued against the catastrophic consequences of a direct military confrontation, the Queen presents her own strategic plan, a classic diplomatic maneuver designed to test Solomon’s true nature without exposing her kingdom to the risk of annihilation. She states, “But I will certainly send him a gift.” This was not a sign of submission or tribute in the political sense, but a calculated probe. In the diplomacy of the ancient world, sending a lavish gift to a powerful monarch was a standard method of testing his character and intentions. The nature and value of the gift would reflect her kingdom’s wealth and sophistication. The response to this gift would be a litmus test. If Solomon were a mere worldly king, hungry for wealth and power, he would likely accept the gift graciously, perhaps be impressed by its opulence, and be open to negotiations, a treaty, or even an alliance. The envoy’s report would confirm that he is a man who can be swayed by riches, just like other kings.

However, if he were truly a prophet of God, as his letter’s invocation of Allah suggested, his reaction would be entirely different. Material wealth would hold no power over him, and his focus would remain solely on their spiritual submission.

The second part of her plan is to “see what response my envoys will return with.” She delegates the mission to her diplomats, trained to observe, listen, and report. She is not relying on the letter alone but on a full intelligence-gathering operation. The envoys would observe Solomon’s court, his demeanor, his power, and his reaction, and then bring back a comprehensive assessment. This strategy is a masterpiece of prudence and wisdom. It buys time, avoids a reckless war, and gathers critical intelligence on the true nature of the man who wrote “In the Name of Allah, the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful.”

=36=

The Queen’s diplomatic mission arrives at Solomon’s court, bearing the magnificent gifts intended to test his character. The encounter is immediate and decisive. Solomon does not even take a moment to admire the treasures. His response is a piercing question that cuts to the heart of the matter: “Do you offer me wealth? ” This question is filled with astonishment and a gentle rebuke. It reveals that Solomon has instantly seen through the diplomatic veneer and recognized the offer as a misjudgment of his mission and his nature. He then provides the definitive reason for his disinterest: “What Allah has granted me is far greater than what He has granted you.

” This is the statement of a man whose heart is filled with contentment and whose perception of value is divinely centered. Prophethood, divine knowledge, mastery over jinn and birds, and a relationship with the Creator are treasures that make all the gold and jewels of Sheba appear utterly insignificant. He is not merely rich; he is satisfied with a divine provision that makes worldly wealth meaningless. He then turns the table on them with a sharp psychological insight: “No! It is you who rejoice in receiving gifts. ” The Arabic verb *tafraḥūn* means to be exuberantly, almost childishly happy. Solomon identifies that their entire worldview is trapped in the materialism they tried to use as a test. Their joy is tied to the accumulation of worldly trinkets. For them, a magnificent gift is a cause for celebration; for a prophet, it is a transparent and almost offensive attempt at bribery. This response is a profound lesson: a true caller to God cannot be bought, and the divine message is not a commodity to be negotiated. The envoy, and through him the Queen, is being taught that the standard of value in a prophetic worldview is completely different from the one based on worldly power.

=37=

Solomon’s rejection of the bribe is immediately followed by a clear and terrifying ultimatum, delivered with the full weight of prophetic authority. He instructs the chief envoy, “Go back to them,” meaning return to the Queen and her chiefs with his final word. The message is one of absolute resolve: “for we will certainly mobilize against them forces which they can never resist.” Solomon shifts the conversation from the realm of gifts and diplomacy to the stark reality of divine-backed military power. He speaks not of a conventional army, but of “forces” (*junūd*) which are irresistible. The Queen’s earlier fear of kings who ruin lands is about to be realized if she does not submit. Solomon’s forces, composed of humans, jinn, and birds, represent a supernatural military might against which no purely human army could stand. He is not bluffing; he is stating a divine promise. The consequence of their resistance is made explicit: “and we will drive them out from there in disgrace, fully humbled.

” The word “disgrace” (*adhilla*) is the opposite of the honor and nobility they cherish. The “full humiliation” (*ṣāghirūn*) implies a state of being utterly subdued, belittled, and broken in spirit.

This is the very fate the Queen sought to avoid by not going to war. Solomon, by delivering this ultimatum, is not being a bloodthirsty tyrant. He is offering them a final chance. The logical path is now starkly clear: either voluntary submission to Allah, which leads to honor and preservation, or forced submission through a devastating, supernatural defeat, leading to humiliation and ruin. The ultimatum is an act of mercy, as it leaves no ambiguity. The envoy is to carry back the undeniable proof of Solomon’s prophethood: he was a king who could not be bribed by the world, and who spoke with the unshakeable confidence of one backed by the Lord of all worlds.

=38=

With the ultimatum delivered, the narrative shifts back to Solomon’s court as he prepares for the Queen’s arrival. He now gathers his own inner circle—his chiefs from among the jinn and the humans—and poses a remarkable challenge. He says, “O chiefs! Which of you can bring me her throne before they come to me in full submission? ” This request reveals several profound things. First, it demonstrates that Solomon’s knowledge and power, granted by Allah, were so immense that he was already looking past the ultimatum to the next phase. He anticipated that her wisdom, combined with the terror of his threat, would lead her to submit and come to him personally. He had no doubt about the outcome. Second, the request itself is a display of divine power meant to be a sign for the Queen. Solomon wants to present her with her own magnificent throne—a symbol of her authority and identity—transported miraculously to his palace before she even arrives. This would be an undeniable miracle, proving that his kingdom was not just materially rich but supernaturally enabled.

The phrase “before they come to me in full submission” sets a time limit, adding urgency and showcasing the intended purpose: the miracle is to greet them upon their arrival, serving as the ultimate proof of the truth of Solomon’s call and the power of the God he serves. It would also be a display of dominion that dismantles her worldly attachment to her throne. By possessing her throne, he symbolically demonstrates that her previous kingdom and all its glory are under his, and by extension Allah’s, complete control. The challenge is thrown down, and the response from his supernatural courtiers is about to show the extent of the powers at his command.

=39=

Solomon’s challenge to his chiefs immediately elicits a swift and confident response from a member of his supernatural forces. The responder is described as “one mighty jinn,” an ʿifrīt—a term denoting a powerful, cunning, and rebellious type of jinn, yet here one that is utterly subjugated to Solomon’s command. His boast is staggering: “I can bring it to you before you rise from this council of yours.” The throne of the Queen of Sheba was located in her palace in Yemen, a journey of immense distance from Jerusalem. To bring it in the time it takes for a king to finish a council meeting—perhaps a few hours—was a feat of unimaginable speed and power. The jinn is claiming the ability to fly to Sheba, seize the heavy and heavily guarded throne, and return, all within that short window. He then vouches for his capability by stating, “And I am quite strong and trustworthy for this task.” The two qualities he highlights are precisely what any leader would need for such a mission: physical strength to carry the massive object, and trustworthiness to ensure he would deliver it intact and not betray the mission or misuse such power. In ancient courts, a powerful and untrustworthy servant was a dangerous liability. This jinn is essentially submitting his credentials, assuring Solomon that the mission is within his formidable power and his character.

However, the very fact that such a feat required a time frame of “before you rise” indicates there were still limitations, a journey and an effort involved. This sets up the stage perfectly for the next response, which will demonstrate an even greater and more instantaneous form of knowledge-based power, completely eclipsing the might of this mighty jinn, and highlighting that the ultimate source of miracles is not brute force but divine knowledge.

The competition of power takes a dramatic and spiritually profound turn. In contrast to the mighty jinn, a different figure steps forward: “the one who had knowledge of the Scripture.” Scholars differ on his identity—some say he was a human scholar from Solomon’s court, others an angel or a righteous jinn—but what is certain is that his power was not physical but intellectual and spiritual, derived from divine revelation. He makes a claim that dwarfs the jinn’s offer: “I can bring it to you in the blink of an eye. ” The Arabic phrase *qabla an yartadda ilayka ṭarfuka* denotes an action so instantaneous it happens before a person’s glance returns to them after looking away. It is faster than a single moment.

This is not a feat of speed; it is a miraculous transcendence of space and time, achieved through knowledge of God’s greatest Name or the sacred knowledge of the revealed Book. It demonstrates that the power of divinely granted knowledge surpasses all physical might. Solomon’s reaction to this stunning miracle is the core lesson. When he saw the throne miraculously “placed before him,” he did not swell with pride or take credit. Instead, he immediately exclaimed, “This is by the grace of my Lord to test me whether I am grateful or ungrateful.” The most powerful king on earth, witnessing an unprecedented miracle, sees it purely as a test. Every blessing, no matter how grand, is a trial of character. He immediately passes the test by turning to gratitude. He then articulates the universal law of gratitude: “And whoever is grateful, it is only for their own good.” Gratitude does not benefit Allah; it purifies and elevates the soul of the one who is thankful. Conversely, “whoever is ungrateful, surely my Lord is Self-Sufficient, Most Generous.” Allah is *Ghaniyy*, free of all need, so human ingratitude does not diminish His majesty in the slightest. And He is *Karīm*, the Most Generous, whose blessings continue to flow even upon those who reject Him, as a sign of His vast forbearance. This beautiful exclamation encapsulates the entire purpose of divine gifts: they are a means to draw nearer to God through a heart overflowing with humble, aware thankfulness.
