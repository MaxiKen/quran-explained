=61=

The sequence of rhetorical questions continues, each one peeling back another layer of creation to expose the irrationality of polytheism. This verse moves from the celestial and botanical signs of the previous verse to the terrestrial wonders that sustain human life directly.

The first inquiry is, “Who made the earth a place of settlement?” The Arabic word used is *qarār*, which implies stability, a fixed resting place that is perfectly suited for habitation. The earth does not wobble chaotically, nor is it so hot or cold that life is impossible; its distance from the sun, its atmosphere, and its gravitational stability are precisely calibrated to make it a tranquil home.

The next question, “caused rivers to flow through it,” draws attention to the fresh water system that snakes across continents. Rivers are not random; they are a divinely ordained circulatory system, bringing life to otherwise barren lands, shaped by mountains and valleys in a grand hydraulic design.

The third question, “placed firm mountains upon it,” speaks of the mountains as stabilizers, pegs driven into the earth to minimize its shaking, and as reservoirs of minerals and sources of rivers. These were not placed haphazardly but with immense wisdom.

The final question, “and set a barrier between the fresh and salt bodies of water,” points to a known phenomenon where two seas meet but their waters do not mix completely due to a physical and chemical barrier, an invisible line that preserves the distinct properties of each. All these phenomena were observable by the Arabs, whether through their travels or their knowledge of the world. The crushing question then follows: “Was it another god besides Allah? ” The disbelievers knew their idols had no share in these cosmic engineering feats.

The verse concludes with a sobering judgment: “Absolutely not! But most of them do not know.” The fault is not with the evidence, but with a lack of deep, reflective knowledge that moves from seeing to believing, from the creation to the Creator. They knew these things as facts, but they did not internalize them as signs.

=62=

The argument now turns from the vast, external world of rivers and mountains to the deeply personal and internal experience of human desperation. The question is piercing: “Who responds to the distressed when they cry to Him, relieving their affliction?

” The word for distressed is *muḍṭarr*, which describes someone who has been pushed into a corner by hardship, whose back is against the wall, and who has exhausted all worldly means. In such a moment of utter helplessness, every human being, even a polytheist, instinctively turns to a single, unseen Higher Power. They do not call upon their idols of stone or their intercessory deities; their primordial nature breaks through the layers of false belief and cries out to the one true God, Allah. The polytheists of Mecca themselves acknowledged this; they would pray to Allah with sincere devotion when a storm at sea threatened to drown them. This question forces them to confront that the One who rescues them in that state of ultimate need is the only One truly worthy of worship at all times.

The second part of the question shifts to a broader divine act of providence: “and Who makes you successors in the earth? ” This refers to the continuous turnover of generations and the inheritance of the land. Nations rise and fall, and Allah allows new groups to take the place of those who passed away. This cycle of succession, with its security and provision, is entirely in Allah’s hands, not in the power of any false god. The inescapable refrain then sounds: “Is it another god besides Allah? ” The implied answer is an emphatic no.

The verse then ends with a note of sorrowful criticism: “Yet you are hardly mindful!” The Arabic term *qalīlan mā tadhakkarūn* means they recall these profound truths only rarely, in fleeting moments of crisis, and they fail to let that realization transform their daily worship and lives. Their mindfulness is tragically sparse.

=63=

The interrogation of the pagan conscience deepens with a focus on guidance and the subtle, life-giving mercies of the natural world. The first question, “Who guides you in the darkness of the land and sea?” addresses a fundamental human vulnerability: being lost. In the ancient Arabian context, traversing vast, featureless deserts and navigating perilous seas at night were matters of life and death. The polytheists relied on the stars, the landmarks, and an innate sense of direction—all of which are creations of Allah.

The question forces them to acknowledge that the entire system of celestial and terrestrial navigation, the very cues that orient them when they are most likely to panic, was put in place by the Supreme Creator, not by Al-Lāt or Al-ʿUzzā. The second question moves to a more delicate mercy: “and sends the winds ushering in His mercy?” The “mercy” here is a beautiful metaphor for rain. The winds are precursors, messengers of good tidings, blowing clouds laden with life-giving water over parched lands. The Arabs, a people acutely dependent on seasonal rains for their pastures and crops, understood this phenomenon intimately. The wind is an invisible force, yet it is a servant of its Lord, carrying a mercy that no idol can summon or withhold.

The verse then places the recurring, devastating question: “Is it another god besides Allah?” But here, the answer is not merely a logical negation; it is elevated to a declaration of divine transcendence: “Exalted is Allah above what they associate with Him! ” The shift from a question to a statement of glorification is a powerful rhetorical move. It shows that after so much clear evidence, the only proper response from the believer is to declare Allah’s absolute transcendence, free and far above the pathetic, powerless associates they falsely attribute to Him. The lesson is that nature’s guidance and mercy are not random but are continuous, living witnesses to the oneness of the truly Merciful.

=64=

The argument now reaches its most profound and challenging stage, touching on the ultimate power of Allah: the absolute beginning and the absolute end of all existence. The question is breathtaking in its scope: “Who originates the creation then resurrects it?” The act of *yabdaʾu al-khalq* is the initial, ex nihilo creation—bringing everything into being from nothingness with no prior model.

This is a power they already acknowledged as belonging to Allah. However, the question immediately links it to “then resurrects it” (*thumma yuʿīduhu*), connecting the first creation to the recreation in the Hereafter. The logical reasoning is crystal clear and devastating to the skeptic’s position: if a Being has the infinite power and knowledge to create a universe from absolute nothing, then surely recreating it after death is a lesser task, a repetition of a known process.

The second part of the question turns to the continuous sustenance of life: “and gives you provisions from the heavens and the earth.” The heavens provide rain, sunlight, and the cosmic order, while the earth yields crops, fruits, and minerals. This daily, dependable provision is an ongoing sign. The challenge, “Is it another god besides Allah?” is now followed by a direct, confrontational command to the Prophet: “Say, O Prophet, ‘Show me your proof, if what you say is true.’” This is the ultimate call for evidence. Faith in the one true God is not based on blind following but on clear, demonstrable proofs rooted in creation and reason. The pagans are being challenged to bring forth any logical or empirical evidence for their polytheistic claims. They are being told that their entire theological framework is a house of cards, built on inherited tradition and speculation, while the divine framework is supported by the undeniable reality of the universe itself. This shifts the burden of proof onto the claimant of falsehood.

=65=

After the powerful, reason-based interrogation of the pagans, the discourse now pivots to a profound teaching about the fundamental limits of all created beings and the exclusive domain of the Divine. The Prophet is instructed to make a definitive, unassailable declaration: “None in the heavens and the earth has knowledge of the unseen except Allah.” The unseen, *al-ghayb*, encompasses everything that lies beyond the reach of human senses and ordinary means of perception—the future, the inner thoughts of hearts, the realities of heaven and hell, the exact time of one’s death, and the secrets of destiny.

The phrase “none in the heavens and the earth” extends this lack of knowledge to all of creation, from the lowest earthly creature to the highest angel in the heavens. It completely shuts the door on the pagan practice of consulting soothsayers, astrologers, and fortune-tellers who claimed to pierce the veil of the unseen. It also refutes any claim that a prophet or a saint inherently knows the unseen; rather, they only know what Allah chooses to reveal to them. The absolute monopoly of Allah on the knowledge of the unseen is a cornerstone of pure monotheism.

The verse then immediately connects this principle to the specific issue the Meccans were mocking: the resurrection. “Nor do they know when they will be resurrected.” This directly answers their repeated sarcastic question, “When will this Day of Judgment be?” The knowledge of the Hour is among the exclusive keys of the unseen. By stating that no creature, not even the Prophet, knows its timing, the verse removes any human authority that could be questioned for a delay. The timing is hidden in Allah’s perfect wisdom, and the very ignorance of creation regarding it is meant to be a test of faith and a motivation for constant preparedness. The finality of this verse is both a comfort to the Prophet against his mockers and a stark warning to the mockers themselves.

=66=

This verse delivers a penetrating, step-by-step diagnosis of the pagans’ spiritual and cognitive state regarding the afterlife, moving far beyond their flippant questions. It begins with an emphatic negation: “No!” This forcefully corrects any assumption that their questioning is rooted in a genuine search for truth.

The first layer of their ailment is described as: “Their knowledge of the Hereafter amounts to ignorance. ” The Arabic word for knowledge here is *ʿilm*, but it is a false knowledge, a shallow set of skeptical arguments that, upon closer inspection, reveals itself to be pure *jahl*, or ignorance. They know the superficial doubts and mockeries, but they lack any deep, certain understanding of the reality that awaits them.

The verse then intensifies the diagnosis: “In fact, they are in doubt about it.” The Arabic *shakk* describes a state of wavering uncertainty, a mental agitation where a person oscillates between two positions and cannot settle on the truth. They are not even firm atheists; they are lost in a fog of skepticism. This doubt is a punishment in itself, as it offers no peace of mind. Then, the Quran drills down to the deepest, most fundamental problem: “In truth, they are totally blind to it.

” The Arabic word is *ʿamīn*, meaning complete blindness. This is not a physical blindness but a blindness of the heart and spiritual insight (*baṣīrah*). They are unable to perceive the overwhelming evidence for the resurrection because the organ of spiritual perception has been completely shut down. A blind person cannot see the sun at noon, and likewise, a heart sealed by arrogance and sin cannot perceive the glaring truth of the Hereafter. This three-tiered description—false knowledge, crippling doubt, and total blindness—perfectly captures the downward spiral of persistent disbelief. It serves as a warning that mocking questions about the unseen are rarely an expression of intellectual superiority, but rather a symptom of a profound and disabling spiritual disease.

=67=

Having diagnosed their spiritual blindness, the Quran now quotes the exact nature of the disbelievers’ mocking question to expose its flawed logic and spiritual arrogance. Their question is framed as an expression of utter incredulity: “When we and our fathers are reduced to dust, will we really be brought forth alive?

” The phrase “reduced to dust” (*kunnā turāban*) emphasizes the complete dissolution of the physical body. They are not just dead; they have undergone a total, material disintegration into the very soil of the earth, scattered and indistinguishable. The inclusion of “our fathers” is a significant detail. It serves a dual purpose: it adds a veneer of historical precedent to their argument, and it reveals an underlying, psychologically-driven refusal to accept a reality that would condemn their beloved ancestors to punishment. Their polytheism was deeply rooted in ancestral tradition. By saying their forefathers also turned to dust and never came back, they are using the vastness of past time as a false argument against the possibility of resurrection. The tone of their question, “will we really be brought forth alive?” is one of sarcasm and amazement that anyone could believe such a thing.

The Arabic interrogation *aʾinnā* carries a heavy note of denial. To them, the idea seemed physically impossible and logically absurd. They saw death as the absolute end. This verse captures the core materialist fallacy of every age: the belief that the observable, physical processes of decay are the ultimate reality. They measured the power of the Creator by the limited standards of human experience and the laws of nature as they understood them. Their question is not a scientific inquiry but an arrogant rhetorical device intended to belittle the Prophet’s warning, mirroring the exact language of those who today see no reality beyond the physical world.

=68=

The disbelievers continue their chain of objections, building upon the first argument of physical impossibility with a second line of defense: the claim of empty, repetitive threats. They state, “We have already been promised this, as well as our forefathers earlier.

” This is a classic argument of denial by fatigue. They treat the warnings of resurrection as a worn-out, generational story that prophets have been using for centuries with no tangible result. They argue that generation after generation of people have died, and the promised resurrection has never materialized.

Therefore, in their reasoning, the threat is a hollow tool of social control, a spiritual manipulation with no grounding in reality. This argument is particularly insidious because it twists the very mercy of Allah—His forbearance in delaying the punishment to give people time to repent—into a proof of falsehood. They interpret the prolonged lifespans of the disbelieving generations as evidence that the Day of Judgment is a myth.

The verse then records their ultimate, contemptuous judgment on the entire message: “This is nothing but ancient fables!” The Arabic phrase is *asāṭīru al-awwalīn*, meaning the legends, myths, and stories of the earlier generations. By labeling the Quran and the warnings of the prophets as “ancient fables,” they are effectively dismissing the entire body of divine revelation as a recycled collection of primitive superstitions.

This is a direct attack on the authenticity and divine origin of the message. They reduce the monumental reality of the Hereafter to the level of campfire stories. The arrogance in this statement is staggering; they judge the knowledge of God by the limited scope of their own lifespan. This verse perfectly captures the psychology of a secular, materialistic mindset that sees all religious truth as the product of ancient human imagination, not realizing that the consistency of the prophetic message across millennia is in fact one of its greatest proofs of a single, divine source.

=69=

In the face of sarcasm and accusations of telling ancient fables, the Quran does not engage in an abstract, philosophical debate. Instead, it issues a powerful, rational, and evidence-based challenge. The Prophet is instructed to “Say, ‘Travel throughout the land and see the fate of the wicked.’” The Arabic command *sīrū*, to travel and journey, is a repeated Quranic invitation to empirical observation. The “land” is not an empty space but a vast, open-air museum of divine judgment. The ruins of the people of ʿĀd, Thamūd, Sodom, and the civilization of Pharaoh lay along the well-traveled trade routes of the Arabs. These were not mythical places; they were physical sites that the Meccans passed by on their summer and winter caravans to Syria and Yemen. The verse is, in effect, a call to archaeology and history as a theological proof. The fate of the “wicked” refers specifically to those past nations who mocked their prophets, demanded divine punishment, and labeled the truth as ancient fables. Their end was not a fable; their destroyed dwellings were there, carved into mountains or lying in ruins, visible to any observer. The invitation to travel is a call to use one’s physical senses and reasoning faculty to draw a conclusion. If the divine warnings were mere fables, why were these ancient civilizations, more powerful and materially advanced than the Quraysh, so utterly and violently obliterated? The pattern is consistent and observable. The ruins are a silent, enduring testimony that the law of Allah is real and that the fate of the mockers is not a myth but a historical reality. This verse is a powerful lesson in the Islamic methodology of using the material world as a means to arrive at spiritual certainty, connecting the physical sign (*āyah*) in the landscape to the moral and metaphysical truth.

=70=

Immediately after the command to present the historical evidence to the disbelievers, the discourse turns to console and strengthen the heart of the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. The verse contains two gentle but firm divine prohibitions, providing immense comfort.

The first is: “Do not grieve for them.” The Arabic word *taḥzan* implies a deep-seated sorrow, a sadness that weighs down the heart. The Prophet, being a mercy to humanity, felt a profound personal pain and grief over the stubborn disbelief of his people. He was not indifferent to their spiritual destruction; he agonized over it, as described in other verses where Allah tells him his soul might nearly be consumed by grief over their rejection. This divine command acts as a balm, reminding him that his responsibility is to deliver the message, not to force its acceptance. His grief, while noble in its compassion, cannot be allowed to overwhelm him, for the ultimate judgment is not his burden.

The second prohibition is: “nor be distressed by their schemes.” The Arabic *ḍayq* refers to a feeling of tightness and constriction in the chest, a state of anxiety and distress. The Meccan pagans were not just passive disbelievers; they were active plotters, scheming in the Dar al-Nadwa, conspiring to assassinate, silence, or discredit the Prophet. This constant plotting could naturally induce a state of psychological suffocation. Allah is commanding His Prophet to let go of that anxiety, assuring him that their schemes are under divine surveillance and control. They plan, but Allah is the Best of Planners. This dual command—not grieving over their fate and not fretting over their plots—perfectly defines the emotional and psychological boundaries of a believer’s engagement with a hostile world. It teaches that our hearts must be anchored in the reality that Allah is in charge, freeing us from the crushing weight of outcomes we cannot control.

=71=

The surah returns to documenting the specific verbal attacks of the disbelievers, revealing their psychology of denial. The question they pose to the believers is dripping with sarcasm and impatience: “When will this threat come to pass, if what you say is true?” The “threat” refers to the Day of Judgment, the resurrection, and the subsequent punishment they were constantly warned about. By asking “when,” they were not seeking genuine information, as the previous verses had already clarified that the knowledge of the Hour is with Allah alone. Their question was a form of mockery and a challenge. The hidden implication was that the delay of the punishment was proof of its falsehood.

This is a common logical fallacy of the disbelievers throughout history: mistaking the forbearance and mercy of God for either His non-existence or His impotence. They wanted a specific date and time, as if the truth of a divine decree is contingent upon its immediate and visible fulfillment according to their schedule.

The phrase “if what you say is true” is a conditional that further underscores their skepticism. It places the believer on the defensive, demanding that the truth of the message be validated by the immediate arrival of its threatened consequence.

This is a deeply irrational demand, akin to a criminal mocking the sheriff’s warrant because the arrest has not happened yet. The underlying arrogance is that they, not God, set the timeline for justice. For the early Muslim community, enduring persecution and mockery, hearing such taunts was a source of psychological pressure. This verse acknowledges that reality and prepares to deliver the perfect, wise response, which neither confirms a specific date nor admits any doubt in the truth of the warning, but reframes the entire perspective on the timing of divine punishment.

=72=

The divine response to the mockers’ demand for a specific date is a masterpiece of psychological and theological precision. The Prophet is instructed to say, “Perhaps some of what you seek to hasten is close at hand.

” The Arabic word *ʿasā*, “perhaps,” when spoken by Allah or through His command, does not indicate doubt on the part of the Divine; rather, it conveys a sense of inevitability and a warning while leaving the exact timing ambiguous. It places the hearer in a state of alertness.

The phrase “some of what you seek to hasten” refers to the punishment they were so arrogantly calling for. The response implies that they might not have to wait for the great, cosmic Day of Judgment to taste a portion of the divine chastisement. A torment in this life—a humiliating defeat, a famine, or a cataclysmic event—could be near, right around the corner. For the Quraysh, this was a chillingly accurate prediction; they would soon face a severe drought and, later, the decisive defeat at the Battle of Badr, where their leaders were killed. This response turns their mockery back upon them. They demanded the punishment to be hastened, and the Quran warns them that they might get their wish in a way they would deeply regret. The wisdom of using “some” rather than “all” is also profound. It indicates that the full, comprehensive punishment of the Hereafter remains, but a foretaste, a sample, might be sufficient as a worldly lesson for those with any sense. The verse masterfully avoids the trap of specifying a time—which is known only to Allah—while simultaneously increasing the psychological pressure on the mockers by introducing the imminent possibility of a proximate calamity. It shifts them from a posture of a smug skeptic demanding a distant deadline to a person suddenly aware that the danger might be much closer than they think.

=73=

After the stark warning that some punishment may be close at hand, the verse immediately balances the discourse with a portrayal of Allah’s predominant attribute of grace. It begins, “Surely your Lord is ever Bountiful to humanity.

” The Arabic word *Dhū faḍl* means the Possessor of immense, overflowing bounty, grace, and favor. This bounty is not restricted to believers; it is general “to humanity. ” The sunshine, the rain, the health in their bodies, the intellect, the provision, and the very life of the disbelievers are all manifestations of this divine bounty. The very fact that they are still alive to mock and demand punishment, instead of being instantly destroyed for their insolence, is itself a profound act of divine grace. This verse is a direct explanation for why the punishment has not yet come. It is not because of a lack of power or because the threat is empty; it is because Allah’s mercy and forbearance precede His wrath. He gives humanity time, space, and countless blessings, hoping they will reflect, turn to Him, and be grateful.

However, the verse ends with a sorrowful reality: “but most of them are ungrateful.” The Arabic word *yashkurūn* means to be grateful, to acknowledge and utilize blessings for their intended purpose. The tragedy of humanity is that the vast majority of people receive this immense, continuous bounty, but they do not acknowledge the Giver. Instead, they attribute their success to their own effort, to chance, or to their false gods, or they simply live in heedlessness, enjoying the blessings without a thought of gratitude. This ingratitude is the root sin that leads to disbelief. The verse, therefore, explains that the delay of punishment is an act of mercy, and the hastening of punishment is a consequence of persistent ingratitude. It is a profound invitation to every human being to pause and recognize that their very existence is a continuous stream of favors from a Bountiful Lord, and that the proper response is thankfulness expressed through faith and obedience.

=74=

Building on the theme of human ingratitude, this verse introduces the attribute of Allah’s all-encompassing knowledge, which serves as both a comfort to the Prophet and a warning to the schemers. It states, “And surely your Lord knows what their hearts conceal and what they reveal.” The heart (*ṣadr*, literally “chest”) is the seat of secrets, hidden motives, unspoken plots, and concealed emotions like jealousy, hatred, and deep-seated arrogance. Human beings can hide these feelings behind a mask of politeness, a fake smile, or a diplomatic statement. They can conceal their conspiracies in the dark of the night and whisper their plans behind closed doors.

However, Allah’s knowledge pierces through all veils. The verb *yusirrūn* (conceal) and *yuʿlinūn* (reveal) together form a comprehensive pair, indicating that every single internal and external state, every hidden thought and every open word, is within His direct knowledge. There is no “darkness of the heart” that is not fully illuminated to Him. For the Prophet, this is a source of immense reassurance. The constant scheming of the Quraysh, their secret meetings to plan his assassination or to discredit the Quran, were not happening in a vacuum. His Lord was fully aware of every detail, and therefore, no plot could take Allah by surprise.

For the disbelievers, this is a terrifying reality. Their outward mockery, which they revealed openly, and their deep-seated, concealed hatred for the truth, are all recorded and will be accounted for.

The verse also connects to the earlier discussion of the unseen. The knowledge of what hearts conceal is part of the *ghayb* that belongs exclusively to Allah.

This reminder re-anchors the discourse in the core theme of divine omniscience, ensuring that both the believer’s patience and the disbeliever’s scheming are placed in their proper cosmic context, under the watchful eye of the All-Knowing Lord.

=75=

This verse expands on the principle of divine knowledge from the previous verse, providing the mechanism through which this comprehensive awareness is manifested and preserved. It declares a universal, all-encompassing reality: “For there is nothing hidden in the heavens or the earth without being written in a perfect Record.

” The Arabic word for hidden is *ghāʾibah*, which relates to the unseen and the deeply concealed. No event is so small, no secret so deeply buried, and no creature so remote—whether in the vastness of the celestial realm or the depths of the terrestrial world—that it escapes being inscribed.

The term “perfect Record” translates *kitāb mubīn*. This is the Preserved Tablet (*al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ*), the central divine register in which the decrees of all things are recorded with absolute, unerring precision. Nothing is omitted, forgotten, or left to chance. The past, the present, and the future are all inscribed in a Book whose perfection reflects the perfection of the Author. This verse provides a profound answer to the disbelievers’ mockery about the resurrection. They wondered how a person reduced to dust and scattered particles could be brought back to life.

The answer is that the information of their entire existence—every atom, every deed, every secret intention—was never lost. It was always meticulously recorded in a clear, original master copy. The reconstruction of a body and the replay of a life story is not a process of guessing or recovering lost data; it is an act of re-creation based on a flawless, pre-existing blueprint. This establishes a strong logical and spiritual foundation for the ease of the reckoning. The universe is not governed by chaos and forgetfulness; it is governed by meticulous divine administration.

For the believer, this verse is a source of comfort, knowing that no injustice or righteous struggle is ever forgotten by God. For the wrongdoer, it is a chilling realization that the secret sins they thought were buried in the past are, in truth, eternally preserved and will be confronted.

=76=

The discourse now shifts to the specific role of the Quran in relation to previous scriptures and their followers, establishing its authority as the decisive criterion. The verse states, “Indeed, this Quran clarifies for the Children of Israel most of what they differ over.” The context of revelation in Mecca meant that the Jewish community, while present in Medina, was known to the Arabs as “People of the Book.” The Quran here asserts a fundamental function of its revelation: it is an arbiter and a clarifier. Over the centuries, the Children of Israel had fallen into deep sectarian divisions and doctrinal disputes. They disagreed about the nature of God, the true stories of the prophets, the laws of the Torah, the status of the Messiah, and the realities of the afterlife. The original message of Moses and the other prophets had become obscured by human interpretation, scribal alterations, and the accumulation of oral traditions. The Quran, as the final, uncorrupted, and preserved revelation, came to settle these disputes. It sorts out the truth from the falsehood in their history and doctrine. For example, it clarifies the truth about Jesus, absolving the Virgin Mary of scandal, confirming his miraculous birth while denying his divinity, and setting straight the narrative of his crucifixion, about which they were in grave confusion. It also retells the stories of their prophets like Moses and Aaron with accuracy, stripping away the later interpolations that had tarnished their image.

The phrase “most of what they differ over” indicates that while the Quran clarifies the major, core differences concerning faith and guidance, some minor matters of past law may be left aside as abrogated or no longer relevant. This verse serves as a proof of the Quran’s divine origin, as only the One who revealed the original Torah could so authoritatively and correctly settle the disputes of later generations. It is a direct invitation to the Jews of Medina and beyond to recognize the Quran as the final judge over their conflicting traditions.

=77=

While the previous verse highlighted the Quran’s function as a clarifier of disputes for the Children of Israel, this verse broadens its description, but with a particular qualification that returns to the theme introduced at the very beginning of the surah. It declares, “And it is truly a guide and mercy for the believers.” The Quran is described with two of the most beautiful attributes: a "guide" (*hudan*) and a "mercy" (*raḥmah*). As a guide, it provides the comprehensive roadmap for all aspects of human life, leading individuals and communities out of the darkness of ignorance, doubt, and moral confusion into the light of certainty, purpose, and righteousness. Its guidance is practical, spiritual, and intellectual, answering the fundamental questions of existence and providing a legal and ethical framework for a just society. As a mercy, the Quran is a direct manifestation of Allah’s compassion. It is a healing for the diseases of the heart, a source of comfort in distress, and a means to attain divine forgiveness and the eternal bliss of Paradise. The teachings of the Quran, when followed, bring mercy in this life through inner peace and social harmony, and in the next life through salvation.

However, the verse pointedly notes that this guidance and mercy are specifically “for the believers.” This is a recurring Quranic theme: the Quran is an objective source of light, but only those who open the eyes of their hearts through sincere faith can perceive and benefit from it. A skeptic who approaches it with a closed, mocking mind will find it a source of confusion and frustration, a mere historical text. But for the one who believes, it transforms into a living, breathing guide that speaks to their deepest needs.

This statement thus connects back to the opening of the surah, creating a beautiful literary and thematic circle, while emphasizing the absolute condition of faith as the key to unlocking the Quran’s treasures. It is a mercy offered to all, but realized only by those who accept it.

=78=

After describing the Quran’s role as a guide and mercy for believers and a clarifier of disputes, the verse turns to the ultimate resolution of all disagreement and the final divine action. It states, “Your Lord will certainly judge between them by His justice.” The “them” refers to all the differing groups mentioned—the Children of Israel with their internal disputes, the believers, the disbelievers, and all of humanity and jinn who have ever differed over the truth. The judgment is certain, indicated by the emphatic Arabic particle *la-yaqḍī*. This judgment is not arbitrary; it is “by His justice” (*bi-ḥukmihi*). Allah’s judgment is the perfect standard of fairness, equity, and truth. There will be no partiality, no mistake, and no hidden evidence left unconsidered. Every wrong will be righted, every secret made public, and every party will receive exactly what their deeds warrant.

This is a terrifying prospect for the wrongdoers who mocked and plotted, and a source of immense hope for the believers who were oppressed and patient. The verse then grounds this perfect justice in two of Allah’s mighty names: “for He is the Almighty, All-Knowing.

” The name “the Almighty” (*al-ʿAzīz*) guarantees that His judgment cannot be resisted, appealed, or overturned. No force in the universe can prevent the execution of His decree. The powerful tyrants of the world, who thought they were invincible, will be utterly powerless before His might on the Day of Judgment.

The name “All-Knowing” (*al-ʿAlīm*) guarantees the perfection of the judgment’s content. A judge can be powerful but ignorant, leading to tyranny. Allah’s might is coupled with absolute, comprehensive knowledge. He knows the hidden intentions, the complex contexts, the secret struggles, and the exact measure of every atom of good and evil.

Therefore, His judgment is not only enforced but is intrinsically, perfectly just. This verse provides a complete and satisfying conclusion to all the debates and disputes; the final word does not belong to any human scholar or court, but to the Lord of Justice Himself.

=79=

After the grand theological truths about the Quran, divine knowledge, and the certainty of judgment, the discourse delivers a direct, personal command to the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, and by extension to all believers. The command is “So put your trust in Allah.

” The Arabic word *tawakkal* implies a comprehensive reliance, a surrendering of one’s affairs to God after having done one’s best, with complete confidence in His wisdom and plan. It is the natural, practical outcome of believing in the Almighty, All-Knowing Judge of the previous verse. If Allah is indeed the irresistible power and the source of perfect knowledge, then the only rational and spiritual response is to entrust everything to Him—one’s mission, one’s safety, the response of the people, and the ultimate outcome. This trust is not passive; it is an active state of the heart that brings tranquility and courage. The reason for this command is then stated: “for you are surely upon the path of clear truth.

” The Arabic *al-ḥaqq al-mubīn* is the truth that is so manifest, bright, and clear that it leaves no room for genuine doubt. The Prophet is not following a subjective opinion, a relative truth, or a probable guess. His path—the message of the Quran, the call to monotheism, the morality he embodies—is objectively, unmistakably true.

This is a powerful reassurance. A person who knows he stands on clear truth has nothing to fear from falsehood, mockery, or schemes. Falsehood, by its very nature, is fragile and destined to vanish. Truth, backed by the Lord of the worlds, is solid and victorious. The logic is: because your path is the unmistakable truth, and the Lord of truth is the Almighty and All-Knowing, then trusting Him is the most obvious and sensible thing to do. This verse connects the intellectual certainty of the previous arguments to a state of deep spiritual reliance, teaching that correct theology must produce a state of complete trust in God, freeing the believer from the anxiety of defending the truth and placing the outcome firmly in God’s hands.

This verse serves as a profound spiritual reality check and a further consolation to the Prophet, using a powerful metaphor to explain the tragic state of the disbelievers and to relieve him of any misplaced sense of personal failure. Allah says, “You certainly cannot make the dead hear the truth.” The disbelievers who are stubbornly rejecting the message are likened to corpses in a grave. A dead body has no sensory perception; no matter how loud and clear a person speaks to it, it cannot hear or respond. Similarly, the spiritual death of the disbelievers’ hearts has rendered them completely incapable of benefiting from the call of the Prophet, despite its clarity.

This is not a statement about their physical ears, but about the organ of spiritual reception, which is their heart. The verse then uses a second, related metaphor: “Nor can you make the deaf hear the call when they turn their backs and walk away.” Here, they are compared to a person who is not only deaf, but is also actively walking away in the opposite direction. The act of turning their backs symbolizes their conscious rejection and their refusal to even face the truth. A deaf person walking away is doubly unable to hear a call; the physical disability is compounded by the behavioral choice of moving farther from the source of the sound. This verse was immensely comforting to the Prophet. He had a deep, merciful desire for all his people to be guided, and their rejection caused him great pain. Allah is informing him that he has fulfilled his duty. The problem is not with the message, nor with the messenger, but with the receiver. A doctor cannot be blamed if a patient refuses to take the medicine. The guidance is in Allah’s hands alone; the Prophet is only a warner and a conveyor of the message.

This teaching liberates the believer from the burden of results, focusing the duty on the clarity of the delivery, while leaving the outcome to the One who alone can open the hearts of the dead and the deaf.
