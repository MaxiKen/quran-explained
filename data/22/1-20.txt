=1=

The surah opens with a powerful call that addresses all of humanity, not just believers, which immediately signals the universal importance of what is about to be said. The command "Fear your Lord" translates the Arabic concept of taqwa, a profound awareness of Allah’s presence that leads a person to protect themselves from His displeasure.

This is not a fear that causes one to run away, but a reverent awe that causes one to draw closer through obedience. The reason for cultivating this fear is then stated: "for the violent quaking at the Hour is surely a dreadful thing. " The word used for quaking, zalzalah, describes a violent earthquake, a terrifying shaking that throws everything into chaos.

This is not an ordinary tremor; it is the cosmic upheaval that accompanies the final Hour. The verse isolates this single event from the many terrors of the Day of Judgment and calls it "a dreadful thing," something immensely colossal and horrifying beyond human comprehension. By beginning with this vivid description, Allah connects an abstract future event to a sensory human experience—an earthquake—so that every person, regardless of their background, can begin to grasp the overwhelming nature of that Day. The verse essentially establishes the foundation for all that follows in the surah: a recognition of divine power, human accountability, and the reality of resurrection. It calls the listener to live not for the fleeting present, but in constant awareness of a moment that will reduce every worldly power and attachment to nothing. This opening serves as a spiritual alarm bell, waking up the heart to take the rest of the message seriously, for what use is safety in this world if one is not safe from the quaking of the Hour.

=2=

Building on the mention of the quaking, this verse paints a graphic picture of that Day's psychological impact. It says, "The Day you see it," making the listener an eyewitness to the scene.

The first image is deeply maternal, which makes it profoundly disturbing: "every nursing mother will abandon what she is nursing." A mother’s bond with her suckling infant is one of the most powerful instincts in creation. For a mother to forget, drop, or completely neglect her nursing child signifies a terror so overwhelming that it erases the strongest natural love and concern.

The verse then adds, "and every pregnant woman will deliver her burden prematurely." The intense horror and shock of the Hour will cause such extreme physical and emotional trauma that pregnancies will not reach their full term; the unborn will be miscarried out of sheer fright. These are not descriptions of a physical earthquake’s direct effects but of the psychological state of humanity facing divine judgment.

The next line reinforces this: "And you will see people as if they were drunk, though they will not be drunk." They will stagger, move without coordination, and appear completely disoriented, yet no intoxicant has touched their lips. Their mental state is shattered from within by sheer terror.

The verse ends by clarifying the cause: "but the torment of Allah is terribly severe. " It is the severity of what they are witnessing and being brought to account for that has produced this universal madness. This verse masterfully moves the discussion from a physical calamity to an internal, personal catastrophe, teaching that the true horror of the Hour is not merely a shaking of the earth but a shaking of the human soul facing its Lord without preparation.

=3=

After presenting the terrifying reality of the Hour, the surah turns in amazement to those who, in the face of such a warning, still engage in pointless argument. The word "Still" connects this verse to the previous warning, expressing a divine astonishment at human stubbornness. "There are some who dispute about Allah without knowledge" describes a person who argues about God’s existence, oneness, power to resurrect, or right to command, not from a place of sincere inquiry but from a foundation of pure ignorance. Their dispute is not based on a revealed book, sound reasoning, or even genuine observation of the world, but on a blind desire to oppose the truth.

The second part of the verse diagnoses the root of this problem: "and follow every rebellious devil. " Such a person is not an independent thinker or a free spirit, as they might claim; rather, they have enslaved themselves to the most destructive influence possible. The devil described as "rebellious" is marid, one who has crossed all bounds in defiance of God. By following him, the disputer is merely a pawn in a cosmic rebellion, echoing his master’s arrogance.

This verse reveals the spiritual psychology of disbelief: it is rarely a conclusion reached through sincere study, but a state of the heart that follows a path of arrogance and inner rebellion against the truth, seeking out the whisperings of the devils among jinn and men to justify its stance. It draws a direct line from the rejection of clear divine warning to the choice of a spiritual guide who leads only to ruin.

=4=

This verse explains the inevitable result of the relationship described in the previous verse. "It has been decreed" indicates a divine law that is fixed and unalterable. The decree is against the rebellious devils themselves, but it directly affects their followers: "whoever takes them as a guide will be misguided." The act of taking a devil as a guide means to follow his path, to adopt his standards, and to obey his suggestions. The consequence is already written into the very nature of that choice. A guide who is himself lost can only lead others to loss.

This is a profound logical and spiritual principle: a source that is fundamentally corrupt cannot produce a wholesome destination. The verse then explains the final destination of this misguided journey: "and led by them into the torment of the Blaze." The devils not only mislead in this life, causing confusion, evil deeds, and a corrupted heart, but they actively lead their followers into the Fire on the Day of Judgment. It is as if they walk ahead of their armies of followers straight into the punishment. This demonstrates that the "rebellious devil" of verse 3 is not merely a passive tempter but an active guide toward Hell.

The verse also contains a hidden warning: human beings who call others to disbelief and sin fall under this same category of rebellious devils, and they will share in this dreadful decree. The logic is simple and chilling: choose a guide who is at war with Allah, and you have chosen a path that ends at war's ultimate prison.

=5=

The surah returns to addressing all of humanity with a sweeping argument for the Resurrection. It begins with the condition: "If you are in doubt about the Resurrection," acknowledging that the concept of life after death can be difficult for the human mind. The response is to point to an observable miracle: the human’s own origin. "We did create you from dust," referring to the creation of the first human, Adam, from the inorganic material of the earth.

This is the first creation. Then, the process moves to procreation: "then from a sperm-drop," a humble fluid, "then developed you into a clinging clot of blood, then a lump of flesh." This describes the embryonic stages in the womb, moving from a thing that clings, to a chewed-like substance, to a formed shape.

The phrase "fully formed or unformed" refers to the potential of the embryo to develop into a complete human or to be miscarried. All of this is "in order to demonstrate Our power to you. " The entire process is a living exhibition of creative power. He then mentions the settling in the womb for an appointed term, birth as infants, growth to the prime of youth and strength, and then the decline: "Some of you may die young, while others are left to reach the most feeble stage of life so that they may know nothing after having known much." This arc from weakness to strength and back to senility is an undeniable sign of a power that gives life and reclaims it.

The verse then shifts from the human body to the earth: "And you see the earth lifeless, but as soon as We send down rain upon it, it begins to stir to life and swell, producing every type of pleasant plant. " The dry, barren ground, which looks utterly dead, becomes vibrant and green with water. If Allah can do this annually before their eyes, bringing life from death in the botanical world, then resurrecting a human being, who was also originally made from dust and water, is not a rationally difficult concept. The two wonders—the creation of a human from a drop and the revival of dead land—work together to dismantle any reasonable doubt about the Resurrection.

=6=

After the detailed evidence of the previous verse, this verse provides the theological conclusion. The phrase "That is because" signals that what follows is the reason and explanation for the creative power just displayed. "Allah alone is the Truth" declares the fundamental reality of existence: God is al-Haqq, the Ultimate Truth. His being is necessary, eternal, and the foundation of all other reality. Everything else derives its existence from Him and is, in comparison, passing and dependent. This contrasts sharply with the idols and false gods which are falsehoods, unreal in any ultimate sense.

The second clause, "He alone gives life to the dead," is the direct answer to the doubt about the Resurrection. If He is the Truth, and if He demonstrated His power in the first creation from dust and a drop, then giving life to the dead is perfectly consistent with His nature and power. The argument here is one of restoration: restoring life is not more difficult than originating it.

The third clause, "He alone is Most Capable of everything," provides the comprehensive scope of His power. The phrase "Most Capable" translates Qadeer, indicating a power that is complete and entirely sufficient. There is no task too difficult, no obstacle too great. This verse acts as the anchor for the entire preceding argument, moving the discussion from signs in nature to the nature of the Sign-Maker. Recognizing Allah as the Truth changes everything: it means our life has purpose, our actions have permanent consequences, and the return to Him is an absolute certainty.

=7=

This verse serves as the definitive reaffirmation of the reality that the surah has been establishing. The use of the emphatic particle "certainly" and the absolute negation "there is no doubt about it" leaves no room for uncertainty. The coming of the Hour is not a possibility, a theory, or a myth; it is a fixed event in the unfolding of divine decree.

The word "Hour" itself, as-Sa'ah, implies suddenness and swiftness, a moment that will arrive without further warning. It is the pivot upon which all of history turns.

The second sentence, "And Allah will surely resurrect those in the graves," makes the nature of this Hour explicit. It will not be a mere cosmic event, but a personal one: the graves will split open, and every human being who ever lived will stand again.

The mention of graves is particularly powerful because it confronts the most tangible evidence the skeptic has: the decay and disappearance of the body in the earth. But the One who tracked and nurtured a human from a sperm-drop in the darkness of the womb and then from the dust of the earth will have no difficulty tracking and reassembling a body from the same earth of the grave. This verse prepares the listener for the detailed descriptions of judgment and punishment that will follow, grounding them in a confident and unwavering declaration of fact. It closes the first section of the surah by moving from the warning of the quake, through the signs in creation, to the firm promise of resurrection, acting as a bridge to the consequences of belief and disbelief.

=8=

This verse echoes verse 3 but adds crucial details about the condition of the disputant. The word "Still" again expresses astonishment. The person described now argues "without knowledge, guidance, or an enlightening scripture." These three negations form a complete indictment. "Without knowledge" means they have no personal insight or sound intellectual basis. "Without guidance" means they lack a teacher, a prophet, or a divine compass to direct their thoughts. "Without an enlightening scripture" means they have no revealed book from God to illuminate their path. This threefold absence means their argument is void of any authority: no reason, no spiritual guide, no revealed text.

The phrase "enlightening scripture" is particularly significant; a true scripture gives light, clarifying matters that human intellect alone cannot settle. The disputant here is arguing in the dark, not even holding the torch that God has sent. This verse is believed to refer to the arrogant leaders of Quraysh like Nadr ibn al-Harith, who would debate about the Qur'an with worldly rhetoric, poetry, and tales of the ancients, but without any divine foundation. It serves as a timeless warning against intellectual arrogance, teaching that valid discourse about Allah must be grounded in sincere reflection, prophetic guidance, and revealed truth. A person who stands on none of these pillars is not a genuine seeker of truth, but merely a performer in a debate whose goal is victory, not guidance.

=9=

This verse describes the posture and motive of the disputant from the previous verse. "Turning away in pride" translates a word that means to bend one’s neck or side out of arrogance. It is a physical manifestation of an inner disease: a refusal to listen, to consider, or to humble oneself before the truth. This prideful turning away is not merely a passive act. It is done with the intention "to lead others astray from Allah’s Way. " The person is not just lost; he actively becomes an obstacle, a barrier placed between people and the straight path. His arrogance makes him a missionary of misguidance.

The verse then delivers the judgment in two phases. "They will suffer disgrace in this world" is a promise of worldly humiliation. This could manifest as defeat, exposure of their falsehood, or a life of inner turmoil and futility. History provides examples of proud figures who fought the truth and were humiliated before their deaths. But the worldly disgrace is only a prelude: "and on the Day of Judgment We will make them taste the torment of burning.

" The use of "We will make them taste" indicates direct, personal experience. It will not be a distant observation but an intimate, sensory punishment. The "torment of burning" refers to the Fire. This double punishment teaches a critical lesson: defying Allah’s truth not only corrupts the soul and damages society but also leads to an inevitable, humiliating end. A person who leads others astray carries not only the weight of their own misguidance but the sin of all those they misled, making their torment multi-layered.

=10=

The scene shifts to the moment of punishment. The disbelievers are addressed directly: "This is the reward for what your hands have done.

" The phrase "your hands" personalizes their actions, making it clear that the torment is a direct, earned consequence, not a random affliction. Their hands, which they used to commit sins, write falsehood, and strike out against truth, are now the witnesses against them.

This statement highlights a principle of perfect divine justice: the punishment matches the action. The second part, "And Allah is never unjust to His creation," is a fundamental declaration of Allah’s nature. The negation of injustice is absolute.

The term used for creation, 'abeed, can mean servants or slaves, emphasizing that Allah has total ownership. A master who inflicts arbitrary punishment on his own possession could theoretically be questioned, but Allah’s absolute perfection precludes this. The verse thus addresses a deep psychological question that the human mind might entertain when contemplating the severity of Hell: is this not harsh?

The answer is definitive: Allah does not wrong anyone. The punishment is not an act of tyranny but a manifestation of a justice so precise that it accounts for every whisper, intention, and deed.

For the believer, this is a verse of comfort, knowing that no one will be treated unfairly. For the wrongdoer, it is a chilling confirmation that the torment is inescapably deserved. It completely removes any ground for complaint on that Day.

=11=

After describing the outright disbelievers and arrogant leaders, the surah now turns to a third category of people: the unstable worshipper. "On the verge of faith" paints a picture of a person standing at the edge of a precipice. Their faith is not deeply rooted in the heart but is a superficial commitment dependent on circumstances. This verse explains their psychology: they worship Allah essentially for worldly benefit. "If they are blessed with something good," like health, wealth, or ease, "they are content with it," interpreting their good fortune as a sign of Allah’s approval, and they remain calm.

However, "if they are afflicted with a trial," like poverty, illness, or fear, "they relapse into disbelief. " They perform an about-face, interpreting their hardship as a sign that Islam has failed them or that Allah has abandoned them, and so they apostatize. The phrase "relapse" translates a word that means to turn on one’s heels, a sudden and complete reversal. This person had entered Islam not for Allah Himself but for a desirable worldly outcome, so when the outcome turns undesirable, their allegiance also turns. The verdict on such a person is severe: they end up "losing this world and the Hereafter. " They lose the Hereafter because they died in a state of disbelief after their apostasy. They lose the world because their apostasy did not magically solve their worldly problems; they remain afflicted, but now without the spiritual anchor and community support that faith provides.

The verse concludes, "That is truly the clearest loss," emphasizing that no loss is more obvious or more absolute than gaining neither the world nor the Hereafter.

=12=

This verse unveils the inner mechanism of the half-hearted worshipper’s relapse. When they turn away from Allah in a moment of trial, they do not turn to nothing; they turn to something else. "They call besides Allah what can neither harm nor benefit them." The objects they invoke are utterly powerless idols, whether made of stone, or being figments of imagination like saints, graves, or worldly powers they believe can rescue them. The reality, however, is that these false gods have no agency whatsoever. They cannot inflict harm if displeased, nor can they deliver benefit if pleased. This exposes the irrational desperation of the half-hearted person: in abandoning the All-Powerful Lord who controls all harm and benefit, they flee to something that controls absolutely nothing. Their relapse, in practical terms, is a flight from a fortress to a mirage.

The verse then delivers its judgment: "That is truly the farthest one can stray. " The straying here is described as "farthest," meaning it has reached the ultimate degree of misguidance. When a person knows the truth, then leaves it for a falsehood that is so obviously impotent, they have wandered into a depth of error from which return is extremely difficult. This serves as a warning to anyone whose faith is contingent on comfort: true faith means turning to Allah in both ease and hardship, knowing that a trial is not a sign of His abandonment, but a test of sincerity, and that fleeing from Him only leads to a far worse loss.

=13=

This verse adds a terrifying dimension to the nature of the false objects of worship. The previous verse said they can neither harm nor benefit. Here, the reality is corrected: "They invoke those whose worship leads to harm, not benefit." Far from being neutral, the worship of false gods actively brings harm. This harm includes spiritual destruction in this world and eternal torment in the Hereafter. The idols themselves will be fuel for the Fire, and those who worshipped them will be thrown in with them. So the half-hearted person who abandons Allah for these gods is not escaping trial but diving headfirst into absolute ruin.

The verse ends with an exclamation of condemnation: "What an evil patron and what an evil associate! " The word "patron" refers to a protector or guardian, and "associate" refers to an intimate companion. On the Day of Judgment, these false gods will disown their worshippers, severing all bonds. The worshipper will be left alone, with the entity they hoped would save them now being their greatest enemy. The language is vivid and despairing, capturing the shock and regret of the one who wakes up to find his trust was placed in something foul. This verse acts as a final warning to the half-hearted, to remind them that if they leave the True Protector, the alternative is not simply emptiness but an actively destructive force.

=14=

After the warning to the half-hearted and the disbelievers, the surah returns to the consistent Quranic pattern of pairing a threat with a promise. The phrase "Indeed, Allah will admit" provides certainty. The condition is simple and balanced: "those who believe and do good." Belief and righteous action must go hand in hand.

This is the opposite of the half-hearted who believe on the edge and whose actions are conditional on divine gifts. Here, the believers trust Allah in all circumstances, and their faith manifests in consistent good deeds. The reward is "Gardens, under which rivers flow.

" This image of a lush, well-watered paradise directly counters the barrenness of a life without true faith. The flowing rivers signify an ever-renewing source of joy, ease, and sustenance, a stability that never runs dry, unlike the fleeting good of this world that the half-hearted so desperately sought.

The verse then concludes, "Surely Allah does what He wills." This is not a random statement but a direct rejoinder to the mentality of those who think their worship obligates Allah to give them a comfortable life. Allah is not bound by human expectations. He admits into His mercy whom He wills, according to His wisdom. For the sincere believer, this phrase is deeply comforting because it means the All-Wise, All-Merciful is in control of their destiny, and His will is perfectly aligned with ultimate goodness. The believer’s trust is placed in the Will of Allah, not in a transactional expectation of worldly comfort.

=15=

This is a powerful, dramatic verse addressed to those who harbor rage and hope for the failure of the Prophet’s mission. The phrase "Whoever thinks that Allah will not help His Prophet" identifies the cynical enemy who believes that Islam will be defeated and that divine support is a myth. For such a person, tormented by the success of the faith, the verse offers a sarcastic but profound challenge. "Let them stretch out a rope to the ceiling" has been interpreted in a few ways by the scholars. The most fitting interpretation here is that a person is told to tie a rope to the ceiling of his house, put it around his neck, and hang himself.

The phrase "and strangle themselves" translates a word that means to cut off breath or life. Then, "let them see if this plan will do away with the cause of their rage." The meaning is devastatingly clear: if you are so enraged and jealous at the success Allah grants His Prophet that you cannot bear to live, then kill yourself in your fury; but even that will not stop Allah’s help from arriving. The victory of the truth is independent of the emotional discomfort of its enemies. This verse is an intense psychological analysis of the enemies of faith; their rage is a self-consuming fire. It also contains a hidden comfort for the believers: the plots and anger of the opponents are essentially futile acts of self-harm. Allah’s help is coming regardless, and their rage will only end in their own destruction, whether literally or metaphorically.

=16=

This verse connects the preceding arguments to the source of guidance, the Qur’an itself. "And so We revealed this Quran" uses the particle "kadhālika," meaning "thus" or "in this way," linking it to the proof of Allah’s power and truth. The Qur’an is described as "clear verses," meaning its signs, proofs, and teachings are lucid, self-evident, and distinct from human speech. The clarity is both intellectual and spiritual.

The verse then states, "And Allah certainly guides whoever He wills. " This is a foundational principle. Divine guidance is not merely a result of human intellect reading a clear text; it requires Allah’s will to open the heart. A person can read the clearest verses, as the Quraysh did, and still not be guided because they lack the will of Allah to guide them.

This statement, however, should not be misunderstood as arbitrary. Allah’s will to guide is linked to a person’s sincerity and striving. As another verse states, those who strive in His way, He guides to His paths. Here, it serves as a humbling reminder for the believer not to be arrogant about their faith, knowing that guidance is a gift from Allah, and a warning to the disputant who relies on his own intellect against the clear verses; without Allah’s will, his intellect will only lead him deeper into darkness.

=17=

This verse expands the scope of divine judgment to all of humanity’s religious groups. It lists six categories: the believers (Muslims), Jews, Sabians (a monotheistic group mentioned in the Qur’an), Christians, Magi (Zoroastrians, fire-worshippers), and the polytheists (idol worshippers). The listing moves roughly from groups with a claim to a scripture to those without. The crucial statement is, "Allah will judge between them all on Judgment Day. " This means that the final truth about theological differences will not be settled by debate, power, or number of followers in this world, but solely by Allah’s judgment on the Day of Resurrection. This takes the pressure off the believers to force conversions; their duty is to convey, while judgment is reserved for Allah. It is also a terrifying warning to those who are comfortable in their inherited religions: all claims will be verified and judged.

The verse ends, "Surely Allah is a Witness over all things. " His judgment will be based on complete, firsthand knowledge. He witnesses not only the outward doctrines but the inner states of every human being: what they truly knew, what doubts they suppressed, and whether their adherence was sincere or an act of tribalism.

This verse establishes the principle of religious accountability and provides a framework for coexistence: we all will return to Him, and He alone will clarify our differences.

=18=

This majestic verse begins with the rhetorical question "Do you not see," urging the listener to open their inner vision. It declares that everything in the universe is in a state of submission to Allah. "To Allah bow down in submission all those in the heavens and all those on the earth" refers to the angels and all creatures, respectively. Their bowing is their obedience to the natural laws and cosmic order decreed for them. Then it lists the inanimate and animate: "the sun, the moon, the stars, the mountains, the trees, and all living beings. " The sun’s rising and setting, the orbit of the moon, the growth of trees, the instinct-driven life of animals—all of this is a form of prostration, an involuntary obedience to the Creator. "As well as many humans" indicates that some humans willingly and consciously join this cosmic worship through their free will, establishing prayer and obeying Allah’s law. But then the verse notes, "while many are deserving of punishment." These are the humans and jinn who refuse the voluntary submission and rebel.

The verse then presents a stark reality: "And whoever Allah disgraces, none can honour. " Disgrace here is the result of their own choices, which Allah confirms as their final state. Once Allah has decreed humiliation for a soul on the Day of Judgment, no intercessor or power can elevate it. This verse is a profound meditation on the harmony of the universe, and the deep discordance of a sinning human being who, unlike a tree or a mountain, refuses to bow.

=19=

This verse explicitly identifies "These" as the two groups just mentioned: "many humans" who are punished and the believers mentioned previously. They are "two opposing groups," literally "two adversaries," who have taken diametrically opposed stances "about their Lord." Their disagreement is about His oneness, His attributes, the Resurrection, and His law. The verse immediately details the fate of one group: the disbelievers. The description is terrifyingly tangible. "Garments of Fire will be cut out for them." In this world, clothing is for protection, adornment, and modesty. In Hell, their garments will be cut from the very material of their punishment: fire.

The word "cut out" suggests a tailor-made suit, as if their specific punishment is precisely tailored to their specific crimes and bodies. Then, "and boiling water will be poured over their heads.

" The word for boiling water here is hameem, a water heated to an unimaginable extreme. The pouring is from above, enveloping them completely. This dual torment—an external garment of fire and a liquid fire poured over the head from which there is no shelter—conveys total, inescapable suffering. It serves as the first detail of the punishment that contrasts directly with the "gardens under which rivers flow" for the believers. Instead of shaded coolness and silk garments, they have fire and scalding liquid.

This is the direct outcome of their adversarial stand against the truth.

=20=

This verse continues the horrifying description of the boiling water’s effect. The word "melting" translates a verb that means to dissolve or liquify. The boiling water does not merely scald the surface; it penetrates and works from the inside out. "Whatever is in their bellies" refers to their internal organs, their intestines, and the very core of their physical being.

This is a punishment that reaches the innermost depths of a person, leaving nothing untouched. The phrase "along with their skin" indicates that the melting is not confined to the interior; the external covering is also dissolved. In the normal world, skin is the barrier that protects the insides. Here, that barrier offers no protection whatsoever. The inside and the outside are melted together. This graphic description serves multiple purposes. It highlights the complete impotence of the human body against the divine punishment. It also serves as a deterrent of unimaginable intensity. The skin, which was the vessel for sin in the world—the hands that struck, the lips that cursed, the ears that listened to slander—is now the direct recipient of the torment. The dissolving of the insides symbolizes the complete exposure and dissolution of the inner evils that were hidden during life.

This is a precise and terrifying depiction of justice, given in verses that are meant to shake the core of the listener, following directly from the warning of the two opposing groups.
