=21=

The description of the punishment intensifies as we move from the internal melting to the external instruments of torment. The verse says, "And awaiting them are maces of iron.

" The word "awaiting" suggests these instruments are prepared and ready, stationed like guards for their arrival. The term "maces" refers to heavy, iron clubs or hammers, weapons of brutal force designed to crush and shatter.

In the context of Hell, these are not ordinary iron implements but creations of the Hereafter, unimaginably massive and wielded by relentless angels. The choice of iron is significant; in this world, iron is a symbol of strength, warfare, and durability. In Hell, it becomes an instrument of unending agony, striking the inhabitants without mercy.

This verse adds a layer of active, personal punishment to the passive suffering of fire and boiling water. The disbelievers are not left to simply burn; they are beaten and crushed. The psychological horror of this verse lies in the anticipation: the maces are awaiting them, meaning the torment is already prepared and inevitable. There is no escape, no appeal, no reduction of sentence.

The mention of these maces immediately after the melting of skin and organs shows that the punishment is multifaceted and comprehensive. No part of their being will be exempt. The body that was used for arrogance, for striking others unjustly, for walking proudly on the earth, will now be struck by rods of iron. It is a precise and terrible recompense.

For the believer, this image reinforces the urgency of humility and justice in this life; for the skeptic, it serves as a dire warning that the power they abused will be turned against them in a form they cannot resist.

=22=

The previous verses described the physical torments, and this verse now captures the psychological dimension of hopelessness. It says, "Whenever they try to escape from Hell—out of anguish—they will be forced back into it." The inhabitants of Hell will not calmly accept their fate; the sheer intensity of the suffering will drive them to frantic attempts at fleeing.

The phrase "out of anguish" explains that their escape attempts are not rational plans but desperate, pain-induced reactions. They might try to climb its walls, run from its flames, or even momentarily leave its boundaries, but the result is always the same: "they will be forced back into it." The angels of punishment, wielding those iron maces mentioned just before, will drive them back violently. This cycle of attempted escape and forced return constitutes a unique torture, where hope is perpetually raised for a fleeting second only to be crushed. Then, they are addressed with a statement that compounds the physical pain with humiliation: "Taste the torment of burning! " The word "taste" implies a direct, sensory experience, as if they are made to savor their punishment. It is also a direct echo of their crime; they tasted the fleeting pleasures of disobedience in the world, and now they are made to taste its eternal consequence.

This command is a form of rebuke, reminding them that this is what they were warned about and what they denied. The verse paints an image of utter captivity; Hell is not merely a place of passive suffering but an active prison where every movement towards relief is violently repelled.

=23=

Following the extended and terrifying portrait of Hell, the surah now pivots with the word "But" to the contrasting fate of the believers. This pattern of pairing warning with glad tidings is a hallmark of the Quran’s merciful style.

The verse begins with certainty: "Allah will surely admit those who believe and do good into Gardens, under which rivers flow." This is the same image of Paradise offered earlier in the surah, but now it is expanded with exquisite details. The believers are not merely admitted; they are adorned. "Where they will be adorned with bracelets of gold and pearls" introduces a physical beauty and luxury that directly counters the iron maces and boiling water of Hell. Gold and pearls are treasures of this world that humans strive for and hoard; in Paradise, they are given freely as ornaments, signifying honor, wealth, and purity. Pearls, in particular, represent something precious formed through a process of patience and transformation, much like the believer’s soul was formed through trials.

The verse then adds, "and their clothing will be silk. " Silk in this world was a fabric often associated with royalty and luxury, and its use in Paradise indicates the highest level of comfort and refinement. Significantly, silk is forbidden for men in this worldly life according to many scholars, yet it is promised in the next, showing that the restrictions of the lower life are replaced by the pure delights of the higher. This detailed description serves to make Paradise tangible and desirable, pulling the heart towards the actions that lead to it.

=24=

This verse explains the reason why the believers received such an honor. It begins with "for," indicating causality. Their external adornment is the result of an internal spiritual state. "They have been guided to the best of speech." The best of speech, al-tayyib min al-qawl, refers first and foremost to the declaration of faith: "There is no god but Allah. " It also encompasses all good words: the recitation of the Quran, the remembrance of Allah, sincere advice, truthfulness, and kind speech. Their tongues were adorned with this pure speech in the world, so their bodies are adorned with gold, pearls, and silk in the Hereafter.

The second quality is "they have been guided to the Commendable Path." The Commendable Path is the path of al-Hameed, which is one of Allah’s names, meaning the Praiseworthy. This path is the way of life approved by Allah, the straight path that leads to Him. It is the path of all the prophets, particularly the path of Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. The guidance to this path is a divine gift that requires the believer's striving. So the verse establishes a profound symmetry: inner purity and beautiful speech are the adornments of the soul, and they manifest outwardly as the luxurious adornments of Paradise. This contrasts with the disbelievers, whose hearts were filled with foul beliefs and whose speech was falsehood, and so their outward reality became garments of fire and boiling water. The connection is clear: what you cultivate inwardly now will become your outward experience for eternity.

=25=

The surah now shifts its focus to a specific historical context while maintaining its universal principles. It addresses the Meccan pagans who not only rejected the truth but actively prevented others from embracing it. "Those who persist in disbelief" indicates an entrenched, stubborn state, not a momentary doubt. Their crime is twofold: they disbelieve themselves and they "hinder others from the Way of Allah." This hindrance could be through persecution, propaganda, or blocking access to sacred spaces. The verse specifically mentions hindering "from the Sacred Mosque," the Masjid al-Haram in Mecca. This was a direct reference to the Quraysh who prevented the Prophet and his companions from performing pilgrimage after the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah or earlier. The parenthetical statement, "which We have appointed for all people, residents and visitors alike," establishes a fundamental principle of the Kaaba: it is a universal house of worship for all humanity, not the exclusive property of any tribe or nation. The Quraysh prided themselves on being the custodians of the House, yet they barred the believers from it, violating the very sanctity they claimed to protect.

The verse then broadens the warning: "along with whoever intends to deviate by doing wrong in it." This covers any future individual who enters the Sacred Mosque with the intent to commit injustice or heresy within its precincts. The punishment is "a painful punishment," a general threat that encompasses both worldly defeat and the Hereafter. This verse laid the groundwork for the eventual liberation of Mecca and the restoration of the Sacred Mosque as a center of pure monotheism.

=26=

To further condemn the Quraysh’s misappropriation of the Sacred Mosque, the verse takes us back to its true origins. "And remember when We assigned to Abraham the site of the House." This reminds the Arabs, who were the descendants of Abraham through Ishmael, of their own noble heritage. It was Abraham, their forefather, who was shown the original foundations of the Kaaba, which had been laid by Adam or lost, and was commanded to rebuild it with his son. The "site" was divinely assigned, meaning the location itself is a matter of revelation, not human choice.

The first command given to him was, "Do not associate anything with Me in worship." This establishes the foundational purpose of the House: pure, unadulterated monotheism. The pagans had inverted this completely, filling the House with idols.

The second command is, "and purify My House. " Purification is both physical and spiritual; removing filth and idols, and dedicating it solely for the worship of the One God. The verse then lists the acts of worship for which the House is designated: "for those who circle the Ka’bah, stand in prayer, and bow and prostrate themselves." Tawaf, the circling, is a unique act of devotion specific to the House. Standing, bowing, and prostrating describe the movements of the formal prayer. This verse thus provides the divine charter for the Sacred Mosque, directly challenging the Quraysh’s authority. It declares that the true legacy of Abraham is not tribal pride, but the worship of Allah alone, and that any who fill it with idols have betrayed that legacy and must be removed.

=27=

Continuing the commands to Abraham, this verse establishes the global and timeless nature of the Hajj. "Call all people to the pilgrimage." According to Islamic tradition, after Abraham completed the building of the Kaaba, he was commanded to proclaim the pilgrimage to humanity. He reportedly said, "O Lord, how can I convey to the people when my voice cannot reach them?" Allah replied that it was upon him to make the call, and upon Allah to deliver it.

Thus, this proclamation transcended time and space, and the response to it continues until the end of time. The verse then promises the outcome: "They will come to you on foot and on every lean camel from every distant path." The response to Abraham’s call is a diverse and determined stream of humanity. People will come "on foot," indicating humility, effort, and sacrifice. Some would walk for months to reach the House. They will also come "on every lean camel." The description of the camels as "lean" highlights the difficulty of the journey; the animals themselves are worn out by the long travel through harsh terrains. "From every distant path" emphasizes the universal draw of this call. It is not a local shrine but a center for all nations. This verse is a miracle in action, visible every year during Hajj when millions of people from every corner of the globe, by every means of transport, arrive in Mecca. They come as the spiritual descendants of Abraham’s guests, fulfilling a prophetic proclamation made millennia ago. It reinforces the theme that the Sacred House belongs to Allah and to all believers, not to one tribe.

=28=

This verse lists the purposes and blessings of the pilgrimage. The first is "so they may obtain the benefits in store for them.

" The word "benefits" encompasses both the spiritual rewards of the Hereafter and the worldly benefits. Worldly benefits include the economic exchange that happens during Hajj, the strengthening of community bonds, and the learning that occurs when Muslims from diverse cultures meet. Spiritual benefits include the forgiveness of sins, the attainment of Allah’s pleasure, and the profound renewal of faith.

The second purpose is "and pronounce the Name of Allah on appointed days over the sacrificial animals He has provided for them." This refers to the ritual of animal sacrifice during the days of Eid al-Adha. The act of pronouncing Allah’s name over the animal is a declaration that life is taken only by His permission and for His sake, a rejection of pagan practices where animals were slaughtered in the name of idols.

The phrase "He has provided for them" reminds the pilgrim that the animal itself is a provision from Allah. The command then follows: "So eat from their meat and feed the desperately poor." This establishes the Islamic etiquette of sacrifice: it is not a burnt offering where the meat is wasted or given only to the priests, but a shared meal. The pilgrim eats, signifying that they are partaking in a divine blessing, and they must give special attention to the "desperately poor," a more emphatic term than ordinary poverty, indicating those in extreme need. This combines gratitude, celebration, and social responsibility in one sacred ritual.

=29=

After mentioning the major rituals of sacrifice, the verse outlines the concluding acts of the pilgrimage. "Then let them groom themselves" translates a word that means to remove one’s untidiness or bodily hair. During the state of ihram, pilgrims are forbidden from cutting their hair, clipping their nails, and other grooming acts.

This command signals the exit from the state of ihram, allowing them to return to a normal state of cleanliness and appearance. It symbolizes a fresh start, a shedding of the physical tokens of a completed, rigorous spiritual journey.

The second command is "fulfil their vows." During Hajj, or in their lives leading up to it, people may make vows to Allah to perform certain acts of worship or charity if a specific need is met.

This command demands that these vows be honored. It teaches the principle of integrity with Allah; a promise made, especially in the sacred context, must be kept.

Finally, "and circle the Ancient House." This refers to the Tawaf al-Ifadah, the essential circumambulation of the Kaaba that is a pillar of Hajj. The House is called "the Ancient House," emphasizing its primordial and timeless nature. It was the first house of worship established for humanity. By circling it, the pilgrim joins a cosmic orbit, aligning with the angels who circle the heavenly Bait al-Ma'mur and the very rotation of planets around their centers.

The sequence in this verse moves from personal cleansing, to ethical fulfillment of oaths, to the culminating act of devotion, providing a complete arc of spiritual renewal.

=30=

The verse begins with "That is so," an emphatic affirmation of the preceding rites. It then declares a general principle: "And whoever honours the rituals of Allah, it is best for them in the sight of their Lord." Honoring the rituals, including the sacred territory, the sacrificial animals, and the rites of Hajj, is not about the physical objects themselves but about the reverence in the heart. The honor one shows is a direct reflection of their taqwa, and its reward is stored with Allah.

The verse then makes a legislative statement: "The meat of cattle has been made lawful for you, except what has already been recited to you." This refers to the general lawfulness of meat from camels, cows, sheep, and goats, with specific exceptions already revealed, such as carrion, blood, pork, and that on which a name other than Allah’s has been invoked. This clarification was necessary because the pagans had invented their own superstitious prohibitions on certain cattle. Following this, the verse delivers two powerful commands: "So shun the impurity of idolatry, and shun words of falsehood. " Shunning idolatry, al-awthan, is a direct attack on the physical idols and the spiritual corruption they represent. Shunning words of falsehood, al-zoor, means avoiding every form of lying, false testimony, and deceitful speech.

The pairing is profound: idolatry is the greatest internal spiritual impurity, and false speech is its external social manifestation. A person cannot be truly pure of faith while their tongue is comfortable with lies. The verse thus links ritual purity with moral and verbal integrity.

=31=

This verse commands a pure, unwavering orientation of the soul. "Be upright in devotion to Allah" translates the term hunafa, the plural of hanif, which refers to one who turns away from all falsehood and inclines solely to the truth. This was the original religion of Abraham. The state of being a hanif is a spiritual posture that rejects every crooked path and faces Allah directly. The command is immediately clarified: "associating none with Him in worship. " This is the essence of the hanif’s devotion.

The verse then uses one of the most powerful similes in the Quran to describe the state of the polytheist: "For whoever associates others with Allah is like someone who has fallen from the sky and is either snatched away by birds or swept by the wind to a remote place." The image is one of utter destruction and loss. The person falling from the sky represents a fall from the natural, high station of pure monotheism, the fitrah, into the void of polytheism. The descent is violent and completely out of control. The falling body is then preyed upon by birds, torn apart in mid-air, its pieces scattered, symbolizing how the devils snatch away the faculties of the polytheist. Alternatively, a fierce wind might sweep the person and hurl them to a "remote place," a far-off, desolate ruin where they are lost and forgotten.

This is a picture of a soul in absolute freefall, disintegrating, with no anchor, no safety, and no hope of recovery. It is the reality of a person who cuts their connection to the One God and attaches themselves to scattered, powerless entities.

=32=

The phrase "That is so" again affirms the preceding commands and truths. The verse then returns to the theme of honoring Allah’s symbols, or sha’a’ir. "Whoever honours the symbols of Allah" refers to showing respect and veneration to the outwardly visible signs of the religion, including the rites of Hajj, the sacred months, the sacrificial animals, and the boundaries of the Haram. This honor is not a superficial or ritualistic act.

The verse clarifies its inner source: "it is certainly out of the piety of the heart. " The act of veneration is not caused by the inherent power of the symbols themselves, but it stems from and is evidence of a heart that possesses taqwa. The heart that is deeply conscious of Allah’s greatness will naturally venerate the things that Allah has designated as sacred.

This statement is a profound principle of Islamic spirituality: the condition of the heart determines the value of the outward act. If the heart is filled with God-consciousness, the believer will treat the rituals with the utmost care, not as empty customs, but as beloved signposts from the Beloved.

This verse also serves as a reply to those who might argue that honoring inanimate objects like the Kaaba or the stone markers is a form of shirk. The Quran clarifies that the honor is not to the stone itself, but is a manifestation of the heart’s piety toward Allah, who commanded that these symbols be respected. The entire pilgrimage is thus an education in obeying God’s commands without needing to fully grasp the rationale, a pure act of loving submission rooted in the heart.

=33=

This verse provides specific regulations for the sacrificial animals, or hady. It begins by permitting a period of utility: "You may benefit from sacrificial animals for an appointed term." This refers to the animals that pilgrims bring to Mecca for sacrifice. They are allowed to derive certain benefits from these animals during the journey, such as riding a camel or drinking the milk of a she-camel, provided the animal is not harmed and it remains designated for sacrifice. This shows the ease and mercy in Islamic law; the animal is not a sacred, untouchable object until the moment of slaughter. The "appointed term" refers to the time of Hajj, specifically the Day of Sacrifice, the tenth of Dhul-Hijjah, and the following days. Once that term arrives, "their place of sacrifice is at the Ancient House." This means the final destination where the blood of the sacrifice is to be shed is within the sacred precincts of the Haram, the area surrounding the Ancient House, the Kaaba. The ruling here emphasizes that the Hajj sacrifice is connected to the Sacred Mosque, and distributing its meat there ensures that the poor of the sacred land benefit. It also harks back to the mission of Abraham, who was commanded to proclaim the pilgrimage. The entire process—from the raising and transport of the animal, to the permitted benefits, to the final sacrifice at the Holy House—is an act of devotion that connects the daily life of the pilgrim to the sacred center of the faith.

=34=

This verse reveals that the institution of sacrifice is not an invention of Islam but a universal prophetic tradition. "For every community We appointed a rite of sacrifice" indicates that every nation that received a prophet was given some form of ritual slaughter as an act of worship. This connects the Muslim practice to a global, historical chain of devotion, refuting the notion that it is a strange or barbaric ritual. The purpose is always the same: "so that they may pronounce the Name of Allah over the sacrificial animals He has provided for them." The core of the rite is the verbal acknowledgement of Allah's bounty and His exclusive right over life and death. Pronouncing His name is an act of consecration, transforming a mundane act of taking a life into a sacred act of worship.

The verse then states the foundational theological reason behind the unity of the rite: "For your God is only One God." Because the Creator is one, the acts of worship directed to Him are essentially the same in their core, even if their outward forms differ across eras. The conclusion is "so submit yourselves to Him alone. " The unity of God demands the unity of submission.

The verse then turns to the Prophet with a comforting directive: "And give good news O Prophet to the humble." The humble, al-mukhbitin, are those whose hearts are soft, who bow to the truth without arrogance. This good news is a reward for a specific spiritual quality that will be detailed in the next verse, providing a beautiful transition from the external act of sacrifice to the internal state of the worshipper.

=35=

This verse continues from the previous one by defining the "humble" who receive the good news. The first quality is "those whose hearts tremble at the remembrance of Allah." This trembling is not a tremor of terror that drives one away, but a tremor of awe, love, and acute awareness. When Allah is mentioned, their hearts are not hard or indifferent; they are moved, filled with a sense of His majesty and their own dependence.

This is a sign of a living heart. The second quality is "who patiently endure whatever may befall them." Patience, or sabr, is the steadfastness in the face of trials, pain, and misfortune. They do not panic, despair, or turn away from Allah when calamity strikes. Instead, they recognize that everything is from Allah’s decree and hold firm to their faith. This directly counters the half-hearted worshipper described in verse 11 who relapses at the first trial.

The third quality is "and who establish prayer." The establishment of prayer is a constant, disciplined turning to Allah five times a day, the practical embodiment of the heart's remembrance. The fourth is "and donate from what We have provided for them.

" This is the translation of internal humility into external generosity. They recognize that their provision is not truly their own but a trust from Allah, so they spend it in His cause. This completes the portrait of a true believer: a heart alive with the remembrance of Allah, a soul patient under trial, and a body engaged in worship and charity. These are the ones who truly benefit from the pilgrimage and all acts of sacrifice.

=36=

The surah returns to the practical details of the animal sacrifice, specifying the camels and cattle, or budn. "We have made sacrificial camels and cattle among the symbols of Allah." These large, valuable animals are elevated to the status of sacred symbols.

The phrase "in which there is much good for you" acknowledges their worldly and spiritual benefits. They provide transport, milk, meat, and hides, and their sacrifice brings immense divine reward. The instruction follows: "So pronounce the Name of Allah over them when they are lined up for sacrifice." The camels are made to stand in a row, their left legs tied, and they are then stabbed in the hollow of the neck while standing, a specific method of slaughter. At that moment, the name of Allah is invoked, turning the animal from a physical asset into a spiritual offering. "Once they have fallen lifeless on their sides," confirming their death, "you may eat from their meat." The owner and his family may partake of the sacrifice, a privilege not found in some other religious traditions. Then the command expands to include two categories of the needy: "the needy—those who do not beg, and those who do.

" The first is the person who, despite their poverty, refrains from asking out of dignity; the second is the person who openly asks. The verse commands feeding both, showing Islam's care for the hidden poor and its respect for human dignity.

The verse ends by reminding that these massive creatures are "subjected" to humans as a mercy from Allah, and the only proper response is "so that you may be grateful."

=37=

This is perhaps the most profound verse on the philosophy of sacrifice in the Quran. It directly confronts a primitive or ritualistic misunderstanding: "Neither their meat nor blood reaches Allah." Allah, the Self-Sufficient, has no need for the physical substance of the sacrifice. He does not consume, nor does the physical offering appease Him in a transactional way, as pagan gods were imagined to be fed. This shatters any notion of a mechanical ritual.

The verse then gives the positive reality: "Rather, it is your piety that reaches Him." The word used is taqwa, God-consciousness. What ascends to Allah and is accepted is the sincerity of the heart, the intention to obey, the love and fear that motivated the act. The butchering of the animal is simply the external expression of an inner state of submission.

This principle transforms every act of worship from a dead motion into a living communion. The verse then restates the purpose of the subjected animals: "This is how He has subjected them to you so that you may proclaim the greatness of Allah for what He has guided you to." The sacrifice is an occasion to magnify Allah, to say "Allahu Akbar," acknowledging that the guidance to perform this rite, and all the blessings that led to it, are from Him.

The verse ends with a repeated command to the Prophet: "and give good news to the good-doers." The muhsinoon, those who achieve excellence in worship by doing it as if they see Allah, are the true beneficiaries of this guidance. Their sacrifice, though its material does not reach Allah, is received with full acceptance because of the taqwa that accompanied it.

=38=

This verse marks a major thematic transition in the surah, moving from the rites of pilgrimage to the reality of struggle and self-defense. It begins with a powerful promise: "Indeed, Allah defends those who believe.

" The word "defends" means He wards off the plots, attacks, and evils of their enemies. This is a statement of divine principle and a direct assurance to the early Muslim community, which had endured over a decade of persecution without permission to retaliate. The believers are defended not because of their strength or numbers, but because of their faith.

The second part of the verse provides the corresponding warning: "Surely Allah does not like whoever is deceitful, ungrateful." The word "deceitful" translates khawwan, an exaggerated form meaning habitually treacherous.

The word "ungrateful" is kafoor, an intensive form of disbelief and ingratitude. This describes the enemies of the believers, specifically the Quraysh, who betrayed treaties, persecuted the innocent, and were utterly ungrateful for the blessings of security and provision they enjoyed. By stating that Allah does not love these types of people, the verse implies that His defense will be withdrawn from them and His punishment will descend. It sets up the moral and legal grounds for the permission to fight that follows in the next verse. The believers are the ones worthy of divine protection, while the treacherous and ungrateful have forfeited it, and the instrument of Allah's defense will soon be the hands of the believers themselves.

=39=

This is the first verse revealed that permitted armed struggle in Islam, a landmark moment in the history of the Muslim community. For approximately thirteen years in Mecca, the Muslims were commanded to endure persecution, insults, torture, and even murder, without raising a hand in retaliation. This verse changed that. "Permission to fight back is hereby granted" uses the verb udhina, meaning a formal allowance is given from the highest authority. The permission is restricted to "those being fought," indicating that the fighting is defensive in nature. The cause is explicitly stated: "for they have been wronged.

" The word "wronged" translates zulimu, an all-encompassing term for oppression, injustice, and violation of rights. The early Muslims were driven from their homes, their property was seized, and their companions were killed. This permission was not a call to aggression but a license to resist aggression.

The verse then provides the guarantee of success: "And Allah is truly Most Capable of helping them prevail." Their eventual victory is not promised by their swords but by the power of Allah. This verse refutes the misconception that Islam was spread by the sword in an aggressive manner. The permission to fight was tied directly to the fact that they were already being attacked and wronged. It also shows the divine balance: the believers were trained in patience and forbearance until they had the spiritual strength to handle conflict without becoming transgressors. Now, the time for passive endurance had passed, and the time for establishing justice through strength had begun.

=40=

This verse continues the description of the wronged ones and expands the philosophy of defensive fighting into a universal principle. It identifies the believers as "those who have been expelled from their homes for no reason other than proclaiming: 'Our Lord is Allah.'" Their sole "crime" was monotheism. Their expulsion from Mecca was an act of ethnic and religious cleansing.

The verse then gives a breathtakingly universal justification for the principle of armed defense: "Had Allah not repelled the aggression of some people by means of others, destruction would have surely claimed monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques in which Allah’s Name is often mentioned. " This is one of the most inclusive and protective statements in the Quran regarding the freedom of worship. The list begins not with mosques, but with the places of worship of previous faiths: the cells of monks, churches, and synagogues. This establishes that the defense of the Muslim community is also, by extension, a defense of all houses where God is worshipped. The fighting is not to conquer or destroy places of worship, but to protect them from being demolished by tyrants. The mechanism is that Allah uses some humans to check the aggression of others; without this, the strong would wipe out the weak and silence all prayer.

The verse ends with the affirmation: "Allah will certainly help those who stand up for Him. Allah is truly All-Powerful, Almighty." Helping Allah means helping His cause and His religion.

This is the conclusion and the assurance: the struggle is not just a fight for survival but a sacred service to protect the light of God on earth.
