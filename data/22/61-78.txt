=61=

The verse begins with "That is because," linking it directly to the preceding promise that Allah would certainly help the one who is wronged again after a just retaliation. The reason this divine help is certain and effortless for Allah is His absolute control over the entire cosmic order. The verse points to a specific sign of this control: "Allah causes the night to merge into the day, and the day into the night." This merging is a gradual, flawless process that happens every single day without fail. The night does not abruptly crash into the day, nor does the day violently shatter the night. Instead, they flow into one another with perfect precision, lengthening and shortening according to an unalterable decree. This daily miracle, which has continued since the creation of the heavens and the earth, demonstrates Allah's effortless power over time, light, and darkness. The One who manages this immense cosmic alternation without any assistant or interruption is certainly not incapable of helping an oppressed believer.

The verse then ends with two divine names that are profoundly connected to the context of the oppressed: "Indeed, Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing." He is All-Hearing of the pleas, the cries, and the supplications of those who are wronged. He is not unaware of whispered injustices. He is All-Seeing of the secret plots, the hidden injuries, and the tears shed in the darkness.

The mention of hearing and seeing right after the sign of the night and day implies that just as the alternation of night and day is under His constant watch, so too are the affairs of the oppressors and the oppressed. The help may be delayed, but it is not absent, because the One who hears and sees everything is also the One who controls the very fabric of time. This verse reassures the believer that their case is not lost in the noise of the universe; it is being monitored by the All-Hearing and All-Seeing, and the power that merges night and day will inevitably bring the light of justice to swallow the darkness of oppression.

=62=

The phrase "That is because" again links back to the divine attributes and the promise of help, providing the ultimate metaphysical reason for Allah's absolute authority and the believers' reliance on Him. "Allah alone is the Truth." This is a foundational declaration of Islamic theology. Allah is al-Haqq, the Ultimate Reality upon which all other realities depend. His existence is necessary and self-subsisting, while everything else is merely possible and dependent. In stark contrast, "what they invoke besides Him is falsehood." The idols, the intermediaries, the saints, the desires, the ideologies—anything worshipped or relied upon outside of Him—are baatil, which means vanishing, perishing, and devoid of real essence. They possess no real power, no real hearing, no real sight. The conflict between the believers and the disbelievers is fundamentally a conflict between Truth and falsehood. Because Truth is inherently strong and enduring, and falsehood is inherently weak and fleeting, the final victory must belong to the truth and its people.

The verse then crowns this declaration with two more majestic names: "and Allah alone is truly the Most High, All-Great." The Most High, al-Aliyy, indicates His transcendence above all creation in His essence, attributes, and power. Nothing is above Him; He is the Highest. The All-Great, al-Kabeer, indicates His supreme grandeur and majesty, before which everything else is small and insignificant. The polytheists may have strutted with arrogance in the land, thinking themselves high and great, but this verse declares that true Highness and Greatness belong exclusively to Allah.

For the believer, this is an immense source of courage. If Allah is the Most High, then no tyrant can truly debase the one who stands with the Truth. If Allah is the All-Great, then no worldly obstacle is large enough to block His help. The verse serves as both a comfort and a warning, placing the entire struggle within the framework of a cosmos governed by the Absolute Truth.

=63=

The surah continues to pile evidence upon evidence of Allah's power and mercy, moving from the cosmic sign of night and day to the botanical sign of rain and vegetation. The rhetorical question "Do you not see" appeals to the reader's direct observation of the natural world. "Allah sends down rain from the sky." Rainwater, pure and life-giving, descends from the clouds. It is a substance that in itself is simple, yet its effect is miraculous. "Then the earth becomes green. " The Arabic word used for "becomes green" is a verb that vividly captures the transformation: the barren, dusty, lifeless ground suddenly puts on a garment of lush vegetation. This transformation is an annual resurrection that the disbelievers witness but fail to internalize. The One who sends down water and revives the dead earth is the same One who will send down the water of life and resurrect the dead from their graves, a theme that has been running through the surah.

The verse then concludes with two beautiful names that illuminate the mechanism of this miracle: "Surely Allah is Most Subtle, All-Aware. " The name Lateef, translated as Most Subtle, carries meanings of gentleness, kindness, and deep, penetrating knowledge. His subtlety is seen in how He delivers the water in precise amounts, how He causes the seed to split underground in a process invisible to the human eye, and how He brings forth the exact colors and shapes of plants with intricate design.

The name All-Aware, Khabeer, indicates His complete awareness of the inner workings of the soil, the needs of every root and leaf, and the perfect timing of the seasons. This verse invites the believer to trust in the same Subtle and Aware Lord who will find the most delicate and precise means to deliver His help and revive the fortunes of His oppressed servants, just as He revives the dead ground with gentle rain.

=64=

This verse provides the logical conclusion to the signs that preceded it. If Allah controls the night and day and revives the dead earth, it is because of the foundational fact that "To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth." His ownership is absolute, exclusive, and comprehensive. He owns every atom, every planet, every creature, and every event. Nothing escapes His dominion. This ownership is not a passive title; it is an active, controlling, and sustaining ownership. The implication is that because He owns everything, He has the absolute right to command, to give, to withhold, to honor, and to humble. The disbelievers who think they own their power or their wealth are deluded; they are merely temporary tenants in a property that belongs entirely to Allah.

The verse then adds two of Allah's most profound attributes: "Allah alone is truly the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy." The Self-Sufficient, al-Ghani, means that He is free of all need. He does not require sustenance, help, or worship from His creation. His ownership is not to fulfill a lack in Him, but is a consequence of His own perfection. The Praiseworthy, al-Hameed, means that He is inherently deserving of all praise, whether His creation praises Him or not. His actions, His laws, and His decree are all wise, beautiful, and praiseworthy in their ultimate outcome. This verse is a powerful antidote to both the arrogance of the oppressor and the anxiety of the oppressed. The oppressor should know that he possesses nothing that is not under Allah's ownership, and the Self-Sufficient One has no need to tolerate injustice. The oppressed should know that his Lord is Ghani and therefore not in need of any payment or favor from tyrants; He will judge with perfect justice because He is Praiseworthy in all His judgments.

=65=

This verse is a direct address to humanity, highlighting the blessings of the natural world that have been made subservient to human beings. "Do you not see that Allah has subjected to you whatever is in the earth." The word "subjected" translates sakhkhara, meaning He tamed, made compliant, and placed at the service of mankind. Everything on earth—the animals, the minerals, the plants, the water, the very terrain—is designed in a way that allows humans to utilize, build, and thrive.

This is not a result of human ingenuity alone, but of a prior divine act of taming. "As well as the ships that sail through the sea by His command. " A ship, a massive structure of wood or steel, floats and moves across the vast, often violent ocean, not by the power of the sailor alone, but by the laws of physics that Allah has decreed and maintains. The sea itself, which could swallow the ship, is held back by His command.

The verse then points to a more terrifying reality: "He keeps the sky from falling down on the earth except by His permission." The sky, with all its celestial bodies, is held aloft without visible pillars, purely by the command of Allah. The possibility of its falling is real if He willed it, but He withholds it out of mercy.

The verse ends with the reason: "Surely Allah is Ever Gracious and Most Merciful to humanity." The subjection of the earth and the safety of the sky are not rights that humanity deserves, but gifts from the Ra'uf, the Ever-Gracious, and the Rahim, the Most Merciful. This verse should fill the heart with gratitude. The One who, out of mercy, prevents the sky from crushing us is certainly the One who, out of mercy, will hear the cry of the oppressed. The grace that holds the cosmos is the same grace that responds to the supplication of the broken-hearted.

=66=

The surah now moves from the external signs in the cosmos and earth to the personal sign within the human life cycle. "And He is the One Who gave you life." The origin of human life is a mystery that no scientist can replicate from absolute nothingness. Allah is the al-Muhyi, the Giver of Life. He initiated the life of every human being, from the first parent to the last child. Then, "then will cause you to die." Death is not a random decay or a failure of medicine; it is a positive act of divine will. The Imamat, the Causing of Death, is as much a divine act as the giving of life. The One who gave life without your asking will take it back at an appointed time. Then, "and then will bring you back to life.

" The final resurrection is the third act in this sequence, and logically, if the first two are acknowledged, the third should not be difficult to accept. The same power that started the cycle will complete it. Despite this obvious logic and the daily evidence of sleep as a minor death and waking as a minor resurrection, the verse ends with a sorrowful observation: "But surely humankind is ever ungrateful. " The word "ungrateful" here is kafoor, an intensive form of kufr. Human beings, in general, tend to be deeply and persistently unthankful. They receive the gift of life without payment, enjoy the subjection of the earth, breathe the air, and yet live in a state of forgetfulness and denial of the Giver. This ingratitude manifests as a refusal to worship the One God, a refusal to use the subjected earth responsibly, and a refusal to prepare for the day when life is given back. This verse is a mirror held up to the soul, asking every reader to check their own level of gratitude and to see if they are among the "ever ungrateful" or among the few who are truly thankful.

=67=

This verse reveals a profound principle in the history of religion: the diversity of divine laws over time. "For every community We appointed a code of life to follow." The word "code" translates mansak, which can mean a ritual, a law, a way of worship, or a comprehensive way of life. This indicates that while the core creed of all prophets was the same pure monotheism, the specific laws, dietary rules, and rituals given to different nations could differ according to divine wisdom, suited to their particular times and circumstances. The Quraysh and the People of the Book would sometimes argue with the Prophet, saying that his law differed from the law of Moses or Abraham. The verse instructs: "So do not let them dispute with you O Prophet in this matter." The Prophet is told not to be drawn into useless arguments about why the Islamic Shariah takes a particular form. The fact that a previous community had a different law does not invalidate the new one. The command is then balanced with a positive action: "And invite all to your Lord. " Instead of arguing about the secondary details of the law, the Prophet’s focus should be on the primary call: inviting all humanity to the worship of the One Lord.

The verse ends with the assurance: "for you are truly on the Right Guidance." The Prophet is not an innovator or a deviant; he is squarely on the path of divine guidance.

This verse also teaches an important lesson for Muslims today: the variations in rulings between Islamic schools of thought or between different prophetic dispensations should not be a cause for internal strife. The focus must remain on calling people to Allah, while knowing that the ultimate truth of the specific rulings rests with Him. It cultivates a spirit of tolerance alongside a firm commitment to the guidance one has received.

=68=

This verse provides a concise and powerful strategy for dealing with argumentative opponents who are not seeking truth but merely engaging in endless debate. "But if they argue with you," meaning if they persist in disputation after the truth has been made clear, and their argument is not a sincere inquiry but a tactic to confuse and delay, the Prophet is given a specific response. "Then say, 'Allah knows best what you do.'" This is not an admission of defeat, but a profound shift of the matter to the ultimate tribunal. It means: the discussion is over between us, and I leave your case to the All-Knowing. Your actions, your intentions, your hidden agendas, and your stubborn denial are all perfectly known to Him. He is the one who will deal with you justly, and no argument you present to me will avail you before Him. This response serves several purposes. It cuts off a futile argument that would only waste the Prophet's energy and time. It maintains the dignity of the caller by not descending into insults or frustration. It plants a seed of fear in the heart of the stubborn opponent by reminding them that their behavior is being recorded by the All-Knowing. And it demonstrates a complete reliance on Allah's judgment. For the modern reader, this verse offers an essential model for handling toxic debates about religion, where the other party is not interested in guidance but in scoring points. Sometimes the most spiritually mature response is to disengage with dignity and entrust the matter to Allah, who sees all deeds.

=69=

The verse expands the principle of entrusting the matter to Allah by specifying the exact time and place of the final resolution. "Allah will judge between you all on Judgment Day regarding your differences." The "you all" includes the Prophet and his opponents, the believers and the disbelievers, and indeed every group that ever differed over religion. The differences are not just minor juristic points but the major issues of faith: the oneness of God, the truth of the resurrection, the authenticity of the scriptures. The promise of judgment on the Day of Resurrection serves as the ultimate source of comfort for the believers and the ultimate source of dread for the unjust. In this world, truth and falsehood often appear mixed, and the truthful may suffer while the liars prosper. The existence of a final court, where the Judge is the All-Knowing and All-Wise, is the only guarantee of complete justice.

This verse teaches patience and long-term perspective. The believer does not need to see the defeat of every opponent in this life, because he knows that the final, irreversible verdict is reserved for the Creator. It also serves as a warning against excessive sectarianism; since Allah will judge all differences, a believer must be humble in their own understanding and not claim infallible knowledge of matters where there is legitimate difference. The verse reinforces the theme introduced earlier in the surah about Allah being the judge between the believers, Jews, Sabians, Christians, Magi, and polytheists, creating a powerful bookend to that discussion.

=70=

The verse continues to establish the basis for Allah's perfect judgment by affirming the completeness and accessibility of His knowledge. "Do you not know that Allah fully knows whatever is in the heavens and the earth?" The rhetorical question appeals to the innate human recognition that the Creator must be All-Knowing. His knowledge encompasses the visible and the invisible, the past, present, and future, the intentions, the actions, and the consequences. This knowledge is not a burden to Him.

The verse then provides a striking image: "Surely it is all written in a Record." The Preserved Tablet, or al-Lawh al-Mahfuz, is the divine archive where every event, every atom, and every destiny is inscribed. This writing predates creation itself. The purpose of mentioning this Record is to emphasize that judgment on the Day of Resurrection will not be based on a hazy memory or a reconstructed file; it is a precise, documented account that leaves no room for error or omission.

The verse then concludes with the ultimate statement of divine omnipotence: "That is certainly easy for Allah." To know everything and to record everything in infinite detail is, for Allah, an act of effortless simplicity. No information overload, no database is too large.

This statement dismantles the analogy of human limitations that might cause a person to doubt the feasibility of a universal, individual judgment. For Allah, it is easy. This verse is profoundly soothing for the believer who fears that their hidden good deeds are lost or that the evil of the oppressors is unseen. Nothing is lost; everything is in a Record that is managed with absolute ease by the Lord of the Worlds.

=71=

After the magnificent description of Allah’s all-encompassing knowledge and power, the surah returns to the pathetic reality of the polytheists. "Yet they worship besides Allah that for which He has sent down no authority." The word "authority" here translates sultan, which implies a proof, a warrant, or a clear permission. Allah has never revealed any scripture or sent any prophet to command or permit the worship of idols. The entire institution of idolatry is a human invention with no divine sanction whatsoever.

This is the first flaw in their worship: it lacks external divine authorization. The second flaw is internal: "and of which they have no knowledge." The polytheists do not even have a rational, evidence-based argument for worshipping these objects. Their "knowledge" is merely blind imitation of their forefathers, based on guesswork and myths. They have no scripture, no miracle, and no logical proof that these stones can bring benefit or harm. Their act is thus doubly baseless: it is neither sanctioned by the Creator, nor supported by the intellect of the creation.

The verse then delivers the sobering verdict: "The wrongdoers will have no helper." The term "wrongdoers" identifies them precisely. Their primary wrong is the injustice of shirk, placing creation in the place of the Creator. On the Day of Judgment, when they desperately need an intercessor or a savior, they will find no one. The idols they called upon will disappear, and no other power will come to their aid.

This is the fruit of building one's spiritual life on a foundation of nullity.

=72=

This verse provides a vivid psychological portrait of the disbelievers' reaction to the Quran. "Whenever Our clear revelations are recited to them, you O Prophet recognize rage on the faces of the disbelievers." The Quran is described as "clear revelations," its proofs are manifest and its language is lucid.

Yet, instead of opening their hearts, its recitation causes an intense, visible rage. Their faces contort, their expressions darken.

This is the rage of the arrogant when their falsehood is exposed by the light of truth. The metaphor intensifies: "as if they are going to snap at those who recite Our revelations to them." They are barely restraining themselves from physically attacking the reciters. Their fury is so overwhelming that they lean forward, ready to pounce and tear apart the believers with their teeth, like enraged beasts.

This image captured the reality in Mecca where the Prophet and his companions were jeered, threatened, and assaulted for simply reciting the words of God. The verse then instructs the Prophet to deliver a chilling, ironic reply: "Say, 'Shall I inform you of something far more enraging than that?'" You are enraged by the recitation of truth? There is something else that awaits you which should, if you truly understood, make you forget this rage in an instant. "It is the Fire with which Allah has threatened those who disbelieve." Your current rage is nothing compared to the terror you will feel when you see the Fire.

The verse ends with the exclamation, "What an evil destination!" This is a profound rhetorical strategy. It acknowledges their worldly anger and redirects it to its ultimate consequence, turning their mockery and fury into a warning against their own selves. It tells them: the thing you should really be worried about is not the words of the Quran, but the Fire you are hurtling toward because of your rejection of it.

=73=

The surah now delivers one of the most powerful parables in the Quran, a devastating intellectual challenge to idolatry. The address is broad: "O humanity! A lesson is set forth, so listen to it carefully." The parable demands full attention because it contains a truth that shatters the very foundations of polytheism. The challenge is stark and simple: "those idols you invoke besides Allah can never create so much as a fly, even if they all were to come together for that." A fly, the smallest, most common, and seemingly most insignificant of creatures, is an absolute miracle of creation. Its tiny wings, compound eyes, and intricate nervous system cannot be replicated by all the false gods of humanity, even if they pooled their powers. The idols are not just less powerful than Allah; they are utterly incapable of creating the most minuscule form of life. The challenge then deepens: "And if a fly were to snatch anything away from them, they cannot even retrieve it from the fly." If a fly lands on an idol and takes away a speck of perfume, a drop of the food placed before it, or a grain of dust, the idol, being an inanimate object, cannot defend itself or reclaim what was taken. The worshippers would have to shoo the fly away themselves. This demonstrates that the idols are not only incapable of creating, but they are also incapable of protecting even themselves.

The verse ends with the conclusion: "How powerless are those who invoke and those invoked!" Both the worshipper and the worshipped are described as powerless. The worshipper is weak for placing his hopes on a dead object, and the object is weak because it has no agency whatsoever. This parable is a masterclass in logical argument, using a tiny fly to expose the cosmic absurdity of worshipping anything other than the All-Powerful Creator. It remains a timeless challenge to any worldview that venerate powerless intermediaries.

=74=

The parable of the fly leads directly to this profound conclusion. "They have not shown Allah the reverence He deserves." The word "reverence" here translates the concept of estimating Allah's true measure, or qadr. The polytheists, by associating with Allah entities that are so pathetically weak that they cannot even create or protect themselves from a fly, have utterly failed to appreciate the greatness, power, and majesty of the Creator. Their estimation of Allah is catastrophically low. To attribute a partner to the One who created the seven heavens and the earth, who holds the universe in perfect balance, is a fundamental failure of cognition and morality.

This verse reveals that the root of all sin, especially shirk, is a failure to properly know and honor Allah. If a human being truly grasped, even in a small measure, who Allah is, they would fall in prostration and never dare to place a created object alongside Him.

The verse then reiterates two of Allah's names that directly contrast with the pathetic weakness of the idols: "Surely Allah is All-Powerful, Almighty. " He is Qawiyy, possessing absolute strength, and Aziz, possessing invincible might. The idols are weak; He is Strong. The idols are despicable; He is Mighty. This verse is a call to the intellect and the heart to recalibrate one's perception of the Divine. It teaches that true reverence for Allah requires recognizing His unique, unparalleled power and rejecting any analogy between Him and His creation. The failure to show this reverence is not just a theological mistake; it is the ultimate injustice that leads to the ultimate ruin.

=75=

In contrast to the idols that are chosen by men out of ignorance, this verse declares the divine selection of messengers. "Allah selects messengers from both angels and people." This is a reference to the fact that Allah has chosen certain individuals from humanity, like the prophets, and certain individuals from the angels, like Gabriel, to be messengers. This selection is an act of pure divine will and wisdom. It is not a position that can be earned or claimed by lineage, wealth, or human election. The Quraysh objected to a human being chosen as a messenger, arguing that an angel should have been sent. This verse responds that Allah selects whom He wills from both categories according to His own criteria.

The verse then grounds this selection in two attributes: "for Allah is truly All-Hearing, All-Seeing." His selection is not arbitrary; it is based on His perfect knowledge. He hears the words and the inner pleas of all beings, and He sees their outward actions and inward qualities. He knows which soul is most fit to carry the burden of revelation and which angel is best suited to deliver it.

The mention of these attributes also reassures the believers that Allah hears the mockery of the disbelievers about His choice of a human messenger and sees their plots. This verse teaches that religious authority and prophetic office are not subject to human opinion. It cultivates a humility in the believer to accept the means and the people that Allah has chosen for guidance, rather than demanding that guidance come on their own terms.

=76=

This verse expands on the basis of Allah's perfect selection. "He knows what is ahead of them and what is behind them." The "them" refers to the messengers, both angelic and human, as well as to all of creation. "What is ahead of them" is the future, the mission, the challenges, and the events leading up to the Day of Judgment. "What is behind them" is the past, their creation, their lineage, their previous deeds, and the entire history leading up to their existence. Allah’s knowledge surrounds them from every side. This comprehensive knowledge ensures that His selection of a messenger is perfectly suited to the needs of the mission. He knows the past of the messenger, proving his integrity, and He knows the future, preparing him for the trials to come.

The second part of the verse provides the ultimate endpoint: "And to Allah all matters will be returned for judgment." All affairs, including the mission of the messengers, the response of the people, the oppression, the patience, and the apparent delays in justice, will be brought back to Allah for a final, comprehensive judgment.

This is a deeply comforting conclusion for the believers. The message they carried, the suffering they endured, and the choices of those they called to faith—all of it will come before the All-Knowing Judge. Nothing is lost, nothing is forgotten. The verse beautifully closes the loop of the entire surah, which has been oscillating between the signs of Allah's power, the truth of the message, and the certainty of the final return.

=77=

The surah now reaches its final instructions to the believing community, a call to action that summarizes their entire religious duty. The verse opens with a direct, loving address: "O believers!

" This is a call to those who have already accepted faith, urging them to embody it fully. The first commands are physical acts of worship: "Bow down, prostrate yourselves. " These are the movements of the formal prayer, the salah. Interestingly, the order here is bowing (ruku') before prostration (sujud), which matches the sequence in the prayer but also highlights that prostration is the highest degree of physical submission. The call to bow and prostrate is a call to constant, disciplined, bodily worship that humbles the flesh and the spirit. Then the command broadens: "worship your Lord. " This encompasses all acts of devotion, seen and unseen, that constitute 'ibadah. Worship is the purpose of life. After establishing the vertical relationship with the Creator, the command extends to the horizontal: "and do what is good." The good, al-khayr, includes every righteous deed, charity, kindness to others, and moral excellence.

The verse then gives the desired outcome: "so that you may be successful." The word "successful" here is the ultimate falah, a word that signifies not just worldly prosperity, but eternal salvation, the achievement of all that is good and the avoidance of all that is evil. This verse is a compact formula for the Islamic life: prayer, worship, and good deeds are the path to true success. It answers the earlier warnings of the surah about the dreadful Hour by providing the practical daily program that ensures one is not among the losers on that Day.

The sequence of commands starts with the body, moves to the heart, and then moves to the community, showing that success is holistic.

=78=

The final verse of the surah is a magnificent, all-encompassing call to the Muslim community, defining their identity, mission, and source of strength. It begins with the command: "Strive for the cause of Allah in the way He deserves.

" This is the call to jihad, a comprehensive striving with one's self, wealth, and life to establish the truth and defend the faith. The striving must be "in the way He deserves," which is a striving that is sincere, according to His law, and undertaken with the utmost effort, recognizing His infinite greatness.

The verse then provides the honor and the motivation: "for it is He Who has chosen you." Allah has selected this community out of humanity to be the bearers of the final message, an immense privilege and responsibility.

The next phrase is a profound declaration: "and laid upon you no hardship in the religion." The religion of Islam, in its essence, is not a burden that breaks the back. Its rulings are balanced, its obligations are bearable, and concessions are granted in times of need.

This is a rejection of monastic extremism and a celebration of the middle path. This religion is then described as "the way of your forefather Abraham." The Muslim community is spiritually linked to Abraham, the hanif, the upright monotheist.

The verse then highlights the divine gift of identity: "It is Allah Who named you 'the ones who submit' in the earlier Scriptures and in this Quran." The word "Muslims" is not a name invented by the Prophet; it is a title given by Allah in previous scriptures and in the final revelation. This gives the community a deep sense of historical and heavenly roots. The purpose of this naming and choosing is then revealed: "so that the Messenger may be a witness over you, and that you may be witnesses over humanity. " The Prophet will testify on the Day of Judgment that he delivered the message to his community, and the Muslims, in turn, will testify that the message was delivered to all mankind.

This is a mission of witness-bearing, calling humanity to account through their own upright example and truthful proclamation. The verse then returns to the practical pillars that sustain this mission: "So establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and hold fast to Allah." Prayer connects them to Allah, charity connects them to the needy, and holding fast to Allah, seeking His protection and relying on Him, is the source of all strength.

The surah ends with the glorious, comforting proclamation: "He alone is your Guardian. What an excellent Guardian, and what an excellent Helper!" After the long journey through the warnings of the Hour, the proofs of resurrection, the rites of pilgrimage, the permission to fight, and the stories of destroyed nations, the final word is one of intimate protection. The Guardian, al-Mawla, is the protector, the ally, the master. The Helper, al-Naseer, is the one who brings victory. The surah that began with a violent quaking ends on a note of serene trust in the Most Excellent Protector and Helper.

This is the ultimate destination of the journey of faith: from the fear of the dreadful thing to the safety of the divine embrace.
