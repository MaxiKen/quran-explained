=41=

This verse defines the character and mission of the believers whom Allah has promised to help and defend. After granting them permission to fight and promising them victory, Allah now describes what they will do with that victory. The verse says, "They are those who, if established in the land by Us, would perform prayer, pay alms-tax, encourage what is good, and forbid what is evil.

" The word "established" means given authority, security, and power in the earth. This is not a promise of power for its own sake, but power with a purpose.

The first thing they would do is "perform prayer." The establishment of prayer is the foundation of their rule, signifying that their governance would not be secular or materialistic, but rooted in a living connection to Allah.

The second is "pay alms-tax," meaning they would institute a system of social welfare and economic justice, purifying wealth and caring for the needy. The third is "encourage what is good," which encompasses every virtue, every righteous act, and every beneficial social norm. The fourth is "forbid what is evil," meaning they would actively work to discourage and prevent vice, corruption, and oppression.

This is a complete political and social manifesto in a single verse. It is a direct refutation of the tyrants who, when established in the land, spread corruption, neglect the poor, and hinder the worship of Allah.

The verse ends with a profound reminder: "And with Allah rests the outcome of all affairs." The believers do not act as independent sovereigns; they are aware that the final result, the ultimate success or failure, is entirely in Allah's hands. This keeps them humble in victory and reliant on Him, not on their own strength.

=42=

After giving the believers the great promise of authority and the permission to fight, the surah now turns to the Prophet to console him in the face of rejection. The Quraysh were denying him, mocking his message, and plotting against him.

This verse begins, "If they deny you O Prophet," acknowledging the painful reality of his situation. The word "if" here does not indicate doubt, but is a conditional clause that sets up the comparison. The response is immediate: "so did the people of Noah before them." The story of Noah is the archetypal story of a prophet who was rejected by his people for centuries, yet remained steadfast. By mentioning him first, Allah reminds the Prophet that he is walking in the footsteps of the most patient of messengers. Then the verse adds, "as well as the tribes of 'Ad and Thamud. " These were two mighty Arabian nations that were destroyed for their denial. 'Ad were the people of the prophet Hud, known for their physical strength and tall buildings. Thamud were the people of the prophet Salih, known for carving homes out of mountains. Both were far more powerful materially than the Quraysh, yet their power did not save them from divine destruction. This historical reference serves a dual purpose: it comforts the Prophet by showing him that rejection is the norm for the call to truth, and it warns the Quraysh that their denial places them in the lineage of nations that were utterly wiped out. The message is clear: history repeats itself, and those who deny the truth are lining up for the same fate.

=43=

The list of destroyed nations continues with two closely related prophets. "The people of Abraham" refers to his people in Mesopotamia who worshipped idols. Abraham was their own countryman, a man of pure reason and logic, who debated them and shattered their idols.

Yet they rejected him and even attempted to burn him alive. Allah saved him and left them in their eventual decline.

This is a particularly pointed example for the Quraysh, because they prided themselves on being the descendants of Abraham through Ishmael. If their own forefather's people had denied him and suffered, then their denial of Muhammad, who came with the same Abrahamic faith, made them hypocrites and worthy of the same fate.

The verse then mentions "the people of Lot." Lot was Abraham’s nephew and was sent to the cities of the plain, a people who practiced sexual immorality of a kind previously unknown. Their rejection was not just of a theological proposition, but of a moral and natural law. They denied Lot and threatened to expel him. Their destruction was spectacular and total; the earth itself was turned upside down on them. Mentioning these two prophets together links the crime of idolatry with the crime of sexual perversity, showing that both represent a rebellion against the divine order, and both lead to severe punishment. The Quraysh, who combined idolatry with a range of social injustices, were being told that they were walking a path that had led to total annihilation before.

=44=

The historical survey continues with "the residents of Midian." Midian was the people of the prophet Shu'ayb, who were guilty of economic crimes: cheating in trade, giving short measure, and spreading corruption in the land. Their rejection led to a devastating earthquake or a cloud of fire that destroyed them. This adds another layer of warning to the Quraysh, as their own economic boycotts and unjust business practices against the Muslims echoed the crimes of Midian. Then the verse states, "And Moses was denied too." Moses is mentioned last, possibly because his story of confronting Pharaoh is the most detailed in the Quran and the closest parallel to the struggle in Mecca. Pharaoh denied Moses despite the clear signs of the staff, the hand, and the plagues.

Yet Moses was patient, and Pharaoh was ultimately drowned. The verse then shifts from the specific examples to the divine principle of justice: "But I delayed the fate of the disbelievers until their appointed time then seized them." This directly addresses the taunt of the Quraysh who would ask why punishment did not fall on them immediately. Allah explains that the delay is a matter of a fixed term, not neglect. When that term ends, the seizure comes. "And how severe was My response! " The word "response" translates nakeer, which carries the meaning of a change from a state of grace to a state of punishment, a drastic and terrifying turn of events.

The question form intensifies the warning, leaving the listener to imagine the unimaginable.

=45=

The general principle of the previous verse is now painted in a visual, almost cinematic image. "Many are the societies We have destroyed for persisting in wrongdoing." The phrase "persisting in wrongdoing" indicates that these societies were not destroyed for a single mistake but for a continuous, entrenched state of oppression and sin. The destruction was complete: "leaving them in total ruin.

" The word "ruin" here can mean their roofs caved in, their walls collapsed, their cities utterly silent and desolate. Then the verse adds two specific and poignant details: "Many are also the abandoned wells and lofty palaces!" The "abandoned wells" are the water sources in the desert that once served thriving communities, now dry and unused, the sand slowly reclaiming them. The "lofty palaces" are the grand, fortified structures built with immense effort and pride, now standing empty, their inhabitants gone. These two images speak to the necessities of life and the peaks of luxury, both rendered useless. The well symbolizes survival, the palace symbolizes power and culture. When a people is destroyed, their wells are left for the wild animals and their palaces become the homes of owls. This verse is a direct invitation to the Quraysh to reflect on the ruins they themselves passed by during their trade journeys to Yemen and Syria. The remains of 'Ad, Thamud, and Midian were visible landmarks in the Arabian landscape. The verse asks them to see these ruins not as mere relics of the past, but as sermons written in stone, testifying to the fate of every wrongdoing civilization that denied its warner.

=46=

Building on the imagery of ruined civilizations, Allah asks a probing question: "Have they not travelled throughout the land so their hearts may reason, and their ears may listen?" The Quraysh were a traveling merchant people; their winter and summer journeys were famous. They passed by the ruins of past nations, they saw the inscriptions, they heard the legends. The purpose of travel, in the divine plan, is not merely commerce or tourism, but to gain an intelligent heart.

The phrase "so their hearts may reason" uses the word ya'qiloona, which means to tie down, to understand rationally. The seat of this reasoning is the heart, not just the brain. A heart that is alive integrates sensory information and draws moral and spiritual conclusions. Similarly, "their ears may listen" means to listen with comprehension and acceptance, not just to hear sound waves.

The verse then delivers one of the most profound statements in the Quran about the nature of disbelief: "Indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but it is the hearts in the chests that grow blind." Physical blindness is a disability and a test, but it does not prevent a person from knowing the truth. Many who could physically see were spiritually blind. The blindness of the heart is a self-inflicted condition; it "grows blind" over time due to repeated rejection, arrogance, and sin. The eyes see the ruined walls and empty wells, but the signal is never processed by a heart that has shut itself down. This verse is a diagnosis of the Quraysh's condition and a timeless warning against the arrogance that prevents a person from learning from the fate of others.

=47=

The Quraysh, in their arrogance, would mockingly ask the Prophet when the punishment he threatened would arrive. This verse addresses that challenge directly: "They challenge you O Prophet to hasten the torment." This was a common tactic of the deniers, believing that the delay in punishment was proof of its falsehood. They mistook divine forbearance for divine impotence. The response comes in layers. First, a definitive statement: "And Allah will never fail in His promise." The punishment is absolutely certain; its delay does not mean it has been cancelled. It will come at the exact moment decreed. Second, an explanation for the seeming delay from the human perspective: "But a day with your Lord is indeed like a thousand years by your counting." This does not mean that a divine day equals exactly one thousand calendar years in a mathematically rigid way; it means that time with Allah is of a completely different nature and scale. What seems like a long delay to a human lifetime of seventy years is but a fleeting moment in the divine timeline. Allah is not slow or forgetful; He is operating on a timescale of perfect wisdom. This concept is deeply comforting for the oppressed believer who is waiting for justice, and terrifying for the oppressor who thinks he has gotten away with his crimes. The thousand-year day reminds us that the Day of Judgment itself will be a day of immense length, and the respite given to the wrongdoers is not a sign of safety, but a part of the very torment that is being prepared for them, allowing them to fill their measure of sin.

=48=

This verse reinforces the message of the previous one by generalizing the principle. "Many are the societies whose end We delayed while they did wrong." The delay, or imla', is an active divine strategy. Allah grants the wrongdoers more time, more wealth, and more health, not as a reward, but as an istidraj, a gradual luring toward destruction. They continue in their wrongdoing, growing more arrogant, more entrenched, and more deserving of a severe punishment.

The word "while they did wrong" indicates that the delay occurred during their active commission of injustice. They were not repenting; they were persisting. Then, at the precise moment, "then seized them." The seizure, akhdh, is sudden and complete. It does not come with a warning; the door of respite simply slams shut.

The verse ends with the ultimate destination: "And to Me is the final return." Regardless of when the worldly destruction occurs, and even if a society seems to die a natural death and fade from history, every single individual from that society will be brought back to Allah for an individual accounting.

The final return negates any final escape. This verse is a chilling reminder that prosperity and longevity are not signs of divine approval if they are accompanied by wrongdoing. The true measure is not the delay, but the return.

For the believer, this creates a mindset that does not envy the temporal success of the unjust, knowing that their account is simply accumulating interest that will fall due.

=49=

After the detailed discussion of past nations and the promise of seizure, the command comes to the Prophet to clarify his role in the clearest possible terms. "Say, O Prophet, ‘O humanity! I am only sent to you with a clear warning.’" The address is to all of humanity, not just the Quraysh, reinforcing the universal scope of his mission.

The word "only" is crucial; it negates any ulterior motives or powers. He is not claiming the ability to bring about the punishment himself, nor is he claiming knowledge of when it will happen. He is not an angel or a lord over them. He is a warner with a clear message. The warning is "clear," meaning its evidence is plain, its language is understandable, and its content leaves no ambiguity about the consequences of disbelief. This verse serves as a boundary between the Prophet's responsibility and Allah's exclusive authority. The Prophet delivers the warning; the outcome of belief or disbelief, and the timing of punishment, is entirely with Allah. This absolves the Prophet of the taunts to hasten the torment, as if the torment were under his control. It also places the full weight of accountability on the listeners: they have received a clear warning from a truthful warner; if they choose to ignore it, they have no excuse. The verse is a model of how religious callers should view their role: as conveyors, not as enforcers of results.

=50=

Following the clear definition of the Prophet's role, the verse now summarizes the two ultimate outcomes. It begins with the positive side: "So those who believe and do good will have forgiveness and an honourable provision." The conjunction "so" links this promise directly to the clear warning. Those who respond to the warning with faith and righteous action are described. Faith is the internal acceptance of the truth, and doing good is the external manifestation of that faith. Both are required; a bare confession without action, or action without faith, does not qualify. The reward has two components.

The first is "forgiveness," which deals with the past. Every human has sins, shortcomings, and failures.

The first gift of Paradise is the erasure of these sins, the wiping clean of the slate, so that the believer enters the next life without the burden of guilt or the anxiety of punishment. The second component is "an honourable provision.

" The word "honourable" translates kareem, which means generous, noble, and delightful. This provision is the never-ending sustenance of Paradise, given without toil, without account, and without the taint of humiliation. Unlike the provision of this world, which can be a test or even a cause for punishment, the provision of Paradise is purely a gift of honor. This verse is a capsule of hope, a short but complete description of salvation that invites the listener to reach for it.

=51=

The contrast to the believers is now presented with a very specific verb: "strive to discredit Our revelations." The word "strive" translates sa'aw, meaning to exert effort, to run, to work hard. These are not passive skeptics who have an honest doubt; they are active campaigners against the truth. They invest their time, energy, and resources in attacking, mocking, and undermining the signs of Allah. Their goal is to render the revelations ineffective, to cause people to doubt them, to create intellectual barriers to faith. This describes the leaders of Quraysh who organized poetry competitions to drown out the Quran, who spread slander about the Prophet, and who tortured the believers to make them recant. The punishment fits the crime: just as they strived to discredit the verses, they will strive to escape the Fire and fail. Their destination is definitive: "they will be the residents of the Hellfire. " The word "residents" indicates a permanent abode; they are not temporary visitors.

This verse establishes that while intellectual mistakes can be forgiven by Allah if they are in good faith, the weaponization of doubt, the intentional effort to lead people away from the truth after it has become clear, is a path to the deepest level of Hell. It serves as a stern warning to those in every era who use their platforms and talents to attack the foundations of faith.

=52=

This verse addresses a deep spiritual reality and a historical event that served as a test. It begins by stating a pattern in prophetic history: "Whenever We sent a messenger or a prophet before you O Prophet and he recited Our revelations, Satan would influence people’s understanding of his recitation." The distinction between "messenger" and "prophet" covers all categories. Whenever a divine message was delivered, Satan would launch a counter-operation. He would cast into the minds of the listeners doubts, mishearings, and false interpretations. In some specific cases, as narrated in certain reports about the "satanic verses" incident (whose authenticity is debated among scholars), Satan might even cause the listeners to mishear words. The key point, however, is the divine guarantee: "But eventually Allah would eliminate Satan’s influence.

" The word "eliminate" means to abrogate, to blot out, to make vanish. Whatever confusion, falsehood, or misguidance Satan tries to inject into the process of revelation, Allah ensures that it is purged and that the pure message remains preserved. "Then Allah would firmly establish His revelations." The revelations stand clear, definitive, and authoritative.

The verse ends with two divine names: "And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise." His knowledge encompasses Satan's plot and the human hearts affected by it. His wisdom dictates that this test should occur, to separate the sincere from the hypocrite, as the next verse will explain. This verse is profoundly comforting to the believers: the truth is divinely protected, and the whispers of Satan, no matter how loud, will be silenced and erased from the final, established scripture.

=53=

The verse explains the divine wisdom behind allowing Satan's temporary influence. "All that so He may make Satan’s influence a trial." The whispers of Satan, the doubts cast around the revelation, serve as a test. They separate people into categories.

The first category is "those hypocrites whose hearts are sick. " The disease in the heart is doubt, hypocrisy, and a craving for fitna. When they hear a confusion, they latch onto it, use it as an excuse, and spread it further.

The second category is "those disbelievers whose hearts are hardened." Their hearts are not just sick; they are calcified, unable to accept the truth at all. The satanic influence gives them ammunition to mock and reject. So the same event that purifies the believers and increases their faith becomes a stumbling block for the sick and hardened.

The verse then concludes, "Surely the wrongdoers are totally engrossed in opposition." The word "opposition" translates shiqaq, a state of being in a separate camp, entrenched in enmity and dispute. They are not merely passive disbelievers; they are active opponents, far off the path. The trial, therefore, brings their hidden disease to the surface, allowing them to be identified and allowing them to dig their own spiritual graves.

This verse teaches that doubt, when it comes, is a crossroads. The sincere believer seeks clarity and remains patient until the truth is established; the sick person dives into the doubt and makes it a home.

=54=

This verse completes the wisdom behind the divine management of satanic interference. It gives the positive outcome for the sincere. "This is also so that those gifted with knowledge would know that this revelation is the truth from your Lord." When Allah eliminates Satan's influence and firmly establishes His clear verses, the people of knowledge, those who have deep understanding and sound intellect, see the process as proof of the Quran’s divine origin. They observe how falsehood is exposed and truth is secured, and their certainty deepens.

The phrase "so they have faith in it" indicates an increase in their iman, a renewal and strengthening. The next stage is even deeper: "and so their hearts would submit humbly to it.

" The word "submit humbly" translates the verb for khushu', a state of profound humility, stillness, and submission of the heart. The knowledge of the truth moves from the mind to the heart, producing a spiritual state of serene surrender.

The verse ends with the general promise: "And Allah surely guides the believers to the Straight Path." This is the guidance of tawfiq, the divine enabling that leads the believer, step by step, through the trials and confusions of life, onto the path that reaches Allah. The verse is a celebration of the intellectual and spiritual elite, the scholars and sincere seekers, who are not shaken by the noise of Satan but are guided through it to a stronger faith.

=55=

The surah now turns from the guided to the stubborn. "Yet the disbelievers will persist in doubt about this revelation." The word "persist" indicates a continuous, unbroken state. They are not moving toward belief; they are stuck in their skepticism. The doubt is not an honest search for truth but a position of comfort, a shield against moral commitment.

The verse then gives the endpoint of this doubt: "until the Hour takes them by surprise." The Hour, the moment of death or the final Resurrection, will come upon them while they are still in their state of doubt. The element of surprise is crucial; they are not given the luxury of a final, calm reflection. Death seizes them in the midst of their denial, and at that point, faith is no longer accepted. Alternatively, "or the torment of a terminating Day comes to them." This could refer to a severe punishment in this world that puts an end to them, or the Day of Judgment itself, described as "terminating" because it leaves no tomorrow. It cuts off all hopes, all plans, and all opportunities. The verse is a sobering reminder that the window for resolving doubt is not indefinite. Procrastination in matters of faith is a gamble with an unknown deadline. The disbelievers think they have time, but the Hour has no calendar, and the torment has no warning siren.

=56=

The focus shifts to that terminating Day itself, describing its fundamental reality. "All authority on that Day is for Allah alone." In this world, authority is distributed, contested, and usurped. Kings, parliaments, judges, and tyrants all claim a share. But on that Day, every false claim collapses.

The word "authority" translates mulk, meaning sovereignty and dominion. Allah's sovereignty, which was always the ultimate truth but hidden by the veil of worldly causes, becomes manifest and uncontested.

The first act of this absolute sovereignty is judgment: "He will judge between them." The "them" refers to all the differing groups mentioned earlier in the surah, the believers and the various types of disbelievers and hypocrites. His judgment is final, just, and based on complete knowledge.

The verse then immediately gives the verdict for the first group: "So those who believe and do good will be in the Gardens of Bliss." The "Gardens of Bliss" is the highest category of Paradise, a place of pure delight, ease, and happiness. The particle "so" connects their good end to the just judgment of Allah.

This is their vindication. The verses that follow will detail the contrasting fate of the other group, but here, the focus is on the blissful security of the believers in a place where no fear touches them, under the shade of the Throne of the One Who holds all authority.

=57=

The other side of the judgment is now declared. "But those who disbelieve and deny Our revelations." The combination of "disbelieve" and "deny" emphasizes both the internal state of rejection and the external act of calling the signs lies. They did not merely have a private disbelief; they actively denied and opposed the truth that came to them. Their fate is a "humiliating punishment. " The word "humiliating" is key. It is not merely a painful punishment but one laced with disgrace. They will suffer the fire physically, but they will also suffer the shame of knowing they were wrong, the regret of lost opportunities, and the public exposure of their secrets. In the world, their arrogance kept them from bowing to the truth; on that Day, they will be abased before all of creation.

This is the perfect justice of Allah: the choice to be arrogant in this world is met with humiliation in the next. The verse, while stark, is also an extension of the warning from the very opening of the surah, tying the violent quaking of the Hour to the final, personal disgrace of those who did not fear their Lord.

=58=

From the broad categories of believers and disbelievers, the surah zooms in on a specific, highly honored subset of believers: the emigrants. "As for those who emigrate in the cause of Allah." Emigration, or hijrah, means leaving one's home, land, and loved ones for the sake of preserving and practicing faith. This verse was a direct reference to the early Meccan Muslims who left everything behind and migrated to Abyssinia and later to Medina. Their sacrifice was immense.

The verse continues, "and then are martyred or die." Whether they die a natural death or are killed in battle, the promise is the same.

The phrase "Allah will indeed grant them a good provision." The emphasis "indeed" shows the certainty of this promise. The "good provision" is comprehensive: it includes the provision of the grave, the safety of the Judgment, and the eternal sustenance of Paradise. It is a provision that is free from toil, free from interruption, and free from any mixture of harm.

The verse ends with a divine name that is perfectly suited to the context: "Surely Allah is the Best Provider." The emigrant left behind the provision of a worldly job or inheritance, trusting in Allah. This verse reassures them that the Provider they relied upon is the absolute best of all providers. His provision is not just quantitatively more, but qualitatively infinitely superior. This verse is a testament to the honor of hijrah and the value of sacrifice in the path of God.

=59=

The good provision promised in the previous verse is now elaborated upon. The focus is on the personal satisfaction of the believer. "He will certainly admit them into a place they will be pleased with.

" The word "place" translates mudkhalan, which can mean both the place of entry and the event of entry. This entry is not just a physical admission to Paradise, but an entrance that is honorable, celebrated, and perfectly suited to their souls.

The phrase "they will be pleased with" indicates complete and total satisfaction. Human beings are creatures of desire and preference; even in a palace, one might wish for a different view or a different neighbor. But the Paradise that Allah prepares for the emigrant and martyr is so perfectly tailored to their unique personality, their unspoken wishes, and their deepest longings that they will want for nothing and regret nothing.

The verse then concludes with two names: "For Allah is truly All-Knowing, Most Forbearing. " He is All-Knowing of the sacrifices they made, the tears they shed in secret, the pain of leaving their homes, and the precise details of what would bring them joy. He is Most Forbearing, meaning He overlooks their shortcomings and does not hasten to punish their minor sins, instead covering them with His forbearance and mercy until He admits them to His pleasure. This verse is a window into the intimate, personal care Allah has for each believer.

=60=

The verse begins with "That is so," an affirmation of the preceding truths and a connection to the themes of justice and retaliation that were introduced in the permission to fight. The verse then establishes a legal and moral principle: "And whoever retaliates in equivalence to the injury they have received.

" This is the Islamic principle of qisas, proportional retaliation. If someone is wronged, they have the right to a response that is exactly equal to the harm, without transgression.

However, the verse adds a second layer: "and then are wronged again." If, after a just retaliation, the original aggressor or others attack them again, the victim becomes an oppressed party once more. In that case, "Allah will certainly help them." This divine help is guaranteed.

The verse then closes with an interesting pair of names: "Surely Allah is Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving." At first glance, this seems to be about the victim's right to retaliation, so why mention pardon and forgiveness? The scholars explain that this is a subtle encouragement for the victim to forgive. By mentioning His own attributes of pardon right after affirming the right to retaliation, Allah is gently pointing the believer toward a higher path: you have the right to strike back equally, but I, your Lord, am Ever-Pardoning. Will you not try to emulate My attributes and forgive? The verse therefore balances justice with mercy, validating the right to self-defense while leaving the door wide open for the superior moral act of forgiveness. It also reassures that if one is wronged again, even after being merciful, Allah’s help is not lost.
