=21=

After laying bare the fleeting nature of worldly life, the surah immediately issues a call to action that channels human ambition in a completely new direction. The command "compete with one another" uses the imagery of a race, where runners strain every muscle to reach the prize first. But the prize is not a perishable trophy; it is "forgiveness from your Lord and a Paradise." Forgiveness is mentioned first because even the most righteous need their shortcomings wiped clean before they can enter eternal bliss. The Paradise is described as being "as vast as the heavens and the earth," a phrase that boggles the mind. When we look up at the night sky and see the countless stars stretching billions of light-years, and then look down at the earth with its oceans, mountains, and continents, we are being told that the garden waiting for the believers matches that entire expanse. This vastness is both a literal description and a metaphor for the fact that in Paradise there is no crowding, no shortage of space or resources, no competition over limited goods. The Paradise is specifically "prepared for those who believe in Allah and His messengers," which tells us that while it is a gift, it is a gift that has been made ready for a particular group, like a host preparing a magnificent home for honored guests.

The verse then emphasises, "This is the favour of Allah," meaning no one can earn such a reward through deeds alone; it is ultimately an act of divine grace. "He grants it to whoever He wills" reminds us that while we are commanded to compete, the outcome rests with Allah’s will, which is always perfectly aligned with His wisdom. The verse closes by declaring "And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty," a phrase that removes any ceiling on hope. His generosity is not a limited reservoir but an endless ocean. For the companion who had given everything, this was the reassurance that no sacrifice was too small to be rewarded and no reward was too great for the Generous to give.

This verse turns our natural competitive drive, which so often leads to envy and conflict, into a sacred engine for spiritual growth, urging us to outdo one another not in wealth but in seeking forgiveness and eternal nearness to Allah.

=22=

This verse establishes one of the most foundational and comforting doctrines of Islam: nothing happens outside of Allah’s pre-knowledge and decree. "No calamity or blessing occurs" covers every event a human being can experience, from earthquakes and famines that strike the earth to illnesses, losses, and joys that affect us personally. The Arabic word used for calamity can also be translated as affliction, and its pairing with blessing shows that the divine decree encompasses both what we perceive as good and what we perceive as bad. The statement that every such event is "written in a Record before We bring it into being" directs our attention to the Preserved Tablet, a comprehensive book that contains everything that will happen until the Day of Judgment. This record was written before the creation of the heavens and the earth, which means that nothing in our lives is a random accident, a stroke of blind luck, or a surprise to Allah.

The phrase "before We bring it into being" emphasises that the writing precedes the event, establishing the certainty of predestination without negating human free will, because we do not know what is written and we still make real choices. The verse concludes with "This is certainly easy for Allah," dismissing any notion that keeping such a detailed record of everything that will ever happen is difficult for the Creator. His knowledge is not acquired over time; He does not need to learn, remember, or update records. For Him, encompassing all events past, present, and future is as simple as a single, timeless act of knowledge.

For the believer, this verse is a profound source of peace. When a calamity strikes, the heart can find rest in the knowledge that it did not happen by mistake or outside of divine control; it was always part of a wise plan. When a blessing comes, it is received with gratitude, knowing it was not earned by sheer effort but was decreed by the Generous. The verse dismantles the anxiety that comes from believing the world is chaotic and meaningless, replacing it with the calm certainty of a purposeful, divinely ordered existence.

=23=

The surah now explains the profound psychological and spiritual purpose behind the doctrine of predestination introduced in the previous verse. "We let you know this" refers to the truth that everything is written in a Record before it occurs. The intended effect is a balanced, healthy heart: "so that you neither grieve over what you have missed nor boast over what He has granted you." When a person deeply understands that every event is decreed, the past loses its power to torment them. The missed opportunity, the business deal that fell through, the relationship that ended, the honor that went to someone else—all of these are seen as things that were never truly meant for them. Grief over the past becomes redirected into trust in Allah’s wisdom. Conversely, when a person achieves success, acquires wealth, or receives praise, the understanding that it was decreed by Allah and not solely the product of their own brilliance prevents arrogance. The successful believer says, "This is from my Lord’s favor," not "I earned this by my own cleverness. " The verse then declares a divine principle: "For Allah does not like whoever is arrogant, boastful. " Arrogance is precisely the disease that grows when a person attributes success to themselves. Boastfulness is the outward expression of that inner disease, where a person constantly reminds others of their achievements and looks down upon those with less. The fact that Allah does not like such people is a severe warning, because Allah’s dislike is not a minor disapproval; it implies the loss of His support and ultimately a painful reckoning. The companions, who experienced dramatic shifts in fortune—from the poverty and persecution in Mecca to the conquests and spoils later—needed this teaching urgently. The wealthy merchant who lost everything and the poor man who became governor both had to learn to see their circumstances as decreed by Allah, freeing them from grief and from arrogance. This verse offers a complete therapy for the two emotional extremes that wreck human happiness: regret over the unchangeable past and pride over temporary worldly gains.

=24=

This verse continues the description of the ungodly traits that Allah dislikes, directly connecting to the arrogance and boastfulness mentioned at the end of the previous verse. The sentence "those who are stingy and promote stinginess among people" completes the characterization of the arrogant. Stinginess, or withholding wealth from its rightful uses, is a natural companion to arrogance because the arrogant person feels that their wealth is entirely their own achievement and that others are undeserving. They hoard their resources, refusing to spend on the needy or the community’s welfare. But the verse goes further, adding that they "promote stinginess among people.

" This is a particularly destructive form of evil because it multiplies the sin. A stingy person not only harms themselves but also influences others to become miserly, either through direct advice ("don't give away your money, look after yourself first") or through a sour attitude that discourages generosity.

The verse then issues a powerful warning: "And whoever turns away should know that Allah alone is truly the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy. " To "turn away" here means to turn away from faith, from charity, from the commands of Allah. Such a person is warned that their turning away does not harm Allah in the slightest. Allah is "Self-Sufficient," meaning He is completely independent of all creation; He does not need our worship, our wealth, or our obedience. The entire universe could disbelieve, and His majesty would not diminish by an atom’s weight. He is also "Praiseworthy," meaning He is deserving of praise in His very essence, regardless of whether anyone praises Him. The implication is crushing to the arrogant: your rebellion is not a blow against Allah; it is only a blow against your own soul. The stingy person who withholds wealth is only depriving himself of reward; the one who turns away is only marching toward his own ruin.

For the believer, this verse is a motivation to be generous and to encourage others toward generosity, knowing that such conduct pleases the One who needs nothing from us but who, out of His pure grace, rewards our tiny acts with unimaginable bounty.

=25=

This is a monumental verse that summarises the entire human mission as established by the prophets and then introduces the surah’s namesake. "Indeed, We sent Our messengers with clear proofs" tells us that no messenger came empty-handed; they were all equipped with evident signs and miracles that left no honest observer in doubt. Alongside these proofs, Allah "sent down the Scripture and the balance of justice." The Scripture provides the moral and spiritual framework, while the balance symbolises the principle of fairness, equity, and measured conduct. The purpose is explicit: "so that people may administer justice. " Religion is not merely about private rituals; it is fundamentally about establishing a just society where rights are honored and wrongs are corrected. Then the verse makes a remarkable shift from spiritual to material: "And We sent down iron.

" The phrase "sent down" for iron has intrigued commentators. Some understand it literally, that iron was sent to earth from the cosmos, a fact now confirmed by astrophysics which shows that iron is formed in the cores of massive stars and arrives on earth via meteorites. Others understand it metaphorically, meaning that Allah created and provided iron for human use. Iron is described as having "great might" because it is the backbone of weapons, armor, and tools of war and industry. Simultaneously, it has "benefits for humanity," in construction, transportation, medicine, and countless daily applications. But the most profound purpose is spiritual: "and means for Allah to prove who is willing to stand up for Him and His messengers without seeing Him. " The iron that can be forged into a sword becomes a test. The believer who faces an enemy on the battlefield is "standing up" for Allah and His messengers, unseen, with no worldly guarantee of survival, driven purely by faith in the unseen promise. This test applies in every age: those who use the strength and resources Allah has provided, whether iron or modern technology, to uphold justice and defend the truth, prove their sincere faith in the Unseen.

The verse concludes "Surely Allah is All-Powerful, Almighty," a reassurance that the struggle is backed by divine strength. The armaments of the world are not the ultimate source of victory; the All-Powerful is.

=26=

Having laid out the general pattern of sending messengers, the surah now mentions two towering patriarchs. "Indeed, We sent Noah and Abraham" brings to mind the entire legacy of monotheistic prophecy. Noah was the second father of humanity after the flood, a warner who called his people for centuries and was saved with a small band of believers. Abraham was the father of monotheistic faith, the one who smashed idols and surrendered entirely to his Lord. The verse states that Allah "reserved prophethood and revelation for their descendants. " This is a profound statement of divine selection: the line of prophethood, from Isaac and Ishmael to Moses, David, Jesus, and finally Muhammad, peace be upon them all, flows through the progeny of these two great men. The "reservation" implies that this was not an accident of history but a deliberate choice by Allah to place the light of guidance in a particular lineage.

However, the verse immediately adds a sobering clarification: "Some of them are rightly guided, while most are rebellious." Being a descendant of a prophet does not automatically confer guidance. In every generation of those blessed descendants, there were those who followed the truth and those who twisted it, rejected it, or used their noble lineage as a cover for disobedience. The Jews of Medina, who traced their lineage to Isaac and were addressed by the Prophet, were a living example of this; they had the scripture and the prophetic heritage, yet many of them rejected the final Messenger. This verse serves as a warning to Muslims as well: being born into a Muslim family or a community that follows the Prophet does not guarantee salvation. Guidance is a personal choice and a divine gift, not an inherited title. It also highlights the principle that Allah’s justice is based on faith and deeds, not on ancestry. The verse thus connects the call of the surah to the broader history of prophecy, showing that the pattern of some believing and many rebelling has been constant, and the followers of the final Messenger should not be surprised when they see this pattern repeat.

=27=

The surah continues tracing the prophetic chain, now focusing on the lineage stemming from the Israelites after Noah and Abraham. "Then in the footsteps of these prophets, We sent Our messengers" refers to the series of messengers sent to the Children of Israel, such as David, Solomon, Zachariah, and John. Then the verse highlights a special moment: "and after them We sent Jesus, son of Mary." Jesus is presented as part of the same chain, not a founder of a rival religion, but a continuation of the same divine message. He was "granted the Gospel," the scripture that confirmed the Torah and brought a renewed spirit of mercy.

The verse then describes a remarkable characteristic: "and instilled compassion and mercy into the hearts of his followers." This indicates that the early followers of Jesus were distinguished by tenderness, care for the weak, healing the sick, and gentle conduct.

This is a positive acknowledgment of the noble qualities present in sincere Christians. However, the verse then points out a significant deviation: "As for monasticism, they made it up—We never ordained it for them. " Monasticism, the practice of withdrawing from society, renouncing marriage, and subjecting oneself to severe ascetic practices in an attempt to come closer to God, was an innovation.

The phrase "they made it up" makes it clear that this was a human invention, not a divine command. Their intention might have been good, "only seeking to please Allah," but good intentions alone do not justify inventing religious practices.

The verse then exposes their failure even in their own innovation: "yet they did not even observe it strictly. " They fell short of the very standards they set for themselves, succumbing to human weaknesses and hypocrisies. Despite this, Allah’s justice is highlighted: "So We rewarded those of them who were faithful." The sincere believers among them, who held onto the true message of Jesus without falling into excess or rejection, received their due reward.

The verse ends with the familiar refrain, "But most of them are rebellious," drawing the parallel to the Muslim community’s own risk of falling into similar patterns of innovation and neglect.

=28=

This verse is a direct appeal that many commentators understand as addressed to the People of the Book—Jews and Christians—who had already believed in their own prophets. The call "O people of faith!" acknowledges that they already possess a measure of belief, but it is incomplete without belief in the final Messenger. The command "Fear Allah" calls them to a reverent consciousness that would lead them to examine the truth of Muhammad’s message honestly, without the obstruction of pride or tribal jealousy. The command "believe in His Messenger" is the specific act required to complete their faith. Then the verse lists three magnificent promises that act as powerful incentives. First, "He will grant you a double share of His mercy." This double share is understood as one reward for their belief in their own scripture and prophet, and a second reward for their belief in the Quran and Muhammad, peace be upon him. It is a striking display of Allah’s generosity: He does not erase their previous faith but builds upon it, multiplying their reward. Second, "provide you with a light to walk in on Judgment Day." The light that the believers will possess, as described earlier in the surah, will be granted to them as a guide across the dark and treacherous path. Third, "and forgive you.

" This is the ultimate need of every soul, the wiping away of all past transgressions. The verse closes with the beautiful names "All-Forgiving, Most Merciful," reminding the listener that this amazing offer flows not from any need Allah has for their belief but from His own boundless capacity to forgive and show mercy. For the Jews of Medina who were hesitant, and for Christians throughout history who have considered the message of Islam, this verse remains an open invitation, a hand extended with incredible promises. It also speaks to Muslims who may have been born into faith but have not fully internalised it, calling them to a deeper, revitalised belief that will result in a double share of mercy for holding firmly to the complete truth.

=29=

The surah concludes with a verse that explains the wisdom behind the open invitation and the remarkable promises given to those who believe. "This is so that the People of the Book who deny the Prophet may know" indicates that the preceding call and the promise of a double share of mercy serve a dual purpose: they are a genuine invitation, but they also function as a decisive argument that removes any excuse. The specific knowledge that they must come to terms with is that "they do not have any control over Allah’s grace." The People of the Book, particularly some Jewish tribes, had developed a sense of exclusive entitlement to divine favor. They believed that prophethood and salvation were reserved for their lineage alone, and they could not accept that Allah would send a prophet from the descendants of Ishmael rather than Isaac. This verse shatters that illusion. No group, no nation, no lineage holds a monopoly over Allah’s grace.

The phrase "all grace is in Allah’s Hands" uses the powerful imagery of something held securely and distributed according to the owner’s will. No one can snatch it away from Him, and no one can demand it as a right based on ancestry or past glory. "He grants it to whoever He wills" reaffirms the absolute freedom of Allah in bestowing prophethood, guidance, and mercy. The choice of Muhammad, peace be upon him, as the final messenger was entirely by Allah’s will, not by the will of any tribe or council.

The surah then ends exactly where the great themes earlier in the surah pointed: "For Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty." This phrase echoes the description from verse 21, creating a frame for the surah’s message. The bounty is "infinite," meaning it is not a limited resource that runs out; there is enough grace for all nations, all times, all individuals who turn to Him sincerely. The conclusion is a comfort for the believers, a warning for the arrogant, and a declaration that the door of mercy stands wide open, not guarded by any human gatekeeper, but freely accessible through the path chosen by the Owner of all grace.
