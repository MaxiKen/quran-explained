=81=

After exposing the solitary and destitute state of the arrogant disbeliever on the Day of Judgement, the surah now unmasks the futility of the idols and false gods that the polytheists rely upon in this world. The verse states, “They have taken other gods, instead of Allah, seeking strength and protection through them.

” The word “taken” implies a deliberate choice; they adopted these deities by their own volition, despite the innate knowledge of the One Creator. These “other gods” include idols, angels, jinn, prophets, saints, and anything worshipped besides Allah. The reason for this adoption is specified: they seek “strength” (‘izzan) and “protection” or support. The Arabs would fashion idols from stone and wood, believing these would intercede for them, grant them victory in battle, protect them from calamities, and bring them honour among the tribes. In times of distress, they would call upon them, thinking these gods were a source of power and a shield against harm. This captures the psychology of shirk: people feel weak and vulnerable, so they fabricate objects of worship, hoping to draw security from them.

Yet this is a grand illusion. The verse subtly indicates the irony: they seek strength through things that are themselves utterly weak, incapable of even defending themselves from a fly. The Qur’an elsewhere challenges: “Indeed, those you worship besides Allah cannot create a fly, even if they all gathered for it” (22:73). The polytheists’ reliance on false gods is a misplaced trust.

The verse also connects to the earlier warning that on the Day of Judgement, every soul will stand alone, and all the supposed protectors will vanish. The disbelievers thought their idols would be a source of honour and safety, but they will find them to be the very cause of their disgrace. This verse serves as a timeless warning against all forms of reliance on other than Allah, whether it be on wealth, status, human beings, or abstract ideologies. True strength and protection come only from the Lord of the heavens and the earth. The false gods can neither harm nor benefit, and the one who clings to them will inevitably be abandoned at the moment of greatest need.

=82=

The previous verse described the disbelievers taking false gods for strength and protection. This verse delivers the crushing reality that awaits them: “But no!” (Kalla) is an emphatic negation, utterly rejecting their expectation. “Those gods will deny their worship and turn against them.” On the Day of Judgement, the very idols, saints, angels, and jinn that were worshipped will dissociate themselves from their worshippers.

This is a recurring theme in the Qur’an. Surah Yunus states that the false gods will say, “It was not us you worshipped. Allah is sufficient as a witness between us and you. We were unaware of your worship” (10:28-29). The idols, which are inanimate objects, will be given life by Allah and will testify against those who worshipped them. The angels and prophets who were deified will declare their complete innocence, affirming that they themselves were only servants of Allah. Jesus will specifically deny telling his followers to worship him and his mother.

The phrase “turn against them” (yaquluna diddan) indicates that these false gods will become adversaries and enemies on that Day. The very beings they hoped would intercede and defend them will become witnesses for the prosecution, adding to their misery.

This is the ultimate betrayal of misplaced trust. The disbelievers spent their lives devoted to these false gods, making offerings and sacrifices, only to find them transformed into a source of regret and punishment.

This verse also highlights the justice of Allah: the false gods are not blamed for being worshipped without their consent; rather, they are granted the opportunity to clear themselves, and the full blame falls upon those who chose to worship them. This profound scene serves as a powerful deterrent against shirk. It reminds believers that any attachment to other than Allah, whether an idol, a charismatic leader, or a worldly desire, will eventually sever itself and stand as a witness against the soul. True loyalty and reliance must be reserved for Allah alone, for He is the only One who will never deny His sincere servants.

=83=

The surah now explains one of the mechanisms behind the disbelievers’ persistence in error. The question is addressed to the Prophet: “Do you, O Prophet, not see that We have sent the devils against the disbelievers, constantly inciting them?

” The phrase “sent the devils” does not mean that Allah forces the devils upon innocent people. Rather, it is a divine decree that as a consequence of the disbelievers’ own choice to reject the truth, the devils are given access and authority over them. They become qarin, constant companions who whisper and incite.

This is a form of punishment for their arrogance and a cause of their misguidance deepening. The Arabic word “ta’uzzuhum” means they incite them vigorously, stirring them up to evil and rebellion. The devils beautify sinful deeds, present false arguments, and encourage them to mock the believers and oppose the prophets. The incitement is continuous and relentless. This verse is connected to the earlier description of the misguided being allowed plenty of time and increasing in misguidance. Allah, in His wisdom, has established that when a person persistently turns away from the light, they are given over to the darkness they have chosen. This does not absolve the disbelievers of responsibility; they made the initial choice, and the devil’s incitement only amplifies what was already in their hearts.

The question “Do you not see” is meant to open the Prophet’s eyes to this hidden reality, reassuring him that the intense opposition he faces is not merely human but has a satanic dimension driving it. This verse also serves as a warning to believers: if one flirts with sin and turns away from guidance, one may fall under the influence of satanic whispers. The protection lies in seeking refuge with Allah and holding fast to the revelation. It is a call to understand the spiritual warfare behind the scenes and to never underestimate the enemy’s efforts to lead humans astray.

=84=

Having revealed that the devils are inciting the disbelievers, Allah comforts the Prophet with a direct instruction: “So do not be in haste against them.” The Prophet Muhammad, like all messengers, was deeply pained by the rejection and mockery of his people. There were moments when he wished for the punishment of Allah to descend swiftly upon the obstinate disbelievers to vindicate the truth. This verse commands him to be patient and not to seek to hasten their destruction. The reason follows: “for indeed We are closely counting down their days.

” The word “counting down” (‘adad) implies a precise enumeration. Allah is meticulously numbering the days of their lives, their breaths, their deeds, and the exact moment appointed for their reckoning. Nothing is random or delayed out of neglect; every single moment is part of a precise divine timetable. This counting down serves both as a threat to the disbelievers and as a reassurance to the believers. The disbelievers may think they are getting away with their crimes, but their time is running out with every sunrise and sunset, and when the count reaches zero, the punishment will arrive without a second’s delay. For the Prophet and the believers, this knowledge fosters patience. The apparent delay is not weakness; it is an exact measure leading to a definite conclusion.

The verse also echoes the earlier statement that Allah “allows them plenty of time.” The respite is part of the countdown. This teaches an important lesson about divine timing. Human impatience often demands immediate justice, but Allah’s wisdom decrees the most appropriate moment. The believer must trust that the countdown is underway and continue the work of da‘wah with steadfastness, leaving the outcome to the One who has perfect control over time and destiny. The verse cultivates a state of calm trust in the face of persistent opposition.

=85=

The surah now transports the listener to the magnificent scene of the final assembly, contrasting the fate of the righteous with that of the wicked. The verse begins with an implied command: “Watch for the Day,” or “Mention the Day.” It is a call to visualise and anticipate the inevitable. On that Day, “We will gather the righteous before the Most Compassionate as an honoured delegation. ” The word “righteous” translates “muttaqin,” those who possessed taqwa, the deep God-consciousness that governed their lives. Their gathering is not a humiliating herding but a reception of honour. They are described as an “honoured delegation” (wafdan). In Arab culture, a wafd is a group of envoys or representatives who are received with ceremony, respect, and hospitality by a king. They do not arrive as criminals in chains but as dignified guests, mounted on noble steeds, invited to the court of the Almighty.

This description fills the heart with hope and longing. They will be brought before “the Most Compassionate,” the very attribute that guarantees them mercy and kindness. Their approach to the Lord will be one of intimacy and honour, not terror and dread, even though all will feel the awe of the occasion. They will be in the company of the angels and the prophets, forming a blessed assembly. This scene is the complete opposite of the earlier image of the disbelievers being dragged out and left on their knees. It also connects to the verse that said they will inherit Paradise as an honoured reward. The vision of being a member of this honoured delegation should inspire every believer to strive for taqwa. It reminds us that the hardships and humiliations suffered for the sake of Allah in this world will be replaced by the highest honour in the most important gathering of all, the gathering before the King of kings. The dignity of that entrance will be beyond any worldly imagination.

=86=

The verse completes the contrast with a terrifying image of the disbelievers’ fate. “And drive the wicked to Hell like a thirsty herd.” The “wicked” (mujrimin) are those who committed crimes against their own souls by disbelieving and transgressing Allah’s bounds. Their journey to the Hereafter is utterly different from that of the honoured delegation. They will be “driven” (nasaqu) with force, pushed and herded mercilessly by the angels of punishment. They are likened to a “thirsty herd” (wirdan).

The word wird refers to a group of animals, particularly camels, being driven to a watering place. In the intense heat of the desert, a thirsty herd rushes towards water in a state of desperate need and disorder.

However, the water they are driven to is not cool and refreshing; it is the boiling water and the blazing fire of Hell. The comparison is bitterly ironic: they are driven like thirsty animals, but their thirst will never be quenched. Their state is one of extreme degradation, stripped of all the dignity they boasted in the world. The chiefs and nobles who once walked with pride will be driven like beasts to the slaughter. This scene also contrasts with the earlier mockery of the disbelievers, who asked “Which of the two of us is better in status?” On that Day, it will be chillingly clear who the honoured ones are and who the humiliated ones are.

The image of being herded as a thirsty herd emphasises the loss of individual honour and the helplessness of the criminals. There is no escape, no argument, no help. This verse is a powerful reminder of the ultimate consequences of rejecting faith. It is designed to strike fear into the hearts of the heedless and to motivate the believers to be among the honoured delegation, not the driven herd.

=87=

After describing the two groups, the verse addresses a crucial aspect of the Day of Judgement: intercession. The polytheists often worshipped their idols hoping they would intercede for them with Allah. Here, Allah shatters that hope. “None will have the right to intercede” means that no one possesses the authority or the power to intercede on their own initiative. No prophet, angel, or saint can simply step forward and plead for someone’s forgiveness without permission. The absolute sovereignty of Allah on that Day is unshared.

However, the verse makes a significant exception: “except those who have taken a covenant from the Most Compassionate.” The majority of commentators, including Ibn Abbas, explain that the “covenant” (‘ahd) refers to the testimony of faith (La ilaha illa Allah) and the belief in the message of the prophets. Those who have taken this covenant are the believers. The exception does not mean that any believer can intercede at will, but that intercession will be permitted for certain people on behalf of certain individuals, all under Allah’s precise command and approval. Authentic hadiths elaborate that the Prophet Muhammad will be granted the great intercession (al-maqam al-mahmud) after the people have waited in immense distress. He will prostrate under the Throne and praise Allah, and then Allah will say, “Raise your head and ask; it will be given to you. Intercede; your intercession will be accepted.” Then the Prophet will intercede for the sinners among his Ummah. Other prophets, angels, and righteous believers will also be permitted to intercede for those with whom Allah is pleased. The “covenant” can also be understood as the pledge that Allah has taken from humanity in the realm of souls, as mentioned in Surah al-A‘raf. This verse, therefore, neither opens the door to unrestricted intercession nor denies it altogether, but establishes it as a divine gift governed by strict conditions. It consoles the believers, assuring them that their covenant with the Most Compassionate will not be in vain, and it rebukes the polytheists, telling them that their idols, which never had any covenant with Allah, will be utterly powerless.

=88=

The surah now returns to refuting one of the most outrageous claims against the majesty of Allah, the very claim that was implicitly denied in the stories of Jesus and John and the accounts of other prophets. “They say, ‘The Most Compassionate has offspring.’” The pronoun “they” encompasses all who have made this terrible statement: the Arab polytheists who claimed the angels were daughters of Allah, the Jews who claimed Ezra was the son of God, and the Christians who claimed Jesus was the son of God. All these groups attributed a child to the Most Compassionate, despite the clear monotheistic teachings of their own prophets.

The use of the name “the Most Compassionate” (al-Rahman) here is deeply significant. The claim of offspring is a direct assault on the very essence of divine mercy and self-sufficiency. One who has a child is, in human conception, incomplete, subject to needs, desires, and the passage of time. The Most Compassionate, however, is the One whose mercy is infinite and whose existence is absolute. He has no need for offspring to carry on a legacy, assist in dominion, or provide comfort. The very idea of a son implies likeness and similarity, whereas Allah is utterly unique. The verse records this statement not to dignify it but to condemn it in the strongest terms. It is presented as a sample of the ultimate blasphemy, the peak of human arrogance against the Creator. The emotional tone of the upcoming verses demonstrates the gravity of this utterance. It is not a harmless theological speculation; it is a monstrous falsehood that causes the entire cosmos to shudder. The verse sets up the cosmic reaction that follows, illustrating that this claim is not merely a human argument but a crime against the very fabric of creation that worships and glorifies the One true God.

=89=

Allah directly addresses those who uttered this blasphemy, and the divine response is immediate and severe. “You have certainly made an outrageous claim.

” The Arabic word used is “iddan,” which denotes something extremely evil, horrendous, and monstrous. It is not a small error or a minor exaggeration; it is a statement of such profound ugliness and falsehood that it defies description.

The word “idd” carries the connotation of something that makes one cringe and tremble because of its sheer enormity. The verse passes a definitive judgement on the claim of divine offspring. It does not engage in a lengthy debate at this moment; it simply pronounces the qualitative nature of the statement as outrageously evil.

This is because the matter is so fundamentally clear to the fitrah and to reason that a detailed refutation is almost beneath the majesty of the truth. Yet the Qur’an has already provided the rational refutation earlier by mentioning the creation of Jesus with the command “Be” and by affirming that Allah has no need for a son. Here, the focus is on the emotional and cosmic weight of the crime. By saying “you have certainly made,” the blame is placed squarely on the claimants. The tone is one of shock and denunciation, as if the universe itself is horrified. This verse serves as a prelude to the powerful imagery of the heavens, earth, and mountains reacting to the statement. It teaches believers the proper attitude towards such blasphemy: it should not be treated as a casual difference of opinion but as a catastrophic violation of the reverence due to the Almighty.

The verse also acts as a comfort to the Prophet, who was distressed by these claims; Allah is declaring that He Himself recognizes the enormity of what they say and will deal with it accordingly.

=90=

The Qur’an now paints a terrifying picture of the cosmic reaction to the outrageous claim just mentioned. “By which the heavens are about to burst, the earth to split apart, and the mountains to crumble to pieces.” The pronoun “by which” refers back to the claim that the Most Compassionate has a child. The entire universe, which was created by Allah and is in constant worship of Him, is so profoundly disturbed by this blasphemy that it almost falls into catastrophic ruin. The “heavens” are nearly torn asunder from above, the “earth” is on the verge of splitting open, and the “mountains,” the symbols of stability and firmness, are about to collapse into rubble. This imagery conveys the unimaginable gravity of shirk. In human scales, a great crime might shock a community; but the claim of divine offspring is a crime so immense that it shakes the very pillars of creation. Inanimate objects, which are far more obedient to Allah than the disbelievers, are depicted as having a consciousness of this offence and reacting with near-destruction.

This is not to be taken as a mere metaphor; it indicates a physical reality that Allah, in His mercy, holds back. The creation is obedient and feels the horror of such a statement being uttered. It also implies that if the mercy of Allah did not restrain the heavens and the earth, they would have already destroyed the disbelievers. This scene echoes the verse: “The heavens almost rupture from above it, and the angels exalt the praise of their Lord” (42:5). It shows the stark contrast: while the disbelievers speak their evil words casually, the entire universe trembles. This verse is a powerful reflection for believers, inspiring awe and fear of uttering any word that belittles the majesty of Allah. It elevates the sin of shirk to its proper cosmic proportion, making clear that attributing a son to God is not just a personal belief but a universe-shaking catastrophe.

=91=

This verse specifies the exact cause of the cosmic upheaval described just before. The heavens nearly burst, the earth almost splits, and the mountains threaten to collapse “in protest of attributing children to the Most Compassionate.

” The phrase “in protest” (an da‘aw) means that this near-destruction is a direct reaction to the specific utterance that the Most Compassionate has a child. It is as if the entire creation is groaning under the weight of this lie and wants to shake it off violently.

The word “da‘aw” comes from the root meaning to claim or to invoke, and here it implies a false attribution, a calumny against the Most Merciful. By naming “the Most Compassionate” again, the verse emphasises the contradiction: how can the One whose mercy encompasses everything, who is utterly self-sufficient, have a need for a child? The very nature of parenthood, in human terms, involves need, desire, and dependency, all of which are impossible for Allah. The universe, in its perfect submission to Allah, knows its Lord and cannot tolerate such a slander against His majesty. The protest of creation is a silent yet thunderous testimony to the truth of Tawhid. Mountains that seem unmovable are moved to the point of collapse by the horror of shirk. This verse serves to imprint on the heart the absolute seriousness of the sin of associating partners with Allah, particularly in the form of claiming He has offspring. It also shows that the disbelievers’ words are not just recorded in a book but are etched into the very fabric of reality, causing a disturbance in the balance of creation.

For the believer, this calls for a profound internalisation of the sanctity of Allah’s names and attributes, and a vigilant rejection of any belief that diminishes His unique oneness. It underlines the Islamic position that Tawhid is the foundation upon which the heavens and the earth were established.

=92=

After depicting the cosmic outrage, the Qur’an now states the clear theological truth in a simple, definitive sentence. “It does not befit the majesty of the Most Compassionate to have children.

” The Arabic “ma yanbaghi” means it is not appropriate, not suitable, not conceivable, and entirely beneath His majesty. The word “majesty” is added in the translation to convey the inherent sense of the divine name “al-Rahman. ” The Most Compassionate, in His perfection, glory, and self-sufficiency, is far above needing, wanting, or having a son. A child implies a beginning, a likeness, a part of the parent, and a need for continuation or support. Allah is the Eternal, the Absolute, the One who begets not and was not begotten. Having a child would contradict His attribute of being As-Samad, the Self-Sufficient Master. It would imply that He is composed of parts or subject to biological processes, which is impossible for the One who is unlike His creation. The verse is a direct refutation of the Christian doctrine of the divinity and sonship of Jesus, the Jewish notion of Ezra, and the polytheistic belief in angels as daughters of God. The statement is categorical and leaves no room for metaphorical reinterpretation. The fact that the verse uses the name “the Most Compassionate” is a powerful theological argument in itself: mercy in its absolute sense is incompatible with the imperfection of procreation. This verse is one of the cornerstones of Islamic monotheism. It teaches that our understanding of Allah must be purified from all human-like attributes. It also encourages believers to feel a profound sense of reverence and to never entertain thoughts that lower the exalted status of the Creator. The simplicity of the statement makes it accessible to every mind, while its depth satisfies the most sophisticated theologian, affirming that true honour and majesty belong exclusively to the One who has no partner, no equal, and no offspring.

=93=

This verse provides a universal proof for the preceding negation of divine offspring. “There is none in the heavens or the earth who will not return to the Most Compassionate in full submission.

” The phrase “none in the heavens or the earth” includes every single created being: angels, jinn, humans, animals, and even inanimate objects, all of which are subject to Allah’s will. Every one of them will come to Allah on the Day of Judgement as a “servant” (‘abd).

The word “full submission” translates the state of being a devoted, obedient servant who acknowledges the Lordship of the Creator. Even those who were worshipped as gods—Jesus, the angels, the saints—will return to Allah as humble servants. This completely dismantles the logic of taking any of them as divine offspring. If Jesus is a servant who will return to Allah in submission, how can he be His son? If the angels are servants who obey without question, how can they be His daughters?

The verse establishes that the relationship between Allah and all creation is exclusively that of Master and servant, not father and child. The polytheists and the Christians had elevated certain servants to divine status, but this verse reveals their true nature: they are all returning to the Lord as submissive servants, and they themselves worship Allah. This truth is further reinforced by the fact that all of creation inherently worships and submits to Allah, except for those humans and jinn who rebel. On the Day of Judgement, even the rebels will come in submission, though it will be too late for their forced submission to benefit them. The verse thus provides both a rational and an eschatological argument against the concept of divine progeny. It reminds the believer to view all beings, no matter how exalted, as fellow servants of the Most Compassionate, worthy of respect but never of worship.

=94=

After stating that all beings will return to Allah as servants, the verse emphasises the perfection of Allah’s knowledge over them. “Indeed, He fully knows them and has counted them precisely.

” The word “fully knows” translates “ahsa,” which implies a complete and exhaustive reckoning that encompasses every detail—their numbers, their actions, their intentions, their lifespans, their provisions, and their innermost thoughts. No one is overlooked; no one is forgotten.

The phrase “has counted them precisely” (‘addahum ‘adda) reinforces the concept of meticulous enumeration. Allah’s knowledge is not a general awareness but a precise, individualised account of every single creature from the beginning of time to its end. This absolute knowledge is incompatible with the idea of having a son, because a son usually arises from a hidden need or a private relationship, whereas Allah’s knowledge is open and all-encompassing. Nothing in creation is hidden from Him, so there is no need for a special intimate relation like that of a child to set someone apart. Everyone is equally naked before His knowledge.

This verse also serves as a threat to those who claim false things about Allah and as a comfort to the believers. The threat is that every one of their blasphemies is precisely recorded and accounted for; they will not escape the consequences. The comfort is that the believers are also fully known, and not a single one of their struggles, good deeds, or silent prayers is lost. The precise counting assures them that the divine promise of gathering the righteous as an honoured delegation is based on an infallible record. The verse instills a profound sense of individual accountability and significance. No soul is lost in the crowd; each is known and counted by the Creator. This encourages a personal relationship with Allah based on the awareness that He knows one completely, better than one knows oneself.

=95=

The surah returns to the theme of individual accountability, which was powerfully illustrated earlier with the fate of the boastful disbeliever. The verse states, “And each of them will return to Him on the Day of Judgment all alone.

” The word “each” (kulluhum) encompasses every single rational being. Despite having lived among families, tribes, and nations, on that Day they will be resurrected and brought before Allah “all alone” (fardan). This solitude strips away every social connection and every source of pride that people relied on in the world. There will be no wealth, no children, no followers, no intercessors unless permitted by Allah. The polytheists who boasted about their “superior assembly” will stand isolated. The Christians who relied on the supposed divinity of Jesus will find themselves alone, and Jesus himself will stand as a separate servant. This verse is a powerful refutation of the concept of vicarious atonement or reliance on lineage. Each soul will bear its own burden, and no one can carry the burden of another.

The repetition of this theme, first with the individual disbeliever (verse 80) and now with all of humanity, underscores its central importance in the Quranic worldview. The Day of Judgement is ultimately a deeply personal encounter between the servant and his Lord. The verse prepares the believer for that inevitable solitary meeting by encouraging the cultivation of a sincere, personal faith that does not depend on the crowd. It also serves as a warning against following the misguidance of the majority, for in the end, everyone will stand alone to answer for their own choices. This stark image of universal solitude on the Day of Judgement is meant to awaken the heedless from the slumber of collective conformity and to focus the heart on what truly matters: a direct and honest relationship with the Most Compassionate.

=96=

From the awe-inspiring images of the Day of Judgement and the condemnation of blasphemy, the surah now turns to a message of immense hope and beauty for the believers. “As for those who believe and do good, the Most Compassionate will certainly bless them with genuine love.” This verse contains a precious promise. The two conditions are, as always, sincere faith and righteous deeds—an integrated combination of inner conviction and outward action. For such people, the reward mentioned here is not a garden or a river, but something far more intimate: love.

The Arabic word “wudd” is translated as “genuine love,” a deep, affectionate, and enduring love. The promise is that Allah will place this love for them in the world. According to an authentic hadith narrated in Sahih al-Bukhari and Muslim, the Prophet (peace be upon him) said: “When Allah loves a servant, He calls Gabriel and says: ‘I love so-and-so, so love him. ’ Then Gabriel loves him, and he announces in the heavens: ‘Allah loves so-and-so, so love him.’ Then the inhabitants of the heavens love him, and then acceptance is placed in the earth for him.

” This is the manifestation of “wudd.” When a believer reaches this station, Allah creates a special affection and acceptance for him among the righteous on earth. People’s hearts naturally incline towards him with respect and love. This love also includes the love that Allah himself has for that servant, which is the supreme blessing. Conversely, when Allah is displeased with a servant, a similar announcement of dislike is made, and that person becomes disliked on earth.

This verse reveals that the ultimate success is not just entry into Paradise but being beloved to the Most Compassionate and beloved to His creation. It also refutes the idea that believers must always live in hostility; rather, righteousness engenders a natural attraction and affection that transcends worldly barriers. It is a powerful motivation to perfect one’s faith and deeds, seeking not worldly fame but the divine love that then reflects as genuine love in the hearts of people.

=97=

As the surah approaches its end, Allah reminds the Prophet of the purpose and nature of the revelation. “Indeed, We have made this Quran easy in your own language, O Prophet.” The Qur’an was revealed in clear Arabic, the language of the Prophet and his immediate audience. This ease refers to its linguistic clarity, its memorability, its comprehensible teachings, and its accessibility to those who approach it with sincerity.

The phrase “in your own language” is a direct address to the Prophet Muhammad, emphasising that he did not receive the revelation in a foreign tongue that would need translation; it was in his native Arabic, allowing him to understand it deeply and convey it effectively. This ease is a manifestation of Allah’s mercy, making the final revelation available to an ummah that includes scholars and laypeople alike. Then the verse states the dual purpose of the Quran: “so with it you may give good news to the righteous and warn those who are contentious.” The “righteous” (muttaqin) are those who fear Allah and follow His guidance. For them, the Quran is a source of constant glad tidings—promises of Paradise, nearness to Allah, forgiveness, and divine love, as just mentioned. The “contentious” (qawman luddan) are those who are extremely argumentative, stubborn, and hostile to the truth.

The word “ludd” describes a person who engages in constant dispute and opposition, refusing to accept clear evidence. This describes the Quraysh leaders and all who share their attitude. For them, the Quran serves as a warning of impending doom. The verse thus encapsulates the dual function of the prophetic mission and the Quran itself. It also connects back to the beginning of the surah, which spoke of the Quran as a reminder of mercy.

The mention of ease is a comfort to the Prophet, assuring him that his mission is made manageable. For all believers, this verse is a call to approach the Quran with the confidence that it has been made easy for remembrance, reflection, and practice, and to use it as a tool to bring hope to the striving and to caution the obstinate.

=98=

The surah concludes with a chilling reminder of the fate of those who rejected the prophets in the past. “Imagine how many peoples We have destroyed before them!” The rhetorical “how many” (kam) points to the countless civilisations and generations that were wiped out due to their denial and sins. The addressees are the Quraysh and all who follow in their footsteps of stubborn disbelief. The verse calls on them to reflect on the ruins and empty lands that once housed mighty nations like ‘Ad, Thamud, the people of Lot, and Pharaoh’s followers. Their destruction is a historical reality that the Arabs passed by in their travels. Then comes the final haunting question: “Do you still see any of them, or even hear from them the slightest sound?

” The answer is a resounding no. All that remains are silent, deserted traces.

The word “slightest sound” (hamsan) refers to the faintest whisper or the softest breath. Their bustling cities, their proud assemblies, their loud boasts—all have been reduced to total silence. Not even a whisper can be heard from them. This stark imagery serves as the ultimate warning. The surah, which began with hidden prayers of a righteous servant and moved through the miraculous births of John and Jesus, the tender pleas of Abraham to his father, the honour of the prophets, and the horror of the Day of Judgement, ends with the silence of the destroyed. It is a powerful call to heed the message before the opportunity is lost, before the final breath is drawn and one joins the silent nations of the past.

The verse also provides a definitive answer to the mockery of the disbelievers who boasted about their status and assemblies; all that is gone, and they too will be a forgotten whisper unless they repent. The final silence of the graves is the loudest of all warnings, urging the living to speak words of faith before they fall into that eternal quiet.
