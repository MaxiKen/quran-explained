=41=

The surah now shifts from the stories of John and Jesus to another towering figure in the prophetic tradition, Abraham, the patriarch of monotheism. The command “And mention in the Book, O Prophet, the story of Abraham” links back to the earlier instruction to mention Mary. This structure shows that the Quran is a book of remembrance, recounting the lives of the chosen servants so that believers may draw lessons and strengthen their hearts. The Prophet Muhammad and his followers, facing persecution in Mecca, needed the example of Abraham, who stood alone against his entire community, including his own family, for the sake of Allah’s oneness.

The verse then describes Abraham as “surely a man of truth and a prophet.” The Arabic word “siddiq” (man of truth) indicates someone who has reached the highest level of truthfulness. A siddiq is not merely one who avoids lies but one whose entire being, words, deeds, and innermost thoughts are in perfect alignment with the truth. He affirms the truth of Allah’s revelations without a trace of doubt, and his life becomes a living testament to that truth. Abraham earned this title because he was tested with immense trials—being thrown into the fire, leaving his family in a barren valley, and being commanded to sacrifice his son—and he passed every test with complete truthfulness and submission. This also connects to the earlier description of Jesus as a “word of truth” and the general theme of the surah, which is to present the truth about Allah’s prophets against the falsehoods spread by disputing factions. Abraham is both a siddiq and a prophet, meaning his truthfulness was the foundation upon which he received divine revelation and was commissioned to guide his people. His story is particularly relevant here because he was the spiritual forefather of the Israelites, the Arabs, and through them, much of humanity. Calling him a prophet also confirms that he was not merely a wise man or a philosopher but a direct recipient of divine guidance, sent to call people away from idol worship and towards the One Creator. This introduction sets the stage for the deeply moving conversation he had with his father, which follows in the next verses.

=42=

The verse transports us to a pivotal moment in Abraham’s early life when he confronts the man who raised him. The Quran says, “Remember when he said to his father.” Abraham’s father, named Azar according to the Quran, was not only an idol worshipper but also a maker of idols by profession. This made Abraham’s task extraordinarily delicate. He was a young man living under his father’s roof, yet he could not remain silent in the face of blatant falsehood. His approach, however, is a masterclass in respectful da‘wah. He begins with “O dear father!

” The Arabic “ya abati” is an affectionate diminutive, expressing endearment, tenderness, and deep respect. He does not insult his father or dismiss him as a hopeless case; he appeals to their bond of love.

This is profoundly instructive for all believers who struggle with family members who are on the wrong path. Even when correcting the gravest error, the tone must remain loving and gentle. Then Abraham presents a logical argument: “Why do you worship what can neither hear nor see, nor benefit you at all? ” He appeals to his father’s intellect. The idols his father made and worshipped were deaf, blind, and inert. They could not hear his prayers, see his devotion, nor provide him any protection, sustenance, or help. Abraham’s question exposes the fundamental irrationality of idol worship. It reduces the idols to what they truly are: lifeless objects, utterly incapable of causing harm or benefit. This rational argument is the hallmark of Abraham’s call; he used the innate human fitrah that knows the Creator must be greater than the created.

The phrase “nor benefit you at all” includes both worldly and spiritual benefits. Abraham is essentially asking: why do you enslave yourself to something that cannot even swat a fly away from you? This gentle but piercing question was intended to awaken his father’s dormant common sense. It also shows that idolatry is not a harmless cultural practice but a profound self-degradation, lowering a human being beneath a piece of wood or stone that he himself carved.

This verse begins a series of four consecutive verses, all starting with “O dear father,” highlighting Abraham’s unwavering compassion.

=43=

Abraham continues his plea with the same tenderness: “O dear father!” Having exposed the futility of the idols, he now offers an alternative—a better way based on divine knowledge. He says, “I have certainly received some knowledge which you have not received.” Abraham speaks with the confidence of a prophet who has been granted direct insight from Allah. This “knowledge” is not the result of his own intellect but is a divine gift of revelation that clarifies the truth about God, creation, and the purpose of life. He is not boasting; rather, he is humbly informing his father that he has access to a source of truth that his father lacks.

This is a crucial point: Abraham is not following his own whims or a new philosophy; he is relaying what his Lord has taught him. By saying “which you have not received,” he acknowledges his father’s ignorance without calling him stupid, keeping the door of learning open. Then he extends the invitation: “so follow me and I will guide you to the Straight Path.

” The phrase “follow me” might sound presumptuous in any other context, but from a prophet it is an invitation to follow the one who is guided by Allah. Abraham positions himself not as a master but as a guide who knows the way and wants to lead his beloved father to safety. The “Straight Path” (sirat sawiyy) is the same path that Jesus declared earlier in the surah: the path of pure monotheism and righteous living that leads to Allah’s pleasure. Abraham promises that if his father follows him, he will reach that path.

This is an expression of profound filial love; he wants nothing for his father except eternal salvation. The verse teaches that true love for one’s family means wanting for them the ultimate good of guidance, and it requires courage to share religious truths even when they contradict long-held family traditions.

=44=

For the third time, Abraham calls out, “O dear father!” Now he sharpens the argument by identifying the real power behind idol worship. He says, “Do not worship Satan.

” This is a shocking statement for someone who saw himself as worshipping ancestral gods. Abraham reveals that by obeying the call to idolatry, his father is actually worshipping the devil. Idolatry originated from Satan’s deception, and by adhering to it, a person falls into servitude to him.

This is a profound insight: any act of worship directed to other than Allah is, in reality, obedience to Satan, because he is the one who beautifies and promotes falsehood. Abraham wants his father to see that he has been tricked into a degrading bondage to a sworn enemy. Then Abraham provides the reason: “Surely Satan is ever rebellious against the Most Compassionate.

” The name “the Most Compassionate” (al-Rahman) is specifically used here. Abraham reminds his father that the Lord he should worship is the All-Merciful, whose mercy encompasses everything. Satan, by contrast, is “rebellious” (‘asiyyan)—a perpetual, arrogant disobeyer of this Merciful Lord. The contrast is stark: on one side is the Most Compassionate who created, nourishes, and forgives; on the other side is a rebellious creature who wants to lead humans to destruction. This argument appeals to both fear and logic: why follow a defeated rebel against the source of all mercy? It also subtly prepares his father to think about the consequences of such allegiance. Abraham’s use of al-Rahman is significant; it was a name that the Arab polytheists themselves recognised, as they believed in a high god called Allah but associated partners with Him. Abraham calls his father back to the pure worship of that same Most Compassionate One, without any intermediaries or rebellious influences.

This verse teaches believers to recognise the spiritual reality behind sinful systems and to warn loved ones against the hidden snare of Satan.

=45=

Abraham concludes his series of loving appeals with a final, heart-wrenching warning, still beginning with “O dear father!” This repetition of the endearing term throughout the conversation shows that even as he delivers the severest warning, his love and concern for his father have not diminished. He says, “I truly fear that you will be touched by a torment from the Most Compassionate.” The fear Abraham expresses is not for himself but entirely for his father’s fate.

This is the fear of a compassionate son, not a cruel judge. The phrase “touched by a torment” suggests even a small portion of divine punishment would be unbearable. Notice that the punishment is described as coming from “the Most Compassionate.” This juxtaposition is profound. It means that this torment is a consequence of rejecting the very mercy that Allah extends. The Most Compassionate invites to salvation, and the rejection of that invitation turns the mercy into justice. Abraham’s fear is that his father’s persistence will sever him from that mercy completely. Then he spells out the ultimate outcome: “and become Satan’s companion in Hell. ” The word “companion” (waliyyan) signifies a close ally, a friend, one who shares the same fate. Abraham does not want his father to be among the companions of the devil in the Fire, sharing in their regret and torture. This was the utmost Abraham could do—deliver the message with love, logic, and genuine fear for his father’s wellbeing. This verse sets the standard for how believers should approach relatives who reject faith: with an unceasing sense of care, a clear conveyance of the truth, and a heart full of fear for their ultimate destiny, without ever resorting to hatred or harshness. It also shows that guidance is ultimately in Allah’s hands, but the duty to warn with compassion remains.

=46=

Abraham’s gentle and rational plea was met with fierce hostility. His father, Azar, completely ignored the logical and spiritual arguments and instead issued a direct threat. He shouted, “How dare you reject my idols, O Abraham!

” The phrase “my idols” reveals his deep personal attachment and ego investment in the false gods. He saw Abraham’s call to pure monotheism not merely as a theological challenge but as a personal insult to his craft, his ancestors, and his identity. He then escalated to a death threat: “If you do not desist, I will certainly stone you to death.” Stoning was a brutal method of execution, and a father threatening his own son with it indicates the extreme rage that the call to truth can provoke, even within the closest family bonds. This mirrors the reactions of the Quraysh leaders to Prophet Muhammad’s message. The father’s final words were one of rejection and banishment: “So be gone from me for a long time!” This was a command to leave his presence and his household, severing the family tie.

The phrase “a long time” (maliyyan) implies an indefinite, lengthy exile. Thus, Abraham’s call was met with excommunication and the threat of death. This sudden, harsh response illustrates a repeated pattern in the history of prophethood: when falsehood has no rational defence, it resorts to force and intimidation.

Yet, the Quran presents this without any narration of Abraham reacting in anger. He did not return the threat; he accepted the verdict of his father with a remarkable composure that would be shown in the following verse. This moment teaches that standing for truth can result in losing the closest of relationships, but the servant of Allah must remain steadfast and maintain his own character despite the bitterness of the rejection.

=47=

In the face of his father’s rage and banishment, Abraham’s response is astonishingly composed and merciful. He said, “Peace be upon you!” This was not the greeting of a coward but the dignified reply of a prophet who refuses to meet anger with anger. By saying “Salamun ‘alayk,” Abraham was declaring that he held no malice and wished no harm to his father, despite the death threat. Some scholars note that this is the peace of a final parting, a peaceful departure from a hostile environment, much like the angels’ greeting to the believers in Paradise. It embodies the Quranic principle of repelling evil with that which is better. Then Abraham added, “I will pray to my Lord for your forgiveness.” This demonstrates an extraordinary level of filial love and hope. Even after being expelled and threatened, Abraham’s heart still yearned for his father’s salvation. He promised to ask Allah to forgive his father, hoping that perhaps one day divine mercy would touch his father’s heart.

However, it is well-known from other verses that once it became clear his father was an enemy of Allah, Abraham disassociated himself and stopped asking for forgiveness. This promise was made in a moment of hope before the finality of his father’s disbelief was completely manifest.

Finally, Abraham said, “He has truly been Most Gracious to me.” This is a recognition of his own undeserved blessings from Allah. Despite the pain of being cast out, Abraham acknowledges that his Lord has always been incredibly kind to him, guiding him, protecting him, and granting him prophethood. This attitude of gratitude in the midst of trial is the hallmark of true faith. Abraham’s response teaches that even when wronged by family, a believer should respond with peace, continued prayer for their guidance while it is permissible, and deep gratitude for whatever good Allah has bestowed upon him.

=48=

Abraham continues his response by clarifying his new, firm position. He declares, “As I distance myself from all of you and from whatever you invoke besides Allah.

” This is a formal announcement of disavowal. The word “distance” indicates a complete physical and spiritual separation from his father’s household, their idolatrous practices, and the entire community that worshipped false gods. Abraham’s emigration was not born out of anger but out of necessity: he could no longer live among those who would compromise his faith.

However, this distance was from false gods and false practices, not from his duty to his Lord. He immediately adds, “I will continue to call upon my Lord alone.

” This is a declaration of exclusive worship and reliance. While he leaves the worship of idols, he clings firmly to the worship of the One God. The act of “calling upon” (du‘a) includes both supplication and worship. He resolves to direct all his requests, hopes, and devotions to Allah. Then he expresses his optimistic faith: “trusting that I will never be disappointed in invoking my Lord. ” The word “disappointed” (shaqiyyan) can also mean “unfortunate” or “wretched.” Abraham is absolutely certain that Allah will answer his call, comfort him in his exile, and grant him a future far better than what he left behind. This trust was based on his past experience, mentioned in the previous verse, that Allah had always been Most Gracious to him. This verse is a powerful model for anyone who must leave a sinful environment or family for the sake of faith. It teaches that physical separation from evil should be coupled with spiritual nearness to Allah through continuous du‘a, and that this sacrifice will not result in misery but in a far greater, undisappointing connection with the Almighty.

=49=

The verse begins with the Arabic particle “fa,” indicating a direct consequence: “So after he had left them and what they worshipped besides Allah.” Abraham acted on his declaration, physically emigrating from the land of his fathers. He left behind his home, his family, and the familiar idols of his people for the sake of his Lord. This emigration, or hijrah, is a recurring theme in the life of prophets: to flee from evil in order to establish a righteous life elsewhere. Abraham’s departure was a leap of faith into an unknown future, trusting solely in Allah’s promise. Then the verse reveals the magnificent reward that awaited him: “We granted him Isaac and Jacob, and made each of them a prophet.” Abraham left a family of idolatry, and Allah replaced it with a righteous prophetic lineage. Isaac was the son born to him in his old age through his wife Sarah, a gift that itself was a miracle. Jacob was Isaac’s son, Abraham’s grandson, who was also made a prophet. The text mentions both as a direct grant to Abraham, showing that his reward was not just a son but a whole line of prophets who would carry the torch of monotheism. This was the fulfilment of his du‘a and the compensation for losing his biological father and community. Abraham had prayed for a righteous heir, and Allah gave him more than he asked.

This is a profound principle: whoever gives up something forbidden or leaves an evil environment for Allah’s sake, Allah will compensate him with something far better in this world and the next. The mention of Jacob alongside Isaac also stresses that prophethood was firmly established in Abraham’s progeny, countering the Arab polytheists’ claim that they were his heirs yet had fallen into idol worship. The true heirs of Abraham are those who follow his monotheistic path, not merely his bloodline.

=50=

The verse continues to enumerate the blessings bestowed upon Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. “We showered them with Our mercy.” The verb “showered” (wahabna) implies a generous, continuous outpouring of gifts, not a one-time favour. The “mercy” here encompasses all aspects of their lives: prophethood, divine guidance, righteous offspring, material provision, the honour of being mentioned in the Quran, and ultimately, admission into Paradise. It was an all-encompassing mercy that covered this world and the Hereafter. This mercy was a direct result of their devotion and their emigration. Then Allah adds, “and blessed them with honourable mention.

” This is a profound gift. The phrase “honourable mention” (lisana sidq) literally means “a tongue of truth.” It signifies that Allah has granted them a lasting, praiseworthy reputation among all nations and across all ages. To this day, when the name of Abraham is mentioned, Jews, Christians, and Muslims all claim a connection to him and speak of him with reverence. In the five daily prayers, Muslims send blessings upon Abraham and his family. This global, eternal remembrance is a divine gift far superior to worldly fame. It teaches that if a believer sincerely serves Allah, He may not only grant rewards in the afterlife but also a beautiful legacy of good repute in this world. This does not mean seeking fame for its own sake, but that righteousness naturally earns a love and respect that Allah places in the hearts of people. This honourable mention stands in stark contrast to the eventual fate of the disbelievers, who are forgotten or cursed in history. The verse encapsulates the beautiful outcome of Abraham’s sacrifice: a life enveloped in mercy and a legacy bathed in perpetual praise.

=51=

The surah now shifts to the story of Moses, the great lawgiver of the Children of Israel. The command “And mention in the Book, O Prophet, the story of Moses” follows the pattern of the earlier mentions of Abraham, Mary, and others, linking these prophetic narratives together. Moses is introduced as “truly a chosen man.

” The Arabic word “mukhlas” can also mean “purified” or “sincerely devoted. ” He was chosen by Allah from among all people for the immense task of confronting Pharaoh and leading the Israelites out of bondage. His selection was purely by divine grace, not due to any worldly qualification.

The verse then describes him as “a messenger and a prophet.” A prophet (nabi) is someone who receives revelation from Allah, while a messenger (rasul) is a prophet who is also given a new law or scripture and is sent to a specific people with a mission to convey it. Moses was both; he received the Torah and was sent to Pharaoh and the Children of Israel. This dual title emphasises his high rank and the comprehensive nature of his mission.

The mention of Moses right after Abraham shows the continuity of the prophetic chain. Abraham’s prayer for a righteous progeny was answered through the line of Isaac and Jacob, and now Moses appears as a direct descendant of that blessed lineage. Moses’s story is a recurring theme in the Quran, filled with lessons of patience, trust in Allah against tyrannical power, and the victory of truth. For the Muslims in Mecca, who were facing oppression from the Quraysh, the mention of Moses served as a reminder that Allah’s chosen servants ultimately prevail over seemingly invincible tyrants. The description “chosen” also indicates his unique privilege of speaking directly to Allah, which the next verse will highlight, making his selection particularly intimate and special.

=52=

This verse describes one of the most glorious events in Moses’s life. Allah says, “We called him from the right side of Mount Ṭûr.” Mount Ṭûr, or Mount Sinai, is located in the Sinai Peninsula. When Moses was travelling with his family and saw a fire on the mountain, he went to investigate, and it was there that Allah called out to him. The “right side” can refer to the right slope of the mountain or to the direction from which the voice came, but it also carries a connotation of blessing and honour, as the right side is often associated with good and dignity in Arabic and Quranic usage. The call was the beginning of his prophetic mission. Then the verse continues with a profoundly intimate description: “and drew him near, speaking with him directly.

” This is a unique honour granted to Moses. While other prophets received revelation through the angel Gabriel or through inspiration and true dreams, Moses was addressed by Allah directly, without any intermediary.

The Arabic word “najiyyan” comes from “najwa,” meaning a private, intimate conversation. This indicates that Allah elevated Moses to a station of extreme closeness, where the servant could hear his Lord’s speech directly, not as a faint inspiration but as a clear conversation.

This is why Moses is called “Kalimullah,” the one to whom Allah spoke. This direct speech is a central tenet of Islamic belief regarding Moses, emphasising the personal nature of his relationship with the Creator. The verse serves to highlight the majesty of the Quranic narrative: what is being recited to the Prophet Muhammad is the same divine care that was shown to Moses. It also reminds believers of the ultimate honour: to be drawn near to Allah and to hear His words, which is partially experienced by every reciter of the Quran, as the Quran is the direct speech of Allah.

=53=

After describing the honour of direct speech, Allah mentions another gift given to Moses in connection with his mission. “And We appointed for him—out of Our grace—his brother, Aaron, as a prophet.” When Moses was commanded to go to Pharaoh, he felt the immense weight of the task. He was a man who had previously killed an Egyptian and had a speech impediment. So he made a du‘a, recorded in Surah Ta-Ha, asking Allah to expand his chest, ease his task, and appoint for him a helper from his family—his brother Aaron. Allah answered that prayer by making Aaron a prophet alongside Moses.

The phrase “out of Our grace” underscores that this was not a right or a payment but a pure gift of mercy. Aaron was older than Moses, yet he was made his assistant and supporter, and he too was honoured with prophethood. This demonstrates the power of du‘a; a prophet’s request for a helper was not only granted but the helper was elevated to the same high rank. It also shows that prophethood is not limited to one person in a family; it can flow through multiple members. Aaron’s role was crucial: he was the eloquent speaker who would convey the message to Pharaoh while Moses provided the leadership and the direct connection to revelation. Together, they formed a perfect team.

The verse reminds believers that in any great mission, seeking support from family and righteous companions is natural and blessed by Allah. It also reveals the importance of brotherhood and collaboration in the prophetic work.

The mention of Aaron also links back to earlier verses about Mary, “sister of Aaron,” drawing attention to the noble lineage of prophethood within the family of Imran and Levi.

=54=

The surah continues with the command “And mention in the Book, O Prophet, the story of Ishmael.” Ishmael (Isma‘il) was the firstborn son of Abraham, born to Hagar. His story is deeply connected to the Meccan environment, as he is the forefather of the Arabs, and with his father Abraham he built the Ka‘bah and established the rites of pilgrimage. The verse singles out a specific quality: “He was truly a man of his word. ” The phrase “sadiq al-wa‘d” means someone who never breaks a promise; whatever he committed to, he fulfilled with absolute fidelity. This characteristic is so rare and praiseworthy that Allah highlighted it as his defining trait in the Quran. Classical commentators, including Ibn Abbas, relate that Ishmael once promised a man to wait for him at a certain place, and he stayed there for an entire day or even several days until the man returned, not moving from the spot. This steadfastness in fulfilling even minor commitments to fellow human beings was a reflection of his unwavering loyalty to his covenant with Allah. Then the verse adds that he was “a messenger and a prophet,” indicating that his mission was to the tribes of Arabia, particularly the Jurhum and the Amalekites, calling them to the monotheistic faith of his father Abraham. His prophethood is further affirmed by the fact that he was one of the ancestors of the Prophet Muhammad.

The mention of Ishmael here, alongside the other great prophets, serves to honour the lineage of the final Prophet and to remind the Quraysh that their own ancestor was a devoted prophet and a man of utmost truth, and they should therefore follow his path instead of the idolatry they had invented.

=55=

The verse elaborates on Ishmael’s prophetic mission and character. “He used to urge his people to pray and give alms-tax.” Ishmael’s primary concern, like that of all prophets, was to establish the connection between the creation and the Creator through prayer and between people themselves through charity. The prayer (salah) is the pillar of faith, the daily renewal of the bond with Allah. The alms-tax (zakah) is the practical demonstration of mercy towards the needy and the purification of one’s own wealth. Ishmael did not just perform these acts himself; he actively and persistently “urged” his family and his community to observe them.

The Arabic verb implies continuous, repeated encouragement. He was a leader who commanded good not by force but by consistent instruction and motivation. This shows that a believer’s responsibility is not just personal piety but also the nurturing of those under his care. Then the verse crowns his description with a divine commendation: “And his Lord was well pleased with him.

” The Arabic “radiyyan” means that Allah was completely satisfied with Ishmael’s conduct, his faith, his actions, and his character. To attain the state where the Lord of the worlds is pleased with a servant is the highest achievable goal. It is better than any worldly rank or pleasure. This divine pleasure was the result of his truthfulness, his commitment to his promises, and his dedication to spreading prayer and charity.

The sequence is instructive: if a person is true to his word and diligently establishes the worship of Allah and care for others, he becomes deserving of Allah’s pleasure. This verse also provides a beautiful link to the earlier description of Zachariah’s prayer asking for a son who is “pleasing to You,” showing that Ishmael was one such servant who achieved that station.

=56=

The surah now brings a brief but powerful mention of another prophet: “And mention in the Book, O Prophet, the story of Enoch.” Enoch is known in Arabic as Idris. His story is mentioned only a few times in the Quran, but always with great honour. The verse says, “He was surely a man of truth and a prophet. ” The description “man of truth” (siddiq) is the same term used for Abraham and Mary, linking Enoch to the highest ranks of veracity. A siddiq is one whose inner reality completely matches his outward expression; he is utterly sincere, never wavering in his confirmation of divine truth. This quality made him a suitable vessel for prophethood. Many scholars identify Idris with the biblical Enoch, who was the great-grandfather of Noah. He is considered one of the earliest prophets to receive revelation after Adam and Seth. Islamic tradition holds that he was the first human to write with a pen, the first to sew clothes and wear stitched garments, and the first to study astronomy and medicine—all forms of knowledge that he used to guide people to reflect on the creation of Allah. The Quran emphasises his prophethood alongside his truthfulness, indicating that his high spiritual station was founded on an unwavering commitment to truth.

The placement of Idris here, among Abraham, Moses, and Ishmael, shows that prophethood is a continuous chain from the earliest of times. Idris was raised to a lofty place, as the next verse will mention, signifying that his truthfulness and prophethood led to an extraordinary spiritual elevation. This serves as an inspiration that those who embody truth are raised in ranks not only in the eyes of people but in the celestial realms.

=57=

The verse concludes the brief mention of Enoch with a statement of immense honour: “And We elevated him to an honourable status.” The phrase “elevated him” (rafa‘nahu) indicates that Allah took him up in a physical and spiritual sense. The “honourable status” (makanan ‘aliyyan) is understood by the majority of commentators to refer to a high place in Paradise or in the heavens. A well-known hadith from Sahih al-Bukhari and Muslim, regarding the Night Journey (Isra’ and Mi‘raj), states that the Prophet Muhammad met Idris in the fourth heaven.

This is the honourable status given to him. The ascension of Idris is not a mere physical elevation but a symbol of his exalted rank near Allah. His life was so pleasing to his Lord that he was taken up without experiencing death, or after a brief death, to a station of perpetual peace and proximity. This elevation is a testament to the fact that truthfulness and righteousness lift a person’s soul far above the mud of worldly attachments. It also serves as a foretaste of the ultimate elevation that awaits the righteous in the Hereafter.

The concept of being raised in ranks is a recurring theme in the Quran, and Idris represents the pinnacle of that for a human being. For believers, this verse is a reminder that the goal is not to cling to the ground of worldly ambitions but to ascend, through faith and good deeds, towards the Most High.

The mention of Idris right before the collective description of prophets in the next verse highlights that despite the differences in their times and missions, all prophets were ultimately elevated by Allah in various ways, culminating in their honoured status in the Hereafter.

=58=

After mentioning several individual prophets, the Quran now groups them together in a magnificent summary. “Those were some of the prophets who Allah has blessed.” The demonstrative “those” refers to the ones just mentioned—Zachariah, John, Jesus, Abraham, Moses, Aaron, Ishmael, and Idris—as well as all the prophets not named. They are the recipients of divine blessing, which encompasses guidance, prophethood, righteousness, and a goodly remembrance.

The verse then categorises them by lineage: “from among the descendants of Adam,” meaning they all trace back to the first human and prophet, Adam. “And of those We carried with Noah in the Ark” refers to the survivors of the great flood, as all humanity after Noah descends from those who were on the Ark. This highlights that prophethood was carried through the righteous progeny of Noah. “And of the descendants of Abraham and Israel” points to the great prophetic families: Abraham’s sons Ishmael and Isaac, and Israel (Jacob), the grandson of Abraham, from whom the twelve tribes and numerous prophets descended. This catalogue is a refutation of the Arab polytheists who claimed lineage to Abraham yet rejected his monotheistic legacy. Then the verse describes a common, beautiful trait of these prophets: “Whenever the revelations of the Most Compassionate were recited to them, they fell down, prostrating and weeping.

” This is a powerful image of their devotion. They did not merely listen passively; they fell into prostration, the highest form of physical submission, and they wept out of awe, love, and fear of Allah. Their hearts were so soft and receptive that the words of the Most Compassionate overwhelmed them completely.

This is the state of true believers, as opposed to the disbelievers who turn away from the Quran in arrogance and heedlessness. This verse encourages every Muslim to approach the Quran with such humility, letting its verses penetrate the heart until they bring one to tears and prostration.

=59=

The contrast is immediate and stark. After describing the sublime devotion of the prophets, the Quran turns to what happened afterwards: “But they were succeeded by generations who neglected prayer and followed their lusts.” The righteous prophets passed away, and the generations that came after them gradually fell into spiritual decline. The key symptom of their decay was that they “neglected prayer” (ada‘u al-salah). They did not necessarily abandon prayer altogether; they neglected its proper times, its essential conditions, its concentration, and its spirit. They turned the prayer into a hollow ritual or postponed it out of laziness. The loss of prayer is the first step towards the total collapse of faith because prayer is the pillar that upholds the connection with Allah.

The second characteristic of these evil successors was that they “followed their lusts” (ittaba‘u al-shahawat). Once the restraint of prayer was loosened, they became slaves to their base desires—sexual immorality, intoxicants, material greed, and all forms of self-indulgence. This describes not just a particular historical generation but a recurring human pattern. Commentators note that this refers to the later generations of the Children of Israel who corrupted the religion, but it is a universal warning for the Muslim Ummah as well.

The verse then delivers a decisive judgement: “and so will soon face the evil consequences. ” The Arabic “ghayyan” implies a severe punishment, error, and destruction. Some scholars explain that “ghayy” is a valley in Hell, or it means they will meet the evil result of their actions in this world and the next. The “soon” (sawfa) indicates certainty, no matter how delayed it might seem. This verse is a dire warning to all believers to not be like those who inherited the scriptures but lost their way. It calls for safeguarding the prayer and resisting the pull of whims, lest one falls into the same doomed pattern.

=60=

After the severe warning, the door of mercy is immediately opened, demonstrating the Quran’s consistent pattern of balancing threat with glad tidings. “As for those who repent, believe, and do good.” The path to salvation is clearly mapped out. First comes “repent” (taba), which means to turn back from the neglect of prayer, the following of lusts, and all forms of sin, with sincere regret and a firm resolve to amend. Repentance must be genuine, not a mere verbal utterance. Second, “believe,” meaning to have correct faith in Allah, His messengers, His books, and the Last Day. This belief must be untainted by shirk and must reside in the heart, not just on the tongue. Third, “do good,” which refers to righteous deeds that conform to the teachings of the prophets. The combination of these three elements—inner purification through repentance, correct creed, and outward righteous action—constitutes the complete formula for redemption.

The verse then announces their reward: “it is they who will be admitted into Paradise, never being denied any reward.” The admission into Paradise is a pure act of divine generosity.

The phrase “never being denied any reward” (la yuzlamuna shay’an) means that not the slightest amount of their good deeds will be reduced or ignored. This negates any form of injustice; they will receive the full recompense for their faith and works, multiplied many times over by Allah’s grace. This verse offers hope to every sinner who fears that his past misdeeds have barred him from eternal bliss. As long as the soul remains in the body, the gates of repentance are open, and the one who truly returns to Allah will find his record cleared and his reward complete. It beautifully caps the discussion of the evil successors, showing that the cycle of decline can be broken through individual transformation.
