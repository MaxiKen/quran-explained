=1=

The surah opens with these five disconnected Arabic letters, known as al-muqatta‘at. Their precise meaning is a matter of the unseen, known only to Allah. Many of the Prophet’s companions and early scholars, such as Abu Bakr and the four righteous caliphs, held that these letters are a divine secret, and we should not speculate about them beyond affirming that they are part of Allah’s speech.

However, a consistent wisdom noted by commentators like Ibn Kathir and al-Tabari is that these letters appear at the beginning of numerous surahs to immediately capture the attention of the listener. They challenge the Arabs, who prided themselves on their mastery of language, to reflect on the fact that this Quran—which is composed of these very letters they use daily—is inimitable and could not have been produced by a human being. The disjointed nature of the letters also serves to jolt the mind out of its routine, preparing it to receive the profound revelations that follow.

In this context, “Kãf-Ha-Ya-’Aĩn-Ṣãd” introduces a surah filled with extraordinary accounts of Allah’s mercy. There is also a spiritual reading that sees these letters as abbreviations of divine names or attributes: the Kaf for al-Kafi (the Sufficient), the Ha for al-Hadi (the Guide), the Ya for al-Ya-Man (the Blessed), the ‘Ain for al-‘Alim (the All-Knowing), and the Sad for al-Sadiq (the Truthful).

Yet, these are only possible allusions. The safest approach is to acknowledge that they are from the mutashabihat—the ambiguous verses—and that true faith requires us to accept them without asserting a definitive interpretation. The very next verse will immediately translate this atmosphere of mystery into a concrete, moving story of divine mercy.

Thus, the letters act like a profound, silent call to reflection before the narrative of Zachariah begins.

=2=

After the attention-grabbing opening letters, the surah clarifies what follows: “This is a reminder of your Lord’s mercy to His servant Zachariah.” The word “reminder” (dhikr) indicates that the Quran revives and reinforces truths already embedded in human nature and previous scriptures. The mercy mentioned here is not an abstract concept but is demonstrated concretely through the story of Zachariah. It reminds the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) and all believers that the same merciful Lord who answered Zachariah’s heartfelt supplication will also support them. The mercy is attributed to “your Lord,” a deeply personal and nurturing connection, emphasising that Allah’s lordship over the Prophet and his followers entails constant care.

The phrase “His servant Zachariah” is significant; Zachariah is honoured with servanthood before anything else. This highlights that the highest rank a human can attain is to be a true servant of Allah. Zachariah was a prophet from the lineage of Aaron, and he served in the temple in Jerusalem. He had grown old without a child, and his wife was barren. In the face of these impossibilities, the mercy of Allah intervened. This verse serves as a prelude, announcing that the entire account is a manifestation of mercy. It also teaches us that reading and reflecting upon the stories of the prophets is, in itself, a means of receiving Allah’s mercy because it strengthens faith and provides comfort. The reminder is not just a historical report but a living message for every believer who feels that his or her prayers are delayed. It assures that Allah’s mercy descends in ways we cannot anticipate, just as it did for Zachariah.

=3=

This verse describes the manner of Zachariah’s supplication: he “cried out to his Lord privately.” The Arabic word “nida” conveys a call that is earnest, soft, and concealed from others. He did not make a public display of his need; rather, he entered the sanctuary, the mihrab, and turned to his Lord in solitude. This privacy of the call teaches a profound etiquette of supplication. While there is a place for communal du‘a, the most sincere and desperate pleas often occur when the servant is alone with his Maker, away from all eyes. In that secret conversation, the heart is most focused, and the act is furthest from showing off. Ibn Kathir notes that praying secretly is more likely to be answered because it reflects true sincerity. Zachariah knew that only Allah could grant his request, so he directed his call to “his Lord” alone, without intermediaries.

The verse also illustrates that even prophets, who are assured of Allah’s nearness, cry out with deep emotion. They are not self-sufficient. They feel their weakness and turn to the source of all strength. The fact that this private call is recorded in the Quran and read across the world shows that Allah hears every whisper of His servants. No silent prayer, no matter how hidden, escapes His knowledge. This private cry was the direct cause for the immense mercy detailed next, showing that the smallest whisper from a broken heart can move the Lord of the worlds to grant what is seemingly impossible.

=4=

Zachariah begins his intimate plea by describing his frailty: “My Lord! Surely my bones have become brittle.” This vivid image conveys old age not just as a number but as a physical reality of weakness. Bones, the body’s framework, have lost their strength, indicating that his body can no longer support what he desires. He follows with, “and grey hair has spread across my head,” the outward sign of ageing that no one can hide. He presents these conditions not as a complaint but as a statement of his desperate need, acknowledging that from a worldly perspective, the hope for a child has all but vanished.

Yet immediately after, he declares, “but I have never been disappointed in my prayer to You, my Lord!” This remarkable statement reveals an extraordinary level of faith. Despite decades of waiting, despite his body telling him it is too late, he has never once felt that his du‘a was in vain.

This is a profound lesson for every believer. We often measure the success of prayer by immediate results, but Zachariah measured it by his continuous trust that Allah hears and will respond with what is best, in His perfect timing. His past experiences of answered prayers filled him with certainty, not doubt. He addressed Allah repeatedly as “my Lord” (Rabbi), reinforcing the intimate, caring relationship. This teaches us that our moments of weakness should be presented to Allah alongside a firm conviction that He never lets down a sincere caller. Zachariah’s hope was not based on his own strength but on Allah’s unfailing generosity.

=5=

Zachariah now clarifies the motive behind his request: “And I am concerned about the faith of my relatives after me.” He does not seek a child merely for personal joy, lineage pride, or help in old age. His worry is spiritual; he fears that after his death, his kinsfolk and those who look to his leadership might deviate from the true worship of Allah if there is no righteous successor to guide them. This reveals the heart of a prophet, always concerned about the continuity of guidance among the people. He then mentions the natural obstacle: “since my wife is barren.” She had been unable to conceive throughout their life together, and now she is also advanced in age. From a human standpoint, the situation is hopeless, which makes his turning to Allah all the more sincere. He says, “So grant me, by Your grace, an heir. ” The word “grace” (ladunka) means a gift that comes directly from Allah’s own bounty, without any human cause or merit. Zachariah is asking for a miracle that would manifest pure divine favour.

The term “an heir” (waliyyan) refers to someone who would inherit his prophetic mission, not his material wealth. The hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari where the Prophet (peace be upon him) said, “We prophets do not leave inheritance; what we leave is charity,” clarifies that the inheritance of prophets is knowledge, wisdom, and the responsibility of calling people to Allah. Zachariah wanted his legacy of prophethood to continue, and so he asked for a son who would carry that burden.

=6=

Zachariah specifies the qualities of the requested heir. The phrase “who will inherit me” has already been explained to mean inheriting prophethood and sacred knowledge. The addition “and the family of Jacob” is rich in meaning. Zachariah himself was a descendant of Aaron, from the tribe of Levi. The family of Jacob, which is Israel, includes all twelve tribes and the wider prophetic legacy. Some scholars, like Qatadah, understood that Zachariah wished his heir to inherit not only his own prophetic line but also the broader spiritual heritage of the house of Jacob, combining the religious leadership of both. This would unite the blessings of the priestly line of Aaron with the kingly and prophetic line that included David and Solomon. Zachariah’s vision was therefore comprehensive.

Finally, he concludes, “and make him, O Lord, pleasing to You!” This is the ultimate seal of the supplication. The son must not simply be a leader in name; he must be beloved and pleasing to Allah in his character, deeds, and very being. This request reflects the perfect priorities of a prophet. Every parent should learn from this prayer: the first and most important quality to seek for one’s children is not worldly success, but that they be righteous and pleasing to their Creator. Zachariah did not ask for a child who would please him, but one who would please his Lord. The supplication demonstrates that when asking Allah for any blessing, the end goal must always be His pleasure. This lofty prayer was about to be answered in the most beautiful way.

=7=

As soon as the private prayer was made, the answer came directly. The verse says, “The angels announced,” indicating that a group of angels, led by Gabriel, was sent to deliver the glad tidings. The exclamation, “O Zachariah!” conveys a direct and personal address from the divine realm. Allah’s response was immediate and warm: “Indeed, We give you the good news of a son. ” The plural “We” is the majestic plural denoting Allah’s grandeur, and the use of “good news” turns the moment of anxiety into one of overwhelming joy. The angels then specify the son’s name: “whose name will be John.” In Arabic, it is Yahya, derived from the root meaning “to live” or “to be alive.” Scholars such as Ibn Abbas noted that this name symbolises that the child would bring life to the religion, or that his mother’s barrenness would be brought to life through him. Most remarkably, the verse adds, “a name We have not given to anyone before.” This means that no person in human history prior to John had ever been named Yahya. Allah Himself chose this unique name, bypassing all family traditions. This special honour sets John apart, showing his distinct rank among prophets. It also signals that his birth was entirely a direct creation of divine will, not subject to ordinary lineage customs. The good news here teaches us that when Allah answers a prayer, He often grants far more than what was asked: Zachariah wanted an heir; he received a prophet with a name handpicked by the Almighty.

=8=

Upon hearing the glad tidings, Zachariah’s human nature was overtaken by astonishment. He asked, “My Lord! How can I have a son?” It is vital to understand that this was not a question of doubt or disbelief in Allah’s power. A prophet cannot disbelieve. Rather, it was a request for clarification—wondering whether this miracle would occur by making his wife fertile, by some other means, or by a new creation that would alter the natural order. He states the obvious obstacles: “when my wife is barren, and I have become extremely old?” In describing himself as “extremely old,” he emphasises that his physical vigour had decayed, leaving no visible cause for fatherhood. This mirrors the earlier description of his brittle bones and grey hair. The conjunction “and” links both barriers: the maternal barrenness and the paternal old age. From a purely material perspective, the conditions for producing a child were absent on both sides. By articulating this, Zachariah highlights the utter impossibility of the situation from a natural standpoint, thereby magnifying the forthcoming miracle. His question is reminiscent of Abraham’s similar query when given the news of Isaac. Both demonstrate that prophets, despite their deep faith, interact with divine revelations with full human awareness, not robotic acceptance. The moment also serves to instruct believers: it is permissible to seek understanding about the way Allah will bring about His decree, as long as the heart remains certain that Allah is able to do all things.

=9=

The angel’s reply is immediate and authoritative: “So will it be!” This phrase seals the decree; it is non-negotiable and certain, regardless of the natural laws that Zachariah just cited. The angel then conveys Allah’s direct words: “Your Lord says, ‘It is easy for Me.’” The attribute “Your Lord” once again personalises the relationship, reminding Zachariah that the One who has always cared for him is speaking. The statement that it is easy for Allah demolishes all human calculations of difficulty. What is impossible for creation is effortless for the Creator. Then comes the rational proof: “just as I created you before, when you were nothing.” Zachariah’s own existence is the greatest evidence. Before his creation, he was literally nothing—not a body, not a soul, not even a mention. The One who brought him into being from non-existence can certainly bring a child from an old man and a barren woman.

This is logical and profound. If the original creation did not require any pre-existing materials or conditions, then re-combining existing elements or restoring fertility is a far lighter matter. This argument is employed elsewhere in the Quran to refute deniers of resurrection. For Zachariah, it was the ultimate reassurance.

The verse teaches that reflecting on one’s own origin is a powerful cure for anxiety about the future. It reminds us that the difficulties we see are only limitations in our perspective; to the Creator, everything is effortlessly executed.

=10=

After the angel’s definitive reply, Zachariah’s heart was comforted, yet he wished for a tangible sign to confirm the coming of this immense blessing and to mark its beginning. He said, “My Lord! Grant me a sign.” This request for a sign is not a sign of weak faith; it is a natural human desire for a palpable marker of a divine promise, something that would transform the waiting period into a time of intimate worship. Allah, in His wisdom, granted him an unusual sign: “Your sign is that you will not be able to speak to people for three nights, despite being healthy.” The key phrase here is “despite being healthy.” Zachariah would not suffer from a disease or a physical impairment that rendered him mute; his tongue would remain sound, but he would be divinely prevented from engaging in ordinary human conversation. This specific sign carried deep significance. It would force Zachariah into a period of silent devotion, where his only communication would be with his Lord through glorification and remembrance. The duration, “three nights,” is complemented elsewhere as three days. By losing the ability to speak to creation, he would gain a heightened ability to communicate with the Creator. This mirrors the spiritual principle that sometimes blessings require us to disconnect from the world to fully absorb the divine gift. The sign also served as an undisputable proof for his people that something extraordinary was happening to him, preparing them for the miraculous birth of John.

=11=

Zachariah emerged from the sanctuary where he had been praying and received the good news. Immediately, his condition matched the sign given to him: he could not speak. The verse says, “signalling to them to glorify Allah morning and evening.” He communicated through gestures, indicating that his people should occupy themselves with the remembrance of Allah at the two ends of the day. Despite his inability to speak, his first concern was to direct his community toward their Lord. This shows the unwavering sense of responsibility of a prophet; even when physically limited, he found a way to lead his people in worship.

The mention of “morning and evening” is not restrictive; it symbolises the continuity of glorification, meaning in all times. Scholars point out that Zachariah’s signalling could have been done by writing, or simply by pointing to the sky and to his mouth to indicate he was commanded to remain silent while they praised Allah. This event teaches that times of personal trial or restriction should not become an excuse to abandon the duty of enjoining good. It also demonstrates that glorifying Allah is so vital that a prophet, struck with temporary muteness, used his first moments to command it. The “sanctuary” (al-mihrab) is often understood as the prayer chamber where he would worship. His exit from it symbolises a transition from private devotion to public leadership, carrying the blessing of the answered prayer into the world, even while under the sign’s temporary constraint.

=12=

After the account of his miraculous birth, the surah shifts to the early life and qualities of John. The address “O John! Hold firmly to the Scriptures” was a divine command given to him once he reached the age of understanding. The “Scriptures” here refer to the Torah, the book of Moses, which was the prevailing divine law for the Children of Israel at that time. The instruction to “hold firmly” means to take it with serious determination, to study it deeply, to act upon its rulings, and to guard it from distortion.

This command was not just for John but a model for every believer: the scripture is not to be treated casually; it requires strength of will and dedication. Then Allah says, “And We granted him wisdom while he was still a child.” Wisdom, or hukm, encompasses sound judgement, deep understanding of religion, and the ability to rule justly among people. The extraordinary aspect is that John received this profound gift in childhood, an age normally characterised by immaturity and play. Ibn Kathir mentions that some of the early scholars said that the children once called John to play, and he replied, “I was not created for play.” This shows his precocious devotion. The bestowal of wisdom on a child demonstrates that Allah’s gifts are not bound by age. A young heart that is pure and attached to its Lord can surpass the understanding of the elderly. Parents should see this as an encouragement to nurture the spiritual and intellectual faculties of their children early, trusting that Allah can open their hearts to wisdom far beyond their years, just as He did with John.

=13=

Continuing the description of John’s divinely bestowed qualities, Allah says, “as well as purity and compassion from Us.” The word “purity” (hanan) in this context, as explained by many exegetes, refers to a profound tenderness, mercy, and affection that John had for others, particularly for his parents and the weak. It was a loving gentleness that flowed from his heart, given to him directly “from Us,” meaning it was a special divine endowment, not merely a human trait. Some scholars like Mujahid understood “hanan” as mercy from Allah toward John, but the consensus leans toward it being a quality placed in John’s soul.

The mention of “compassion” (zakat) points to spiritual purity, righteousness, and growth in goodness. It signifies that John was purified from sins and character flaws, and his soul was continuously growing in virtue. Together, these two gifts made him a person who combined inner purity with outward love for others.

The verse then adds, “And he was God-fearing,” which translates the Arabic term “taqiyya. ” Taqwa is the crown of all virtues: a constant consciousness of Allah that leads one to obey Him and avoid what displeases Him. John embodied this taqwa in its fullest sense. He lived with the constant awareness that Allah sees, hears, and knows all. This combination—wisdom in childhood, affectionate tenderness, spiritual purity, and profound God-consciousness—made John an ideal prophet and a role model for all believers, especially the youth, showing that true honour lies in internal character, not in worldly status or physical strength.

=14=

The verse immediately highlights a specific manifestation of John’s purity and compassion: he was “kind to his parents.” The Arabic word used is “barr,” denoting extensive righteousness, dutifulness, and consistent goodness toward one’s parents. John’s kindness was not merely a matter of polite behaviour; it encompassed complete devotion to serving them, obeying them in all that was right, speaking gently to them, and caring for them in their old age. This quality is emphasised right after his God-fearing nature because in Islam, kindness to parents is second only to the worship of Allah. John’s example proves that even a prophet, who is busy with scripture and guiding a nation, never neglects his filial duties. Then, the verse presents a double negation: “He was neither arrogant nor disobedient.” Arrogance means considering oneself above others, including one’s parents, and refusing the truth. A person who is arrogant would not humble himself to serve his ageing parents, but John was the opposite. Disobedience, here rendered as “asiyya,” implies rebellion against Allah’s commands, which of course includes disrespecting parents. By stating that he was neither arrogant nor disobedient, the Quran erases any possible thought that a person of John’s high spiritual station might be excused from these basic duties. Rather, his proximity to Allah only increased his humility and obedience.

This is a powerful reminder for all generations: the truest signs of piety are reflected in how one treats one’s parents. John’s life demonstrates that great spiritual stature and humble family service go hand in hand.

=15=

This verse bestows upon John a unique salutation of peace from Allah, encompassing the three most critical and frightening stages of human existence: birth, death, and resurrection. “Peace be upon him the day he was born” guarantees his safety from the distresses that normally accompany birth and from any touch of Satan. It is a greeting of security at the very moment he enters this world of trials. “And the day of his death” assures that he will be granted peace and ease at the time of his soul’s departure, protected from the agonies and the potential trials of the grave. “And the day he will be raised back to life” extends that divine peace to the greatest terror—the Day of Resurrection, when all creation will be gathered and judgement will be passed. On that day, John will be safe from fear and will be raised with honour. This threefold peace is not stated about many prophets in the Quran with this explicit structure; it is a mark of special distinction. Theologically, this verse confirms that the trials of these three moments are real and that only those whom Allah grants peace can navigate them with tranquility. It also serves as a beautiful conclusion to the story of John’s noble qualities. After listing his wisdom, purity, compassion, God-fearing nature, and kindness, Allah himself sends peace upon him. This reinforces that the ultimate goal for every believer is to receive Allah’s peace in all phases of existence.

The mention of resurrection here also links forward to the similar salutation that will be given to Jesus, creating a subtle thread between the two miraculous sons.

=16=

Transitioning from the story of John, the surah seamlessly moves to the even more miraculous story of Jesus and his mother, Mary. The command “And mention in the Book, O Prophet, the story of Mary” calls on the Prophet Muhammad to recite this narrative to his followers as a lesson and a sign. The address “O Prophet” is understood within the structure. Mary, or Mariam, is honoured uniquely in the Quran; she is the only woman mentioned by name, and a whole surah is named after her. The verse pinpoints the start of the momentous event: “when she withdrew from her family to a place in the east.” This seclusion occurred when Mary was a young woman dedicated to the service of the temple in Jerusalem under the guardianship of Zachariah. She moved away from her family and the main area of worship to a chamber or location on the eastern side of the temple precinct. The east could signify the direction where she sought solitude for prayer and purification, or simply a spot that received the first light of day. Her purpose was to engage in pure worship away from people. This physical separation from people was a preparation for a spiritual encounter of immense magnitude. It teaches that sometimes seclusion from the noise of daily life is necessary for one to focus entirely on Allah. In Mary’s case, it set the stage for the angelic visit that would change history, demonstrating that great divine gifts often descend during moments of sincere isolation and devotion.

=17=

Mary did not simply withdraw; she took a further step by “screening herself off from them.” She placed a physical barrier, likely a curtain or a wall, between herself and the people, ensuring complete privacy for her worship.

This emphasis on hijab, or screening, highlights Mary’s modesty and her desire to focus solely on her Lord without any distraction. It was in this state of secluded devotion that the divine encounter occurred: “Then We sent to her Our angel, Gabriel. ” The use of “Our angel” indicates the high status of the emissary; it was the archangel Gabriel, the spirit of revelation, who was sent. He appeared to her not in his original awe-inspiring angelic form but “as a man, perfectly formed.” The description “perfectly formed” (sawiyyan) means that he appeared in a complete, well-proportioned human shape, without any defects, looking like a handsome and ordinary man. This was to prevent her from being terrified at the sight of an angelic being. If Gabriel had appeared in his true form with six hundred wings, she would have been overwhelmed. Instead, Allah’s mercy manifested in making the angel approach her in a familiar and gentle guise. This details the care with which Allah prepared Mary for the unimaginable news.

The verse shows that when Allah sends a test or a blessing, He also provides the means to bear it. For Mary, the means included a secluded space and an angel in a reassuring human form. It also reminds believers that modesty and seclusion for worship are honoured by Allah, and that His angels attend to those who are sincere.

=18=

The instant Mary saw the man in her private space, her instinct was not curiosity but immediate alarm and a strong commitment to her chastity. She “appealed,” or said with firmness, “I truly seek refuge in the Most Compassionate from you!” She invoked Allah’s protection using the divine name “the Most Compassionate” (al-Rahman), which reflects mercy. She was, in effect, calling upon the all-encompassing mercy of Allah to shield her from what she perceived as a potential threat to her honour. Her words are a model for any believer facing a sudden and frightening temptation or danger: the first reflex should be to seek refuge in Allah. She then added a powerful moral challenge: “So leave me alone if you are God-fearing.” This phrase appeals to any piety that might be in the stranger. It implies that if he had any consciousness of God, he would immediately respect her modesty and depart. This was a brilliant combination: she first fortified herself with divine protection, then she appealed to the better nature of the unknown man. It shows her presence of mind and deep faith. Mary did not scream or panic; she reasoned with the fear of God. This verse is frequently cited in discussions of modesty and dealing with the opposite gender. It demonstrates that a believer, when placed in a compromising situation, should immediately turn to Allah and, if possible, remind others of God-consciousness. Her reaction was a direct result of her lifelong dedication to purity, and it forms the perfect moral backdrop for the angel’s astonishing reply.

=19=

Upon hearing Mary’s invocation and challenge, the angel immediately revealed his true purpose to remove her fear. He said, “I am only a messenger from your Lord.” This clarification was essential. He wanted her to understand that he was not a man with human desires, but an envoy carrying a divine command.

The phrase “from your Lord” reconnected her heart back to the source of all comfort; the very One she sought refuge with had sent this messenger. The wording “only a messenger” assured her that he had no personal intent; he was there solely to deliver a specific gift. Then he disclosed the nature of his mission: “sent to bless you with a pure son.

” The word “bless” (li-ahaba) denotes a gift, a grant of something wonderful. What he was announcing was a son, but specifically “a pure son” (ghulaman zakiyyan).

The term “zakiyy” implies purity, righteousness, growth, and being free from all forms of sin and blame. Jesus would be a son of unparalleled purity, born without the touch of any human father.

This description directly answered Mary’s unspoken fear about her chastity: the child would be a sign of her virtue, not a cause for suspicion. Thus, the angel transitioned Mary’s state from fear to amazement. The verse highlights how quickly Allah can turn a moment of terror into one of the greatest honours. It also shows that the gifts of Allah sometimes come in forms that initially appear frightening, but are in fact pure blessings.

=20=

Despite the angel’s reassuring words, the enormity of the news left Mary bewildered. She responded with a question that any chaste woman would ask: “How can I have a son when no man has ever touched me, nor am I unchaste?” This query was not an expression of disbelief in Allah’s power; it was a logical inquiry about the method. She presented two irrefutable facts about her state. First, “no man has ever touched me,” meaning she had never been married, nor had any lawful or unlawful physical relationship with a male. She was a virgin in the most complete sense. Second, she added “nor am I unchaste,” rejecting even the possibility of being a woman of loose morals or any form of impropriety. Her choice of words indicated that she guarded her chastity as her most precious possession. By stating both the physical and moral dimensions of her purity, she sought to understand how the conception would occur without compromising her virtue. The angel would soon explain that it would be a direct divine command, “Be,” and it is. Mary’s question, like Zachariah’s earlier, is a testament to the Quranic principle that faith does not mean silencing the rational mind; it means seeking understanding while maintaining deep trust. Her wonderment also sets the stage for the explicit defence of her honour later in the surah. For every woman who values her modesty, Mary’s reaction is the gold standard: even when receiving what seems like the highest honour, she immediately sought confirmation that her moral standing would remain intact.
