=61=

Having declared that the repentant believers will enter Paradise without the least reduction in their reward, the surah now unveils the nature of that blissful abode. “They will be in the Gardens of Eternity.

” The phrase “Gardens of Eternity” translates “Jannatu ‘Adn,” which linguistically points to a permanent, settled residence from which one never departs. Unlike the fleeting pleasures of this world, the delights of Paradise are everlasting; there is no fear of eviction, decay, or death. This permanence is one of the greatest joys, as the human heart is naturally anxious about loss.

The verse then describes these gardens as “promised in trust by the Most Compassionate to His servants.” The promise is attributed directly to “the Most Compassionate” (al-Rahman), highlighting that the ultimate source of this unimaginable gift is sheer divine mercy.

The word “promised” (wa‘ada) gives it the weight of a binding commitment from Allah, and “in trust” (ghayb) means it is an unseen promise—something the believers accept by faith in this world, trusting that it awaits them despite never having seen it. The servants referred to are those described in the previous verse: those who repent, believe, and do good. They are honoured with the title “His servants,” a direct possessive that conveys closeness and special honour. The verse closes with the powerful reassurance: “Surely His promise will be fulfilled.

” The Arabic emphasis with “kana” indicates that this fulfilment is an absolute certainty, an established fact in Allah’s decree. No force can prevent it.

For the believer facing hardship, this verse plants a firm anchor of hope. It reminds us that the toil of this life is a temporary path leading to an eternal home, promised by the Most Merciful whose word is unbreakable. This certainty transforms the believer’s perspective, making every sacrifice for the sake of Allah a worthy investment.

=62=

The verse continues painting the internal atmosphere of the Gardens of Eternity. “There they will never hear any idle talk.

” The Arabic word “laghw” refers to vain, frivolous, or sinful speech—anything that brings no benefit and might cause harm or boredom. In this world, even in the best gatherings, some idle or hurtful words inevitably slip in. But Paradise is completely purified of such pollution. The ears of its inhabitants will be shielded from gossip, arguments, falsehood, and all unpleasant sounds. Instead, they will hear “only greetings of peace.

” The word “salam” here encompasses the greetings they will exchange, the salutations from the angels, and the very atmosphere of safety, tranquillity, and well-being that pervades their existence. This peace is both external and internal; they will be free from conflict, anxiety, and all causes of disturbance. It is a return to the original greeting of Islam, now fully realised as a lived reality. Then the verse mentions their sustenance: “And there they will have their provisions morning and evening.” This does not mean that there is a day-night cycle in Paradise in the physical sense; rather, it indicates that they will enjoy continuous, delightful provision at regular intervals, much like the familiar rhythm of morning and evening meals in this life, but with infinite perfection. The provisions will be served to them without any effort on their part, always fresh and satisfying. Some scholars note that these periods correspond to the times of the worldly prayers they observed: the morning and the late afternoon, showing that their spiritual sustenance in the world has become tangible delight in the Hereafter. This verse tells believers that the peace and provision of Paradise are utterly free from the blemishes of the world, providing a vision of serene, joyful community life that motivates one to strive for that final home.

=63=

After describing the Gardens of Eternity with their peace and provision, the verse concludes the description with a clear statement of inheritance: “That is Paradise, which We will grant to whoever is devout among Our servants.” The word “that” (tilka) points back to the splendid abode just portrayed, emphasising that this is the specific reward. The verb “We will grant” (nurithu) is deeply significant. It comes from the root “waratha,” meaning to inherit. Paradise is not something earned in a strict transactional sense, as no human deeds can match its infinite value. Rather, it is a bequest, an inheritance that the believers receive from their Lord out of His mercy, conditioned upon their faith and righteousness. It is the “inheritance” promised to the righteous in many Quranic verses. The recipients are identified as “whoever is devout among Our servants. ” The word “devout” translates “man kana taqiyyan”—those who possess taqwa. Taqwa is a comprehensive quality of God-consciousness: a constant awareness of Allah that motivates a person to obey His commands and avoid His prohibitions. It encompasses the inner state of the heart and the outward behaviour. The restriction “among Our servants” indicates that this inheritance is only for those who truly belong to Allah’s devoted servants, not merely those who claim belief with their tongues. This verse powerfully ties the description of Paradise’s delights to a practical condition: taqwa. It is not enough to admire the description; one must actively cultivate devotion. It also addresses the exclusivity of the reward, which serves as a warning to those who are negligent.

The use of “whoever” (man) makes the offer universal; anyone, regardless of background or past, who becomes among the people of taqwa, will inherit this Paradise. This is the ultimate encouragement, linking the magnificent unseen promise directly to the attainable quality of a pious heart.

=64=

This verse records the speech of the angel Gabriel, though the context links it to a specific incident. According to a sound hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari narrated by Ibn Abbas, the Prophet Muhammad once noticed a delay in the coming of Gabriel and when the angel finally arrived, he asked him about it. Gabriel replied, and these verses were revealed as his answer. The angels, including Gabriel, do not act on their own accord. They declare: “We only descend by the command of your Lord.” Their movement between the heavens and the earth, and the bringing of revelation, is entirely subject to Allah’s order. This immediately refutes any notion that the Prophet had control over revelation or that the angels come at his beck and call. The timing, the content, and the frequency of revelation are entirely decreed by Allah’s wisdom. The angel continues, “To Him belongs whatever is before us, and whatever is behind us, and everything in between.

” This is a comprehensive acknowledgement of Allah’s absolute ownership and knowledge over all creation, including the angels themselves. Whatever is in their future, their past, and their present, all belongs to the Lord. They have no independent power or knowledge. Then the angel adds, “And your Lord is never forgetful.

” This is a response to any implication that the delay might be due to forgetfulness on Allah’s part. Allah never forgets His servants, His promises, or the right moment for revelation. If there is a delay, it is out of profound wisdom, not negligence. This entire statement is a profound lesson in the relationship between the Creator and His creation. Even the highest angel professes total subservience and absolute dependence. The verse reassures the Prophet and the believers that the seeming pauses in revelation are not signs of abandonment; everything is under perfect control. It also teaches that all sincere servants must echo this attitude: we act only by Allah’s leave, and we trust His flawless timing and perfect memory.

=65=

The angelic speech seamlessly flows into a declaration of monotheism, directing attention to the Creator who sent him. “He is the Lord of the heavens, and the earth, and everything in between.” This comprehensive statement establishes Allah’s exclusive lordship over the entire cosmos. “Heavens” includes all the celestial realms, stars, galaxies, and what is beyond human perception. “Earth” includes the planet with its mountains, oceans, and all living beings. “Everything in between” covers the atmosphere, the clouds, the birds in flight, the angels descending and ascending—everything that exists in the space between the sky and the ground. All of it belongs to Him and is governed by His command. Because He is the sole Lord of all, the natural conclusion follows: “So worship Him alone. ” Worship must be directed only to the One who owns and controls everything. To worship anything else is a logical absurdity, as those things are themselves part of the creation and owned by Him. Then the command is intensified: “and be steadfast in His worship.” It is not enough to worship occasionally; one must persist patiently and consistently in the face of difficulties, fatigue, and distractions. The call to steadfastness is especially relevant given the context of delayed revelation; the Prophet is being told to remain firm in worship even when he experiences periods of seeming silence.

Finally, a rhetorical question is posed: “Do you know of anyone equal to Him in His attributes?” The expected answer is an emphatic “No.” There is absolutely nothing and no one comparable to Allah—not in His names, His attributes, His actions, or His rights. This question is a challenge to the polytheists and a reminder to the believers. The entire verse encapsulates the core message of the surah and the Quran: the Lordship of Allah implies His exclusive right to worship, which must be performed with steadfastness, based on the firm conviction that He is uniquely without equal.

=66=

Having reinforced the foundations of monotheism and the truth of revelation, the Quran now turns to another central doctrine that the disbelievers denied: the resurrection. “Yet some people ask mockingly, ‘After I die, will I really be raised to life again?’” The transition “yet” indicates that despite all the clear signs of Allah’s power and the certain promise of the Hereafter, there are people who respond with mockery.

The question they pose is not a sincere inquiry seeking truth; it is delivered in a sarcastic, dismissive tone. The phrasing “will I really” (a’idha) carries the connotation of incredulity—they find the idea of being brought back to life after decomposition utterly preposterous. They see death as the final end, a permanent dissolution of the body, and they cannot conceive that the same person could be reassembled and resurrected. This question reflects a narrow, materialistic worldview that limits reality to what can be physically perceived and tested in this life. The mockery highlights the arrogance of the disbelievers; rather than humbly considering the possibility, they treat it as a joke. Their question also contains an implicit denial of divine power, as if to say that Allah cannot bring them back.

However, the question itself is ironically self-defeating. If death is total non-existence, why speak of “I” after death? The very question reveals the innate human intuition of an enduring soul. The Quran records this mockery to expose the weakness of the disbelieving position and to comfort the Prophet, showing him that the ridicule he faced from the Quraysh was part of a pattern repeated by deniers across history. It sets the stage for the rational and forceful responses that follow, which will dismantle their objection by appealing to their own origin.

=67=

The immediate response to the mockery is a concise, powerful rational argument. Allah asks, “Do such people not remember that We created them before, when they were nothing?” The rhetorical question strikes at the heart of their denial. Every human being’s own existence is the greatest proof against the impossibility of resurrection. Before their creation, they were literally nothing—not a body, not a soul, not even a mention. Then, by a drop of fluid, Allah brought them into a fully formed, conscious being. If He had the power to originate them from non-existence, what difficulty could there be in bringing them back a second time? The Quran repeatedly uses this argument: “As He began the first creation, He will repeat it” (Surah Al-Ankabut, 19). The act of recreation is logically easier than the first creation, even though both are equally effortless for Allah.

The phrase “do they not remember” (a-wa-la yadhkurun) is an appeal to the basic faculty of recall and reason. The disbelievers are not asked to believe in something unfamiliar; they are asked to reflect on their own undeniable origin. The fact that they were once nothing should humble them and make them realise their complete dependence on the Creator.

The verse also contains a subtle rebuke: their mockery stems from a failure to think. If they would only “remember” and ponder their beginning, the idea of resurrection would cease to seem absurd. This teaches believers that the way to combat doubts about the Hereafter is to reflect deeply on the signs within themselves and in the universe around them, for the proofs are woven into the very fabric of their being.

=68=

After the rational argument, Allah reinforces the certainty of the Day of Judgement with a powerful oath: “By your Lord, O Prophet!” This is an oath sworn by Allah Himself, adding the greatest possible weight to the statement. The direct address “O Prophet” reassures the Messenger and threatens the deniers. The oath confirms three terrifying events: “We will surely gather them along with the devils. ” The disbelievers will be resurrected and herded together with the satans—the jinn devils who misled them and the human devils who were leaders of disbelief. In this world, they were allies in rebellion; in the Hereafter, they will share in the punishment. They will be gathered in a humiliating manner, not as honoured guests but as criminals. Then, “and then set them around Hell on their knees. ” The image is one of extreme degradation. They will be forced to kneel around the brim of Hell, witnessing its blazing flames and hearing its raging sounds, awaiting their turn to be cast into it. The posture of kneeling indicates submission, weakness, and terror; the arrogant who once stood proudly in defiance will be brought to their knees. Some narrations explain that all of humanity will initially kneel around the chasm of Hell before the righteous are separated, as per the upcoming verses. This verse evokes a vivid, horrifying scene meant to shake the heedless heart.

The mention of “the devils” alongside them emphasises the evil of their chosen companionship and the total abandonment that will occur when the devils disown their followers. It serves as a warning to those who mock the Prophet and deny the resurrection: the One who created them from nothing will surely assemble them for judgement, and no amount of arrogance can withstand that reality.

=69=

The horrifying scene at the edge of Hell is elaborated. “Then We will certainly begin by dragging out of every group the ones most defiant to the Most Compassionate.” After the criminals are gathered on their knees, the sorting will commence. From each community or religious group, Allah will first single out the ringleaders of defiance—those who were the most stubborn in their rebellion against the Most Compassionate. The verb “dragging out” (lananzi‘anna) suggests a forceful, painful extraction, treating these rebels without any dignity. The description “most defiant” refers to those who combined persistent disbelief with active opposition to the truth, oppression of believers, and incitement of others to misguidance. They are singled out first because their crime was the greatest. Notice the specific use of the name “the Most Compassionate” (al-Rahman) in this context. These people defied the very mercy of Allah, rejecting His invitations and mocking His prophets. Their defiance against mercy itself makes their punishment especially fitting. This also echoes the earlier pleas of Abraham, who warned his father about becoming a companion of Satan because of defying the Most Compassionate.

Thus, the surah consistently links rebellion against al-Rahman with a terrible end. This verse demonstrates the absolute justice of the divine judgement. It is not a random punishment; the most guilty are identified with precise knowledge and are punished first, serving as an example. For the believers, this scene encourages perseverance in the face of arrogant oppressors, knowing that their defiance is noted and will be the cause of their first and most severe reckoning.

The verse also instills a healthy fear of becoming defiant oneself by consistently rejecting the call of the Most Compassionate.

=70=

The sorting continues with an affirmation of Allah’s perfect knowledge and justice. “And We truly know best who is most deserving of burning in it.

” The word “We truly know” (la-nahnu a‘lam) uses an emphatic construction, indicating that no one can escape the precise knowledge of Allah regarding their inner and outer states. The judgement is not based on superficial appearance, worldly status, or human assumptions, but on a complete, unfailing record of deeds and intentions. “Who is most deserving” means that the punishment is proportioned exactly to the degree of one’s defiance and corruption. Not everyone in Hell will experience the same torment; the levels of punishment vary according to the severity of the individual’s sins, and Allah alone knows the exact measure deserved by each soul. This verse answers an unspoken question: how will the angels know which ones to drag out first?

The answer is that the command will be based on divine knowledge that sees all, even the secrets of hearts. The statement also serves as a warning against complacency; no one can hide behind a facade of religiosity, and no one can rely on lineage or past reputation. The One who knows all will allocate the fire precisely.

Furthermore, it contains an implicit comfort for those who were oppressed and misjudged in the world. Their oppressors may have escaped worldly justice, but they will not escape the perfect knowledge and justice of Allah. The verse reinforces the theme of ultimate accountability and the meticulous nature of the divine court on the Day of Judgement.

For the believer, it is a call to constantly purify one’s inner state, knowing that the final judgement will be based on a complete and infallible evaluation.

=71=

This verse contains a profound and much-discussed statement about the Hereafter. Allah declares, “There is none of you who will not pass over it.” The “it” refers to Hellfire, based on the preceding context where the disbelievers are set around it on their knees.

The phrase addresses all of humanity—both believers and disbelievers—affirming that every single person will “pass over” or “arrive at” Hell. The Arabic “wariduha” can mean to approach, to arrive at the edge, or to enter. The authentic interpretation, supported by many companions including Ibn Abbas, Ibn Mas‘ud, and the majority of scholars, is that this refers to the passage over the Sirat—the bridge that spans over Hell, sharper than a sword and thinner than a hair. All people, believers and sinners alike, will have to cross this bridge. The believers will cross safely and swiftly according to their deeds, as light will illuminate their path, while the disbelievers and unrepentant sinners will slip and fall into the Fire below.

This is the “passing over” that no one can avoid. Some early scholars held that even believers will briefly enter the Hellfire but will not be harmed, as a purification, but the stronger view is that of the passage over the bridge. Then Allah says, “This is a decree your Lord must fulfil.” The event is an irrevocable, binding decree (hatman maqdiyyan) from which there is no exemption. No human, no matter how righteous, will bypass this stage. The terror of this crossing is the ultimate trial before entering Paradise, and it is a manifestation of divine justice that all souls experience the reality of the Fire and the immense mercy that saves the believers from it. This verse thus combines profound fear with profound hope, and it has led believers throughout history to weep and beseech Allah for a safe passage.

=72=

Following the universal passage over Hell, the final separation occurs. “Then We will deliver those who were devout.

” The word “deliver” (nunajji) means to rescue and save from peril. The “devout” are the people of taqwa—those who maintained God-consciousness in their lives by obeying Allah’s commands and avoiding His prohibitions. After the immense terror of the crossing, Allah’s mercy will intervene specifically for them, pulling them to safety and admitting them into Paradise. Their deliverance is not because of their own merit alone but because of Allah’s grace and their devotion. The contrast is stark and horrifying: “leaving the wrongdoers there on their knees.” The wrongdoers—those who denied the truth, mocked the resurrection, and oppressed others—will remain in the humiliating kneeling position described earlier, around the edge of Hell or fallen from the bridge into it.

The phrase “on their knees” (jithiyyan) underscores that the pride they exhibited in the world has been crushed, and they are now fixed in a posture of abject humiliation, unable to move or escape. This verse completes the picture of divine justice. The passage over Hell is the ultimate sorting ground; no one can cheat that final exam. The deliverance of the devout is an eternal mercy, while the abandonment of the wrongdoers is an eternal justice. The verse ties back to the earlier promise that Paradise is for the devout servants, and it warns that a mere claim of faith without the substance of taqwa will not suffice on that Day. It encourages the believer to cling to devotion, knowing that the terror of that Day is real and that salvation is exclusively through Allah’s mercy granted to those who truly feared Him.

=73=

The surah now returns to the scenes of worldly arrogance, contrasting the eventual humiliation of the disbelievers on the Day of Judgement with their current boastfulness. “When Our clear revelations are recited to them, the disbelievers ask the believers mockingly.” The “clear revelations” are the verses of the Quran, whose truth is self-evident.

Yet, when these verses are recited, instead of reflecting on their meaning, the disbelievers turn to the weak, poor, and oppressed believers—people like Bilal, Ammar, and Suhayb in Mecca—and issue a mocking challenge. They ask, “Which of the two of us is better in status and superior in assembly?

” The word “status” (maqaman) refers to social standing, wealth, and prestige in the community. “Assembly” (nadiyyan) refers to their councils, clubs, and gatherings where the elite met. The Meccan chiefs like Abu Jahl and al-Walid ibn al-Mughirah looked at their own fine clothes, large houses, and influential circles, and compared them to the humble state of the believers, many of whom were slaves or poor. They equated worldly success with divine favour, arguing that if the message of the Prophet were true, they, the social leaders, would be its followers. They failed to understand that divine favour is based on faith and righteousness, not on wealth and status. This verse exposes the mentality of materialistic arrogance that has plagued humanity across ages. It shows how the elite justify their rejection of truth by pointing to their worldly privileges, as if these are rewards from God for their inherent superiority. The Quran records their argument not to endorse it but to refute it decisively in the verses that follow, revealing the emptiness of their criteria.

=74=

The rebuttal comes immediately. “Imagine, O Prophet, how many peoples We have destroyed before them, who were far better in luxury and splendour!” The rhetorical question forces the arrogant disbelievers to look at history. Numerous civilisations before them—the people of Noah, ‘Ad, Thamud, Pharaoh’s entourage—possessed far greater material wealth, imposing buildings, advanced industries, and military might than the Quraysh could ever dream of.

The word “luxury” (athathan) refers to beautiful furniture, wealth, and possessions, while “splendour” (ri’yan) refers to outward appearance, fine clothing, and impressive physical presentation. Despite their superior riches and grandeur, these peoples were completely destroyed when they rejected the prophets sent to them. Their ruins bear silent testimony that material prosperity is no defence against divine punishment. If Allah’s favour were truly measured by worldly status, those mighty civilisations would not have been wiped out. This argument shatters the disbelievers’ logic. The verse calls the Prophet to “imagine” or “consider” these past nations as a source of comfort and strength; the powerful who now mock him are insignificant compared to those whom Allah destroyed with ease. It also serves as a warning to the Meccans that their wealth and assemblies will not save them if they persist in their denial. The lesson is timeless: civilisations rise and fall, but the truth remains. Worldly superiority is a fleeting illusion, and to consider it a sign of divine approval is a fatal delusion. True honour lies in faith, as the next verse will further clarify through the fate of those who are deluded by their temporary success.

=75=

Allah instructs the Prophet on how to respond to the mockers’ question about status. “Say, O Prophet, ‘Whoever is entrenched in misguidance, the Most Compassionate will allow them plenty of time.’” This is a profound explanation of the phenomenon the disbelievers mistake for divine favour. Those who stubbornly persist in error are not being blessed; they are being given respite.

The Arabic “madd” means to extend and supply abundantly, giving them a long rope. Allah, in His forbearance, does not punish them immediately but grants them time, wealth, and opportunities.

This is called “istidraj”—a gradual leading to destruction through apparent blessings. Then, suddenly, “until—behold!—they face what they are threatened with: either the torment or the Hour.” The respite ends abruptly when the punishment descends, whether in this world as a calamity, at the moment of death, or on the Day of Judgement. At that instant, all illusions shatter. “Only then will they realize who is worse in position and inferior in manpower.” The words “position” (makanan) and “manpower” (jundan) directly echo their earlier boast about “status” and “assembly.” The disbelievers claimed superiority based on their worldly councils and supporters. But when the divine punishment comes, it will be manifestly clear that they are in the worse position—the fire of Hell—and that their legions of followers and protectors are utterly powerless and inferior.

In fact, they have no real manpower at all; their armies will avail them nothing. This verse teaches believers to never envy the worldly success of the unrighteous, for it may be a prelude to their ruin. True wisdom is to look at the end, not the present glitter.

=76=

The verse shifts from the fate of the misguided to the path of the believers, providing a beautiful contrast. “And Allah increases in guidance those who are rightly guided.

” This is a profound principle of spiritual growth. When a person accepts the initial guidance and walks towards Allah, Allah rewards that effort by granting them more guidance. The more one acts upon knowledge, the more insight and clarity one receives. This increase can be in understanding of the Quran, in the ability to distinguish truth from falsehood, in the love of good deeds, and in steadfastness. It is a virtuous cycle: guidance leads to more guidance. The verse confirms that guidance is not a static gift but a dynamic, growing light that expands with sincere effort. Then, the verse presents a powerful comparison: “And the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord in reward and in outcome.” The “everlasting good deeds” (al-baqiyat al-salihat) refer to acts of worship and righteousness whose benefit endures beyond this world, such as prayer, glorification of Allah, charity, beneficial knowledge, and righteous offspring. These deeds are contrasted with the fleeting wealth and assemblies of the disbelievers. Their value is with “your Lord,” meaning they are stored in the divine treasury where they are preserved, multiplied, and never lost. They are better in “reward” (thawaban)—the immediate spiritual compensation and the infinite reward in the Hereafter—and better in “outcome” (mardadan)—the final return and consequence. The worldly possessions of the disbelievers will vanish or become a source of regret, while the good deeds of the believers will yield eternal joy. This verse encourages believers to focus their energy on accumulating these everlasting deeds, rather than envying or competing with the worldly elite. It is an assurance that the true riches are those that survive death.

=77=

The surah now presents a specific example of the deluded mentality just discussed. The verse asks, “Have you seen, O Prophet, the one who rejects Our revelations yet boasts…?

” The question draws attention to a particular individual as a case study of arrogance and foolishness. According to sound reports, including a hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari and Muslim, this refers to al-‘As ibn Wa’il al-Sahmi, a staunch Meccan disbeliever. He owed some money to Khabbab ibn al-Aratt, a poor Muslim companion. When Khabbab asked for repayment, al-‘As mockingly said that he would not pay until Khabbab renounced Muhammad. Khabbab replied that he would never renounce the Prophet, even after death and resurrection. Al-‘As then sneered, “Well then, if I am raised up after death, I will surely have wealth and children there, and then I will pay you back! ” This statement was dripping with sarcasm; he did not actually believe in the resurrection but used it to mock the concept. His “boast” implied that the same worldly logic would apply in the Hereafter—that he, as a chief in this life, would automatically be wealthy and have many sons in the next life, if such a thing existed. This reveals a profound misunderstanding of the Hereafter. It is not a continuation of worldly social hierarchies; it is a complete reset based purely on faith and deeds. The verse exposes the absurdity of such a claim.

The question “Have you seen” expresses amazement at such blatant ignorance, inviting all to witness the depths of human arrogance. This specific story also illustrates the broader theme: the disbelievers reject the revelations, yet when forced to contemplate the possibility of an afterlife, they project their worldly privileges onto it, assuming God’s favour mirrors their own self-serving standards.

=78=

The Quran rhetorically interrogates the baseless claim of al-‘As ibn Wa’il and those like him. “Has he looked into the unseen?” The “unseen” (al-ghayb) is that which is hidden from human perception, including the events of the Hereafter.

The question is sarcastic: did this man somehow peer into the hidden realms and obtain knowledge that he would be wealthy in the afterlife? The obvious answer is no; he has no access to such knowledge. His claim is pure conjecture, built on arrogance and wishful thinking.

The second question is even more piercing: “or taken a pledge from the Most Compassionate?” A “pledge” (‘ahd) means a formal covenant or a binding promise. Did the Most Compassionate enter into an agreement with this disbeliever guaranteeing him wealth and children in the Hereafter?

This is an absurd proposition. Allah does not give such promises to those who reject His revelations and mock His messengers.

The name “the Most Compassionate” is used deliberately, reminding the listener that Allah’s mercy is vast, but it is accessed through faith, not through a presumptuous guarantee secured while one is in a state of disbelief. The disbeliever is effectively claiming a special deal with Allah, bypassing the conditions of belief and righteousness. Both questions highlight the complete lack of evidence for his assertion. He is speaking without knowledge and without authority.

This is a characteristic of those who argue against the truth: their arguments are not based on reason or revelation but on empty boasts and groundless assumptions. The verse teaches believers not to be shaken by the confident claims of deniers; behind their bravado is nothing but ignorance and self-deception. True knowledge of the unseen belongs exclusively to Allah, and the only way to secure a good outcome in the Hereafter is through the covenant of faith, not through wishful thinking.

=79=

The verses connect with a decisive rejection of the disbeliever’s boast. “Not at all!” (Kalla) is a strong Arabic particle of refutation, completely denying his claim. It is as if Allah is saying: “Absolutely not! The matter is not as he imagines. ” The verse then states the actual divine response to such arrogant assertions: “We certainly record whatever he claims.

” This is a terrifying truth. Every word uttered, especially the mocking and false claims against Allah’s power and justice, is meticulously documented by the angels. The boaster’s sarcastic statement about receiving wealth and children in the Hereafter is not forgotten; it is inscribed in his book of deeds as a record of his blasphemy and mockery. The recording of his claim is itself a proof against him on the Day of Judgement. Then the verse adds, “and will increase his punishment extensively.

” The word “increase” (namuddu) is the same verb used earlier for granting the misguided plenty of time; here it is applied to punishment. His punishment will be augmented because of his boasting. Not only will he suffer for his disbelief and rejection of the revelations, but an extra measure of torment will be added for his arrogant and derisive remarks. This serves as a stark warning against mocking religious truths. Words are not empty; they carry weighty consequences in the divine court. Al-‘As ibn Wa’il’s casual sarcasm, intended to belittle a poor believer, will become a source of multiplied agony for him in Hell. This verse comforts those who are ridiculed for their faith, assuring them that every insult and every arrogant boast is being recorded by the Lord of the worlds, and the perpetrators will face a punishment intensified by their own tongues.

=80=

The final verse of this passage deals a crushing blow to the worldly pride that fuelled the disbeliever’s arrogance. “And We will inherit what he boasts of.” Everything that al-‘As ibn Wa’il prided himself on and presumed would carry over into the Hereafter—his wealth, his children, his social standing—will be stripped away entirely. Allah, the Eternal Inheritor, will take back all that was temporarily in his possession. When he dies, he will leave every ounce of his wealth and every child he relied upon behind, and they will become the property of others.

Ultimately, they will all perish, and Allah alone will remain. The disbeliever boasted of what he would have in the afterlife, but in reality, he will have nothing, for Allah inherits the earth and all upon it. Then comes the most chilling image: “and he will come before Us all by himself. ” On the Day of Judgement, this once-proud individual, who surrounded himself with supporters and sons and thought his assemblies were a sign of his superiority, will stand completely alone in the divine court. No wealth, no children, no clan, no protectors. Every soul on that Day will be concerned only with its own fate.

The phrase “all by himself” (fardan) emphasises utter isolation, vulnerability, and helplessness. This is the true state of every human being before the Creator, stripped of all worldly props. The contrast is stark: in the world, he was “superior in assembly,” but before Allah, he is solitary and destitute. This verse delivers a profound lesson on the ultimate reality of human existence. All the relationships and possessions that we accumulate are temporary loans; they do not define our worth in the sight of Allah, and they will not accompany us through the grave. True preparation for that solitary meeting is a heart filled with sincere faith and a record of good deeds.
