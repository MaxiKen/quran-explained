=21=

After Mary expressed her astonishment at how she could have a son while remaining chaste, the angel delivered the decisive answer. He began with the same firm phrase given earlier to Zachariah: “So will it be!” This removes any room for uncertainty; the matter is settled by divine will. Then the angel conveyed Allah’s direct speech: “Your Lord says, ‘It is easy for Me.’” The repetition of this phrase in both the story of John and Jesus links the two miracles, showing that creating a child without a father is just as effortless for Allah as creating one from aged, barren parents.

The phrase “And so will We make him a sign for humanity” reveals the higher purpose behind the virgin birth. Jesus himself, in his very being and miraculous arrival, would be a sign pointing to Allah’s limitless power and to the reality of resurrection. A sign, by nature, is something extraordinary that breaks the normal pattern to draw attention. Jesus’ entire life—from his fatherless birth, to speaking in the cradle, to healing the blind and leper, to raising the dead—was a constellation of signs.

The verse adds “and a mercy from Us,” underscoring that Jesus was not merely a wonder but a source of immense mercy: he would bring a scripture, guidance, healing, and compassion to the Children of Israel. This echoes the description of Jesus elsewhere as a mercy for mankind.

Finally, the angel states, “It is a matter already decreed,” meaning that this event was inscribed in the Preserved Tablet long before it occurred. Nothing could prevent it.

The word “decreed” (maqdiyyan) implies a divine command that has already been issued and settled. For Mary, this removed any sense that she had any say in the matter; it was an honour bestowed upon her by an irrevocable divine choice. This verse provides immense comfort: when Allah decrees something for a believer, no apparent impossibility can stand in its way. The child would be both a sign and a mercy, and his coming was predetermined in eternity.

=22=

Following the angel’s announcement and the divine decree, the conception occurred immediately. The verse says simply, “So she conceived him.

” The Arabic particle “fa” indicates a direct, uninterrupted sequence. Without any further human involvement, by the mere command of Allah and the breath of Gabriel, Jesus began to form in Mary’s womb. This conception was entirely miraculous, bypassing all biological norms. The well-known hadith narrated by Abu Hurairah in Sahih al-Bukhari states that Satan touches every child at birth except Mary and her son Jesus. This exception highlights the unique purity surrounding this conception, as the touch of Satan was averted by the special prayer of Mary’s mother, Hannah, when she dedicated her child to the service of Allah. After conceiving, Mary “withdrew with him to a remote place. ” Faced with the social realities of bearing a child without a husband, she left her usual dwelling and the temple precincts to a distant location. This act shows her profound wisdom and modesty. She knew that people would not understand her situation; they would accuse her of the very unchastity she had spent her life guarding against. Rather than exposing herself to immediate scandal, she sought solitude, placing her trust in Allah to ultimately vindicate her. The “remote place” (makanan qasiyyan) indicates a far-off, secluded spot, far from the eyes of the community. Some historians mention she went to Bethlehem, a distance of about six miles from Jerusalem. This withdrawal was not an escape from responsibility but a divinely guided retreat to protect the sanctity of the miracle and to allow her to prepare for the immense task of raising this extraordinary child. It mirrors the earlier seclusion of Mary for worship, but now intensified by the need to protect the divine gift she carried.

=23=

In her isolated state, the time of delivery arrived. The “pains of labour” (al-makhad) are described as driving her to the trunk of a palm tree. Alone and in physical distress, she leaned against this tree for support. The imagery is deeply human and poignant, showing that even the purest of women, chosen for a unique miracle, was not exempt from the ordinary agonies of childbirth. The pain prompted a powerful emotional outburst: “Alas! I wish I had died before this, and was a thing long forgotten!” This cry does not come from a lack of faith or dissatisfaction with Allah’s decree; rather, it reflects her acute awareness of the impending accusation of unchastity. She was a woman who had guarded her honour all her life, and now she faced the prospect of returning to her people with a newborn baby, expecting to be met with slander and disgrace. Her wish for death or to be utterly forgotten reveals the depth of her anxiety over her reputation and the potential scandal, not a desire to escape Allah’s plan. Scholars like Ibn Kathir explain that it is permissible for a person overwhelmed by fear of trial to wish for death, as the Prophet himself once said, “Do not wish for death due to a calamity that befalls you, but if one must wish, let him say: O Allah, keep me alive as long as life is better for me, and cause me to die if death is better for me.” Mary’s exclamation, however, was a spontaneous, momentary expression of pain and fear, not a settled desire. It also magnifies the subsequent divine comfort, showing that relief from Allah comes precisely when the servant’s distress reaches its peak.

=24=

In that moment of extreme despair, divine comfort arrived instantly. “So a voice reassured her from below her.” There is a difference of opinion among commentators about the source of this voice. Some, like Ibn Abbas and Sa‘id ibn Jubayr, held that it was the voice of Jesus himself, who had just been born and now spoke from beneath her. Others, such as al-Hasan al-Basri, said it was the angel Gabriel calling from the lower slope of the hill. The pronoun “her” in the Arabic reading supports the interpretation that the caller was below Mary’s level, which would be appropriate if the newborn Jesus, placed on the ground, was speaking.

However, the more commonly accepted view is that it was Gabriel, given his role as the angel of revelation and comfort. Regardless, the message was one of immediate solace: “Do not grieve! ” The first thing needed was emotional calm, as her heart was breaking with distress. Then came the tangible provision: “Your Lord has provided a stream at your feet.” In the dry terrain of that remote place, a water source miraculously appeared right beside her, offering her water to drink, wash, and regain strength after labour.

The phrase “has provided” (ja‘ala) signifies that Allah created this stream for her in that very moment. It shows that when Allah places a servant in a difficult situation, He also places within it the means of relief, often in ways that were previously unseen. The instruction to not grieve, combined with the immediate material gift, demonstrates the inseparable link between sincere reliance on Allah and His swift support for His righteous servants.

=25=

After the stream, a second source of nourishment was provided. The voice commanded her, “And shake the trunk of this palm tree towards you.” The palm tree, which was dead or dormant as it was likely not the season for dates, was to be shaken. The command required Mary to take an action, however small, even though she was physically exhausted. This teaches a profound principle: divine help does not negate the need for human effort. Allah could have made the dates fall without her moving, but He instructed her to shake the tree to link cause and effect, to give her an active role, and to test her obedience. As soon as she obeyed, the miracle occurred: “it will drop fresh, ripe dates upon you.” The tree, which had no fruit, suddenly became laden with fresh, ripe dates that fell at her feet. Some scholars note that this was a date palm that had been dead, and by the command of Allah it came to life and bore fruit, which served as a sign for Mary and a precursor to the many signs of her son. The provision of dates is particularly fitting, as fresh dates are nutritionally perfect for a woman who has just given birth, providing quick energy and essential nutrients. The detail that they were “ripe” (janiyyan) indicates they were perfectly ready to eat, requiring no further processing. This miraculous feeding echoes the provision of food for Mary in the sanctuary as mentioned in Surah Al-Imran, showing that Allah consistently sustains His devoted servants from sources they could never anticipate. The entire scene transforms a place of despair into a garden of divine hospitality.

=26=

With the stream and the dates provided, the voice issued a series of calming commands. “So eat and drink” gave her permission to nourish her body after the exhausting delivery. “And put your heart at ease” (qarri ‘aynan) is a beautiful Arabic expression meaning to find comfort, joy, and tranquillity; literally, to cool the eyes with happiness. The immediate needs of the body were met so that her soul could be at peace.

However, a greater test still loomed: facing her people. The voice prepared her for this by giving a specific strategy: “But if you see any of the people, say, ‘I have vowed silence to the Most Compassionate, so I am not talking to anyone today.’” Mary was to undertake a vow of silence. Among the Children of Israel, fasting from speech was a recognized form of worship, and Mary was commanded to use this as a shield. She would not defend herself verbally; instead, her silent state would direct all attention to the infant who would speak in her defence. This divine tactic was twofold: it protected Mary from the immediate onslaught of accusations and set the stage for the miracle of Jesus speaking in the cradle, which would be a far more powerful testimony to her innocence than any words she could utter. The vow was made to “the Most Compassionate,” reinforcing that her silence was an act of worship, not just a strategy.

The phrase “so I am not talking to anyone today” was to be her only spoken declaration. This entire plan demonstrates that sometimes the best response to slander is not argumentation but a dignified silence coupled with trust that Allah will manifest the truth in His own way.

=27=

After receiving these instructions, Mary left the remote place and went back to her community with the newborn baby in her arms. The phrase “carrying him” (tahmiluhu) paints a vivid image of her walking into the public eye, holding the infant Jesus. Her obedience was complete; she did not hide or run away permanently. She faced the inevitable confrontation with the calmness of one who trusts Allah’s promise. As soon as her people saw her, they erupted in shock and condemnation. The verse captures their immediate reaction: “They said, ‘O Mary! You have certainly done a horrible thing!’” The expression “horrible thing” (fariyyan) denotes an unprecedented, heinous act—something deeply shameful and shocking. To them, the daughter of the pious Imran family, a woman dedicated to the temple since childhood, had apparently committed fornication and brought home a child. Their shock was not simply due to her having a baby out of wedlock; it was the stark contradiction between her lifelong reputation for purity and the visible evidence of a child. Their words, though harsh, highlight the severity of the trial Mary underwent. She was accused by those who should have known her best. The Quran records this to illustrate the depth of her patience and to set up the dramatic vindication that follows.

For believers today, this scene is a reminder that the righteous may sometimes be subjected to the worst of accusations, but their vindication ultimately comes from Allah, not from human judgement. It also shows that Mary’s silence was the perfect answer, as any attempt to explain would have been drowned out by the outcry.

=28=

Their rebuke grew more specific and cutting. They addressed her as “O sister of Aaron!” This phrase has been explained in several ways. The most authentic interpretation, supported by a hadith in Sahih Muslim, is that the people of that time used to name their children after the prophets and righteous. Mary had a brother named Aaron, who was a pious man, and they were referring to her by her relation to him. It was not the prophet Aaron, the brother of Moses, as some Christian polemicists mistakenly argue. The Prophet Muhammad clarified this when confronted with the question, saying that the Israelites used to name after their prophets. By invoking her brother’s name, they were saying, “You who come from such a righteous brother, how could you do this? ” Then they sharpened the contrast: “Your father was not an indecent man, nor was your mother unchaste.” They acknowledged the impeccable character of her parents, Imran and Hannah. Her father was known to be righteous and not a fornicator, and her mother was famously pure and dedicated. By stating this, the accusers were implying that Mary had betrayed her entire noble lineage. The intense psychological pressure of this moment cannot be overstated.

Yet, Mary remained silent. This verse exposes the baselessness of the later Jewish slander against Mary and Jesus. The Quran here acknowledges that her parents were upright, which indirectly refutes the extreme accusations leveled against her by some factions. The scene illustrates how sometimes the very fact of being from a pious family can make a trial more severe, because the expectations are higher and the fall, in the eyes of people, seems greater. Mary’s steadfastness in the face of this emotional assault is a lesson in enduring unjust accusations.

=29=

In perfect obedience to the divine strategy given to her, Mary did not utter a word in her defence. Instead, “she pointed to the baby.” This simple gesture was her answer, shifting their attention from her to the child. It was an act of profound faith; she was relying entirely on the promise that her son would speak. The people’s reaction was a mixture of exasperation and mockery: “How can we talk to someone who is an infant in the cradle?

” The word “infant” (sabiyy) refers to a very young baby, and “cradle” (mahd) indicates the small bed of a newborn. Their question was natural from a human perspective. It is impossible for a newborn to speak. They interpreted Mary’s gesture either as a desperate plea or as an indication that she expected the child himself to answer, which they found absurd. This moment captures the peak of the tension. From a worldly standpoint, Mary had placed all her eggs in an impossible basket: the speech of an infant. But precisely at this moment of maximum impossibility, the divine power manifested. The people’s rhetorical question set the stage for the earth-shattering miracle that was about to occur. The lesson here is profound: when a believer has done all that is humanly possible and follows divine guidance, the answer from Allah may come from the most unexpected source. The very thing that people mock as impossible becomes the instrument of truth. It also illustrates that Allah’s miracles often occur when all natural means have been exhausted, so that the divine hand is unmistakably clear.

=30=

In response to the people’s astonished question, the infant Jesus spoke clearly, miraculously breaking the laws of nature. The first words he uttered were not to demand attention or to prove his miraculous birth, but to establish the absolute foundation of faith: “I am truly a servant of Allah.” This was the immediate refutation of any future claim to his divinity. By calling himself a servant first, Jesus began his life and his message with the most honoured title a human can have—‘Abd Allah.

The word “truly” (innī) emphasises certainty and sincerity. Then he outlined his divinely ordained purpose: “He has destined me to be given the Scripture and to be a prophet.

” The mention of the Scripture, the Injil (Gospel), shows that his mission would involve receiving a revealed book from Allah. He would not be a mere preacher but a prophet with a divine law and wisdom.

This statement, spoken miraculously from the cradle, established his prophethood from his very first day on earth, making it undeniable for those present. It also served as the immediate vindication of Mary; the baby himself declared that his existence was a divine decree, not the result of sin. This moment also powerfully demonstrates the Islamic view of Jesus: a noble prophet and servant of Allah, not a partner in divinity.

The sequence is crucial—he declared servitude before prophethood. By doing so, he removed any possible excuse for his followers to later elevate him beyond his human status. His speech in the cradle is a sign of Allah’s power and a mercy to his mother, clearing her name in the most dramatic fashion.

=31=

Continuing his miraculous proclamation, Jesus described the qualities Allah had decreed for him. “He has made me a blessing wherever I go.

” The word “blessing” (mubarakan) indicates that his very presence would be a source of immense goodness, guidance, healing, and spiritual benefit for those around him. Wherever he travelled—across the hills of Galilee, through the streets of Jerusalem, or into the wilderness—he would bring divine mercy, teach the truth, heal the sick, comfort the broken-hearted, and guide the lost. His whole life would be a conduit of blessings, not just for the Children of Israel, but for all who followed him. Then he added, “and bid me to establish prayer and give alms-tax as long as I live.

” This detail is profoundly significant. Even though Jesus was a prophet born of a miracle, and even though he was a blessing to the world, his personal obligations to worship were exactly the same as those of any other servant. He was commanded to perform prayer, which is the direct connection between a servant and his Lord, and to give zakah, which is the fulfilment of duties towards the poor and needy. The combination of prayer and alms-tax encapsulates the two dimensions of piety: the rights of Allah and the rights of creation.

The phrase “as long as I live” indicates that these obligations are lifelong, never ceasing even for a prophet. This demolishes any notion that Jesus came to abolish the law or that he was above the need to worship. Instead, he was a model of worship. His declaration from the cradle teaches that the essence of religion is servitude through prayer and charity, and that true blessedness is expressed through these acts of devotion and compassion.

=32=

After mentioning prayer and alms, Jesus highlighted a specific moral duty: “and to be kind to my mother.” This is a direct response to the slander against Mary. By declaring his mission to be kind to his mother, the infant Jesus instantly restored her honour. He would be her comfort, her caretaker, and her devoted son, treating her with the utmost respect and love. This also refutes the later Christian doctrine that would venerate Mary as the “Mother of God,” a concept that elevates her to a status bordering on divinity. Instead, Jesus simply affirms that he will be a dutiful and kind son.

The Arabic “barr” is the same word used for John’s kindness to his parents, connecting the two prophets. The duty to one’s mother is placed immediately after the duties to Allah, underscoring its paramount importance. Then Jesus added a negation: “He has not made me arrogant or defiant.

” This statement, coming from an infant who just performed an impossible miracle, is immensely powerful. The natural human tendency after such an event would be pride, but Jesus was divinely protected from arrogance. “Arrogant” (jabbaran) describes someone who is tyrannical, overbearing, and sees himself as above others, while “defiant” (shaqiyyan) refers to one who is disobedient to Allah and wretched in conduct. By explicitly stating that he is neither, Jesus removes any grounds for his later followers to claim that because he performed miracles, he was divine or above the law. He is a humble servant, kind to his mother, and submissive to his Lord. This verse provides a complete portrait of ideal human character: devotion to Allah, service to humanity, and gentleness to parents, all crowned with humility.

=33=

Jesus concluded his cradle speech with a salutation that mirrors precisely the blessing bestowed upon John the Baptist earlier in the surah: “Peace be upon me the day I was born, the day I die, and the day I will be raised back to life!” The symmetry between the two statements is deliberate, linking the two prophets and highlighting their shared humanity and servitude. The peace upon Jesus on the day of his birth is an affirmation of his purity and innocence from the false accusations hurled at his mother. It also refutes the idea that his birth was anything other than a blessed, peaceful event under Allah’s care.

The second part, “the day I die,” is a clear proof from the Quran that Jesus will indeed experience death. This verse is often cited in the Islamic refutation of the Christian belief that Jesus was crucified and resurrected in a divine sense, or that he did not die at all. The Islamic position, based on the Quran, is that Jesus was not killed or crucified, but Allah raised him to Himself, and he will return to earth, live, and eventually die a natural death before the Day of Judgment. So the peace upon the day of his death will occur at that future time.

Finally, “the day I will be raised back to life” affirms the general resurrection of all humans, including the prophets. Jesus being raised like any other servant underscores that he is not an immortal deity. The threefold peace encapsulates the entire human journey and places Jesus firmly within the ranks of honoured, mortal servants. It is a profound rebuttal of any claim to divinity and a beautiful conclusion to his miraculous infant testimony.

=34=

After narrating the extraordinary story of the virgin birth and the cradle speech, the Quran now draws a firm conclusion. “That is Jesus, son of Mary.” The demonstrative “that” (dhalika) points to the complete account just given as the definitive truth about Jesus.

The emphasis on “son of Mary” is crucial. In the entire Quran, Jesus is frequently called “Jesus son of Mary,” attributing him to his mother alone. This naming convention serves as a constant reminder of his miraculous, fatherless birth and his complete humanity; he is not the “son of God,” but the son of a chaste human woman.

The verse continues, “And this is a word of truth, about which they dispute.” The “word of truth” refers to the Quranic narrative itself, or according to some scholars, to Jesus himself, as he is called a “word” from Allah in other verses—meaning he was created by the divine command “Be. ” The statement here leaves no room for ambiguity: what the Quran conveys about Jesus is the absolute truth, the haqq.

Yet, despite this clarity, “they dispute.” The disputants are the various groups that emerged after Jesus’ time, including the Christians who split into factions over his nature, some claiming he was God, some that he was the son of God, and some that he was one of three. The Jews also disputed his prophethood entirely. The verse sets the stage for the upcoming direct refutation of the claim that Allah has taken a son. It serves as a stark warning that the truth is often the subject of contention, and that believers must hold firmly to the divine revelation while others argue based on conjecture and inherited falsehood.

=35=

Having mentioned the dispute, the Quran now categorically refutes the most egregious falsehood. “It is not for Allah to take a son!

” This is not just a prohibition; it is a statement that such an act is impossible and contrary to Allah’s very nature and majesty. Taking a son would imply a need, a deficiency, a partner in dominion, or a limitation, all of which are inconceivable for the Almighty. The declaration “Glory be to Him” (Subhanahu) is an exclamation of transcendence, declaring Allah far above any such imperfection. It is a form of worship, negating any similarity between the Creator and His creation. Then, the verse provides the logical refutation of the need for a son: “When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, ‘Be!’ And it is! ” The creation of Jesus is the ultimate proof. Allah willed Jesus to exist, said “Be,” and he was in Mary’s womb, without any human father. This divine creative command demonstrates that Allah does not need offspring to assist Him or continue His line. If He can create a human being with no father whatsoever, what need could He possibly have for a son? The creation of Adam without father or mother is an even greater miracle. This verse dismantles the entire theological basis of the divinity or divine sonship of Jesus. It redirects the mind to the sheer omnipotence of the Creator: He is not subject to biological processes; His will is the sole cause of everything. This short, powerful statement is a central pillar of Islamic monotheism, teaching that all worship and praise belong to Allah alone, who is unique in His essence, attributes, and actions.

=36=

The Quran reinforces the true message of Jesus by quoting his own words, linking back to the cradle testimony. Jesus declared, “Surely Allah is my Lord and your Lord.

” The word “surely” (inna) adds emphasis, leaving no doubt. Jesus first establishes that Allah is his personal Lord, which immediately negates any claim to personal divinity. If Allah is his Lord, then he is a servant who submits to his Master. Then he extends this Lordship to all people: “and your Lord.” This unites Jesus with his audience in a shared servitude to the One true God. On this foundation, he issues the command: “so worship Him alone. ” This is the very essence of his message, as it is the message of every prophet. Worship must be directed exclusively to Allah, with no partners, no intermediaries, and no deification of creation. Then Jesus concludes with, “This is the Straight Path.” The Straight Path (al-Sirat al-Mustaqim) is the path of pure monotheism, the path that leads directly to Allah’s pleasure and eternal salvation. By using these exact words, which are part of the opening surah of the Quran recited daily by Muslims, the verse creates a powerful connection between the message of all prophets. Jesus guided to the same path that the Prophet Muhammad guided to. This verse is a clear response to the Christians who claim to follow Jesus yet worship him. It is as if Jesus is testifying directly to them, saying: I never told you to worship me; I told you to worship Allah, my Lord and yours. The path of monotheism is the only straight way, and any deviation into trinitarian concepts or saint worship is a departure from what Jesus himself preached.

=37=

Despite the clarity of Jesus’ own words and the Quranic truth, human beings split into factions. “Yet their various groups have differed among themselves about him.” The pronoun “their” refers to the people who received Jesus’ message, mainly the Christians but also the Jews who rejected him. After his ascension, Christian communities fragmented into numerous denominations debating the nature of Jesus. Some said he was God incarnate, others that he was the son of God, and yet others, like the Arians, held that he was a created being. The Jews, on the other hand, denied his prophethood and made false accusations against his mother. This bitter division over the truth of Jesus is a historical fact that continues to this day.

The verse then delivers a severe warning: “so woe to the disbelievers when they face a tremendous Day! ” The Arabic word “wayl” denotes destruction, severe punishment, and intense grief. It is reserved for those who reject the truth after it has come to them. The “tremendous Day” is the Day of Judgement, whose terrors are unimaginable. Those who believed false doctrines about Jesus, elevating him to divinity or slandering him, will face an awful punishment.

The mention of this woe serves to place the whole preceding narrative into an eschatological frame. The disputes over Jesus are not mere intellectual debates; they carry consequences that will manifest on that great Day when all falsehood will be exposed and the truth will stand clear. This verse emphasises that religious division and disbelief in the clear revelation carry a heavy price.

=38=

This verse contrasts the spiritual blindness of the disbelievers in this world with the complete clarity they will have on the Day of Judgement. “How clearly will they hear and see on the Day they will come to Us!” On that Day, the veils will be lifted, and their faculties will be at their sharpest. They will hear the truth with undeniable clarity, and they will see the reality of the resurrection, the angels, the scales, and the punishment they once denied. The rhetorical question “How clearly” (asma‘ bihim wa absir) is an Arabic eloquence expressing astonishment at the perfection of their hearing and sight on that Day.

However, this clarity will come too late; it will only deepen their regret. The verse then contrasts this with their current state: “But today the wrongdoers are clearly astray.” In this worldly life, they are lost in a manifest error. They cannot see the truth even when it is recited to them, and they cannot hear guidance with acceptance. Their idols, their false doctrines about Jesus, and their arrogance have made them deaf and blind to the Quran. The juxtaposition is a powerful warning. The very people who are so confident in their disbelief now will on that Day have perfect perception, but it will benefit them nothing. This echoes other Quranic verses, such as the one that says, “So remove your cover; your sight today is sharp” (50:22). The lesson is urgent: one should not wait for the forced clarity of the Hereafter when the time for action has passed. True wisdom is to seek and accept the truth now, while faith can still lead to salvation.

=39=

Turning directly to the Prophet Muhammad, the command comes: “And warn them, O Prophet, of the Day of Regret.” The Day of Judgement is named “the Day of Regret” (Yawm al-Hasrah) because it will be filled with intense remorse. The disbelievers will see the rewards they missed and the punishments they earned, and they will regret every moment spent in heedlessness. The righteous, though saved, may also feel regret for not having done more good. The warning is to be delivered to the people who are presently heedless.

The phrase “when all matters will be settled” indicates that judgement will be passed, and the fate of every soul will be sealed. There will be no opportunity for return or amendment.

The final clause, “while they are engrossed in heedlessness and disbelief,” describes their current worldly state. They are so absorbed in their daily lives, their pleasures, and their denials that they pay no attention to the impending reality. This creates a tense contrast: the Prophet is to warn them about the settled, decisive Day while they are in a state of complete neglect. The verse links directly to the previous one: they are clearly astray because of their heedlessness, and this heedlessness will be the source of their ultimate regret. The warning is an act of mercy, a chance to awaken from the slumber before it is too late. It also underscores the responsibility of the believer to live with the awareness of that Day and to remind others, so that regret may be avoided. The Day of Regret is a recurring Quranic theme that calls for immediate reflection and repentance.

=40=

This verse provides the reasoning behind the warning and the Day of Regret. “Indeed, it is We Who will succeed the earth and whoever is on it.” Allah declares that He will inherit the earth and all its inhabitants. Everything that people boast about—their properties, status, wealth, children—will revert to Allah.

The term “succeed” (narithu) means that after all creation perishes, Allah will remain as the sole Owner of everything, just as He was before anything existed. This is an echo of the verse, “All that is on earth will perish, but the Face of your Lord will remain” (55:26-27). The notion of inheritance here is not that Allah was not the owner before, but that all temporary human claims to ownership will vanish, leaving absolute dominion to the Almighty. Then, “And to Us they will all be returned. ” The physical death is not the end; all will be resurrected and brought back to Allah for judgement.

This statement reinforces the previous warnings about the tremendous Day. There is no escape, no alternative destination. The combination of succession and return underlines the futility of the disbelievers’ arrogance and their debates over Jesus. The very earth they walk on and the bodies they inhabit belong to Allah and will return to Him.

For the believer, this is a profound comfort: the injustices of the world are temporary, and the ultimate victory and ownership belong to Allah. It also serves as a powerful deterrent against worldly attachment, reminding humanity that everything they hold dear will pass away, and they will stand alone before their Creator.
