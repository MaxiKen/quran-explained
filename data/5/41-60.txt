=41=

“O Messenger, do not be grieved by those who race into disbelief—those who say, ‘We believe,’ with their mouths while their hearts have not believed, and some among the Jews who eagerly listen to lies and to other people who have not come to you. They distort words after they have been placed properly, saying, ‘If you are given this ruling, accept it, but if you are not given it, beware.’ Whoever Allah allows to fall into trial, you can do nothing for them against Allah. Those are people whose hearts Allah does not will to purify. For them is disgrace in this world and tremendous punishment in the Hereafter.”

The Prophet, peace be upon him, grieved when people ran toward rejection. His concern was not wounded ego; he wanted their rescue. Allah tells him not to let their chosen haste consume him. A messenger conveys and cares but does not control hearts.

The first group professes belief only with mouths. The second description concerns particular Jewish figures in Madinah who listened eagerly to false reports and carried messages for leaders unwilling to face the Prophet directly. The verse says “among,” not all Jews, and judges conduct rather than ancestry.

Reports connect the passage to people seeking a convenient ruling in a case of adultery, hoping Muhammad would replace the Torah’s judgment with something lighter. “If you are given this, take it” reveals verdict-shopping: accept the judge only if his answer matches the decision already chosen.

Distortion occurs “after” words have been properly placed. It may involve altering text, removing context, hiding evidence, or recasting a clear ruling through selective interpretation. The problem is not legitimate scholarly disagreement but manipulation designed to predetermine judgment.

Allah’s allowing fitnah corresponds to persistent choice. They race into disbelief, love lies, and condition obedience on convenience until purification is no longer what they seek. The Prophet cannot force purity upon people determined to protect corruption.

This verse must not become a licence for antisemitism or reckless accusations of hypocrisy. The Quran praises upright People of the Book elsewhere, and hidden faith belongs to Allah. Its traits should first test the reader: do I seek truth from a scholar or shop for permission?

A modern version appears when clients hide facts from advisers, pressure experts for a desired answer, or quote a ruling stripped of its conditions. A fatwa obtained through deception does not make an act lawful before Allah.

Verse 42 continues the description: avid listeners to lies and consumers of unlawful gain. If they bring a case, the Prophet may judge or decline; if he judges, justice is mandatory.

=42=

“They are avid listeners to lies and devourers of unlawful gain. If they come to you, either judge between them or decline. If you decline, they cannot harm you in the least. But if you judge, then judge between them with justice. Surely Allah loves those who act justly.”

Listening can be sinful when a person seeks falsehood, gossip, or propaganda because it serves desire. These people did not merely encounter a lie; they were samma‘un—eager, repeated listeners who helped lies travel and become useful.

Suht is unlawful gain that destroys blessing, often understood here to include bribes. A judge or religious leader who consumes bribes becomes prepared to hear false evidence and distort law. Corrupt information and corrupt money reinforce one another.

The Prophet was given a choice in this stage of dealing with certain non-Muslim litigants: hear their dispute or turn away. Their internal affairs and manipulative approach did not always require his jurisdiction. Later jurists discuss how this choice relates to treaties, citizenship, and the development of Islamic public authority.

If he accepted the case, only one outcome was permitted procedurally: judgment bil-qist, with justice. Their dishonesty could not justify a dishonest ruling against them. Judicial integrity is not a favour reserved for cooperative litigants.

“Allah loves the just” gives impartial judgment a reward beyond professional reputation. A judge may be disliked by both parties and loved by Allah. That love is worth more than the bribe, factional praise, or fear that might bend the decision.

Declining a case is not cowardice where law grants discretion. Judges should recuse themselves from conflicts of interest or matters beyond competence. But once they undertake judgment, they cannot abandon fairness because a party has bad character.

The verse applies to arbitrators, parents, managers, teachers, online moderators, and anyone distributing rights. Refusing gossip, unlawful gifts, and private lobbying protects the eventual decision.

Verse 43 exposes the litigants’ inconsistency. Why seek Muhammad’s ruling while possessing the Torah’s divine judgment, then turn away even after asking him? Their aim was not revelation from either source but escape from an unwelcome law.

=43=

“But why do they come to you for judgment when they have the Torah containing Allah’s judgment, and then turn away after that? Such people are not truly believers.”

The question uncovers selective submission. They possessed a ruling in the Torah relevant to their case but hoped another court would provide a preferred result. When the Prophet confirmed the truth rather than their preference, they turned away again.

This verse affirms that the original Torah contained hukm Allah—real divine judgment. The Quran does not teach that every line of earlier scripture was always false. It confirms original revelation while exposing concealment, alteration, and selective practice.

Their appeal to Muhammad was not itself wrong; as Messenger and leader he could judge. The contradiction lay in using him against their own scripture and rejecting him when he agreed with it. Every authority was acceptable only while serving desire.

“They are not believers” addresses the described pattern of calculated rejection, not every person who struggles to accept a judgment or uses an appeal process. Lawful appeal seeks correction of error; verdict-shopping seeks escape from known truth.

Muslims can repeat the conduct by moving between scholars, schools, or civil courts solely to obtain advantage. Using a lawful court to secure an actual right is different from changing forums to defeat another’s right. Intention, facts, and valid jurisdiction matter.

The verse also warns teachers not to weaponise earlier scripture selectively in polemics. If the Torah’s divine origin is affirmed, it must be discussed with accuracy and respect alongside the Quran’s role as final criterion.

Verse 44 now describes that Torah as guidance and light. Prophets who submitted, rabbis, and scholars judged by it because they were entrusted with Allah’s Book and witnessed its truth. The verse then warns against selling revelation for a small price and calls deliberate rejection of Allah’s judgment disbelief.

=44=

“Indeed, We revealed the Torah containing guidance and light. The prophets who submitted to Allah judged the Jews by it, as did the devoted rabbis and scholars, because they were entrusted with preserving Allah’s Book and were witnesses to it. So do not fear people; fear Me. Do not trade My revelations for a small price. Whoever does not judge by what Allah has revealed—such people are the disbelievers.”

The Torah was guidance and light in its revealed form. Islam does not portray Musa as bringing an inferior false religion. He and later prophets submitted to Allah and judged their communities through revelation appropriate to them.

Rabbaniyyun and ahbar—devoted teachers and learned scholars—received an istihfaz, a trust to preserve and apply the Book. Scholarship is therefore custody, not ownership. A scholar must transmit what revelation says even when patrons, rulers, or audiences prefer another answer.

“They were witnesses” means their knowledge made them responsible to testify. Concealment after expertise is graver than ignorance. Documents, memorisation, teaching chains, and honest judgment all serve preservation, while lived obedience preserves meaning in community.

Fear of people and sale for a small price identify two engines of corruption. A judge may distort through threat or payment, a teacher through career anxiety, and a politician through votes. Every worldly return is “small” beside the truth exchanged.

The closing warning about not judging by revelation is severe. Scholars distinguish the person who denies Allah’s ruling, regards another contrary rule as superior, or rejects divine authority—which can be major disbelief—from one who accepts the ruling but sins through bribery, desire, or weakness, which is grave wrongdoing and may be called lesser disbelief in early explanation. Facts, belief, intent, and impediments matter.

This verse is not a warrant for individuals to declare rulers or societies unbelievers, rebel violently, or administer punishments. Takfir has strict conditions and consequences, and political action remains governed by scholarship, capacity, law, and prevention of greater harm. Extremists who use the verse to spill protected blood violate revelation while claiming to defend it.

Every believer exercises some judgment in family, money, and speech. The first application is personal: do not deny or knowingly replace Allah’s standard when it restrains you.

Verse 45 gives one Torah ruling: proportionate retribution for life and bodily injury, while praising voluntary pardon as expiation.

=45=

“We ordained for them in the Torah: a life for a life, an eye for an eye, a nose for a nose, an ear for an ear, a tooth for a tooth, and equal retribution for wounds. But whoever waives it as charity, it will be an expiation for them. Whoever does not judge by what Allah has revealed—such people are the wrongdoers.”

Qisas limits retaliation to equivalence. In societies of tribal revenge, one death could provoke many deaths and an injury against a powerful person could be repaid beyond measure. Revelation says the offender’s actual liability sets the boundary.

The listed body parts communicate precision, while “wounds” requires the possibility of safe, measurable equality. Where equivalence cannot be assured without excess, compensation or another lawful remedy applies. Medical expertise is essential to prevent a second injustice.

The verse reports what was written for the Children of Israel, and Islamic law also recognises qisas with its own Quranic and prophetic details (2:178-179). No victim or family may personally carry it out. A competent court must establish intention, causation, identity, and applicable rights.

Pardon is called sadaqah because the victim gives up a claim genuinely possessed. It can become expiation—understood as cleansing for the pardoner, and by some explanations a means concerning the offender. The promise honours forgiveness without making it compulsory.

Pressure is not charity. Families, officials, or offenders may not shame a victim into waiving qisas or compensation for reputation. A valid pardon must be informed and willing. Public safety can also require consequences not wholly erased by private forgiveness.

“Wrongdoers” in the closing fits judges who knowingly deny due proportion, whether by protecting the powerful or punishing beyond law. Together verses 44, 45, and 47 use disbelief, wrongdoing, and defiant disobedience to expose different dimensions of abandoning revelation.

The verse’s deepest practical lesson is controlled justice: human anger has a right to redress but not to excess. Verse 46 now moves to Isa, who confirmed the Torah and received the Gospel as guidance, light, and instruction for the mindful.

=46=

“Then in the footsteps of those prophets We sent Isa son of Maryam, confirming the Torah revealed before him. We gave him the Gospel containing guidance and light, confirming what was before it in the Torah, and as guidance and instruction for those mindful of Allah.”

Isa stands in continuity, not rivalry, with Musa. He confirms the Torah’s divine origin and restores obedience to its truth while receiving revelation suited to his own mission. The same Lord sent both.

Calling him “son of Maryam” preserves miraculous birth and human identity. He is an honoured messenger sent in the prophets’ footsteps, not the God who sent the footsteps or the revelation.

The Injil given to Isa was guidance and light. Muslims must honour that original Gospel. It is not necessarily identical to the four later canonical biographies in form, though those writings may preserve elements of his history and teaching. The Quran serves as criterion over claims about what Allah revealed.

Confirmation does not mean every legal detail remained unchanged. Prophets can confirm the source and central creed while Allah modifies certain rulings. Isa says in 3:50 that he made lawful some things previously forbidden to his people.

Hudan wa maw‘izatan lil-muttaqin joins information to heart-softening counsel. Taqwa makes revelation beneficial; knowledge without willingness can become another argument for pride.

Muslims should speak about the Gospel without ridicule. Theological disagreement with Christianity does not cancel reverence for what Allah truly sent to Isa. Nor should shared language be used to pretend the Quran approves later doctrines it explicitly corrects.

The continuity places responsibility upon Christians: honouring Isa includes receiving his call to one God and recognising the final Messenger confirmed by the prophetic chain. Verse 47 tells the People of the Gospel to judge by what Allah revealed within it and calls departure from divine revelation fisq.

=47=

“Let the people of the Gospel judge by what Allah revealed in it. Whoever does not judge by what Allah has revealed—such people are the defiantly disobedient.”

The command held the Christian community accountable to genuine revelation given to Isa. They were not permitted to replace Allah’s teaching with ecclesiastical desire, political convenience, or doctrines without divine authority.

For people living after Muhammad’s mission becomes established to them, judging the Gospel faithfully includes responding to the one God, prophetic servanthood, and the final revelation that confirms and guards earlier truth. The verse is not a command to reject the Quran and freeze history before the final Messenger.

Fasiqun are those who step outside obedience. The closing term adds another moral lens to verses 44-45: rejection can express disbelief in authority, injustice in result, and rebellion in conduct. The labels should not be mechanically assigned without examining the person and act.

The verse affirms meaningful responsibility among Christians while maintaining Islamic theology. Interfaith respect does not require saying revelation contains no objective truth or that contradictory claims are equally valid.

Muslims should apply the warning to themselves. Quran can be recited while family custom, nationalism, or economic interest actually governs. The failure of earlier communities is preserved as a mirror, not a platform for superiority.

Judging by revelation also requires competence. A literal fragment detached from context can produce the opposite of Allah’s law. Language, prophetic explanation, objectives, and the full evidence guide application.

Verse 48 now identifies the Quran’s final role. It confirms earlier scripture and stands as muhaymin—a guardian and authoritative criterion over it. Muhammad must judge by what Allah revealed, while communities are told that differing laws are a test calling them to compete in good before their final return.

=48=

“We have revealed to you the Book in truth, confirming the Scriptures before it and as a guardian and criterion over them. So judge between them by what Allah has revealed, and do not follow their desires away from the truth that has come to you. For each community We appointed a law and a clear way. Had Allah willed, He could have made you one community, but He intended to test you through what He gave you. So compete with one another in good deeds. To Allah you will all return, and He will then inform you concerning what you differed about.”

The Quran is musaddiq, confirming original revelation, and muhaymin, witnessing, preserving, and ruling over competing claims about it. It says yes to the tawhid and prophethood Allah sent and no to concealment, alteration, and later deification.

Muhammad, peace be upon him, judges by the Quran rather than the preferences of litigants. Compassion cannot mean leaving truth, and firmness cannot mean judging without justice. Desire may approach from every party, including one’s own followers.

Shir‘ah and minhaj indicate a revealed law and path. Prophets share one essential religion of submission while legal details can differ by community and period. Sabbath rules, dietary restrictions, and forms of worship illustrate variation under one divine authority.

“If Allah willed, He could have made you one community” affirms that diversity exists within His test, but it does not say contradictory beliefs are all true. People are responsible for how they respond to what reaches them. Coercively eliminating every difference is not the mission.

The command is fastabiqu al-khayrat—race toward good. Debate has a place, but communities reveal claims through worship, justice, care for the poor, honesty, and mercy. Competition in good is not a substitute for truth; it is truth made visible.

Final return belongs to Allah, who resolves differences with complete knowledge. This should produce conviction without pretending human beings possess the final court. Muslims convey, judge where authorised, and remain just.

The verse supports principled plural coexistence: each community can live and be addressed within law while Muslims maintain the Quran’s criterion. It does not endorse persecution or theological relativism.

Verse 49 repeats the command to judge by revelation and warns the Prophet not to let litigants tempt him away from even part of what Allah sent. Turning away may itself be consequence of sins.

=49=

“Judge between them by what Allah has revealed, do not follow their desires, and beware that they do not tempt you away from any part of what Allah has revealed to you. If they turn away, know that Allah intends to afflict them for some of their sins. Indeed, many people are defiantly disobedient.”

Repetition reflects pressure. The litigants did not merely request judgment; they tried to condition participation on a preferred result. The Prophet must guard “some” revelation as carefully as the whole. Selective abandonment can become the first breach in a covenant.

Following desire includes changing a verdict to win approval, prevent a faction’s departure, or secure political advantage. The Messenger’s compassion never authorised him to purchase acceptance by altering law.

Fitnah here can mean tempting, turning, or putting him to trial. An adviser who says “give us this ruling or we leave” is attempting to make numbers govern truth. Religious leaders need consultation and sensitivity, but not surrender to threats.

If they turn away after fair clarification, the Prophet is not responsible for forcing them. Their withdrawal can be a consequence Allah permits because of prior sins. Sin makes another sin easier and can deprive a person of the very guidance he treats lightly.

The verse says punishment for “some” sins, reminding that consequences need not exhaust the account in this world. Final judgment remains.

“Many people are fasiqun” is a realistic warning, not permission for despair about everyone. Popularity cannot establish law, and minority status cannot prove it either. Revelation is criterion.

Ordinary judges are not prophets and can misunderstand what Allah revealed. Accountability, appeal, scholarship, and evidence remain necessary. A ruler’s claim to divine law does not make his every policy sacred.

Verse 50 asks whether the alternative desired is the judgment of ignorance. For people with certainty, no judgment is better than Allah’s—not because every human application is flawless, but because the Lawgiver’s knowledge and justice are.

=50=

“Is it the judgment of ignorance they seek? Who is better than Allah in judgment for people of sure faith?”

Jahiliyyah is not merely a historical date before Islam. It is a condition in which desire, tribal power, superstition, and status replace revealed justice. A modern system can possess technology and still reproduce ignorance if the powerful become law unto themselves.

The question contrasts sources, not the infallibility of every person claiming to apply them. Allah’s judgment is perfect; human judges require learning and can err. Criticising a corrupt “Islamic” ruling may be defence of Allah’s justice rather than preference for ignorance.

People of yaqeen trust that the Creator knows bodies, families, markets, victims, and long-term consequences better than creation. Their certainty does not remove study. It motivates careful effort to understand what He actually commanded.

Human regulations are often needed in matters revelation leaves to policy, expertise, and changing circumstances. Traffic codes, building standards, procedures, and administrative rules are not automatically jahiliyyah. They are judged by whether they serve lawful aims and contradict no divine boundary.

Likewise, using a non-Muslim court to recover a right where no Islamic court exists is not necessarily choosing ignorance over Allah, as discussed around 4:60. Seeking a false result is the problem; lawful procedure can be a necessary means.

The verse should never be used as a slogan for violent rebellion or blanket excommunication. The khawarij historically quoted broad divine-judgment language while violating life and communal order. Knowledge, justice, and due process guard against repeating them.

Having established the Quran as criterion, the surah turns to alliances in a tense communal environment. Verse 51 forbids taking Jews and Christians as awliya in a manner that displaces faithful loyalty and joins hostile blocs. The surrounding permissions of food, marriage, justice, and kindness define what the term cannot mean.

=51=

“O believers, do not take Jews and Christians as protecting allies in place of the believers; they are allies of one another. Whoever among you takes them as such becomes one of them. Surely Allah does not guide wrongdoing people.”

Awliya in this setting carries protection, strategic allegiance, and intimate political loyalty, especially where communities were aligned in conflict against the vulnerable Muslim polity. It cannot mean every friendship, neighbourly bond, business partnership, or civic cooperation, because this same surah permits their food and marriage to chaste women among them.

“They are allies of one another” describes alliances relevant to the warning, not a claim that Jews and Christians have always formed one religious or political bloc. Their theologies differ and their histories include conflict. Quranic context should not be replaced by a racial conspiracy theory.

“Whoever takes them” means joining such hostile allegiance at the expense of the believers and their entrusted safety. Becoming “one of them” reflects chosen solidarity and support, potentially reaching the level of sharing their opposition. The exact legal judgment depends on belief, conduct, and circumstances; it is not automatic takfir for ordinary interaction.

The verse does not require supporting a Muslim oppressor over a non-Muslim victim. Allah commands justice even against oneself and permits goodness toward peaceful non-Muslims. Loyalty to faith is loyalty to its justice, not factional wrongdoing.

Muslims living as minorities can honour citizenship, defend neighbours, serve public institutions, and cooperate for good. Covenants must be fulfilled. Betrayal of a peaceful society cannot be excused as religious loyalty.

At the same time, approval and status can tempt people to assist actual hostility, expose community secrets, or feel shame about faith. The warning remains real. Strategic trust should be assessed through evidence, competence, covenant, and risk—not prejudice.

“Allah does not guide wrongdoers” identifies betrayal as zulm. Verse 52 now shows people with diseased hearts rushing into these alliances, saying they fear a reversal of fortune. Fear turns a prohibited dependence into apparent prudence.

=52=

“You see those with sickness in their hearts rushing toward them, saying, ‘We fear that fortune may turn against us.’ But perhaps Allah will bring victory or another outcome by His command, and then they will become remorseful over what they concealed within themselves.”

The heart’s disease is hypocrisy, doubt, and dependence upon worldly calculations. These people do not maintain ordinary peaceful relations; they rush into a compromising alliance so whichever side gains power will protect them.

Their stated fear is a da’irah—a turn of events, defeat, famine, or political reversal. Prudence about future risk is normally wise, but fear becomes disease when it leads to betrayal of principle and people. Not every insurance policy is lawful.

“Perhaps Allah will bring victory” points to an outcome their calculation omitted. Fath can be military victory, decisive judgment, or opening. “Another command” allows unexpected events by which hidden allegiances lose their value.

Regret will concern what they concealed, not merely a bad prediction. They knew the alliance involved disloyalty while presenting it as neutral risk management. When circumstances expose the heart, the worldly strategy becomes shame.

Believers should not read every cautious relationship as hypocrisy. Governments and communities need treaties, diplomacy, and contingency plans. The distinction lies in assisting hostility, displacing faithful obligations, and secretly preferring opponents against the believers.

The verse also teaches that fear should be counselled, not simply mocked. Economic dependence and security vulnerability can push people into bad choices. Building strong, just, and supportive Muslim institutions reduces the temptation to seek protection through betrayal.

No one should claim certainty about hidden disease without evidence. The Prophet received revelation about his context; later believers judge outward acts and leave inner states to Allah.

Verse 53 shows the believers’ astonishment after exposure. Were these the same people who swore their strongest oaths that they were with the believers? Their works collapse and they become losers.

=53=

“The believers will say, ‘Are these the ones who swore their strongest oaths by Allah that they were truly with you?’ Their deeds have come to nothing, and they have become losers.”

The strongest oaths created confidence that ordinary words could not. The hypocrites used Allah’s name to gain access and suppress suspicion. Exposure therefore shocks the believers: public certainty concealed private alignment.

An oath is not evidence strong enough to defeat contradictory conduct. Islam honours sworn testimony within legal rules, but repeated dramatic vows can be tools of manipulation. The Prophet taught that evidence and facts remain essential.

Their deeds collapse because the foundation was false. Public prayer, apparent service, and promises performed for strategic membership lack the faith and sincerity that carry deeds into the Hereafter. Habita evokes an animal grazing poisonous vegetation until its body is ruined—activity that ends in loss.

This does not mean every sin nullifies all good deeds. Complete nullification is tied in Quranic teaching to disbelief and apostasy at death, while lesser sins damage, erase particular rewards, or require repentance according to evidence. Believers should not weaponise the phrase against anyone they dislike.

The verse validates the pain of a betrayed community without permitting collective suspicion. One person’s false oath does not make every convert, outsider, or politically cautious member untrustworthy. Institutions should use accountable access and sound safeguarding instead of prejudice.

A person should examine promises made under Allah’s name. Is “I am with you” still true when loyalty costs something? An oath increases responsibility; it does not manufacture sincerity.

Verse 54 warns believers themselves about apostasy. If they turn away, Allah’s religion does not collapse. He will bring another people whom He loves and who love Him, humble toward believers, firm against hostile rejection, striving in His cause without fearing blame.

=54=

“O believers, whoever among you abandons their faith, Allah will bring another people whom He loves and who love Him—humble toward believers, firm toward hostile disbelievers, striving in Allah’s cause and fearing no critic’s blame. That is Allah’s favour which He grants to whom He wills. Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing.”

Apostasy does not injure Allah or leave His religion helpless. The individual loses an immense gift, while Allah can replace a failing generation with people better prepared to carry the covenant. This echoes 4:133 and 47:38.

The first quality is mutual love: Allah loves them and they love Him. Their service grows from relationship, not only fear or identity. Love is proved by following the Messenger (3:31), obeying boundaries, and preferring His pleasure.

They are adhillah toward believers—gentle, accessible, and humble rather than humiliating. Strength inside the community appears as mercy and service, not domination. Toward hostile disbelievers they are a‘izzah: dignified, firm, and not easily coerced. This does not permit cruelty toward peaceful non-Muslims; the context is antagonism and betrayal.

Their jihad is sustained effort in Allah’s cause through faith, wealth, knowledge, service, and lawful defence. Force remains subject to authority and the protections established earlier. Violence against innocents is not firmness but corruption.

They do not fear lawful criticism in the sense of abandoning truth to avoid blame. This is not refusal of advice. A believer should listen to correction, distinguish evidence from social pressure, and remain steady when obedience is mocked.

These virtues are fadl, divine favour, not grounds for ethnic pride. Any people can receive them and any group can lose them. Allah’s vastness means replacement never exhausts His resources; His knowledge identifies sincere hearts.

The verse should make communities ask whether believers experience their humility and enemies their principled firmness—or the reverse. Harshness inside and dependence outside signals disorder.

Verse 55 now identifies the secure wilayah they should seek: Allah, His Messenger, and believers whose prayer, zakah, and humble worship demonstrate their allegiance.

=55=

“Your true protecting ally is only Allah, His Messenger, and the believers—those who establish prayer, give zakah, and bow humbly in worship.”

After warning against compromising alliances, the Quran does not leave believers isolated. Wilayah begins with Allah, is embodied through His Messenger, and extends through a community formed by worship and generosity.

Allah’s wilayah is absolute guardianship. The Messenger’s is authorised leadership and care, never independent divinity. Believers support one another through counsel, protection, justice, financial help, and shared responsibility.

Prayer and zakah summarise vertical and social faith. Those who stand before Allah and give obligatory wealth are less likely to make community loyalty a slogan without sacrifice. Ruku‘ can describe the bowing posture and the wider humility saturating their worship.

Some reports connect the verse to a particular act of charity by Ali while bowing, but their chains and interpretation are disputed, and the plural wording gives a general description. The verse should not be reduced to sectarian argument when its clear call is believing solidarity.

“Only” concerns ultimate and defining protective allegiance, not prohibition of lawful cooperation. A Muslim doctor can work with a Christian colleague, a government can sign a treaty, and neighbours can protect each other from harm without replacing Allah and faith as the moral centre.

Believers do not earn loyalty merely by name. Prayer, zakah, humility, and justice describe the community worthy of trust. A corrupt Muslim cannot demand assistance in oppression based on this verse.

The verse also places vulnerable believers within everyone’s concern. Wealthy and powerful members are not the only strategic assets; the person bowing and giving may be closer to Allah than the one controlling institutions.

Verse 56 promises that whoever takes Allah, His Messenger, and believers as allies belongs to Allah’s party, and Allah’s party will be ultimately victorious.

=56=

“Whoever takes Allah, His Messenger, and the believers as protecting allies should know that Allah’s party will certainly prevail.”

Hizb Allah is not the name of a modern organisation in this verse. It means the people aligned with Allah through the Messenger and faithful community. No party can claim the title merely through branding while violating His commands.

Their victory is ultimate: truth’s preservation, spiritual success, and triumph in the Hereafter. It may include worldly victory when Allah wills, but it is not a guarantee that believers never suffer defeat, occupation, poverty, or martyrdom. Prophets and companions endured all of these.

Ghalibun means the final balance belongs to Allah. A community can look weak while holding the winning covenant, and an empire can look dominant while approaching collapse. This frees believers from selling principle for immediate protection.

Alliance with Allah requires obedience. One cannot oppress people, break treaties, consume interest, and then assume membership in His party creates immunity. Verse 8’s justice toward enemies remains part of the identity.

The promise should produce steadiness, not triumphal contempt. Believers want guidance for others and fear their own failures. Confidence without humility can turn into the arrogance for which earlier communities were criticised.

A practical sign of hizb Allah appears in the next verse: they refuse protective allegiance to people who turn their religion into mockery and play. Loyalty shapes which voices are permitted to define what is sacred.

=57=

“O believers, do not take as protecting allies those among the people given Scripture before you, or the disbelievers, who treat your religion as mockery and amusement. Be mindful of Allah if you are truly believers.”

The qualification matters: the verse targets those who mock and trivialise the religion, including some from earlier scriptural communities and other disbelievers. It does not classify every Jew or Christian as a mocker. The Quran has already permitted close social bonds through food and marriage.

Mockery turns what believers hold sacred into entertainment, often to weaken confidence and gain social dominance. A person who repeatedly humiliates faith is not a safe source of intimate strategic loyalty, even if charming in other settings.

This does not mean believers may attack or insult mockers. The command regulates their own allegiance. Elsewhere they are told to leave a gathering of ridicule until the subject changes (4:140) and to respond with wisdom. Lawful authorities handle threats; private violence remains forbidden.

Friendship can include disagreement, but contempt corrodes trust. A Muslim should distinguish a sincere critic, a comedian’s careless remark, a person asking a difficult question, and an organised campaign of derision. Proportion and context matter.

Taqwa governs the response. Anger for religion can itself cross Allah’s limits through lies, collective blame, or cruelty. Defending sacred things through disobedience contradicts the sacred.

Modern work and citizenship may require interaction with people who mock Islam. Professional fairness and covenant remain obligatory; one can set boundaries, challenge a statement calmly, or disengage without betrayal or abuse.

“If you are believers” connects loyalty to faith but should not be used for casual takfir of someone who made a poor friendship decision. It is a serious warning calling conduct back to creed.

Verse 58 gives a recurring example of mockery: when believers call to prayer, some take the adhan as amusement because they do not understand its meaning and gravity.

=58=

“When you call to prayer, they take it as mockery and amusement. That is because they are a people who do not understand.”

The adhan publicly proclaims tawhid, Muhammad’s messengership, prayer, and true success. Mocking it therefore ridicules the religion’s central testimony and daily rhythm, not merely a sound preference.

“Do not understand” identifies moral and spiritual failure beneath ridicule. Someone may know the Arabic words yet fail to grasp why people interrupt trade, sleep, and conversation to answer their Creator. Mockery often substitutes for engagement with meaning.

The verse concerns those who deliberately turn the call into derision. It does not forbid reasonable discussion about volume, scheduling, local law, or neighbourly impact. Muslims should uphold worship while considering legitimate rights and agreements.

The Sunnah teaches answering the mu’adhdhin’s phrases and praying for the Prophet after the call. Believers respond to mockery best by deepening their own reverence and conduct, not only by anger. An adhan followed by dishonest business weakens its public witness.

Where ridicule occurs, a calm explanation may open understanding. In other situations, leaving or reporting targeted harassment is wiser. The Quran does not prescribe one interpersonal tactic for every incident.

Religious symbols of other communities also deserve protection from vandalism and gratuitous insult. Justice cannot demand respect for Muslim worship while celebrating contempt toward others. The Quran forbids insulting false objects of worship when it would provoke ignorant insult against Allah (6:108).

The next verse instructs the Prophet to ask the People of the Book what exactly they resent: is it merely that Muslims believe in Allah, the Quran, and the genuine revelations before it, while many of the opponents have departed from obedience?

=59=

“Say, ‘O People of the Scripture, do you resent us for any reason other than that we believe in Allah, in what has been revealed to us, and in what was revealed before, while most of you are defiantly disobedient?’”

The question exposes an odd basis for hostility. Muslims believe in the one God and honour the original Torah, Gospel, and prophets. Their faith includes Musa and Isa rather than insulting them. Why should that continuity provoke resentment?

The real point of conflict is the Quran’s correction and the final Messenger. Accepting him would require some leaders to surrender inherited authority and acknowledge distortions. Resentment can therefore hide threatened status beneath theological argument.

“Most of you” refers to the opponents in the addressed setting or the prevalence of fisq among those resisting; it is not permission to declare every member of the People of the Book corrupt in every age. The same passage has praised truthful scholars and believers among them.

Fasiq means one who steps outside obedience. The verse does not say ethnic identity creates rebellion. Conduct does. A Muslim who rejects Allah’s limits can share the trait even while reciting this criticism.

The verse models direct but reasoned dialogue: name shared beliefs, identify the actual disagreement, and ask why it warrants hostility. It does not require hiding doctrine for social comfort.

Muslims should avoid turning the rhetorical question into taunting. The Prophet’s character, justice, and concern for guidance governed delivery. Debate that feeds ego may be technically accurate and spiritually harmful.

Verse 60 answers mockery with a question about a genuinely worse standing before Allah: the historical people whom Allah cursed, became angry with, transformed into apes and swine, and who worshipped false powers. Their path was further from the level way.

=60=

“Say, ‘Shall I inform you of those with a worse recompense from Allah? They are those whom Allah rejected, with whom He became angry, some of whom He made into apes and swine, and who worshipped false powers. They are worse in position and further astray from the level path.’”

The verse responds to people mocking believers by recalling specific historical punishments. Those who violated the Sabbath after clear warning were told to become despised apes (2:65, 7:166). The reference to swine is likewise a divine punishment upon particular offenders; the Quran does not make later populations biologically descended from them.

This language must never be used as a racial slur against Jews, Christians, or any living group. The verse says “some of whom” and ties punishment to acts and divine judgment. The Prophet prohibited injustice and contempt toward protected non-Muslims, and the Quran repeatedly distinguishes upright individuals.

Allah’s curse is removal from mercy after persistent rebellion; anger is His just response, unlike uncontrolled human rage. Transformation displayed outwardly the degradation already chosen inwardly. Believers affirm the account without inventing sensational details.

Taghut worship includes idols, satanic authority, and created powers obeyed as rivals to Allah. A community possessing scripture can still fall into false worship if revelation becomes identity without submission.

“Worse in position” reverses the mockers’ scale. They treated prayer and faith as ridiculous while histories they claimed to understand contained severe rebellion. Social prestige does not determine standing before Allah.

The verse is also a mirror for Muslims. If they violate covenants, manipulate law, mock others, and obey oppressive power, the story cannot save them merely because the named offenders came from an earlier community. Moral lessons cross generations.

One should state theological warning firmly while refusing dehumanisation. Transforming a historical punishment into a nickname for neighbours repeats the ignorance the Quran came to cure.
