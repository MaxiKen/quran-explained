=21=

Moses continues his impassioned address to the Israelites, building on the remembrance of divine favours. The command is direct and momentous: “Enter the Holy Land.” This refers to the land of Palestine, specifically Jerusalem and its surroundings, which had been promised to the descendants of Abraham.

The word “destined” in the translation captures the term *kataba*, which linguistically means “written. ” Allah had written this destiny for them, ordaining it as a place of settlement and worship, but this divine decree was conditional upon their active obedience and willingness to strive for it. The command therefore demolishes the notion of a passive reliance on destiny. It is not enough that God has written something good for a community; they must rise and walk into that promise with their own feet, facing its challenges.

The second part of the verse is a severe warning: “And do not turn back or else you will become losers. ” Turning back, in this context, refers to retreating from the battlefield of obedience out of fear of the enemy. It is a psychological and physical flight that reveals a deep failure of trust in God’s promise. The consequence of such a retreat is explicitly labelled as “losers.

” This is not a loss of land or wealth, but a comprehensive state of failure in this world and the next. For the Muslim community in Medina, who were about to face major military tests and who would one day be called to spread the message, this verse was a direct parallel. They too had been given a divine mission and a covenant. The lesson was clear: the sacred mission cannot be abandoned at the first sight of a powerful adversary. When God has decreed a duty, the only path that avoids loss is to move forward with courage. Fear of the enemy's power betrays a heart that has forgotten the power of the One who made the promise. This verse thus serves as a timeless criterion for any generation of believers called to a difficult collective duty, reminding them that retreat from God’s command, when the path is clear, is the very definition of spiritual bankruptcy.

=22=

This verse records the immediate, disheartening response of the Israelites to Moses’ command, revealing the vast gulf between a prophetic leader’s trust in God and the masses’ trust in material power. Their reply begins with the respectful address “O Moses,” yet the content betrays a complete psychological defeat. They describe the inhabitants of the Holy Land as “an enormously powerful people.” The description fixes their gaze on the apparent strength of the enemy, a strength of body and military might that their own eyes magnified into an insurmountable obstacle. Their conclusion is absolute and cowardly: “so we will never be able to enter it.” They shut the door on the possibility of victory before a single step was taken, because their assessment was based purely on physical calculation.

However, they add a condition that exposes their passive, conditional obedience: “until they leave.” This is a condition they know is unrealistic. God did not command them to wait for a miracle that required no effort; He commanded them to enter and fight. Their statement is effectively a refusal dressed up in pious-sounding language.

The phrase “If they do, then we will enter” is a mockery of faith. Faith does not wait for the path to be cleared by circumstances before it obeys; it steps forward trusting that God will clear the path. The psychological parallel for the early Muslims was powerful. They too faced “enormously powerful people” in the Quraysh and the Byzantine and Persian empires. The lesson from this narrative was a warning: if they respond to the Prophet’s call to struggle with the same materialistic analysis and conditional submission, they will share in the same humiliation. The verse serves as a permanent diagnosis of a diseased mindset in any believing community, a mindset that magnifies the strength of the opponent and minimises the power of the Lord, thereby paralysing all action and reducing faith to a mere theory that never touches the battlefield of life.

=23=

In the midst of the collective failure of nerve, a righteous minority steps forward to speak the language of courage and divine reliance. The verse introduces them as “Two God-fearing men—who had been blessed by Allah.” The description “God-fearing” highlights their deep consciousness of God, which prevented cowardice from taking root in their hearts.

The phrase “who had been blessed by Allah” indicates they possessed a special gift of insight, firmness, and wisdom. According to many commentators, these two men were Joshua and Caleb, the only two from that generation who maintained their faith and were later permitted to enter the Holy Land. Their strategic advice is brilliant in its simplicity: “Surprise them through the gate.” They acknowledge the strength of the enemy, but instead of being paralysed by it, they propose a tactical approach that relies on initiative and shock, not on waiting for the enemy to vacate the land. They do not deny the reality of the powerful inhabitants, but they see a way to victory that the fearful masses missed. Following the tactical advice, they make a strong promise grounded in faith: “If you do, you will certainly prevail.” This certainty is not based on military superiority but on the divine promise that God grants victory to those who obey Him. The key condition for this victory is then stated: “Put your trust in Allah if you are truly believers.” Trust, or tawakkul, is the active state of the heart that combines taking all worldly means with a complete reliance on God for the outcome. The statement “if you are truly believers” is a sharp rebuke. It ties the very authenticity of their claim to faith to their willingness to act and trust. This teaches that belief is not a static identity but a dynamic force that manifests as courage in the face of danger. For the Muslims, this was a direct lesson: the consultation of the righteous few who remind the community of God’s power and the necessity of trust must be heeded, and true iman is proven not in times of safety but in the moments that demand stepping through the gate.

=24=

Despite the powerful counsel of the two righteous men, the response of the Israelites moves from fearful refusal to an astonishing level of insolence and rebellion. The verse begins with “Yet,” indicating a stubborn persistence in their stance. They reiterate their refusal with even stronger emphasis: “O Moses! Still we will never enter as long as they remain there. ” They are now completely entrenched in their psychological paralysis, impervious to both spiritual motivation and sound strategic advice. But the true horror of this verse lies in what follows. They say, “So go—both you and your Lord—and fight; we are staying right here!

” This statement crosses a line from cowardice into blasphemous disrespect. First, they speak of God and Moses as two separate entities who should go fight alongside each other, revealing a distorted and crude conception of the divine. Second, they absolve themselves of all responsibility, relegating the very prophet who led them out of slavery to the status of their mercenary, while they recline in comfort. Their declaration “we are staying right here” is a full embrace of a static, faithless life that refuses any cost. This verse captures the ultimate breakdown of the covenant community. When a people reach this stage of brazen defiance, they have not only lost their courage but have corrupted their understanding of God. The Quran immortalises this moment as a dire warning to the Muslim Ummah. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, was sent to call the believers to strive in God’s path. The response of the hypocrites and the weak of heart in Medina sometimes mirrored this attitude, with them offering excuses to avoid the heat of battle, leaving the Prophet and his closest companions to face the dangers alone. The verse serves as a painful mirror, asking every generation of believers if their response to the call of duty is one of active commitment or a shameful, seated refusal.

=25=

The profound grief and frustration of Moses reach their peak in this intimate prayer. Having exhausted his persuasive efforts and witnessed the public rebellion of his people, he turns entirely to the only source of help.

The word “pleaded” translates a supplication made in a state of deep distress. He confesses his human limitation with a tone of lament: “My Lord! I have no control over anyone except myself and my brother.” Moses, the mighty prophet who faced Pharaoh, acknowledges that his authority is powerless against hearts that have chosen rebellion. He can command, advise, and warn, but he cannot force belief or courage into a heart that has sealed itself shut.

The mention of “my brother” refers to Aaron, his co-prophet and supporter, who stood with him in this trial. By stating that he only has control over himself and his brother, Moses declares the limits of his responsibility. A prophet is not accountable for the sins of his people after he has delivered the message and done his utmost.

This is a profound comfort for every leader and caller to God who is met with stubborn rejection. Having established the limits of his control, Moses then asks for a divine judgment that separates: “So set us apart from the rebellious people. ” This is not a prayer born of hatred but of a desire for a clear divine verdict. He asks God to distinguish between the obedient minority and the defiant majority, both in destiny and in worldly affairs, so that justice can be seen. For the Prophet Muhammad, this verse was a direct consolation. He too would witness his followers waver and some hypocrites refuse the call. The lesson was that a prophet’s duty is to convey, strive, and then entrust the final judgment to God. The prayer of Moses becomes a model for the believer who has done all they can: turn to Allah, acknowledge human limits, and leave the fate of the rebellious to the All-Wise Judge.

=26=

The divine response to Moses’ plea is a decree of punishment that directly answers their own words. The rebellious Israelites had declared, “We are staying right here,” refusing to enter the Holy Land. So Allah decreed that they would remain in the wilderness, barred from their destination: “Then this land is forbidden to them for forty years.” The very thing they refused to fight for would be denied to them, and the state of wandering they chose out of cowardice would become their enforced reality. The forty-year period was not a random number; it was a precise duration during which an entire generation of slave-minded cowards would die out in the desert of Sinai, so that a new generation, born in freedom and hardened by the desert, would be the ones to enter under Joshua’s leadership. The description of their punishment continues: “during which they will wander through the land.” This wandering, or *tīh*, was a state of utter confusion and aimlessness. Despite the vastness of the desert, they would roam lost, unable to find a way out, a psychological torment that mirrored their spiritual lostness. Then Allah directly addresses the grieving prophet: “So do not grieve for the rebellious people.

” This command is a balm for the heart of Moses. It is natural for a sincere prophet to grieve for his people’s fate, but God instructs him that such grief should not be wasted on those who have wilfully chosen rebellion and earned His wrath. This line is a crucial lesson in emotional and spiritual boundaries. The caller to truth can feel sadness but must not let it consume them, because the divine judgment is perfectly just and the rebels have brought their fate upon themselves.

For the Muslim community, the story ends with a clear moral: the consequence of refusing to struggle in God’s path, of prioritising safety over obedience, is not immediate annihilation but a prolonged, wasted existence of spiritual and existential wandering, a life barred from the true blessings and victories God had in store for them.

=27=

The narrative lens now shifts from the collective story of the Israelites to the very first human story of envy and murder, which serves as a timeless archetype of the human condition. The command “Relate to them in truth, O Prophet,” instructs Muhammad, peace be upon him, to recite this story to his people with absolute precision, for it contains essential wisdom for the community.

The story begins with Adam’s two sons, Abel and Cain, each offering a sacrifice. The core of the event is that “Abel’s offering was accepted while Cain’s was not. ” The Quran does not detail the sacrifices here, but authentic traditions explain that Abel, a shepherd, offered his finest, healthiest animal, while Cain, a farmer, offered some of his worst produce. Their hearts were different, and thus the divine acceptance was different. The reaction of Cain is immediate and violent: he “threatened, ‘I will kill you!’” The first murder in human history was not over wealth or territory but over religious envy—a sick heart that could not bear to see his brother favoured by God.

This is a powerful warning to all believers that a soul lacking sincerity may turn on those who are more pious, accusing them of hypocrisy or self-righteousness, when in reality the problem is a corrupt inner state. Abel’s response is a masterpiece of righteous composure. He says, “Allah only accepts the offering of the sincerely devout.” He identifies the root cause with clarity: his brother’s rejection was not a matter of ritual but a matter of the heart’s orientation.

The word *muttaqīn*, translated as “sincerely devout,” refers to those who possess taqwa, a God-consciousness that purifies intention. Abel’s reply is not a boast about his own piety; it is a theological explanation meant to direct his brother’s anger towards self-reform, not murder. The story thus teaches that the correct response to being surpassed in virtue is introspection, not aggression, and that the foundation of every accepted deed is the quality of taqwa residing in the heart.

=28=

This verse continues Abel’s dignified and principled response to his brother’s murderous threat, presenting one of the most powerful declarations of moral restraint in scripture. Abel is fully aware of the danger he faces; Cain’s hand is poised to strike.

Yet he states, “If you raise your hand to kill me, I will not raise mine to kill you.” This is not a statement of physical inability or cowardice. Abel is not saying he lacks the strength or the right to defend his life. Rather, he is making a conscious, ethical choice to not enter into a spiral of retaliation. He refuses to become a murderer even in the act of self-defence, because killing his brother would make him the same kind of transgressor. This sets an extraordinarily high moral standard, one that is not legislated as a general rule—Islamic law later permits self-defence—but is presented here as a personal, saintly virtue in a specific context. The reason for this choice follows immediately: “because I fear Allah—the Lord of all worlds.” The fear of Allah, this profound taqwa, completely overrides the instinct for vengeance. Abel’s vision of the world is not limited to this fleeting life. He sees beyond the immediate threat, connecting his action to the Lord of all worlds, the Master of every creation. His fear of God’s punishment and his hope for God’s pleasure are stronger than his fear of Cain’s hand. This teaches a profound lesson about the hierarchy of fears in a believer’s heart. When the fear of the Creator is truly alive, the fear of any created being shrinks to insignificance. Abel becomes the archetype of the peaceful believer, the one who absorbs violence rather than propagates it, whose faith is so deep that he entrusts his very life to divine justice. This spirit is reflected in numerous hadiths that praise the believer who controls anger and refrains from strife, even when he is in the right, leaving his ultimate vindication to God.

=29=

Abel’s speech reaches its conclusion with a statement that reveals not a passive submission to death, but a profound understanding of divine justice and an unexpected form of care for his own soul. He says, “I want to let you bear your sin against me along with your other sins.

” The phrase “I want” shows that Abel is not merely resigned to his fate but actively wills it for a higher purpose. He does not desire his brother’s destruction out of hatred; he is laying out the stark reality of what Cain’s action will bring upon himself. By murdering him, Cain will not only carry the sin of the murder but will also add that to his “other sins,” meaning the earlier sins of envy, a rejected offering, and a corrupted heart. Abel is essentially saying, “My blood will be a burden around your neck on the Day of Judgment.” The result is then stated with clarity: “then you will be one of those destined to the Fire.” Abel is not making a judgment from his own ego; he is stating the divine law as revealed to him. The act of murder, especially of a righteous believer, is a sin that leads directly to Hellfire unless met with sincere repentance.

The final sentence, “And that is the reward of the wrongdoers,” universalises the principle. It is not just Cain who receives such a punishment; every wrongdoer who transgresses against others will face the same crushing justice. Abel’s words serve as a mirror for all tyrants and murderers. They may succeed in spilling blood in this world, but they are bankrupting their own Hereafter in a transaction of ultimate loss.

For the believer who reads this verse, it inspires a sombre reflection: the one who refrains from retaliation is not weak; he is ensuring that he stands on the Day of Judgment with a clean record, while his oppressor appears laden with the sins he committed. It is the ultimate victory of the victim, a victory that is not seen by the eyes of this world but is eternal in the next.

=30=

After the brilliant and truthful exhortation of Abel, the Quran records the tragic climax with a devastating brevity. The phrase “Yet Cain convinced himself” translates the Arabic *fa-ṭawwa‘at lahu nafsuhu*, which literally means his soul made the act easy and agreeable for him.

This is the terrifying anatomy of sin. The human soul, when left unchecked by taqwa, slowly rationalises the worst actions until they appear not only acceptable but desirable. Cain’s initial envy brewed into anger, his anger was not checked by his brother’s wisdom, and now his own inner whispers have decorated the heinous act of fratricide as a justified solution to his wounded pride. There is no external devil mentioned here; the focus is on the internal enemy, the nafs, which can become a greater enemy than any external shaytan.

The verse then states the grim action simply: “so he killed him.” The Quran does not linger on the gruesome details of the first murder. The starkness of the statement itself conveys the horror. In one moment of uncontrolled rage, the sacred bond of brotherhood was shattered, and a human soul, innocent of any crime, was sent to meet its Lord. Immediately, the divine verdict is pronounced: “becoming a loser.” The loss here is absolute and total. He lost his brother, his innocence, his peace of mind, his relationship with his Lord, and his Hereafter. In a worldly sense, he might have thought he had won by eliminating the source of his envy, but the Quranic reality flips this perception completely. He is the *khāsir*, the one in a state of utter bankruptcy.

This is the final destination of every person who lets their envy and anger dictate their actions. The verse serves as a timeless warning that the one who transgresses against another human being ultimately destroys nothing but their own soul. The loss in the divine ledger is total, even if the worldly ledger shows a temporary, blood-stained gain.

=31=

After the horrific act of murder, the narrative takes a darkly ironic and deeply instructive turn, detailing the immediate aftermath that compounds Cain’s misery. The verse states that “Allah sent a crow digging a grave in the ground.” The crow is an animal of lowly status, a scavenger. God inspired this crow to scratch at the earth to bury another dead crow. The purpose was “to show him how to bury the corpse of his brother.

” This is a moment of profound humiliation for the human being who had been honoured with knowledge. Cain, a son of a prophet, had just committed the first murder but had absolutely no idea what to do with the body. He was carrying his brother’s corpse on his back, confused and haunted. The fact that he needed a bird to teach him the most basic act of covering the dead revealed the utter regression of his intelligence caused by his sin. Seeing the crow’s action, he cried out in anguish, “Alas! Have I even failed to be like this crow and bury the corpse of my brother?” This exclamation is a confession of his own inferiority in that moment. A creature of instinct displayed more practical wisdom than the human who had let his passion destroy his reason. He was not like the crow; he was lower, for the crow disposed of the dead with dignity while he had caused the death.

The verse ends with a note on his emotional state: “So he became regretful.” However, the scholars of tafsir note that this regret, or *nadamah*, was not the regret of sincere repentance that wipes out sin. It was the regret of a criminal who is sorry for the consequences of his crime, the inconvenience and the humiliation, not a remorse that turns back to God with a broken heart seeking forgiveness. There is no record of Cain repenting. His regret was the bitter fruit of guilt, a fire that burns in the heart but does not illuminate the path back to the Lord.

=32=

This verse transitions from the narrative of Cain and Abel to a grand universal principle, which is the direct legal and moral lesson derived from that first murder. The opening phrase “That is why” connects the decree directly to the story. Because a single act of envy led to the first unjust killing, Allah ordained a law that magnifies the weight of human life to an almost incomprehensible degree. The law was specifically written for the Children of Israel, but its principle is enshrined in Islamic ethics as well. The decree states that “whoever takes a life—unless as a punishment for murder or mischief in the land—it will be as if they killed all of humanity.” The unjust killing of one soul is equated with the murder of all people.

This is not a mere exaggeration; it is a profound ethical truth. The person who kills one innocent human being demonstrates a willingness to violate the sanctity of life itself. They have shredded the universal respect for human existence, and if left to their own devices, their disregard for life could extend to many. It is the principle of the sanctity of life in its most powerful expression. The only exceptions are clearly defined: lawful retribution for murder, and punishment for “mischief in the land,” which scholars interpret as crimes that destabilise society, such as terrorism and highway robbery. The opposite side of the coin is equally powerful: “whoever saves a life, it will be as if they saved all of humanity. ” Saving a life here refers to any act that prevents a wrongful death—rescuing someone from danger, advocating for a wrongly accused person, or even refraining from killing when one has the power to do so.

The verse then adds a sorrowful historical note: “Although Our messengers already came to them with clear proofs, many of them still transgressed afterwards through the land.” This indicates that even with this sacred law revealed, the Israelites, like many later communities, still violated it, shedding blood and causing corruption, proving that laws alone are not enough without hearts filled with taqwa.

=33=

This verse reveals the severe worldly punishment for a specific category of criminals who combine terrorism, apostasy, and banditry. The crime is defined as “waging war against Allah and His Messenger and spreading mischief in the land.” This refers to acts of armed aggression that terrorise a peaceful society, such as highway robbery, murder, and destabilising the state that implements divine law. The verse is often called the verse of *hirābah*. The prescribed punishments are listed, and scholars have extensively discussed their application based on the severity of the crime. The options are “death” for those who kill and terrorise, “crucifixion” after execution as a public deterrent for those who combine murder with robbery, “cutting off their hands and feet on opposite sides” for those who engage in armed robbery without taking a life, and “exile from the land” for those who cause terror but stop short of these acts. The range of penalties gives the Islamic judge discretion to match the punishment to the crime. The purpose is explicitly stated as a “disgrace for them in this world,” meaning a public humiliation that serves as a deterrent and an affirmation of the state’s authority to protect its citizens.

However, the verse does not stop at the worldly punishment. It adds the terrifying spiritual consequence: “and they will suffer a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter.” This means that even if a criminal is executed, the worldly punishment does not expiate the sin before God, unless they repented before being apprehended, as the next verse clarifies. The severity of this law is a direct extension of the sanctity of life principle in the previous verse. Because human life is so valuable, those who shamelessly violate it and disrupt the peace of society face the sternest possible recompense in this life and the next, establishing a climate of safety for the common people.

=34=

Immediately after the harsh and detailed punishment for hirabah, the Quran opens a wide door of divine mercy, demonstrating that the law’s ultimate goal is not vengeance but the reformation of society and the individual. The condition for this escape from the worldly punishment is clear and time-bound: “those who repent before you seize them.” Repentance must occur before the criminal is captured by the authorities. If they turn themselves in, overcome by remorse and a genuine desire to reform, the prescribed worldly punishment for the crime of waging war against the society is dropped.

This is a powerful legal and spiritual incentive. It encourages the criminal to abandon their path of violence and surrender peacefully, knowing that they can find complete amnesty from the state’s penalty. This fosters a practical mechanism for reducing violence without a protracted manhunt.

However, the phrase “before you seize them” is crucial. Once the criminal is captured, the door for amnesty from the worldly punishment closes, because at that point surrender is no longer a voluntary act of repentance but a consequence of being overpowered.

The verse then highlights the divine attributes that ground this leniency: “then know that Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” The guarantee of forgiveness for the one who repents is absolute, because these two names of God envelop the sinner in a promise of pardon. This does not necessarily mean they are absolved from the rights of their victims; many scholars state that the victims’ rights, such as blood money, remain intact and must be fulfilled. But the state’s right to punish is waived.

The sequence of these two verses is a profound illustration of the balance between justice and mercy in Islamic law. The severity of the previous verse protects society, while the mercy of this verse protects the soul of the sinner who desires to change. It tells every human being that no matter how grave the crime, as long as the heart is still beating and the person is free to choose, the path back to God is open.

=35=

After the intense legal passages dealing with murder, terrorism, and theft, the Quran once again addresses the believers with a comprehensive call to spiritual and practical action, providing the formula for true success. The verse opens with the core command of the faith: “O believers! Be mindful of Allah.

” This is the foundational taqwa, the constant awareness that acts as an internal moral compass. Immediately, a second command is added, which explains how to achieve and deepen that taqwa: “and seek what brings you closer to Him.

” The word for “seek” is *ibtighā'*, implying active, persistent searching. The “what brings you closer” translates *al-wasīlah*, a term that encompasses every permissible means that elevates one’s rank before God. This includes obligatory acts of worship, abundant voluntary prayers, charity, seeking beneficial knowledge, maintaining family ties, and serving the creation. It is a call to be proactive in one’s spiritual life, not merely to avoid sin but to aggressively pursue the practices and stations that draw the divine love.

The third command forms a triad of personal piety, devotional pursuit, and societal struggle: “and struggle in His Way.” The term *jāhidū* is used, the root word of jihad, which encompasses every form of striving for God’s cause, from the internal struggle against one’s ego to the external struggle of defending the community and conveying the message. This struggle is the testing ground of the faith.

The verse ends with the goal of all these actions: “so you may be successful.” The word *tufliḥūn*, from *falāḥ*, denotes the ultimate, comprehensive success that is not just material prosperity but the eternal well-being of the soul.

The sequence is beautiful: taqwa is the inner light, seeking wasilah is the active devotional life, jihad is the external proof of sincerity, and falāḥ is the promised fruit of this complete programme. This verse gives the Muslim a concise roadmap for a life that leads to true victory.

=36=

The surah now shifts back to the fate of those who reject faith, painting a vivid and terrifying picture of the Day of Judgment that serves as a stark contrast to the "success" promised to the believers. It begins with "As for the disbelievers," immediately defining the group whose destiny is about to be described. The hypothetical scenario presented is one of absolute material wealth: "even if they were to possess everything in the world twice over.

" This is a rhetorical device that pushes the imagination to its limits. Imagine a person who owns not just the entire earth's riches—all the gold, oil, lands, and treasures—but double that amount. Such wealth, in worldly terms, would be sufficient to buy anything a human heart could desire.

The verse then places this unimaginable fortune in the context of the Afterlife: "and offer it all to ransom themselves from the punishment of the Day of Judgment." In this world, a wealthy person can often buy their way out of legal trouble. The Quran shatters this illusion. On that Day, there is no currency, no negotiation, and no bail. The divine verdict is stated with crushing finality: "it would never be accepted from them." The rejection is absolute. Their fate is sealed not by a lack of resources but by the nature of that Day, where the only acceptable currency is a sound heart, faith, and righteous deeds.

The verse concludes with the sentence: "And they will suffer a painful punishment." The word "painful," translating *alīm*, indicates a pain that permeates the very core of one's being, a suffering that is both physical and spiritual. This verse is meant to shake the conscience of the listener. It warns the believers against being impressed by the material wealth of those who reject God, for on the Day of Judgment, that wealth will be less than worthless. It also instills a deep gratitude for the promise of forgiveness given to the believers, a gift that all the treasures of the earth could never purchase.

=37=

This verse adds further terrifying details to the fate of the disbelievers, moving from the Day of Judgment’s courtroom to the actual experience within Hell. The psychological state of the damned is captured in the phrase "They will be desperate to get out of the Fire." The desperation is an intense, panicked craving, a desire for escape that overwhelms every other thought. When a person is trapped in a worldly fire, every fibre of their being screams to flee the flames. This verse magnifies that instinct to an eternal scale. They will wish for death, they will wish for oblivion, they will wish for any exit.

However, the divine will is unalterable: "but they will never be able to." Their powerlessness is absolute. There is no eventual release, no parole, no annihilation of the self. Their wish is perpetually denied, adding a profound psychological torment to the physical pain.

The verse then concludes with the definitive statement: "And they will suffer an everlasting punishment." The word "everlasting" translates *muqīm*, meaning established, permanent, and continuous without interruption. This affirms the Islamic doctrine that Hell is an eternal abode for those who die upon disbelief.

This teaching is not a matter of divine cruelty but of divine justice, as they had been given a lifetime of opportunities, clear signs, and warnings, yet they persistently rejected the truth and would have remained in that state had they lived forever. The sequence of verses 36 and 37 serves a clear function in the surah. After detailing the punishments for worldly crimes like hirabah and theft, the Quran reminds the listener that these punishments, however severe, are finite and can even be nullified by repentance. The punishment of the disbelievers, however, is a stark reminder that the ultimate catastrophe is not a worldly penalty but the eternal loss of the Hereafter.

This contrast is designed to reorder the believer’s priorities and to instill a healthy fear that propels one toward the success mentioned in verse 35.

=38=

The surah returns to the realm of criminal law, having established the grand ethical principles about the sanctity of life and the need for societal peace. This verse addresses the crime of theft, a violation of property rights that erodes economic trust and security. It begins by explicitly mentioning both genders: "As for male and female thieves.

" This is a profound statement of legal equality, ensuring that a woman's punishment for theft is the same as a man's, unlike the pre-Islamic customs that often treated women with harsher or more lenient standards arbitrarily. The prescribed punishment is stated with stark clarity: "cut off their hands.

" The Arabic word *yad* refers to the hand, and the juristic consensus is that for a first-time offence meeting all strict conditions, the right hand is amputated from the wrist. The condition for this punishment is that the theft must involve a minimum value of stolen goods, taken from a secure location, without any plausible claim of ownership or need-driven justification.

This is not a punishment for a hungry person stealing food out of necessity, which is excused by the law of necessity. The rationale is immediately stated: "for what they have done—a deterrent from Allah.

" The word "deterrent," *nakālan*, implies an exemplary punishment that serves to prevent others from committing the same crime. It is a measure that protects the property of the entire community by making the cost of theft so severe.

However, the verse closes with two divine names that frame the entire ruling: "And Allah is Almighty, All-Wise. " The name "Almighty" indicates that He has the authority to enact such a law, and no human sentimentality can override it. The name "All-Wise" is crucial; it affirms that this punishment, which may seem harsh to modern sensibilities that prioritise the criminal's comfort over the victim's security, is rooted in a perfect wisdom that understands human nature, the need for a safe society, and the ultimate balance of justice and mercy. The wisdom is seen in the high standards of evidence required, the detailed conditions for amputation, and the provision for repentance in the next verse.

=39=

Following the same merciful pattern seen after the punishment for hirabah, this verse opens the door of divine forgiveness for the thief who reforms, though it does not explicitly cancel the worldly punishment if the crime has already been proven and the punishment is due. The verse states, "But whoever repents after their wrongdoing and mends their ways." True repentance here is defined by two elements: remorse for the past sin and a concrete, visible amendment of behaviour. The thief must not only feel sorry but must also return the stolen property to its rightful owner if possible, and change their lifestyle to one of honesty and integrity. This act of genuine, transformative repentance triggers a magnificent divine promise: "Allah will surely turn to them in forgiveness.

" The term *yatūbu*, from *tawbah*, means that Allah Himself turns towards the servant with His mercy and pardon. It is a beautiful image of a reciprocal relationship: the servant turns away from sin, and the Lord turns towards the sinner with forgiveness. The certainty of this promise is emphasised with the word "surely. " Then the verse concludes with the deeply comforting pair of divine names: "Indeed, Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

" The word "All-Forgiving" (*Ghafūr*) means He covers the sin and wipes its record clean, while "Most Merciful" (*Raḥīm*) means He bestows blessings and love upon the repentant soul. This verse, when read with the previous one, perfectly illustrates the Islamic balance. The law on earth serves to protect society and deter crime through a visible, severe punishment. The law of the Hereafter serves to redeem the individual soul that genuinely changes. No matter how grave the sin—whether terrorism, murder, or theft—the penitent heart that turns to its Creator before death finds an ocean of mercy. This prevents the criminal from falling into despair, assuring them that their past does not permanently define them as long as they perform a sincere tawbah that mends their relationship with God and with the creation they have wronged.

This verse serves as a majestic conclusion to the extended legal discourse, bringing the reader’s attention back to the absolute sovereignty and authority of the Lawgiver. It begins with a rhetorical question: "Do you not know that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs to Allah alone?

" This is a gentle rebuke aimed at awakening the innate knowledge that every soul possesses about God’s absolute ownership. Everything in the universe, from the vast galaxies to the cells in a thief's hand, belongs to Him. The laws He decrees are not the impositions of an outsider but the rulings of the true Sovereign over His own dominion. This realization is meant to foster a deep submission to the rulings that preceded it. Why should a human being object to the punishment for theft or for waging war, when the One who commanded it owns the very hand being cut and the life being taken?

The verse then declares a principle that governs the operation of this divine kingdom: "He punishes whoever He wills and forgives whoever He wills. " This statement is not a license for arbitrary will devoid of wisdom, as the names Al-Hakīm (All-Wise) and Al-‘Adl (The Just) are fundamental. Rather, it means that no one can force Allah’s hand. His punishment and forgiveness are not subject to external approval. He punishes those who meet the conditions for His punishment according to His perfect knowledge, and He forgives those who meet the conditions for His mercy. It silences the human arrogance that questions why certain sins are met with certain penalties, reminding the believer that the criteria belong to the All-Knowing.

The verse ends with the comprehensive affirmation: "And Allah is Most Capable of everything." His capability encompasses the enforcement of His laws, the resurrection of the dead for judgment, and the execution of His perfect will in both worlds. This final note is a warning and a comfort: a warning to those who think they can evade the divine law, and a comfort to those who trust that the Almighty is fully able to grant them the success and forgiveness He promised in the earlier verses.
