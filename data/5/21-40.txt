=21=

“‘O my people, enter the sacred land which Allah has assigned for you, and do not turn back, or you will become losers.’”

Musa’s command follows his reminder of favour. The people had been freed from Pharaoh, guided by prophets, and given signs. Gratitude now required movement. Memory of miracles was meant to produce courage for the next obligation, not nostalgia about the last one.

The sacred land is generally understood as the region of Bayt al-Maqdis and its surroundings in the Levant. Kataba can mean Allah wrote or assigned entry for them under the command then given. The narrative itself shows that a divine grant did not excuse disobedience: when that generation refused, the land was forbidden to them for forty years.

The verse addresses Musa’s people in a particular prophetic period. It should not be turned into a stand-alone modern property deed that cancels every later covenant, the rights of existing people, or the Quran’s universal commands of justice. Contemporary territorial and political claims require their full historical, legal, and moral evidence; no scripture permits oppression of civilians.

“Enter” joined trust to action. Allah could have removed every obstacle without them, but He commanded them to advance under prophetic leadership. Reliance on decree is not waiting idly for the duty to complete itself. Means and obedience belong within qadar.

“Do not turn back” warns against retreat after clarity. Fear is natural; allowing fear to overrule a lawful command is the failure. The loss would include denied opportunity, weakened character, and the consequence soon described as wandering.

Not every retreat is sinful. Lawful commanders may withdraw tactically, and an individual may lack capacity. This verse concerns explicit refusal of Musa after signs and collective ability, not an instruction to pursue every difficult project regardless of wisdom.

Believers can apply the principle to clear duties: returning a right, leaving a forbidden income, protecting someone vulnerable, or undertaking needed service. One should verify that the command is truly from Allah and not a leader’s ambition, then refuse to let discomfort masquerade as impossibility.

Verse 22 records the people’s answer. They fixed their attention on the strength of the inhabitants and announced they would enter only after those people left—the outcome that would make obedience cost nothing.

=22=

“They replied, ‘O Musa, there is an immensely powerful people in that land. We will never enter it until they leave. If they leave, then we will enter.’”

The people did not deny the destination; they denied the path required to reach it. Reports describe the inhabitants as formidable in body and military strength. The Quran does not need legendary exaggerations about their size. It is enough that the Israelites perceived them as overpowering.

Their assessment contains a real observation and a faithless conclusion. Opponents can be strong, and good planning begins with accurate risk. The error is “we will never enter,” spoken against a divine command and promise after extraordinary rescue from Pharaoh.

Their condition—“until they leave”—empties obedience of sacrifice. If the challenge disappears first, entering is no longer courage. Conditional submission often sounds reasonable because it asks only for ideal circumstances: I will obey when there is no cost, temptation, opposition, or uncertainty.

Faith does not require denial of material reality. Musa did not teach them to ignore weapons, geography, or strategy. Tawakkul uses evidence and means while refusing to treat visible power as independent of Allah. The sea had already taught them that apparently closed routes can open by His command.

A formerly enslaved people may carry learned fear even after physical freedom. Their response reflects how oppression can shape imagination. This explains weakness without making rebellion harmless. Communities emerging from trauma need truthful encouragement, training, and leadership, but still possess responsibilities according to capacity.

The verse should not be used by modern leaders to shame citizens into reckless conflict. Musa was a confirmed prophet conveying Allah’s command. Ordinary rulers can miscalculate, deceive, or seek personal glory; their claims require consultation, law, evidence, and accountability.

The fearful majority is not the only voice. Verse 23 introduces two God-conscious men whom Allah had favoured. They acknowledge the challenge but call the people to enter through the gate, act decisively, and trust Allah if their claim to faith is true.

=23=

“Two men from among those who feared Allah, and whom Allah had blessed, said, ‘Enter upon them through the gate. Once you enter it, you will prevail. Put your trust in Allah if you are truly believers.’”

The Quran leaves the men unnamed. Early reports commonly identify them as Yusha‘ ibn Nun and Kalib, known as Joshua and Caleb, but the lesson does not depend on the names. Their defining quality is fear of Allah rather than fear of the powerful inhabitants.

Allah’s blessing may include faith, insight, courage, and freedom from the crowd’s panic. Courage is not always held by the numerical majority. Two truthful voices can diagnose possibility where a whole assembly has accepted defeat.

Their advice includes a means: enter through the gate. Tawakkul is not a substitute for tactics. They identify a decisive point and connect action to Allah’s promised support. Believers plan as if means matter and trust as if results belong entirely to Him.

“Once you enter, you will prevail” expresses confidence in the divine promise conveyed by Musa. An ordinary person should not imitate this certainty about a plan lacking revelation. Strong hope is encouraged, but leaders must distinguish faith from claiming guaranteed outcomes Allah never guaranteed.

“If you are believers” links faith to reliance without declaring every frightened person an unbeliever. Iman can weaken and fear can overwhelm. The statement calls their professed belief into action: the Lord believed in by the tongue must become greater in calculation than the obstacle seen by the eye.

Minority counsel deserves evaluation rather than romantic approval merely because it is minority. These two were right because their advice aligned with revelation, sound strategy, and taqwa. Unpopular speech can also be reckless. Criterion, not headcount, decides.

Communities need people who resist panic without concealing risk. Such advisers remind others of past help, propose a lawful next step, and place hope in Allah rather than their own charisma.

The people reject even this counsel in verse 24. Their words move beyond fear into insolence: Musa and his Lord should go fight while they remain seated.

=24=

“They said, ‘O Musa, we will never enter it as long as they remain there. So go—you and your Lord—and fight. We will stay right here.’”

The repeated “never” shows the refusal has hardened. Evidence, prophetic command, remembered favour, and the counsel of the two believers have all been heard. The problem is no longer lack of information but unwillingness to bear responsibility.

“Go, you and your Lord” is shocking disrespect. They do not necessarily articulate a developed belief that Allah physically walks beside Musa; the statement expresses insolent detachment: let divine power and prophetic leadership handle the cost while we remain outside it.

“We are sitting here” turns passivity into declaration. The same people who wanted liberation from Pharaoh now refuse the duty attached to freedom. Oppression can train dependence, but freedom must eventually become responsible obedience.

Their response stands in deliberate contrast to the companions before Badr. When the Prophet, peace be upon him, consulted them, al-Miqdad declared that they would not speak as the Children of Israel spoke to Musa; they would fight on his right, left, front, and rear. The comparison encouraged a new covenant community not to repeat the old failure.

The verse does not command uncritical following of every leader into war. Musa was an infallibly guided messenger in this command. Modern armed decisions remain restricted to legitimate authority, just cause, capacity, consultation, treaties, and protection of non-combatants.

The seated attitude also appears outside battle: demanding that scholars, parents, activists, or a few volunteers carry every communal obligation while others criticise from safety. Capacity differs, but a person should contribute through whatever lawful means are actually available.

Musa has reached the limit of persuasion. Verse 25 records his prayer: he controls only himself and his brother, so he asks Allah to judge between them and the defiantly disobedient people.

=25=

“Musa pleaded, ‘My Lord, I have control over no one except myself and my brother. So separate us from the defiantly disobedient people.’”

A prophet can convey, command, model, and pray, but cannot force courage into a rejecting heart. Musa’s confession is not failed leadership; it is honest recognition of human agency after he has fulfilled his duty.

His brother is Harun, peace be upon him, the prophet and supporter for whom Musa had prayed at the beginning of his mission. The two remain a unit when the crowd withdraws. A small company upon truth is not abandoned merely because the majority refuses.

“I possess only myself” does not imply absolute ownership or perfect control of outcomes. It means Musa can answer for his own decision and, with Harun’s agreement, rely upon his brother’s obedience. Every leader eventually meets the boundary where another adult’s choice belongs to that person.

Fafruq baynana can mean distinguish or judge between us and the rebellious people. Musa asks for a clear divine separation in responsibility and consequence. He does not personally invent a punishment in anger; he returns the case to the Judge.

Fasiqin are those who step outside obedience knowingly. Fear initiated the refusal, but repeated defiance turned it into a moral breach. Naming that breach does not erase the context; it refuses to make trauma an endless exemption.

Parents, teachers, and callers can learn emotional boundaries here. They should examine whether they taught wisely, correct their failures, and keep praying. They are not required to imagine they control every result. Grief can coexist with surrender to Allah’s judgment.

The prayer also protects the righteous minority from sharing approval of collective sin. Separation may be inward, legal, or physical according to circumstance. It must be sought through lawful means, not self-righteous abandonment at the first disagreement.

Allah answers in verse 26 by forbidding the land to that generation for forty years of wandering and telling Musa not to grieve over the persistently rebellious.

=26=

“Allah said, ‘Then this land is forbidden to them for forty years, during which they will wander throughout the land. So do not grieve over the defiantly disobedient people.’”

Their punishment mirrors their choice. They refused purposeful movement into the land, so movement without arrival became their condition. Tih is confused wandering—motion that consumes years but reaches no destination.

Forty years allowed a historical transition. Commentators often note that a generation shaped by enslavement passed and another grew under different conditions before entry became possible. The Quran itself focuses on the moral decree rather than supplying every later detail.

The land was “forbidden to them” for this period despite having been assigned in verse 21. This confirms that the assignment was tied to covenant and obedience, not an unconditional licence independent of conduct. The passage cannot honestly be quoted from verse 21 while hiding verse 26.

Wandering is more than geography. A community may possess activity, meetings, spending, and slogans while lacking obedient direction. Refusal of a clear duty can turn immense energy into circles. The remedy is not more motion but restored covenant.

“Do not grieve” does not portray Musa as cold. Prophets grieve because they care. Allah tells him not to let sorrow over chosen rebellion consume him or imply that just judgment was wrong. Compassion has limits where people continually refuse rescue.

This command should not be used to stop grief for sinners or abandon outreach. The Prophet Muhammad grieved deeply for his people and continued inviting them. The point is to entrust persistent rejection to Allah after fulfilling duty.

The narrative has shown collective fear leading to loss. Verse 27 shifts to a more ancient story in which envy leads to murder: the two sons of Adam and their offerings. The first lesson is that acceptance belongs to taqwa, not jealousy.

=27=

“Relate to them in truth the story of Adam’s two sons, when each offered a sacrifice. It was accepted from one and not accepted from the other. The latter said, ‘I will kill you.’ The former replied, ‘Allah accepts only from those mindful of Him.’”

The Quran does not name the brothers. Cain and Abel are familiar names from biblical and later tradition, but commentary should not confuse those names with Quranic wording. Nor does the Quran specify exactly what each offered. Reports about crops and livestock are not needed for the central lesson.

Both performed the outward ritual, but only one offering was accepted. Allah judges intention, lawfulness, sincerity, and taqwa rather than visible performance alone. Two deeds can look alike while their weight differs completely.

The rejected brother responds to another’s acceptance by threatening him instead of examining himself. Envy treats another person’s blessing as the problem. It would rather remove the blessed person than remove the disease from the heart.

The accepted brother does not boast, “I am better.” He states a principle: acceptance belongs to the muttaqin. This redirects the conversation from personality to reform. If his brother wants acceptance, the door is taqwa, not violence.

Taqwa does not mean only sinless people have deeds accepted, since every believer sins and repents. It means the deed is offered within sincere awareness of Allah, avoiding known corruption and returning from failure. The companions feared rejection even while doing good because acceptance is a gift, not a personal certificate.

Religious envy is especially dangerous. A person may resent another’s knowledge, charity, recitation, or influence while claiming concern for purity. Healthy competition asks Allah for bounty and imitates good; hasad wants the gift gone.

The threat “I will kill you” shows how an inward comparison can escalate when repeatedly fed. Early treatment matters: gratitude, du‘a for the other person, private self-accounting, and distance from inflammatory speech.

Verse 28 continues the righteous brother’s answer. He refuses to become an initiating murderer even if the other raises his hand, grounding restraint in fear of Allah, Lord of all worlds.

=28=

“‘If you stretch out your hand to kill me, I will not stretch out my hand to kill you. I fear Allah, the Lord of all worlds.’”

The threatened brother refuses to answer murderous aggression with murderous intent of his own. His statement displays exceptional restraint and unwillingness to enter the same sin. Fear of Allah governs him more strongly than fear of his brother.

The verse does not establish a general prohibition on self-defence. The Quran permits defence against aggression and commands protection of life under lawful limits. Scholars understood this statement in its particular setting—possibly that he would not initiate or intentionally murder his brother, or that he chose personal non-retaliation.

Self-defence must remain proportionate. Permission to stop an attacker is not permission for revenge after the threat ends, nor to harm uninvolved people. A person may flee, seek help, restrain, or defend according to necessity and law.

His words reveal moral clarity before violence occurs. He does not wait until rage has taken control to decide his boundary. Pre-commitment can protect believers today: decide before conflict that insults will not become assault, that a weapon will not be carried in anger, and that help will be called early.

“I fear Allah” is not cowardice. The Lord of all worlds is greater than the immediate aggressor and will judge both. This fear liberates the victim from needing to prove courage through imitation of wrong.

At the same time, victims should not be pressured to remain physically accessible to danger because of this verse. Safety planning, reporting threats, leaving, and lawful protection are compatible with taqwa. The brother’s chosen response is not a tool for abusers to disarm victims.

Verse 29 states the moral burden the aggressor is preparing to carry: the sin of killing his brother added to his own sins, leading toward the Fire as the recompense of wrongdoers.

=29=

“‘I intend to let you bear the sin of killing me along with your other sin, so you will become one of the people of the Fire. That is the reward of the wrongdoers.’”

The threatened brother is not transferring his unrelated sins to the aggressor by personal choice. He means that if the murder occurs, the killer will carry the guilt of that crime in addition to the guilt already carried—envy, threat, and the causes of his rejected offering.

Some commentators also connected the wording to the transfer of burdens between oppressor and victim on Judgment Day, described in the hadith of the bankrupt person. Yet the Quranic principle remains that no bearer carries another’s guilt arbitrarily. A wrongdoer carries what he earned through harming others.

“I intend” can express refusal to fight murder with murder: if one of them must meet Allah with blood guilt, the righteous brother will not choose it for himself. This is not delight in the other’s damnation. His earlier statement invited taqwa and this one warns before the act, when retreat is still possible.

Murder can lead to the Fire, but repentance before death remains possible according to the Quran’s wider teaching. The warning describes the unrepented path and should not be turned into a final verdict humans pronounce over every killer.

“The wrongdoers” universalises the principle. The story is not ancient family tragedy alone. Anyone willing to take innocent life enters the same moral category, regardless of ideology, honour claim, or wounded pride.

Words of consequence can sometimes interrupt violence, but a threatened person should not rely only on persuasion when practical safety is available. Scripture gives moral truth; responsible action uses protection as well.

The aggressor does not listen. Verse 30 describes his soul making the murder seem acceptable, then the killing and immediate entry into loss. Sin crosses from thought into irreversible action after being rehearsed inwardly.

=30=

“Then his soul persuaded him to kill his brother, so he killed him and became one of the losers.”

Fa-tawwa‘at lahu nafsuhu means the self gradually made the act easy, compliant, or attractive. Murder did not begin with the hand. Envy, wounded pride, threat, rationalisation, and repeated permission inside the soul prepared the final movement.

Satan tempts, but the verse highlights nafs. People cannot assign every crime to an external devil and deny their own cultivation of desire. Responsibility includes the thoughts repeatedly entertained and the safeguards deliberately removed.

Not every unwanted violent thought is intention or sin. Intrusive thoughts can distress people who do not want them. The danger lies in choosing to nurse grievance, planning harm, dehumanising the target, and refusing help until action feels justified. Mental-health support and immediate safety intervention are important where risk is real.

“So he killed him” is brief. The Quran does not turn the first murder into spectacle. Attention remains on moral cause and consequence rather than sensational method.

He “became among the losers.” He may have imagined removing a rival, but he lost brother, innocence, peace, and standing before Allah. Envy promises relief after the other’s blessing disappears; instead it enlarges the emptiness.

The verse offers no heroic identity to the murderer. Human history’s first unjust bloodshed is pathetic surrender to a diseased self. The Prophet taught that the first son of Adam bears a share of the guilt of every later unjust murder because he inaugurated the practice, without reducing the later killer’s burden.

Loss need not be the final identity of a living sinner if sincere repentance occurs, but the Quran does not record repentance here. Verse 31 records regret after a crow teaches him how to cover his brother’s body. Regret is necessary for tawbah but not always identical to it.

=31=

“Then Allah sent a crow scratching into the ground to show him how to cover his brother’s body. He cried, ‘Alas! Have I failed even to be like this crow and cover my brother’s body?’ Then he became regretful.”

The murderer knew how to take life but not how to honour the body left behind. Allah taught him through a crow, a creature acting by the instinct and guidance given to it. Human intelligence becomes humiliated when moral corruption outruns wisdom.

The Quran says the crow scratched the earth; it does not explicitly state that two crows fought or that one buried another, though such details appear in commentary. The certain point is that the bird demonstrated covering the corpse.

Burial honours the deceased, protects the living, and acknowledges bodily dignity. The victim does not lose that dignity because a murderer treated him as disposable. Islamic funeral law continues this respect through washing, shrouding, prayer, and burial according to capacity.

“Woe to me” expresses shock at his own degradation. Comparison with the crow breaks the self-image that had allowed murder. A person may feel powerful while committing harm and discover afterward that the act reduced him beneath basic mercy.

He became nadim, regretful. The verse does not specify clearly whether this regret matured into accepted repentance or remained grief over consequence and humiliation. Believers should not claim certainty beyond the text. True tawbah would require remorse for disobeying Allah, abandonment, and a changed direction.

Regret after irreversible harm cannot restore the victim in this life. That fact makes prevention urgent. Apology is valuable, but some choices leave consequences that must be carried even after forgiveness is sought.

Allah’s use of a crow also teaches humility toward knowledge gained from creation. Animals can display patterns through which humans learn practical wisdom. Observation becomes a form of receiving what Allah teaches.

Verse 32 derives a sweeping principle from this first murder. The Children of Israel were told that unjustly killing one soul resembles killing all humanity, while saving one resembles saving all. Yet many still exceeded limits after messengers brought clear proofs.

=32=

“Because of that, We decreed for the Children of Israel that whoever kills a soul—unless in lawful retribution for murder or for grave corruption in the land—it is as though they killed all humanity; and whoever saves a soul, it is as though they saved all humanity. Our messengers certainly came to them with clear proofs, yet many of them still committed excesses in the land.”

“Because of that” links law to the first murder. One act revealed how contempt for a single life threatens the moral security of every life. The decree was explicitly written for the Children of Israel, while the Quran’s retelling and Islamic law affirm the sanctity as guidance for Muslims.

The verse says nafsan—a soul—without limiting its moral weight by race or wealth. Human life is sacred. Killing one innocent person attacks the principle protecting everyone, while saving one honours that universal principle.

The exceptions do not authorise private revenge. Retribution for murder and punishment for grave corruption belong to lawful public authority, evidence, due process, and strict conditions. A family cannot execute a suspect, and a political group cannot define opponents as “corruption” and kill them.

Saving life includes rescue, medicine, food during famine, preventing suicide, freeing someone from lethal danger, and defending a wrongly accused person. It can also mean choosing not to kill when one has power. The comparison with all humanity magnifies value; it does not mean one rescue legally equals every possible duty.

“Mischief in the land” is not ordinary dissent or private sin. The following verse addresses hirabah—violent public aggression such as organised armed robbery, murder, and terror that destroys communal safety. Expansive use of the phrase by rulers against critics contradicts the safeguards surrounding life.

Messengers brought clear proof, yet law alone did not prevent transgression. Hearts, institutions, and enforcement all matter. A society can display sacred-life slogans while excusing bloodshed by its own side.

“Many,” not all, transgressed. Again the Quran avoids collective blame. Muslims must do the same when learning from earlier communities.

Verse 33 now gives severe penalties for those who wage violent war against society and spread grave corruption, protecting the very lives whose worth verse 32 established.

=33=

“The penalty for those who wage war against Allah and His Messenger and strive to spread grave corruption in the land is that they be executed, crucified, have their hands and feet cut off on opposite sides, or be expelled from the land. That is disgrace for them in this world, and in the Hereafter they will have a tremendous punishment—”

This is the law of hirabah, directed at armed public violence that terrorises society—classically including highway robbery, murder, violent seizure, and attacks that destroy public security. “War against Allah and His Messenger” describes assault on the order and lives their law protects, not the possibility of physically fighting the Creator.

The four penalties correspond to gravity according to a major juristic interpretation: killing, killing with robbery, armed robbery without killing, and terror short of those outcomes. Other jurists treated the options as judicial discretion within strict law. In either approach, a trained court determines facts and penalty; individuals do not choose.

Crucifixion and cross-amputation are severe and belong only to a valid judiciary after demanding proof. They cannot be performed by families, militias, extremist groups, or officials bypassing trial. Confession must be valid, evidence reliable, and doubts and competing classifications examined.

The verse is not a tool against peaceful political opposition, religious disagreement, protest, or ordinary theft. Governments that label criticism “war against God” to silence citizens turn law meant to stop terror into state terror. Zulm remains forbidden even when legal vocabulary is invoked.

The Hereafter’s tremendous punishment applies to those who persist without repentance and deserve it before Allah. A worldly sentence does not give humans knowledge of the final heart. Nor may officials humiliate beyond what law permits; “disgrace” describes the ordained consequence, not permission for torture.

Why such severity? Hirabah makes roads, homes, commerce, and ordinary life unsafe. It attacks many people through fear even when only a few are physically injured. Verse 32’s elevation of life is matched by strong protection of public safety.

The sentence continues with an exception in verse 34. Those who repent before authorities gain power over them escape the public hirabah penalty, though private rights of victims remain to be resolved.

=34=

“—except those who repent before you gain power over them. Then know that Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

The exception creates an incentive to abandon violent rebellion voluntarily. Before capture, the offender can lay down arms, stop terror, and return. Society may end danger without more bloodshed, showing that the purpose is protection and reform rather than vengeance.

Repentance must be genuine and timely. Surrender only after being overpowered does not automatically remove the prescribed public consequence, though repentance can still benefit the person before Allah. Courts must investigate rather than infer sincerity carelessly.

The hirabah penalty owed to public order is lifted according to the verse’s rule, but private rights do not simply disappear. Stolen property must be returned, injuries compensated, and murder claims addressed by victims’ families and the applicable law. Allah’s forgiveness does not turn another person’s right into nothing.

Legal schools discuss details about which claims remain and how surrender is established. This is another reason the verse cannot be administered by private actors. A qualified judiciary must separate Allah’s right, public right, and individual claims.

Al-Ghafur and al-Rahim stand immediately after one of the Quran’s sternest penalties. No criminal identity is beyond spiritual return while life remains. A person can move from terrorising others to sincerely serving safety, though trust and worldly consequences may require time.

Mercy toward the repentant also protects potential victims. A system offering no route out may encourage fugitives to continue violence. Revelation opens a principled exit while retaining accountability for harm.

Verse 35 addresses believers with the positive road: taqwa, seeking a lawful means of nearness to Allah, and striving in His path. Security from sin is not achieved through penalties alone; hearts must actively seek their Lord.

=35=

“O believers, be mindful of Allah, seek the means of nearness to Him, and strive in His cause so that you may succeed.”

Taqwa is the foundation: awareness of Allah that guards His limits. The previous laws need inner conscience as well as courts. A person can avoid conviction while remaining guilty before the One who knows.

Wasilah is whatever lawful means brings a servant closer to Allah: faith, obligatory worship, repentance, du‘a, Quran, charity, service, and voluntary deeds. The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught that Allah says a servant draws nearest through obligations, then continues through voluntary works until Allah loves him.

The verse commands seeking a means, not worshipping the means. Du‘a as worship is directed to Allah. Scholars have discussed forms of tawassul, but no created being becomes an independent source of forgiveness, provision, or unseen control. The safest universally agreed means are Allah’s names, one’s faith and good deeds, and the prayer of a living righteous person.

Jahidu fi sabilih means exert sustained effort in Allah’s cause. It includes striving through self, wealth, speech, patience, learning, and—under its lawful conditions—armed defence by legitimate authority. The immediate surah includes public security, but the command should neither be reduced only to combat nor detached from its serious martial use.

Striving is “His cause” only when goal and means obey Him. Terror, compulsion, theft, and killing innocents cannot become jihad through branding. The hirabah verses directly condemn violent corruption.

Falah is complete success: moral growth, beneficial worldly outcome as Allah wills, and eternal salvation. It is not guaranteed by intensity without guidance. Taqwa directs, wasilah draws near, and striving proves commitment.

The verse asks believers to be active without imagining action purchases Allah. Nearness and success remain mercy. Verse 36 shows wealth’s total inability to purchase rescue after disbelief reaches final judgment: even twice the earth’s riches would not be accepted as ransom.

=36=

“Surely if the disbelievers possessed everything on earth and twice as much with it, and offered it to ransom themselves from the punishment of the Day of Resurrection, it would never be accepted from them. They will have a painful punishment.”

The image gives a person the whole earth’s wealth, then doubles it. Land, metals, energy, businesses, art, and every stored treasure still fail as ransom. The Hereafter does not operate through the economic power that often bends worldly institutions.

Ransom is offered only after the truth can no longer be denied. During life, a small sincere charity joined to faith may carry immense reward. After death upon rejection, an impossible fortune cannot replace the faith and repentance that were freely available.

The verse does not teach that wealth is evil. Lawful wealth can feed, build, and draw near to Allah. It becomes delusion when treated as independent security from judgment. Qarun’s keys and Pharaoh’s kingdom could not protect them.

“It would never be accepted” reflects not divine need for a higher payment but the wrong currency and time. Allah is Self-Sufficient and does not sell acquittal. Judgment concerns truth, worship, and deeds.

Disbelievers are named as a final category here; Muslims should not decide casually that a particular living or deceased person possessed full proof and died upon rejection. General doctrine is clear, while individual knowledge belongs to Allah.

The painful punishment should make invitation sincere, not contemptuous. A believer who hears it should fear for his own end and hope rescue for others while the door remains open.

Verse 37 adds that they will long to leave the Fire but will not leave it, and their punishment will remain. What worldly wealth could not prevent, later desire cannot reverse.

=37=

“They will wish to leave the Fire, but they will never leave it. They will have an enduring punishment.”

The desire for escape proves full awareness of loss. In life, warnings could be dismissed as distant; inside the Fire, reality is immediate. Yet the time of morally free response has ended.

Kharijina suggests repeated longing to come out, while the negation is absolute for those described. Muqim is established and lasting. The punishment does not fade because its inhabitants discover consequences after seeing them.

Other verses describe them asking for death, return to earth, or one more chance. Each request is denied because earthly life already supplied the arena of choice. Knowledge forced by direct sight is not the same as faith in the unseen.

The Quran’s eternal warning should not be softened into metaphor merely because it is difficult. At the same time, humans should not speculate beyond revelation or take emotional pleasure in imagined suffering. The Prophet warned with grief and urgency.

No believer is authorised to imitate this confinement through torture or indefinite cruelty. Earthly punishment is limited by law, evidence, proportionality, and rights. Hell belongs to the judgment of the One with total knowledge.

Fear becomes healthy when it produces tawbah, not obsessive despair. A living person is not inside verse 37’s closed condition. Allah’s mercy remains accessible through sincere faith and return.

After describing wealth that cannot ransom, the surah returns to property protected in earthly law. Verse 38 prescribes the fixed punishment for qualified theft by a man or woman, a ruling surrounded by stringent conditions and followed immediately by the possibility of repentance and reform.

=38=

“As for the male thief and the female thief, cut off their hands as a recompense for what they earned and a deterrent from Allah. And Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.”

The verse establishes a hadd for sariqah, legally defined theft, not every taking called “stealing” in ordinary speech. Classical law requires property reaching a minimum value, taken secretly from recognised secure custody, owned by another, with criminal intent and no plausible ownership claim or necessity. Evidentiary standards and legal capacity also apply.

The Sunnah specifies the right hand at the wrist for a first qualifying offence. Because the penalty is irreversible, doubts about ownership, value, custody, coercion, hunger, mental capacity, and proof must be examined strictly. The established juristic principle of averting fixed punishments where legally significant doubt remains shapes application.

A hungry person taking essential food in a social crisis is not treated like a professional thief. Umar’s non-application during famine is commonly cited as recognition that the legal conditions and social responsibility were not met, not as cancellation of the verse. Authorities must address poverty and withheld wages rather than punish victims of institutional theft.

The ruling belongs only to a legitimate court. Employers, families, vigilantes, armed groups, and private religious bodies cannot amputate anyone. Confession obtained by torture is invalid wrongdoing, and mob certainty is not due process.

Theft by force, armed robbery, embezzlement, breach of trust, and minor unlawful taking may fall under different legal categories and discretionary penalties. Precision prevents the hadd from expanding beyond its revealed offence.

Nakal is a deterrent and exemplary consequence protecting property security. Al-‘Aziz has authority to legislate; al-Hakim knows the balance of individual and society. Invoking wisdom does not release officials from the detailed mercy and justice by which the law is administered.

Modern states debating or applying hudud must consider lawful authority, full procedural safeguards, social conditions, and the complete legal tradition. Quoting the penalty while ignoring its standards is not fidelity.

Verse 39 opens repentance: whoever turns back after wrongdoing and reforms will find Allah turning toward him in mercy. Spiritual redemption remains possible even where human rights or a duly established worldly sentence still require resolution.

=39=

“But whoever repents after wrongdoing and reforms, Allah will surely turn to them in forgiveness. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

Tawbah is joined to islah. Regret without changed conduct does not restore the social trust theft damaged. Reform includes stopping, returning property or its value, seeking lawful settlement, earning honestly, and removing the habits that supported the crime.

Allah “turns” toward the servant who turns back. The wording gives hope after a severe penalty: a person is not permanently defined before Allah as “the thief.” Genuine repentance can rebuild a truthful identity.

Private rights remain. If property can be returned without creating greater injustice, it must be. When direct disclosure would cause complex harm, qualified counsel can help restore value appropriately. Forgiveness from Allah is not ownership of the victim’s claim.

Jurists distinguish repentance before a theft is reported and proven from repentance after the case reaches authority. The verse clearly promises divine forgiveness and reform; whether an established hadd is waived depends on procedural stage and legal doctrine. No offender or official should invent a convenient rule.

Where a court sentence remains, undergoing it does not mean repentance failed. Worldly accountability and divine forgiveness can coexist. Conversely, escaping prosecution does not prove Allah has forgiven.

Al-Ghafur covers and removes guilt; al-Rahim grants care beyond acquittal. Communities should permit rehabilitated people to work and live rather than pushing them back toward crime through permanent exclusion, while proportionate safeguards may protect others.

The verse’s sequence mirrors hirabah: public safety receives firm protection, then the sinner receives a path back. Justice without repentance can become hopeless punishment; talk of mercy without rights can abandon victims. Revelation holds both.

Verse 40 ends the section with sovereignty. Heaven and earth belong to Allah; He punishes and forgives according to His will, and He is capable of all things. The Judge who legislates is also the Lord to whom the penitent returns.

=40=

“Do you not know that the kingdom of the heavens and earth belongs to Allah? He punishes whom He wills and forgives whom He wills, and Allah is capable of all things.”

The rhetorical question returns every law to ownership. Allah is not an outsider regulating a world belonging to someone else. The body, property, society, time, and final court all exist within His dominion.

Sovereignty does not mean arbitrary will. The Quran repeatedly joins Allah’s will to knowledge, wisdom, justice, promise, and mercy. He has told people that shirk at death is not forgiven, that sincere repentance is accepted, and that no soul is wronged. No external power compels Him, but He never contradicts His perfection.

“He punishes whom He wills and forgives whom He wills” prevents both entitlement and despair. A criminal cannot demand forgiveness while keeping stolen rights, and a repentant sinner cannot be told that Allah lacks power to pardon. The proper posture is fear, hope, and reform.

Divine ownership does not authorise human rulers to act as unlimited sovereigns. Their authority is delegated, bounded by evidence and law, and answerable to Allah. They cannot borrow this verse to justify arbitrary punishment. Human judgment must remain humble precisely because Allah’s is perfect.

Capability includes enforcing justice no earthly court reached, forgiving hidden repentance no community recognised, restoring victims, and resurrecting every body. No case is too complex for the final Judge.

The legal section therefore ends not in fascination with penalties but knowledge of Allah. Hudud protect society, repentance repairs the servant, and sovereignty ensures both remain under one Wise Lord.
