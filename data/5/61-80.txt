=61=

“When they come to you, they say, ‘We believe,’ although they entered with disbelief and left with it. Allah knows best what they were concealing.”

The verse describes people whose visit to the Prophet, peace be upon him, changes only their outward language. They arrive carrying rejection, speak the words expected in a believing gathering, and depart in the same inner state. Physical closeness to revelation does not benefit a heart committed to performance.

The context follows particular hypocritical figures and opponents in Madinah. It is not a declaration that every Jewish visitor or every person of uncertain faith acted this way. The Quran itself distinguishes upright and corrupt people within every community.

Their statement “we believe” sought social trust without the covenant of belief. Such profession can secure access, information, reputation, or protection. Hypocrisy turns sacred words into credentials for worldly use.

Allah knows what they hide. The believers may judge outwardly and use reasonable safeguards, but they do not possess the unseen. Revelation exposed particular cases to the Prophet; later Muslims may not declare hidden disbelief merely because someone leaves a gathering unimpressed or struggles with faith.

The verse invites self-examination rather than suspicion. Does a person enter Friday prayer resentful of a command and leave determined to evade it, while speaking religious language socially? The legal category may differ, but the heart should fear becoming unchanged by repeated reminders.

A gathering is not automatically transformative. Benefit requires listening, humility, and intention to act. One can sit near scholars, in a mosque, or beside the Quran and remain distant inwardly. Conversely, a sincere questioner may leave with unresolved doubts while still moving toward truth.

The phrase “Allah knows best” comforts the Messenger. Deception cannot alter the real account. It also prevents believers from exhausting themselves trying to expose every heart; transparent procedures and judgment of conduct are enough.

Verse 62 points to visible evidence of inward disease: many race toward sin, aggression, and consumption of forbidden gain. Their movement is eager rather than reluctant, and Allah calls what they do evil.

=62=

“You see many of them racing toward sin, aggression, and consumption of unlawful gain. Evil indeed is what they have been doing.”

Yusari‘un suggests speed and competition. These people do not merely slip and regret; they hurry toward opportunities for wrong. The Quran elsewhere tells believers to race toward forgiveness and good deeds. Direction, not energy alone, determines moral worth.

Ithm is sin that burdens the conscience and record. ‘Udwan is crossing limits against others through aggression and injustice. Suht is forbidden gain that consumes blessing—especially bribery and money obtained by corrupt judgment in this context.

The three are connected. Unlawful money funds aggression; aggression protects the system producing money; repeated sin dulls resistance to both. Corruption is rarely one isolated transaction once institutions reward it.

The verse says “many,” not all. This precision matters in a passage concerning religious communities. Condemning every Jew by ancestry would itself be ‘udwan and contradict the Quran’s praise of upright individuals.

Visible behaviour may indicate risk, but it does not grant access to final inner judgment. Courts and communities address proved actions. Allah alone knows whether a person’s heart contains hypocrisy, confusion, coercion, or repentance.

Modern racing toward suht appears in competitive bribery, fraudulent procurement, predatory lending, and professional cultures where refusing corruption is treated as incompetence. A believer may need records, collective action, and lawful reporting rather than isolated moral speech.

“Evil is what they do” develops a moral vocabulary that does not admire successful corruption. Wealth and influence can make vice look sophisticated; revelation judges the method and harm rather than the result displayed.

The next verse turns from sinners to those entrusted with knowledge. Why did devoted teachers and scholars not forbid sinful speech and unlawful consumption? Their silence becomes a separate evil craft.

=63=

“Why do their devoted scholars and rabbis not forbid them from sinful speech and consuming unlawful gain? Evil indeed is what those scholars have been doing.”

Knowledge creates responsibility. Rabbaniyyun and ahbar knew the scriptural rules and had influence to teach, warn, and refuse participation. Their failure allowed sin to become normal and profitable.

The verse distinguishes the people’s acts from the leaders’ manufacture. Verse 62 says evil is what people “do”; verse 63 uses a word that can suggest what leaders deliberately produce or craft. Silence by knowledgeable institutions can help build the environment in which wrongdoing thrives.

Forbidding wrong is not shouting at everyone. It requires accurate knowledge, wisdom, capacity, attention to consequence, and the proper sphere of authority. A private mistake may need private advice; a public financial system may require policy, collective scholarship, and lawful enforcement.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned that when people see an oppressor and do not restrain him despite ability, a general punishment may reach them. He also taught changing wrong according to capacity—with hand for lawful authority, tongue where one can speak, and heart when no greater action is possible.

This hierarchy does not authorise vigilantism. Private citizens cannot punish suspected sinners, invade privacy, or create a greater evil. “The hand” operates within legitimate responsibility: a parent in a proper duty, an employer over policy, or authorities under law.

Scholars can be silenced by fear, salary, access, faction, or desire to remain popular. Speaking truth may cost worldly benefit, but concealing it sells revelation for a small price. At the same time, communities should not demand instant verdicts from scholars before facts are verified.

The criticism applies to Muslim teachers before it becomes a weapon against earlier rabbis. If interest, abuse, racism, or theft becomes public and learned people manufacture excuses, the same disease is present.

Verse 64 gives an example of corrupt speech about Allah’s generosity, then exposes the hostility and war arising from persistent rejection while affirming that Allah does not love corruption.

=64=

“Some Jews say, ‘Allah’s hand is restrained.’ May their own hands be restrained, and may they be rejected for what they said! Rather, both His hands are outstretched; He gives however He wills. What has been revealed to you from your Lord will certainly increase many of them in defiance and disbelief. We have allowed hostility and hatred among them until the Day of Resurrection. Whenever they kindle a fire of war, Allah extinguishes it. They strive to spread corruption in the land, and Allah does not love corrupters.”

The claim attributed stinginess or inability to Allah, possibly in mockery of the command to “lend Allah a good loan” or complaint about restricted provision. It came from some speakers; it is not a Jewish creed held by every Jew. The response mirrors their insult: hands that accuse the Most Generous of restraint deserve restraint themselves.

“Both His hands are outstretched” affirms immense generosity in Quranic language and, within Sunni theology, an attribute of Allah in a manner befitting Him without imagining created limbs or asking how. Nothing resembles Him (42:11). The phrase certainly rejects miserliness: His giving is unrestricted by shortage.

“He gives as He wills” explains why different provision does not indicate inability. Wealth and scarcity are tests arranged with wisdom. A person may receive less because of consequence, protection, or a wisdom unseen; no creature has a claim that exhausts the divine treasury.

Revelation increases sincere people in guidance, but proud rejection can increase defiance because every new clarification becomes another opportunity to resist. The Quran causes no injustice; the heart’s response determines the added distance.

Hostility and hatred describe consequences among factions that abandoned unifying truth. “Whenever they kindle war” refers to repeated hostile schemes in the prophetic context and beyond, not a conspiracy by every Jewish community through all time. Allah’s extinguishing assures the Messenger that no plot can cancel his mission.

The verse condemns fasad—violence, manipulation, covenant-breaking, and social ruin. Muslims cannot answer alleged corruption with their own corruption. Allah’s love is the standard, and He does not love it from any community.

The next verse reopens hope explicitly. If the People of the Book believed and became mindful of Allah, their sins would be erased and they would enter Gardens of Bliss. Race does not predetermine destiny; response does.

=65=

“If only the People of the Scripture had believed and become mindful of Allah, We would certainly have erased their sins and admitted them into Gardens of Bliss.”

After severe criticism, the invitation remains open. The verse does not describe a people cursed by blood or permanently unable to return. Iman and taqwa can transform the entire account.

After Muhammad’s coming, complete faith includes accepting the Messenger and Quran once their truth is established, because belief in Allah includes receiving whom He sends. Taqwa then turns conviction into guarded conduct.

Nukaffir ‘anhum sayyi’atihim means sins are covered and erased. The past can include distortion, unlawful wealth, blasphemous speech, or hostility; sincere faith and repentance are greater than the record. Where human rights were taken, repentance includes returning them.

Gardens of Bliss are more than escape from punishment. Na‘im is complete, enduring delight without anxiety or loss. Allah does not merely tolerate the repentant outsider; He invites him into full reward.

The conditional “if only” also expresses the tragedy of refused mercy. Warning is not divine delight in punishment. It shows the door people are choosing not to enter.

Muslims should carry this tone in da‘wah. Clear theological disagreement belongs beside real hope for every listener. Ethnic contempt and assumptions that another person can never believe contradict the verse’s invitation.

The same principle applies inwardly. A Muslim family name does not replace iman and taqwa, while someone with a deeply troubled past can become beloved through return. Allah judges endings and realities.

Verse 66 adds a worldly image of blessing. Had they truly upheld the Torah, Gospel, and what their Lord revealed, provision would have come from above and below. Yet the verse carefully says an upright community exists among them, while many act badly.

=66=

“Had they truly upheld the Torah, the Gospel, and what has been revealed to them from their Lord, they would have enjoyed provision from above them and beneath their feet. Among them is an upright community, but evil indeed is what many of them do.”

Iqamah of scripture means establishing it as living guidance, not merely preserving a text, reciting it ceremonially, or using it as group identity. Genuine adherence to original Torah and Gospel would lead toward tawhid, justice, and recognition of later revelation from the same Lord.

“What was revealed from their Lord” has been understood to include all relevant divine revelation, culminating in the Quran. One cannot claim to uphold an earlier Book by rejecting the authority that sent its confirming criterion.

Provision from above and below evokes well-timed rain, fertile earth, security, and broad abundance. Obedient social life also creates material causes: honest markets, protected property, charity, and reduced corruption. Spiritual and practical blessing meet.

This is not a simplistic prosperity doctrine. Prophets and faithful communities can suffer poverty and persecution, while disbelievers can be wealthy as a test. The verse states a covenantal blessing for the addressed communities, not a formula by which every rich person is righteous and every poor person guilty.

“Among them is an upright community” again forbids blanket condemnation. Ummah muqtasidah is a balanced, moderate group not carried into the excesses described. Some recognised truth, practised justice, and later believed in Muhammad.

“Many” nevertheless acted badly. Fairness neither hides widespread corruption nor turns it into an inherited racial essence. Both truths must be held: a serious majority problem in the addressed setting and genuine upright exceptions.

Muslims should apply the criterion to themselves. Establishing Quran means prayer, economics, family justice, and truthful leadership. Decorative verses in a corrupt office are not iqamah.

Verse 67 commands the Messenger to deliver everything revealed, without fear-driven omission, and promises Allah’s protection from people so the mission can be completed.

=67=

“O Messenger, convey everything revealed to you from your Lord. If you do not, then you have not conveyed His message. Allah will protect you from the people. Surely Allah does not guide the disbelieving people.”

Balligh demands complete delivery. The Prophet cannot hide a ruling because opponents may mock, allies may leave, or powerful people may threaten. A message selectively edited around audience desire is no longer faithfully delivered.

“If you do not” emphasises the unity of the trust; it does not suggest Muhammad actually concealed revelation. Aishah, may Allah be pleased with her, said anyone claiming he hid part of what was revealed had lied, and she recited this verse.

Allah’s protection ensured people could not kill the Prophet before he completed the mission. Reports say he had been guarded and, after this assurance, told guards they could withdraw. Protection did not mean he would never be wounded, poisoned, insulted, or tested; his life contains all of those. It meant hostile people could not extinguish the entrusted message.

Later teachers do not receive an identical personal guarantee. They should take lawful safety measures and need not disclose sensitive information recklessly. Their duty is to convey what they know accurately, without claiming prophetic protection or exposing others needlessly.

Complete delivery includes mercy, context, concessions, and conditions alongside prohibitions. Quoting only the harshest line is also concealment of the message’s balance. A teacher should distinguish certainty, disagreement, and personal opinion.

The closing reminds the Prophet that guidance is Allah’s. Failure to accept after clear conveyance does not prove he should distort the message. Nor may callers use “Allah does not guide them” to stop inviting living people; many former opponents later believed.

Verse 68 now tells the People of the Book they have no sound religious footing unless they uphold their scriptures and what their Lord has revealed. The Prophet is again told not to be consumed by those whose defiance increases when revelation comes.

=68=

“Say, ‘O People of the Scripture, you have no sound foundation unless you uphold the Torah, the Gospel, and what has been revealed to you from your Lord.’ What has been revealed to you from your Lord will certainly increase many of them in defiance and disbelief, so do not grieve over the disbelieving people.”

Lastum ‘ala shay’ means their claim has nothing stable to stand upon while scripture is selectively ignored. Religious identity without obedience becomes a building without foundation.

Upholding Torah and Gospel means following their original monotheism, morals, and prophetic direction. After the Quran arrives as confirming guardian, faithful response to the same Lord includes accepting it. This does not require pretending every current textual form is entirely false; it places the final criterion over what remains.

Revelation increases many opponents in defiance because truth confronts interests they have chosen to protect. The sun does not wrong an eye that a person insists on closing. Every clarification can soften humility or harden pride.

“Many” again leaves room for those who respond differently. Abdullah ibn Salam and others did not increase in disbelief; they recognised continuity. The Prophet’s grief came from seeing what the invitation could have given those who refused.

“Do not grieve” sets a boundary, not a ban on compassion. He should continue to convey, pray, and deal justly while refusing despair and self-blame for choices outside his control.

Muslims should fear the same loss of foundation. Claiming Quran while normalising interest, abuse, or corrupt judgment creates an identity unsupported by practice. The verse is a mirror before it is a polemic.

Verse 69 then states a broad principle involving Muslims, Jews, Sabians, and Christians: whoever truly believes in Allah and the Last Day and acts righteously will have no fear or grief. Its meaning must be held with the Quran’s demand to believe all messengers.

=69=

“Indeed, the believers, the Jews, the Sabians, and the Christians—whoever truly believes in Allah and the Last Day and does righteous deeds—will have no fear, nor will they grieve.”

The verse removes salvation from ethnic ownership. Before Muhammad’s mission, a sincere follower of Musa in his time or Isa in his time who worshipped Allah and acted righteously followed valid revelation. Labels alone never guaranteed acceptance.

After the final Messenger’s truth reaches a person recognisably, genuine faith in Allah includes believing the messenger Allah sent. The Quran elsewhere says believers make no rejecting distinction among messengers (4:150-152) and that a religion other than submission to Allah as finally revealed is not accepted (3:85).

This does not authorise humans to pronounce confidently on every individual outside Islam. Allah knows whether the message arrived, whether it was grossly distorted, a person’s capacity, and the actual heart at death. The verse affirms perfect divine justice without making all contradictory creeds equally true.

The Sabians are an uncertain historical community, described variously as monotheistic seekers or a scriptural group between familiar communities. Their inclusion reinforces that Allah’s judgment is not confined to famous labels.

A nearly identical promise appears in 2:62, showing that the principle is deliberate rather than incidental. Neither verse teaches salvation through inherited names; both move immediately from labels to real faith and righteous work. The repeated formula also assures converts that leaving an old communal identity for fuller obedience does not erase sincere good that preceded guidance.

Faith in the Last Day turns belief into accountability; righteous action demonstrates sincerity. A verbal identity without either condition receives no promise. Conversely, someone overlooked by society is not overlooked by Allah.

“No fear” concerns what lies ahead at judgment, and “no grief” what was left behind or lost. Complete security comes from acceptance by the Lord, not from winning a communal argument.

Muslims should present this verse without two distortions: pluralism claiming all doctrines are equally valid regardless of the final Messenger, and arrogant blanket damnation ignoring Allah’s knowledge of access and circumstance. Truth and justice belong together.

Verse 70 returns to the Children of Israel’s covenant. Messengers repeatedly came, but when a message contradicted desire, some were denied and others killed.

=70=

“We certainly took a covenant from the Children of Israel and sent them messengers. Whenever a messenger came to them with something their souls did not desire, they denied some and killed others.”

Covenant and messengers are repeated favours. Allah did not leave the community with only inherited memory; He renewed guidance through living teachers who challenged drift and corruption.

The criterion of rejection was hawa—what souls did not want. Instead of measuring desire by revelation, they measured revelation by desire. Some messengers were called liars; others faced murder. Rejection escalated when truth threatened power.

The verse uses historical community language, but guilt belongs to perpetrators and those who approved their path. “Some” messengers and implied factions prevent the claim that every Israelite killed prophets. No living Jew inherits murder guilt biologically.

Tradition identifies prophets who were killed, but the Quran need not provide names here. Its point is the recurring pattern. Isa was targeted but, according to Islamic belief, Allah raised him and they did not kill him.

Muslims can repeat the moral structure without killing a prophet physically. Accepting hadith only when convenient, silencing scholars who challenge donors or rulers, and attacking reformers’ reputations because evidence hurts desire all echo the first steps.

Not every disagreement with a preacher is rejection of a messenger. Later scholars are fallible and their claims require evidence. The verse concerns confirmed prophets, so no modern leader may demand prophetic obedience.

The covenant asks a timeless question: when truth conflicts with interest, which becomes judge? Verse 71 says the offenders imagined no trial would follow, became blind and deaf, received an opportunity to repent, and then many relapsed while Allah saw everything.

=71=

“They assumed there would be no trial or punishment, so they became blind and deaf. Then Allah turned to them in mercy, yet afterward many again became blind and deaf. And Allah sees all that they do.”

Fitnah can mean trial, consequence, or punishment. They felt secure from accountability despite rejecting and attacking messengers. Presumed chosenness became immunity in their imagination.

Blindness and deafness are moral metaphors. Eyes saw signs and ears heard warning, but the heart refused to use them. Physical disability carries no blame; the Quran criticises chosen non-response, not blind or Deaf people.

“Then Allah turned to them” reveals another opening of tawbah. Even after grave communal wrong, repentance and reform were accepted. Divine mercy interrupts cycles humans might consider unforgivable.

Many then relapsed. Forgiveness was treated as proof that consequence would never come, rather than a reason for gratitude. A forgiven person remains in need of vigilance; past tawbah does not make future choices harmless.

The verse says “many,” preserving the minority who remained faithful. It also avoids simplistic history in which one repentance or relapse defines every later generation.

Allah is Basir. He sees repentance’s sincerity, relapse, hidden resistance, and the people who quietly remained upright. Historical narratives can be manipulated, but His record is complete.

Believers should never feel safe from Allah’s judgment while persisting in sin, nor despair after a relapse. The right response is immediate renewed tawbah and stronger safeguards. Repetition makes sin dangerous but does not close mercy before death.

Verses 72-73 now address Christian doctrines directly. Isa himself called the Children of Israel to worship Allah, “my Lord and your Lord.” Making him divine or placing Allah within a trinity contradicts the Messiah’s message and risks exclusion from Paradise if maintained without repentance.

=72=

“Those who say, ‘Allah is the Messiah, son of Maryam,’ have certainly disbelieved. The Messiah himself said, ‘O Children of Israel, worship Allah—my Lord and your Lord.’ Whoever associates partners with Allah, Allah has forbidden Paradise to them, and their home is the Fire. The wrongdoers will have no helpers.”

The Quran answers deification through Isa’s own teaching. He has a Lord and directs worship to that Lord. The worshipper cannot be the God he worships; the messenger cannot be the Sender.

“Son of Maryam” grounds him in honoured human birth. His miracles do not create independent deity. He healed and raised the dead bi-idhn Allah—by Allah’s permission—and remained dependent upon the One whose permission made every sign occur.

Shirk gives worship or divine attributes to a created being. If a person dies upon it after accountable rejection, Paradise is forbidden as stated. During life, repentance remains open; verse 74 will invite precisely these speakers to seek forgiveness.

“No helpers” denies any being the ability to overturn Allah’s judgment independently. Isa will not support people who worshipped him against what he taught. In 5:116-117 he disowns the claim that he told people to take him and his mother as gods.

The verse is theological, not a warrant for hostility toward Christians. Their persons, property, worship places under covenant, and neighbourly rights remain protected by justice and law. Muslims can state that a doctrine is disbelief without abusing its adherents.

Individual final judgment still belongs to Allah, who knows what a person understood and the state at death. The Quran gives the doctrinal category and urgent call to tawhid.

Muslims should also resist exaggeration toward Muhammad or saints. The Prophet forbade being praised as Christians praised the son of Maryam and insisted on the titles servant and messenger.

Verse 73 addresses the trinitarian formula: those saying Allah is one of three have disbelieved, for there is only one God. Yet the warning itself says “if they do not stop,” leaving the path of return visible.

=73=

“Those who say, ‘Allah is one of three,’ have certainly disbelieved. There is no deity except the One God. If they do not stop saying this, a painful punishment will afflict those among them who persist in disbelief.”

The Quran refuses a divided or tri-personal deity. Whatever philosophical distinctions are offered between “being” and “persons,” Islamic tawhid affirms one undivided God who does not share deity with Son or Spirit.

Christians generally reject the description that they worship three separate gods and use the Trinity to affirm one God. Fair dialogue should represent that claim accurately. The Quran nevertheless judges the formula incompatible with the oneness revealed by every prophet.

“There is no deity except one God” gives the correction in the simplest language. Allah’s attributes are perfect and many, but they are not separate divine persons. His word and spirit do not become coequal beings.

The phrase “if they do not stop” is an invitation inside a warning. A person can leave inherited theology, affirm tawhid, and be forgiven. “Those among them who disbelieve” also avoids converting a communal label into an automatic verdict without regard to response.

Painful punishment is not permission for Muslims to cause pain. Earthly relations remain governed by covenant and justice; final theological consequence belongs to Allah.

A Muslim should hear the warning against subtle division of ultimate loyalty. Wealth, ego, leaders, or spiritual intermediaries can receive functional devotion that belongs to Allah even where the tongue says one.

Verse 74 expresses astonishment that people would not repent and seek forgiveness when Allah is Forgiving and Merciful. The strongest doctrinal correction is followed immediately by open mercy.

=74=

“Will they not turn to Allah in repentance and seek His forgiveness? Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

The question is tender and urgent. After naming shirk and punishment, Allah does not say the people are permanently beyond hope. He asks why they will not return.

Tawbah is a change of direction: leaving worship of creation, affirming Allah’s oneness, believing His messengers, and correcting life. Istighfar asks Him to cover and erase what came before. Conversion is not merely a new social label; it is sincere surrender.

Even deifying Isa or confessing a trinity can be forgiven before death. The principle from 4:48 that shirk is not forgiven concerns dying upon it without repentance. Many companions had once worshipped idols and became among the best believers.

Al-Ghafur forgives vast and repeated sins; al-Rahim grants care after forgiveness. The repentant is not kept forever at the door as a tolerated outsider. Allah can turn past error into a path of humility and service.

The verse models da‘wah: identify the belief clearly, explain consequence, then invite warmly. Condemnation without a door can feed despair; invitation without truth leaves danger unnamed.

Muslims should not pressure or manipulate conversion. Faith must be chosen with understanding. Their task is clear conveyance, good character, prayer, and practical welcome for those who return.

The same question addresses every sinner. Why remain in what Allah offers to forgive? Shame often claims return is hypocritical after repetition; the divine names answer that lie.

Verse 75 reinforces Isa’s created humanity. He is a messenger like messengers before him, Maryam is a woman of complete truth, and both ate food—an ordinary sign of bodily dependence no God possesses.

=75=

“The Messiah, son of Maryam, was only a messenger; other messengers passed away before him. His mother was a woman of complete truth. They both ate food. See how We make the signs clear to them, then see how they are turned away.”

“Innama”—only—confines Isa’s category here to honoured messengership, not deity. Other messengers came before, carried revelation, and passed through human life. His miracle birth does not change his Creator-creature relationship.

Maryam is siddiqah, intensely truthful and faithful. This is among the Quran’s highest praises for her and answers those who slandered her. Yet immense sainthood is still servanthood; honour does not become divinity.

“They both ate food” is a simple argument from dependence. Eating requires hunger, digestion, provision, and bodily processes. Allah is al-Ghani and al-Qayyum, needing nothing and sustaining everything. A being dependent on food cannot be the self-sufficient Creator.

The verse does not belittle bodies or eating. Prophets ate and walked in markets, making human life a place of worship. It denies that created limitations belong to God.

“Messengers passed before him” does not by itself state Isa died, because the Quran has already said Allah raised him. It places him within the prophetic class whose members are created and whose earthly missions have limits. Authentic hadiths teach his future return and eventual death.

Signs can be intellectually clear while emotional tradition resists them. “How they are turned away” expresses amazement at the distance between evidence and conclusion. Clarity should make callers patient, since identity and inherited love can make revision painful.

The argument should be delivered respectfully, not as mockery of Christian sacrament or practice. The Quran’s tone is firm reasoning about dependency.

Verse 76 asks why anyone worships beside Allah what possesses no independent power to harm or benefit, while Allah alone hears and knows completely.

=76=

“Say, ‘Do you worship besides Allah those who possess no power to harm or benefit you, while Allah alone is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing?’”

Worship directs ultimate love, fear, hope, supplication, and surrender. It belongs only to the One with independent power and complete knowledge. Every creature’s ability is received, limited, and subject to Allah.

Isa brought benefit through teaching and miracles, but always by Allah’s permission. Prophets can pray, advise, and intercede when Allah permits; none controls forgiveness, provision, or destiny independently. Honouring a means must not turn the means into Lord.

The question includes idols and any created object of worship. A statue cannot hear, and a living or departed servant cannot become omniscient. Disputed questions about what the dead may hear in limited circumstances do not change the decisive point: they possess no independent divine control and are not worshipped.

Allah is al-Sami‘, hearing every language, whisper, and simultaneous prayer. He is al-‘Alim, knowing the need before it is spoken and the wisdom of response. No intermediary is required because He is distant or uninformed.

This does not make asking ordinary help from people shirk. Asking a doctor for treatment, a friend for assistance, or a living person for du‘a uses capacities Allah created. Worship begins where divine dependence or power is assigned to the creature.

The verse also challenges functional idols. People may not bow to wealth or political power, yet treat them as if they independently control safety. Tawhid returns means to their proper scale.

Verse 77 traces theological deviation to ghuluw—exceeding truth in religion—and following desires of earlier people who strayed and led many away from the level path.

=77=

“Say, ‘O People of the Scripture, do not go to extremes in your religion beyond the truth, and do not follow the desires of people who went astray before, misled many, and wandered from the level way.’”

Ghuluw is exceeding the boundary in praise, blame, law, or practice. Christians raised Isa beyond messengership; opponents among the Children of Israel degraded and rejected him. Truth honours him without worship and follows him without slander.

The verse attributes later error to ahwa’—desires—not merely innocent reasoning. Pride, inherited identity, political authority, and fascination with mystery can shape doctrines while appearing devotional. Religious intensity is not proof of revelation.

The Quran does not require Muslims to reconstruct every stage of Christian doctrinal history in order to obey this warning. Complex historical developments should be studied accurately, without simplistic claims that one foreign philosophy alone invented everything. The revealed criterion is enough to judge the final doctrine.

Earlier leaders misled many, so teaching carries multiplied responsibility. Followers remain accountable according to knowledge and capacity, while authorities bear an additional burden for creating the path.

Muslims are equally vulnerable to ghuluw. Exaggerating Muhammad, peace be upon him, attributing independent unseen power to saints, declaring lawful things forbidden from ascetic zeal, or turning scholars into unquestionable authorities can cross revealed limits.

The opposite of extremism is not indifference. The “level way” requires strong devotion held inside truth. Moderation means the correct measure, not automatically the midpoint between obedience and sin.

Respectful love asks what the beloved prophet taught. Isa directed worship to Allah; Muhammad insisted he was Allah’s servant and messenger. Staying within their words is the deepest honour.

Verse 78 recalls that disbelieving offenders among the Children of Israel were cursed through Dawud and Isa because of disobedience and repeated transgression. Prophetic love includes accepting their condemnation of injustice.

=78=

“Those among the Children of Israel who disbelieved were rejected through the words of Dawud and Isa son of Maryam. That was because they disobeyed and repeatedly transgressed.”

The verse says “those who disbelieved,” not every child of Israel. The curse follows belief and conduct, not lineage. It was pronounced by revered prophets from within their own history, showing that criticism is not an ethnic feud introduced by Muhammad.

Early commentators linked Dawud’s curse to Sabbath violators and Isa’s to particular later offenders, but the Quran centres the moral causes rather than requiring one detailed identification. Disobedience and aggression produced distance from mercy.

‘Isyan is refusal of Allah’s command; i‘tidā’ is crossing limits repeatedly, especially against others. Private rebellion becomes social harm. A community cannot claim prophetic loyalty while practising what its prophets condemned.

A curse in revelation is a solemn divine judgment, not a model for casual abusive speech. The Prophet did not teach believers to curse every sinner by name, especially while repentance remains possible. Correction should seek guidance.

This verse offers no basis for antisemitism. Dawud and Isa themselves are Israelites, and faithful Israelites are honoured elsewhere. Racial hatred would punish people for ancestry and repeat transgression.

Muslims must hear the universal law. If they disobey and exceed limits, calling themselves Muhammad’s ummah does not make the acts acceptable. Prophets are witnesses against followers who use their names without their ethics.

Verse 79 identifies the social mechanism that allowed transgression to continue: they stopped forbidding one another from wrongdoing. Silence transformed individual sins into a communal culture.

=79=

“They did not forbid one another from the wrongdoing they committed. Evil indeed was what they used to do.”

Mutual correction is a community’s moral immune system. When wrongdoing becomes public and no knowledgeable, capable person objects, repetition makes it ordinary. Eventually the person who speaks truth appears disruptive rather than the sin.

The verse condemns both the original evil and failure to restrain it. Silence can communicate approval, protect access, or leave victims isolated. Scholars, officials, families, and peers each have responsibility within their actual capacity.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, said whoever sees a wrong should change it with his hand if able, then tongue if unable, then reject it in the heart. Ability is interpreted through lawful authority and consequence, not raw physical power.

Forbidding wrong has conditions: the act must truly be wrong, facts reasonably clear, advice wise, and the response unlikely to produce a greater evil. Matters of valid scholarly disagreement are not treated like clear sin. Private faults are not hunted through spying.

“The hand” does not authorise assault or vigilantism. Parents, employers, platform administrators, and public authorities can change matters within legitimate jurisdiction. Others use counsel, reporting, support for victims, or refusal to participate.

Heart rejection is not useless if no safe outward step exists, but someone should not claim inability merely to preserve comfort. Collective action often creates capacity an isolated person lacks.

Communities should also protect those who speak responsibly. Whistleblowers, victims, and scholars cannot be told to forbid wrong and then abandoned to retaliation. Legal support, confidential reporting, financial independence, and shared public advocacy can turn an individual risk into sustainable correction.

Online amplification matters. Liking, sharing, and monetising harmful content can become the opposite of forbidding it. Sometimes refusing attention is the most effective correction.

The verse also demands mercy in advice. The goal is stopping wrong and helping return, not enjoying superiority. Verse 80 now gives another symptom of corrupted loyalty: many took disbelievers as protecting allies, storing up deeds that brought Allah’s anger and lasting punishment.

=80=

“You see many of them taking disbelievers as protecting allies. Evil indeed is what they have sent ahead for themselves: Allah is angry with them, and they will remain in punishment.”

The verse describes many among the offending group aligning with pagan or hostile disbelievers against the Prophet and believers. Their theological knowledge did not prevent political hatred from making idolatrous allies preferable to a monotheistic messenger.

Awliya again refers to protective and strategic allegiance in hostility, not every friendship or treaty across religions. The surah has allowed food, marriage, adjudication, and cooperation in good. Reading those permissions together prevents prejudice.

“They sent ahead” presents alliance as an investment in the Hereafter. It may produce short-term leverage, but the stored return is anger. People often call betrayal pragmatism because only immediate benefit is visible.

Allah’s anger is a perfect response to persistent, knowing rebellion, not uncontrolled emotion. Lasting punishment concerns those who die upon the disbelief described, without repentance. Living people remain invited to change.

The wording “many” preserves exceptions. It cannot support collective blame against Jews or any scriptural community. Actions and allegiance are judged individually.

Muslims should also examine alliances that support oppression, whether the partner is Muslim or not. A Muslim label does not purify assistance in aggression, and non-Muslim identity does not make every cooperation forbidden. The determining questions are justice, covenant, harm, and faithfulness to Allah.

The verse closes this file with the cost of replacing principle by hostile protection. Verse 81, opening the next file, argues that if these people truly believed in Allah, the Prophet, and what was revealed to him, they would not take such allies—but many are defiantly disobedient.
