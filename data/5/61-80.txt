=61=

This verse continues the detailed portrait of the hypocrites from among the Jewish tribes in Medina, exposing the profound duplicity that characterised their interaction with the Muslim community. The phrase “When they come to you, believers” describes their physical approach to the gatherings of the Prophet and his companions. Upon entering this sacred space, their tongues utter the words, “We believe.

” This is a verbal claim of faith, a public declaration meant to secure the social and political benefits of being considered part of the Muslim ummah. However, the verse immediately shatters this outward profession by revealing their inner state: “But they are committed to disbelief when they enter and when they leave.

” The Arabic construction here, using the word *dakhalū*, indicates that they are already immersed in a state of kufr when they arrive, and they remain in that same state when they depart. The encounter with the believers and the hearing of the Quran changes nothing in their hearts. They came as disbelievers and they leave as disbelievers, their outer confession being nothing more than a theatrical performance.

This is a terrifying diagnosis of a heart that has become immune to the truth; it can sit in the company of the Prophet, hear the words of God, and still walk away completely unmoved because its inner commitment to rejection is absolute. The verse then seals this exposure with the statement, “And Allah knows what they hide.

” This is both a comfort to the believers, who might be deceived by the hypocrites’ apparent friendliness, and a dire warning to the hypocrites themselves. They may conceal their mockery, their secret alliances with the enemies of Islam, and their true theological positions, but the One before whom all secrets are laid bare is fully aware. This knowledge is not passive; it is the prelude to a just reckoning. The lesson for all believers is the immense value of sincerity, ikhlas. A person can be physically present in the most blessed gatherings, yet gain nothing because the heart is absent or, worse, hostile. The verse prompts a regular self-examination: when I enter the company of the faithful or the house of God, and when I leave, has my state of faith been increased, or have I merely performed a social ritual while my heart remained locked in its own world of disobedience and doubt?

=62=

After exposing the hidden disbelief of the hypocrites, this verse turns to their visible, outward behaviour, which provides the evidence of their inner corruption. The Prophet is addressed, and through him every observer: “You see many of them racing towards sin, transgression, and consumption of forbidden gain.” The verb “racing” is highly significant. It indicates not a reluctant fall into error, but an eager, competitive haste to commit wrongdoing. They are not merely sinners; they are athletes of sin, striving to outdo one another in wickedness. The three categories of their vice are then listed. “Sin” here is *ithm*, the general category of any deed that violates divine law and harms the soul. “Transgression” is *‘udwān*, the act of overstepping limits, of being aggressive and unjust towards others, a direct violation of the command to cooperate in goodness and not in sin.

The third and most specific is “consumption of forbidden gain,” translating *suht*, which, as explained earlier in the surah, refers primarily to bribery, usury, and any wealth acquired through utterly corrupt means, such as taking money to alter a judgment. This greed was a hallmark of the leadership that had lost all fear of God.

The verse ends with the divine verdict: “Evil indeed are their actions!” This is not a human judgment but God’s own assessment of their deeds. It teaches the believer to develop a moral clarity that is not swayed by the apparent worldliness or sophistication of such people. Their cleverness, their wealth amassed through suht, and their political manoeuvring are all, in the final analysis, nothing but evil.

The verse also serves as a warning about the social environment. When a community sees its members racing towards sin and no one stops them, it is a sign of deep sickness. It calls the believers to be a people who race towards forgiveness and good deeds, as described elsewhere in the Quran, rather than towards the things that earn divine condemnation. The visible conduct of a person or a group is a reliable indicator of the state of their heart, and the actions of these hypocrites, a frantic scramble for sinful profit and bribery, revealed the emptiness of their claim to faith.

=63=

The Quran now turns its scrutiny from the sinful masses to the religious leadership, exposing a failure that is even more catastrophic than the sins themselves. The question is a sharp rebuke: “Why do their rabbis and scholars not forbid them?” The rabbis, *rabbāniyyūn*, and the scholars, *aḥbār*, were the custodians of the Torah, the spiritual guides entrusted with teaching and correcting the community. They possessed the knowledge of what God had prohibited. They could see their people racing towards sinful speech, such as mocking the Prophet and distorting scripture, and towards suht, the consumption of bribery and usury.

Yet they remained silent. The verse demands an answer for this silence.

This is a foundational principle in Islam: the obligation of enjoining good and forbidding evil, *al-amr bil-ma‘rūf wa-n-nahy ‘ani-l-munkar*, is not merely an individual virtue but a collective responsibility. When those with knowledge and authority fail to speak out against clear violations of divine law, they become complicit in the sin. Their silence is not neutral; it is a form of approval that allows evil to spread and corrupt the entire society.

The verse then pronounces a separate condemnation specifically on their inaction: “Evil indeed is their inaction!” The Arabic word used is *mā kānū yaṣna‘ūn*, which refers to their habitual practice, their “crafting” of deeds. Their inaction is their main product, their defining work, and it is declared to be evil. A hadith of the Prophet, peace be upon him, powerfully illustrates this principle: “When people see an oppressor and do not restrain him, it is likely that Allah will punish them all with a widespread punishment.” The failure of the scholars is often the first step towards the downfall of a nation. This verse is a direct address to every Muslim scholar, teacher, and community leader. It is a warning that knowledge is a trust, and the one who sees a sin being committed openly and does not raise a voice of correction out of fear, laziness, or a desire to maintain worldly status, has betrayed that trust. The silence of the learned is the funeral prayer of a righteous society.

=64=

This verse records an appalling statement of blasphemy uttered by some among the Jewish community and delivers a multi-layered divine response. The historical context, as reported by commentators, is that some Jewish leaders, when the verse “Who will lend Allah a good loan?” was revealed, mockingly said, “Allah is poor and we are rich,” or, as this verse states, “Allah is tight-fisted,” implying that God withholds provision from them, and thus they cannot be expected to spend in charity. The immediate divine reaction is a curse: “May their fists be tied and they be condemned for what they said.” The punishment mirrors the sin; because they attributed stinginess to the Most Generous, their own hands are symbolically tied in miserliness and they are distanced from God’s mercy.

The verse then corrects the blasphemy with the beautiful truth: “Rather, He is open-handed, giving freely as He pleases.” The Arabic term *mabsūṭatān*, translated as “open-handed,” literally means “spread out,” a vivid image of unrestricted, abundant giving without limit. Allah’s generosity is not constrained by any economy; He gives to the believer and the disbeliever, the righteous and the sinner, in this world as a manifestation of His name Ar-Razzāq.

The verse then delivers a tragic prophecy about the effect of the Quran on such people: “That which has been revealed to you from your Lord will only cause many of them to increase in wickedness and disbelief. ” The same revelation that heals and guides the sincere heart becomes a poison that increases the disease in a jealous, arrogant heart. Next, the verse proclaims a divinely imposed social curse: “We have stirred among them hostility and hatred until the Day of Judgment.” Because they rejected the unifying truth, they were left to the chaos of their own tribal and sectarian divisions, a state of perpetual internal conflict.

The verse then gives a powerful assurance of divine protection against their military schemes: “Whenever they kindle the fire of war, Allah puts it out. ” Every plot and alliance against the Prophet was foiled by God’s intervention. The final note condemns their ultimate activity: “they strive to spread corruption in the land. And Allah does not like corruptors.” The verse thus offers a complete cycle of their sin, punishment, God’s nature, the paradoxical effect of truth, and the ultimate failure of their conspiracies.

=65=

This short but profoundly merciful verse opens a window of hope immediately after the severe condemnations of blasphemy and corruption. It presents a conditional statement that reveals the heart of divine mercy and the true purpose of God’s dealings with humanity. The condition is placed on “the People of the Book,” the Jews and Christians, the very groups whose leadership had just been described with such dark imagery. They are not eternally condemned by race; their fate is tied to their response. The condition is that they become “faithful and mindful of Allah.

” The word “faithful,” *āmanū*, means to embrace the full faith, which after the coming of the Prophet Muhammad necessarily includes believing in him and the Quran. The phrase “mindful of Allah” translates *ittaqaw*, meaning they would live with a constant, active consciousness of God, avoiding His prohibitions and fulfilling His commands. Had they done this, two magnificent divine acts would have followed. First, “We would have certainly absolved them of their sins.

” The word *kaffarnā* means to cover, blot out, and expiate completely. Every sin from their past, including the blasphemies and the distortions, would have been erased as if they had never existed.

This is the tremendous promise of forgiveness that awaits every sincere repentant, regardless of the depth of their prior transgression. Second, “and admitted them into the Gardens of Bliss.” The Gardens are not merely ordinary paradises; they are *Jannāt an-Na‘īm*, the Gardens of pure, untainted delight and ease, the highest levels of eternal pleasure where every desire is fulfilled and every sorrow is absent. This verse thus demonstrates that God’s door is never shut. The very people whose beliefs and actions have been scrutinised and condemned are simultaneously being invited to a complete transformation that would change their eternal destiny from the Fire to the highest Bliss. It is a profound lesson in balancing fear with hope: the warnings are severe because the danger is real, but the invitation remains open because the Creator is, above all, the All-Forgiving and Most Merciful. No past is too stained to be purified by a sincere return.

=66=

This verse extends the conditional promise of the previous one, moving from the spiritual reward of forgiveness and Paradise to the tangible, worldly consequences of true adherence to divine guidance. The condition now is specifically about the scriptures: “had they observed the Torah, the Gospel, and what has been revealed to them from their Lord.” The “what has been revealed to them” is understood as the Quran, which is addressed to all humanity and is the final fulfillment of the scriptural promise. True observance is not merely a ritualistic reading but a comprehensive implementation of the guidance contained in these books, which would naturally lead the sincere follower to recognise and accept the final message. The result of this observance is described in breathtaking terms: “they would have been overwhelmed with provisions from above and below.

” The phrase “from above” refers to the blessings of the sky: abundant rain in its proper seasons, spiritual illumination, and the descent of divine mercy. “From below” refers to the blessings of the earth: fertile soil, abundant crops, mineral wealth, and general economic prosperity. This teaches a direct correlation between a society’s collective righteousness and its material well-being. When a people live by God’s law, the natural world, which is also subject to God’s command, cooperates with them.

This is not a “prosperity gospel” in the simplistic sense, but a divine principle repeatedly affirmed in the Quran. However, the verse immediately balances this ideal picture with a realistic assessment of the present reality: “Some among them are upright.

” This is a crucial recognition that generalisation is not absolute. Among the People of the Book, there was a minority that genuinely sought truth, who were just and humble, like Abdullah ibn Salam and the Negus of Abyssinia. But then the verse concludes with the sad reality: “yet many do nothing but evil.” The majority, due to their rejection and corruption, had made evil their only occupation.

This verse teaches the Muslim community the vital link between obedience and provision, and it also commands fairness in judgment, acknowledging the righteous minority while not being naive about the corrupted state of the majority.

=67=

This verse is a direct, powerful mandate to the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, and through him to every caller to truth until the end of time. The address “O Messenger” singles him out for the weightiest of tasks. The command is absolute: “Convey everything revealed to you from your Lord.

” The Arabic term *balligh* means to deliver completely, without omission, distortion, or concealment. The Prophet is not allowed to hide any part of the revelation, even if that part might provoke the anger, mockery, or opposition of the powerful. Some reports suggest that the Prophet, aware of the intense hostility from the Jews and hypocrites as detailed in the preceding verses, might have felt a natural human hesitation to deliver certain rulings that would be particularly difficult for them to hear, such as the exposure of their scriptural distortions or the condemnation of their theological errors. This verse orders him to suppress that hesitation entirely. The severity of the obligation is then emphasised with a striking statement: “If you do not, then you have not delivered His message. ” This does not suggest that the Prophet ever failed in his duty, but it underscores the principle that the messenger’s entire mission hinges on complete, fearless proclamation. A partial delivery is a nullification of the prophethood itself. Immediately following this command, a divine guarantee of protection is given: “Allah will certainly protect you from the people.” This was a specific promise to the Prophet that he would not be assassinated before his mission was complete. After this verse was revealed, it is reported that the Prophet dismissed his personal guards, saying, “Leave me, for my Lord has protected me.” This assurance is for every dai‘i: the one who delivers the truth faithfully is under a divine shield, and no harm can befall them except what Allah permits for a greater good.

The verse concludes, “Indeed, Allah does not guide the people who disbelieve,” meaning that the Prophet’s job is to convey, not to convert. The hearts that remain in disbelief despite the clear message are beyond his responsibility; their guidance is a matter for God alone.

=68=

This verse is a direct challenge to the People of the Book, stripping away their false sense of religious security. The Prophet is instructed to declare to them plainly, “You have nothing to stand on.

” The phrase “nothing to stand on” translates *lastum ‘alā shay’*, meaning they have no valid religious foundation, no true faith, and no acceptable argument before God. This is a sweeping negation of their claim to be the chosen people of God based merely on their lineage or their lip service to their scriptures. The condition for having a legitimate standing is then stated: “unless you observe the Torah, the Gospel, and what has been revealed to you from your Lord.” True observance of the Torah and Gospel inherently entails believing in the prophecies within them that foretell the coming of a final Messenger and a final scripture. To claim adherence to Moses and Jesus while rejecting Muhammad is a self-contradiction, because Moses and Jesus, in their authentic teachings, commanded the worship of one God and foretold the seal of the prophets.

Thus, their very salvation, according to their own books, depends on their acceptance of the Quran. The verse then revisits the tragic reality of their response: “And your Lord’s revelation to you, O Prophet, will only cause many of them to increase in wickedness and disbelief.” The very truth that should have been their salvation becomes a means of their spiritual destruction because they meet it with envy and arrogance instead of humility. As in verse 64, this is a law of spiritual psychology: the same sun that softens wax hardens clay.

Finally, the Prophet is consoled, “So do not grieve for the people who disbelieve. ” This is a direct echo of earlier consolations in the surah. The Prophet’s heart was heavy with the desire for their guidance. The command “do not grieve” is a liberation from an emotional burden that is not his to carry. He has delivered the message, established the clear proof, and the consequences of rejection are now between them and their Creator.

This is a lesson for every believer who feels sorrow over the misguidance of loved ones: the grief must not become a paralysing despair, and the responsibility ends where the clear delivery of truth ends.

=69=

This is one of the most famous yet often misunderstood verses of the Quran, and it must be understood within its clear context in Al-Ma'idah. The verse lists four categories: “the believers,” meaning the Muslims; “Jews”; “Sabians,” a group often described as monotheistic followers of a prophetic tradition prior to Islam; and “Christians.” The key to unlocking the verse is the conditional clause that follows: “whoever truly believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good.” The verse does not say that anyone who calls themselves a Jew or a Christian is automatically saved. It says that among all these groups, those individuals who possess true, authentic faith in God and the Last Day, combined with righteous deeds, are the ones who will be saved. Before the coming of the Prophet Muhammad, a Jew who sincerely followed the uncorrupted Torah, or a Christian who sincerely followed the authentic Gospel that preached pure monotheism, would be on the path of salvation.

However, after the advent of the final Prophet, the very definition of “truly believes in Allah” must include belief in all His messengers, including Muhammad, peace be upon him. A person today who is aware of the Prophet’s message and rejects him cannot be described as having true faith in God, because they have rejected the very messenger and book that God sent to guide them. The great companion Ibn Abbas clarified that when Surah Al-Baqarah’s similar verse was revealed, it was later explained and contextualised by verses like “Whoever seeks a religion other than Islam, it will never be accepted from him.” This verse, therefore, is not a charter for religious pluralism where all paths are equally valid. It is a profound statement of divine justice: God judges individuals according to their sincere response to the truth that reached them. The reward for such sincere believers, from any age, is complete peace: “no fear for them, nor will they grieve. ” They will be spared the terror of the Day of Judgment and the sorrow of regret. The verse is a call to all humanity that the essential formula for salvation is unwavering monotheism, eschatological accountability, and virtuous action, a formula that is completely and perfectly embodied in Islam.

=70=

After the universal statement of salvation’s conditions, the Quran returns to the historical narrative of the Children of Israel to provide a concrete example of a people who broke the covenant and failed the test of responding to divine truth. The verse begins with the reminder, “Indeed, We took a covenant from the Children of Israel and sent them messengers.” This covenant was the solemn pledge to worship God alone, obey His law, and follow the prophets He would send. God, in His mercy, did not leave them with just a book; He continuously sent a stream of messengers to revive their faith, clarify their law, and warn them against deviation.

However, the response to these divine favours is tragic: “Whenever a messenger came to them with what they did not desire, they denied some and killed others.” The phrase “with what they did not desire” is the psychological key to their rebellion. The prophets did not come to affirm their whims; they came with the truth, which often clashed with their material interests, their pride, and their deeply ingrained habits. When the message contradicted their desires, instead of subjugating their egos to the truth, they judged the truth by their egos. The result was a bifurcated atrocity. “They denied some” refers to those prophets they rejected as liars, refusing to accept their prophethood. “And killed others” is the chilling escalation. They murdered prophets like Zechariah and John the Baptist, and they attempted to kill Jesus.

This is the ultimate crime against divine guidance. The verse is a warning to the Muslim community not to repeat this pattern. The Quran and the Sunnah will also bring teachings that challenge the desires of the lower self. The true believer is the one who submits when the revelation comes, even if it is difficult, while the loser follows the path of the Israelites, accepting only what suits their convenience and rejecting, or even violently opposing, what does not. It is a lesson that the criterion of truth is not its alignment with human desire, but its divine origin.

=71=

This verse delves deeper into the psychology behind the Israelites' persistent rebellion, explaining the false sense of security that emboldened them. The phrase “They thought there would be no consequences” translates a very powerful Arabic expression that suggests they calculated that their crimes—denying and murdering prophets—would not result in a divine trial or punishment, a *fitnah*. They believed they were immune, as the supposed chosen people, or they thought God was too merciful to hold them severely to account.

This is the spiritual disease of *amnu makr Allāh*, feeling safe from the plan of God. This false hope led directly to their hardened attitude: “so they turned a blind eye and a deaf ear.

” This is a metaphorical description of their wilful refusal to see the clear signs of the prophets and to hear the divine words they brought. It was not a physical disability but a chosen spiritual blindness and deafness. They had shut down the very faculties that could guide them to salvation. Despite this profound rebellion, the verse reveals a cycle of divine mercy: “Yet Allah turned to them in forgiveness after their repentance.” Every time they sank to a new low, if a sincere repentance emerged, Allah, in His boundless forbearance, accepted it and forgave them. This shows that the door of tawbah is never closed as long as the sun rises from its east.

Yet, the tragedy continues: “but again many became blind and deaf. ” After being forgiven and restored, the majority relapsed into their old ways, taking divine mercy for granted and returning to their arrogance. The verse concludes with the ever-present divine surveillance: “And Allah is All-Seeing of what they do.

” This is a warning that the cycle of sin and relapse is fully documented, and while forgiveness is available, a final, decisive reckoning awaits. The lesson for the believer is to never feel secure from divine consequences, to constantly renew one’s repentance, and to avoid the hardened state where the eyes and ears no longer function for the truth.

=72=

The surah circles back to the core theological deviation of Christianity, having earlier dealt with their broken covenant and historical errors. The pronouncement is definitive: “Those who say, ‘Allah is the Messiah, son of Mary,’ have certainly fallen into disbelief.

” This is an unambiguous declaration that the deification of Jesus is a negation of Islam’s fundamental truth. The verse immediately provides the internal evidence from the life of Jesus himself to refute this claim: “The Messiah himself said, ‘O Children of Israel! Worship Allah—my Lord and your Lord.’” This is a direct quote of Jesus’ core message as recorded in the Gospels, where he consistently directed worship to the Father. If Jesus worshipped God, called God his Lord, and instructed others to do the same, he cannot logically be that same God. The argument is simple and irrefutable.

The verse then states the universal law that applies to all who commit this act: “Whoever associates others with Allah in worship will surely be forbidden Paradise by Allah. ” The word for “associates others” is *yushrik*, the act of shirk, which is the only sin God explicitly states He will not forgive if a person dies upon it without repentance. The consequence is absolute exclusion from Paradise. The home of such a person is “the Fire,” an eternal abode.

The final clause, “And the wrongdoers will have no helpers,” strips away any hope of an intercessor who could overturn this decree. On the Day of Judgment, no saint, no angel, and not even Jesus himself will be able to help those who worshipped him instead of God.

This is a verse that draws the clearest possible red line. It does not merely state that the Christian theology is incorrect; it states that it expels a person from the boundaries of faith. The verse serves as a powerful call to pure monotheism, reminding all believers that the greatness of any prophet, even one as magnificent as the Messiah, should never lead to an elevation of his status to that of the Creator.

=73=

This verse addresses the second major Christian doctrine, the Trinity, which attempts to reconcile the divinity of Jesus and the Holy Spirit with a form of monotheism. The phrase “Allah is one in a Trinity” translates *thālithu thalāthah*, literally “the third of three.” This refers to the concept of three co-equal persons in one Godhead: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. The Quran declares that those who espouse this formula have “certainly fallen into disbelief,” just like those who simply say Jesus is God. The reason follows immediately: “There is only One God.

” This is the bedrock of Islamic theology, *tawḥīd*, a singularity that is indivisible, uncompounded, and absolute. The idea of a plural Godhead, however philosophically explained, is a contradiction to the divine nature.

The verse then delivers a dire warning: “If they do not stop saying this, those who disbelieve among them will be afflicted with a painful punishment. ” The condition “if they do not stop” contains both a threat and a hidden invitation. The very fact that they are warned to “stop” implies that the door to repentance is still open. If they cease this belief and return to the pure monotheism that Jesus preached, they can escape the punishment. The punishment promised is “painful,” *alīm*, a torment that is intense and pervasive. This punishment can be understood as both worldly chastisement and the eternal punishment of the Hereafter. This verse, combined with the previous one, confronts the two primary theological pillars of historical Christian orthodoxy. It demonstrates that the Islamic critique is not a superficial polemic but a direct engagement with specific, defined doctrines. The lesson for the Muslim is the absolute importance of preserving the purity of tawḥīd in one’s belief and language, always resisting any subtle blurring of the line between the Creator and the most honoured of His creations.

=74=

In the immediate wake of the most severe condemnations of shirk, the Quran’s tone shifts into a tender, pleading invitation. The rhetorical question, “Will they not turn to Allah in repentance and seek His forgiveness?” is an expression of divine longing for the return of the sinners.

The word “turn” is *yatūbūn*, the same root as tawbah, indicating a complete turning back, a change in direction. The verse does not say, “Will they not become Muslims,” but focuses on the internal act of repentance, which is the heart of Islam. This teaches that the core of conversion is not just a change of label but a profound inner revolution where the soul turns from the worship of created beings to the worship of the Creator alone. The invitation to “seek His forgiveness” is a promise of a clean slate. Even the sin of declaring God to be a trinity or a man, a sin that fills the heavens and the earth, can be completely erased by a sincere seeking of divine pardon.

The verse then anchors this invitation in two of God’s most beautiful names: “And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. ” The name Al-Ghafūr is in the intensive form, meaning He forgives again and again, covering vast, repeated sins. The name Ar-Raḥīm means His mercy is perpetual and flowing, enveloping the repentant sinner with love and care, not just a cold legal pardon. This verse is a masterpiece of da‘wah. It follows the Quranic method of using the threat of punishment to break the ego’s resistance, and then immediately applying the balm of mercy to attract the now-softened heart. It shows that no human being, no matter how deeply entrenched in false theology, is beyond the reach of divine forgiveness if they take the step of sincere repentance.

For the Muslim reader, it cultivates a hopeful outlook towards all people, reminding them that the greatest sinners of yesterday can become the greatest saints of tomorrow through the miracle of tawbah.

=75=

This verse lays out the Islamic doctrine of Jesus in its most straightforward, demythologised form, using pure logic to dismantle any claim to his divinity. The statement “The Messiah, son of Mary, was no more than a messenger” is the positive declaration of his true status. The emphatic phrase “no more than” is a rebuke to the two extremes: the Jews who rejected him as a false prophet, and the Christians who elevated him to God. The truth is the middle path. To prove his non-divinity, the verse provides a simple historical observation: “Many messengers had come and gone before him.” Jesus was not a unique phenomenon in the chain of prophethood. Prophets like Abraham, Moses, David, and John the Baptist came, delivered their message, and died. Jesus is part of this same human procession. His mother, Mary, is then described as “a woman of truth,” *ṣiddīqah*, a title of immense honour indicating her complete sincerity and affirmation of God’s word, but crucially affirming her full humanity. Then comes the final, devastatingly simple logical argument: “They both ate food.” Eating is the most basic biological function of a creature that is dependent on sustenance for survival. God, the Self-Subsisting, needs no food and experiences no hunger. If Jesus was God, he would not have been subject to the needs and limitations of a physical body. This single, observable fact of his human life shatters the logical foundation of his divinity.

The verse concludes with an expression of astonishment at the human capacity for self-deception: “See how We make the signs clear to them, yet see how they are deluded from the truth!” The signs are made manifest and clear, like a brilliant sun in the sky, yet the human mind, clouded by tradition, emotion, and blind following, can be turned away from such an obvious truth. It is a lesson in epistemology: clear evidence is not enough for a heart that does not wish to see.

=76=

This verse instructs the Prophet to confront the idolaters and the Christians with a rational argument that appeals to the most basic human logic regarding worship. The question “How can you worship besides Allah those who can neither harm nor benefit you?” is a challenge to the very purpose of worship. Worship, at its core, is an act of seeking benefit and protection from harm. A rational person directs their worship, their ultimate hope and fear, only towards a being that possesses the power to grant benefit and avert harm. The idols worshipped by the pagans, and the human beings like Jesus and Mary worshipped by the Christians, in their own earthly existence could not control their own destiny. Jesus could not prevent the plot of the Jews who sought to crucify him, nor could he provide sustenance for himself without God’s will. So why would a human being devote their ultimate devotion to another creature who is, in terms of independent power, completely impotent?

The second half of the verse provides the positive alternative, highlighting the attributes that make God alone worthy of worship: “And Allah alone is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing. ” The name “All-Hearing” means He hears every prayer, every cry for help, every whispered secret, no matter the language or the number of simultaneous supplicants. The idols and the saints in their graves hear nothing.

The name “All-Knowing” means He is fully aware of every need, every pain, and every intention, even before it is expressed. The one who truly hears and the one who truly knows is the one who can truly respond. This verse is a masterclass in Islamic theology, reducing the entire debate to a simple criterion of power and knowledge. It teaches the believer to examine the object of their ultimate reliance. If it cannot independently hear, know, benefit, or harm, it is irrational to worship it. The only rational choice is the One who possesses the totality of these attributes in absolute perfection.

=77=

This verse is a direct prohibition of *ghuluww*, religious extremism and exaggeration, which is identified as the root cause of the theological deviations of the People of the Book. The call “O People of the Book” is an address to both Jews and Christians. The command “Do not go to extremes in your faith beyond the truth” encapsulates the Islamic position as the balanced middle way. For the Christians, the extreme was to raise a human prophet, Jesus, to the status of God. For the Jews, their extreme was the opposite: an excessive rejection and denigration of Jesus and a rigid, hypocritical legalism that killed the spirit of the law. The truth lies in between: honouring all prophets without deifying any of them.

The verse then traces the origin of these extremes: “nor follow the vain desires of those who went astray before you.” This refers to the early innovators and heretics who introduced these extreme ideas into the pure monotheistic tradition. The doctrine of the Trinity and the excessive veneration of saints and icons were not the teachings of Jesus but the products of later generations influenced by Greek philosophy and pagan culture. To follow these ideas is not to follow divine revelation but to chase the vain desires of misguided ancestors.

The final part of the verse describes the devastating impact of their straying: “They misled many and strayed from the Right Way.” The error of the leaders was not a private matter; it caused the mass misguidance of countless followers, a responsibility they will carry. The verse is a timeless warning for Muslims as well. The danger of *ghuluww* is a constant threat. Extremism in venerating the Prophet Muhammad or awliya’ to the point of attributing divine knowledge or power to them is the same error that led the Christians away from pure monotheism. The straight path is the path of moderation, love without deification, respect without worship.

=78=

This verse provides a powerful testimony that the condemnation of the rebellious Israelites is not unique to the Quran but is a consistent theme across the chain of scriptures. It states that the disbelievers among the Children of Israel “were condemned” on the tongues of two of their most revered prophets: David and Jesus, the son of Mary. David, to whom the Psalms, the Zabur, were revealed, pronounced divine curses upon the lawbreakers among his people. The Psalms are filled with imprecations against the wicked who oppress the poor and forsake God’s law. Jesus, the son of Mary, likewise pronounced woes and condemnations upon the hypocritical scribes and Pharisees who had corrupted the religion and rejected the truth he brought. The New Testament contains his severe rebukes, calling them a “brood of vipers” and foretelling the destruction of those who rejected the prophets. The Quran here appeals to the very scriptures the Jews and Christians hold sacred to prove that their own prophets testified against their rebellious behaviour. The cause of this prophetic condemnation is then stated: “That was for their disobedience and violations. ” Their transgression was of two kinds. First, *‘iṣyān*, a general state of disobedience to God’s commands, a lifestyle of sin. Second, *‘udwān*, which is specific and aggressive violation, overstepping the bounds and committing injustices against fellow human beings. The two terms together cover the vertical breach with God and the horizontal breach with humanity. This verse serves as a powerful lesson in accountability. It tells the believers that the narrative of the Quran about the Children of Israel is not a biased account but a truth that aligns with the witness of the prophets who lived among them. It also teaches that divine condemnation is not arbitrary; it is the just consequence of persistent disobedience and transgression, a law that applies to all nations, including the Muslim ummah, if they were to tread the same path.

=79=

This verse pinpoints the societal sin that accelerated the downfall of the Israelite community, a sin that is as relevant today for any community as it was then. The statement “They did not forbid one another from doing evil” is a succinct diagnosis of a collapsed moral immune system. The community had reached a point where evil was committed openly, brazenly, and no one had the moral courage or the spiritual concern to stand up and say, “Fear Allah; this is wrong.” The duty of mutual admonition, which is the safeguard of any righteous society, was completely abandoned. The elite were silent in exchange for favour, the scholars were silent in exchange for status, and the common people were silent in exchange for a false peace. This collective silence creates an environment where sin is normalised and ultimately becomes the dominant culture. When that happens, the entire community becomes a target for divine punishment, because the good people, by their silence, have implicitly endorsed the evil.

The verse then delivers the divine evaluation of their actions: “Evil indeed was what they did! ” The word for “evil” here is *labi’sa*, a term of strong condemnation. Their deeds, both the original sins and the sin of failing to forbid them, were utterly vile. This verse is a direct call to action for the Muslim ummah. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, said, “Whoever among you sees an evil, let him change it with his hand; if he cannot, then with his tongue; and if he cannot, then with his heart, and that is the weakest of faith. ” The silent spectator of sin, who has the ability to speak but remains quiet, shares in the collective guilt. The lesson of this verse is that a community’s health is not measured merely by the piety of a few individuals but by its capacity to collectively reject and correct wrongdoing. When the voice of correction dies, the community has effectively signed its own death warrant in the spiritual sense, and often in the worldly sense as well.

This verse serves as a concluding remark on the political and social behaviour of the rebellious Jews of Medina, bringing the theme of false guardianship that has run throughout the surah to a sharp close. The Prophet is told, “You see many of them taking the disbelievers as allies.” This refers to the Jews forming political and military alliances with the pagan Quraysh and other idolatrous Arab tribes against the Muslim community. This was a profound betrayal of any shared Abrahamic principle, because they were choosing the polytheists, who worshipped idols, over the believers, who worshipped the one God. The motivation was pure worldly interest and hatred of the Prophet. This act is immediately condemned: “Truly wicked are their misdeeds.

” The word “wicked” translates *bi’sa*, expressing a deep repulsiveness. Their deeds are not just mistaken; they are morally repugnant.

The verse then explains the direct consequence of these misdeeds: “which have earned them Allah’s wrath.” The phrase “earned them” indicates that their actions were the direct cause, the *sabab*, that made them deserve the divine anger, *ghaḍab*. Allah’s wrath is the removal of His mercy, the state of being distanced from the Lord’s favour, a state that manifests as humiliation in this world and punishment in the next.

The verse then delivers the final, grave sentence: “And they will be in everlasting torment. ” This affirms the eternal nature of the punishment for those who die upon such a state of active, knowing rebellion and alliance against the truth. It echoes the warning earlier in the surah about those who take the disbelievers as guardians. This verse is a stark warning about the eternal cost of political and social compromises that betray the faith. In every age, believers are tested with the temptation to seek the favour and protection of powers that oppose the truth, justifying it under the guise of pragmatism. The verse declares that such a choice, when it involves active support against the community of faith, is a direct path to divine wrath and eternal loss.
