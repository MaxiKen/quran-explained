=1=

“O believers, fulfil all binding commitments. Grazing livestock have been made lawful for you, except what will be recited to you, but hunting is not lawful while you are in the sacred state of pilgrimage. Surely Allah commands whatever He wills.”

Al-Ma’idah opens with awfu bil-‘uqud—fulfil the knots you have tied. An ‘aqd is a binding commitment: the covenant of faith with Allah, marriage, sales, employment, treaties, trusts, vows, and every lawful agreement between people. A believer’s word is not disposable when circumstances become inconvenient.

The command includes obligations Allah imposed even where a human signature is absent. By entering faith, believers accept His distinctions between lawful and unlawful. The surah therefore moves immediately from contracts to food and pilgrimage. Worship, commerce, and daily consumption all belong inside covenant.

Bahimat al-an‘am refers broadly to grazing livestock such as camels, cattle, sheep, and goats, including what properly falls within that class. They are lawful unless a revealed exception applies. Verse 3 will list carrion, flowing blood, swine, animals dedicated to another, and deaths that do not meet lawful slaughter.

Lawfulness is still limited by sacred state. A pilgrim in ihram may eat lawful livestock but may not hunt land game. The restriction trains restraint at a time and place of heightened peace. Something permitted ordinarily can become temporarily prohibited because worship changes the circumstance.

“Allah commands whatever He wills” does not mean arbitrary rule detached from wisdom. The Creator knows what He made and has the authority to set boundaries. Human beings may understand some benefits and not others; obedience does not wait for every wisdom to become experimentally measurable.

Contracts containing sin are not binding in the sinful term. No promise can make interest, fraud, abuse, or assistance in aggression lawful. Faithfulness means fulfilling lawful commitments and ending invalid ones through a just process rather than using religion as an excuse for betrayal.

Modern applications include employment duties, subscriptions, leases, confidentiality, digital agreements genuinely accepted, and promises made to vulnerable people. Dense terms designed to trap a weaker party may themselves violate justice and consent; “contract” is not a sacred label covering exploitation.

The surah’s opening sets its theme. Earlier communities will be examined by their covenants, and Muslims by theirs. Verse 2 applies the principle to sacred symbols, pilgrims, enemies, and cooperation: even hatred must not push believers beyond the limits they promised to uphold.

=2=

“O believers, do not violate Allah’s sacred symbols, the sacred months, sacrificial animals, garlanded offerings, or those journeying to the Sacred House seeking bounty and pleasure from their Lord. Once you have left the sacred state, you may hunt. Do not let hatred for people who once barred you from the Sacred Mosque lead you to transgress. Cooperate in righteousness and mindfulness of Allah, and do not cooperate in sin and aggression. Be mindful of Allah; surely Allah is severe in punishment.”

Sha‘a’ir are visible signs through which Allah’s worship is honoured. The sacred months, marked sacrificial animals, and pilgrims heading toward the Ka‘bah were not to be treated as ordinary targets or property. Garlands identified animals dedicated for sacrifice, giving them recognised protection.

Pilgrims seek Allah’s bounty and pleasure. Bounty can include lawful trade during pilgrimage, as 2:198 permits, while ridwan is the greater spiritual aim. Islam does not force a choice between lawful livelihood and worship when the former remains subordinate to the latter.

After ihram ends, hunting returns to its normal ruling. The temporary ban should not be extended from personal taste, just as ordinary permission cannot be used inside the sacred state. Law follows the boundary Allah set.

The verse then names a powerful test of principle. Quraysh had barred Muslims from the Sacred Mosque at Hudaybiyyah. Believers now had strength and reason for anger, but shana’an—intense hatred—could not justify retaliatory transgression. Justice is most truthful when applied to someone who denied it to you.

This does not erase accountability. Wrongdoers may face lawful consequences, but punishment must be imposed by proper authority, with evidence and within limits. Revenge that harms uninvolved people or violates sanctuary becomes a second injustice.

“Cooperate in birr and taqwa” is a charter for collective life. Build systems that feed, educate, reconcile, protect, and enable obedience. Cooperation can involve Muslims and non-Muslims in any lawful good. “Do not cooperate in sin and aggression” forbids providing money, expertise, publicity, transport, or institutional cover for wrong merely because one is not the final actor.

A person should ask not only whether a task is legal or profitable but what larger project it serves. A technically small contribution can carry a share of a harmful outcome, just as good intercession carries reward.

Severe punishment closes the verse because group cooperation multiplies power. The next verse details foods whose consumption or method of death violates the covenant, while preserving a necessity exception for someone driven by extreme hunger.

=3=

“Forbidden to you are carrion, blood, the flesh of swine, what has been dedicated to other than Allah, what has been strangled, beaten to death, killed by a fall, gored, or partly eaten by a predator—unless you slaughter it before death—and what is sacrificed on stone altars. Also forbidden is seeking decisions through divining arrows. All this is grave disobedience. Today the disbelievers have lost hope of overcoming your religion, so do not fear them; fear Me. Today I have perfected your religion for you, completed My favour upon you, and chosen Islam as your way. But whoever is compelled by severe hunger, without intending sin, then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

The list begins with animals not lawfully slaughtered. Maytah is an animal dying without valid slaughter; “blood” is explained elsewhere as flowing blood (6:145), not every trace remaining in meat. Swine flesh is forbidden, and dedicating slaughter to another being corrupts worship even if the species is otherwise lawful.

Strangling, blunt force, falling, goring, and predation describe causes that often leave an animal dead before slaughter. If it is reached alive in a condition where valid slaughter can be completed, “unless you slaughter it” applies. Animal welfare and certainty about death matter alongside ritual words.

Stone altars belonged to pagan sacrifice. Divining arrows were used to seek supposed supernatural direction about travel, marriage, and other decisions. Islam replaces superstition with prayer, consultation, evidence, and istikharah. Random devices do not reveal the unseen.

The declaration of perfection was revealed, according to the authentic report from Umar, on Friday at Arafah during the Farewell Pilgrimage. A Jewish man observed that his community would have treated such a verse as a festival. The religion’s essential structure was complete; later understanding and application do not constitute a new revelation.

Perfection does not mean every technology or modern case is named. The Quran, Sunnah, legal principles, and qualified reasoning provide a complete framework through which new circumstances are judged. Innovation in worldly means differs from inventing worship or creed.

“Do not fear them; fear Me” came when enemies had despaired of erasing the established religion. Fear of social pressure must not rewrite halal and haram. Yet this confidence should not become arrogance toward others; completed favour increases responsibility.

Necessity remains inside perfected law. A person facing genuine starvation may consume enough prohibited food to survive, without desire for sin or excess. Allah’s forgiveness and mercy show that preservation of life is not rebellion. Necessity is assessed proportionately and ends when the need ends.

Having listed prohibitions, verse 4 answers the natural question: what is lawful? The answer begins broadly with wholesome things and then addresses game caught by trained hunting animals.

=4=

“They ask you what has been made lawful for them. Say, ‘All wholesome things are lawful for you, as well as what trained hunting animals catch for you. You train them according to the knowledge Allah has taught you. So eat what they catch and hold for you, and mention Allah’s name over it. Be mindful of Allah; surely Allah is swift in reckoning.’”

The default announced is tayyibat—things lawful and wholesome. Tayyib carries the sense of goodness and cleanliness, but personal disgust alone cannot forbid what Allah permits. Halal is a legal category; wholesome quality also asks about safety, purity, and responsible production.

Jawarih are hunting animals or birds that capture prey, such as trained dogs, falcons, and similar animals. Their training is shown when they respond to release and recall and catch for the hunter rather than simply devouring for themselves, with details explained by the Sunnah and jurists.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, told Adi ibn Hatim to mention Allah’s name when releasing his trained dog and to eat from what it caught, subject to conditions. If another dog joined whose release and dedication were unknown, uncertainty affected lawfulness. The hunter’s intention and control distinguish trained hunting from finding an animal killed by an unknown predator.

“You teach them from what Allah taught you” turns skill into gratitude. Human beings observe, train, and refine techniques, but intelligence, animal capacity, and accumulated knowledge are divine gifts. Expertise should humble the expert.

Mentioning Allah’s name joins provision to worship and acknowledges that life is taken only by permission. Hunting for food and genuine benefit differs from killing animals for cruel entertainment or waste. Islamic teaching forbids needless suffering and commands excellence in slaughter.

“Swift in reckoning” warns against using technical hunting rules to hide greed, waste, or cruelty. Allah knows whether the animal caught for the hunter, whether His name was intentionally omitted, and whether necessity or sport motivated the act.

Modern hunting methods raise questions about projectiles, stunning, timing of death, and trained animals that require qualified guidance. The verse gives the foundation without replacing technical investigation.

Verse 5 broadens lawful social exchange with the People of the Book: their food is lawful within its conditions, and chaste women among them may be married by Muslim men through genuine marriage, dowry, and chastity—not fornication or secret affairs.

=5=

“Today all wholesome things have been made lawful for you. The food of those given the Scripture is lawful for you, and your food is lawful for them. Also lawful in marriage are chaste believing women and chaste women from those given the Scripture before you, provided you give them their dowries in lawful marriage, seeking chastity—not fornication or secret affairs. Whoever rejects faith, their deeds have become worthless, and in the Hereafter they will be among the losers.”

“Food” here especially concerns slaughtered animals, because ordinary grain and fruit need no separate permission. Animals validly slaughtered by Jews or Christians fall under the allowance, while swine, blood, carrion, or what is knowingly dedicated to another remains forbidden.

Modern industrial slaughter creates disagreements about stunning, mechanical methods, invocation, and whether contemporary producers meet the scriptural category. Muslims should follow reliable scholarship and certification rather than making sweeping claims from a label. The permission is real, and so are the conditions established by the Quran and Sunnah.

Mutual food law enables neighbourliness, hospitality, and trade. It does not require eating anything doubtful, nor permit contempt toward someone who takes a cautious qualified view. Dietary discipline should not become social hostility.

The marriage permission is addressed to Muslim men and requires a genuinely chaste woman from the People of the Book, a valid contract, and payment of mahr. The repeated contrast with fornication and secret lovers shows this is covenant, not an interfaith loophole for dating without responsibility.

Muslim jurists have understood Muslim women as not permitted to marry non-Muslim men, based on the Quran’s marriage framework and consensus. For a Muslim man, legal permission does not mean every marriage is wise. Faith of children, worship in the home, dietary life, extended family, and the spouse’s actual beliefs require serious consideration. Some early leaders discouraged such marriages when harm was likely.

A wife from the People of the Book retains dignity and religious identity; permission does not authorise coercion, insult, or denial of agreed rights. Her husband remains bound to justice and good treatment.

The warning about rejecting faith keeps social openness from becoming theological surrender. Eating together and marrying lawfully do not mean all creeds are interchangeable. Deeds lose their final foundation when a person abandons iman.

After food and marriage, verse 6 turns to preparation for the daily covenant of prayer. Water purification and tayammum cleanse without making worship an unbearable hardship.

=6=

“O believers, when you rise for prayer, wash your faces and your hands up to the elbows, wipe your heads, and wash your feet up to the ankles. If you are in a state of major impurity, purify yourselves fully. But if you are ill, travelling, have relieved yourselves, or been intimate with women and cannot find water, then seek clean earth and wipe your faces and hands with it. Allah does not intend to place hardship upon you, but intends to purify you, complete His favour upon you, and make you grateful.”

The verse gives the obligatory framework of wudu: washing the face, washing arms including elbows, wiping the head, and washing feet including ankles. The Sunnah demonstrates sequence, amounts, rinsing mouth and nose, and other details. Waste remains wrong even when water is abundant.

“When you rise” is understood as when preparing for prayer while ritual purity is absent, not a requirement to renew wudu before every prayer if it has not been broken. Renewing it voluntarily can be virtuous, but should not be confused with obligation.

Janabah after intercourse or sexual discharge requires ghusl, a complete ritual bath. Ritual impurity is not a claim that the body or lawful intimacy is morally filthy. It is a worship state ended through the method Allah appointed.

Where illness makes water harmful, travel or circumstance makes it unavailable, or purification is needed after using the toilet or sexual contact, clean earth provides tayammum. Jurists differ over some details, including what counts as suitable earth and how “touching women” affects wudu, based on language and Sunnah. Valid school differences should not become accusations.

Tayammum protects both prayer and health. A patient with dressings, limited mobility, or medical risk should learn the relevant concessions instead of abandoning salah or harming the body. Allah explicitly denies intending haraj—constricting hardship.

Purification is nevertheless a real discipline. Body, clothing, water use, and schedule are ordered before standing before Allah. Repeated wudu can train awareness and erase sins, as the Prophet described water carrying away the traces of wrongdoing.

Completion of favour echoes verse 3. A complete religion includes concessions, not only demands. Gratitude appears through using water when able, earth when needed, and neither pridefully rejecting ease nor lazily inventing incapacity.

Verse 7 asks believers to remember this favour and the covenant in which they said, “We hear and obey.” The God who cleans outward limbs also knows what hearts contain.

=7=

“Remember Allah’s favour upon you and the covenant by which He bound you when you said, ‘We hear and obey.’ Be mindful of Allah; surely Allah knows what is hidden within the hearts.”

The believers’ covenant includes their acceptance of Islam and obedience to the Messenger. It recalls pledges such as those at Aqabah and the general commitment expressed whenever faith says sami‘na wa ata‘na. Hearing in the Quran means receiving with readiness to act.

Favour includes guidance, the Messenger, a completed religion, lawful good things, purification, security, and the community formed from former rivals. Remembering favour makes obligation feel like response to grace rather than a bargain with a stranger.

“We hear and obey” contrasts with earlier communities who said in rebellion, “We hear and disobey” (4:46). It also differs from hypocrites who said obedience before the Prophet and planned otherwise at night (4:81). Covenant is measured after the gathering ends.

Obedience is to Allah and authentic prophetic guidance. It does not make every command from a leader, parent, or institution sacred. Created authority is obeyed in what is right, never in disobedience to the Creator.

Allah knows dhat al-sudur, what breasts contain: intention, reluctance, sincerity, and the private exception a person imagines for himself. Public compliance may satisfy a court, but covenant reaches the heart.

Remembering is active. Families and communities preserve covenant through Quran, prayer, truthful history, and teaching why practices matter. A promise forgotten in consciousness is easily broken in conduct.

Taqwa guards the gap between words and action. A believer may fail after sincerely promising; repentance renews the commitment. Hypocrisy instead protects the failure with performance.

Verse 8 now applies covenant to testimony and opposition. Believers must stand firmly for Allah in justice and may not allow hatred of any people to bend the scale. Justice toward an enemy is nearer to taqwa than revenge disguised as loyalty.

=8=

“O believers, stand firmly for Allah as witnesses with justice. Do not let hatred for any people cause you to be unjust. Be just—that is nearer to mindfulness of Allah. And be mindful of Allah; surely Allah is fully aware of what you do.”

This verse complements 4:135. There, love for self, parents, rich, or poor must not distort testimony. Here, hatred must not distort it. Justice is threatened by attachment on both sides of the heart.

Qawwamin means sustained standing. A person does not become just through one courageous statement while bending in cases involving his faction. Witness is “for Allah,” so truth is not owned by tribe, nation, party, race, or religious group.

Hatred may have a real cause. The people mentioned may have harmed believers or barred them from worship. The Quran does not command pretending the wrong never happened; it forbids using anger to exceed the offender’s actual liability or harm the innocent.

Justice includes accurate quotation, evidence, proportional consequence, treaty faithfulness, and acknowledgement of good even in an opponent. Fabricating accusations because someone holds false beliefs remains falsehood. Defending a non-Muslim against a Muslim oppressor is not betrayal of Islam; it is obedience to this verse.

“Nearer to taqwa” shows that inward piety has public evidence. Ritual appearance cannot compensate for a tilted scale. The person most conscious of Allah should be hardest to recruit into collective revenge.

Modern applications include courts, journalism, academic research, policing, employment, online disputes, and war reporting. Selective images, dehumanising labels, and refusal to verify enemy casualties can become forms of injustice even when a broader cause is just.

Allah is Khabir, aware of fact and motive. He knows the accurate statement delivered reluctantly and the polished distortion defended as strategy. Fear of His knowledge is stronger protection than reputation.

Verses 9 and 10 now state the two destinations: believers who act righteously receive forgiveness and immense reward; those who reject and deny Allah’s signs become companions of the Blaze.

=9=

“Allah has promised those who believe and do righteous deeds forgiveness and an immense reward.”

Divine promise answers the cost of justice. A believer may lose advantage by telling truth against his side, but Allah promises something larger: maghfirah and ajrun ‘azim. No honest loss is absent from His account.

Forgiveness comes before reward because even righteous people carry deficiencies. Their faith and deeds do not make them independent of mercy. Allah covers faults, then grants beyond the value of limited action.

Belief and righteous deeds remain joined. Iman gives the work its foundation; conduct shows the claim living in the world. In this passage, righteous action includes contract faithfulness, lawful consumption, purification, and justice toward people one dislikes.

A promise from Allah differs from satanic promises described in the previous surah. He has perfect knowledge, never lies, and possesses the power to fulfil. Delay until the Hereafter is not uncertainty.

The verse’s brevity invites hope without turning reward into a detailed wage table. “Immense” includes Paradise, divine pleasure, security, and blessings no earthly vocabulary fully captures. The Prophet taught that Allah prepared for righteous servants what no eye has seen, ear heard, or human heart imagined.

Hope should not become entitlement. The promise names faith and action, and acceptance remains Allah’s favour. A believer works, repents, and asks rather than announcing his own final rank.

Verse 10 gives the contrast just as briefly. Rejection joined to declaring Allah’s signs false creates companionship with al-Jahim, the fiercely blazing Fire.

=10=

“But those who disbelieve and deny Our signs are the companions of the Blaze.”

Kufr here is joined to takdhib—treating divine signs as lies. The signs include revelation, prophetic proof, and the truth it explains. Persistent denial does not make reality disappear; it changes the denier’s relationship to the final destination.

Ashab means companions or people belonging to a place. They treated rejection as settled company in life and receive a settled company in the Blaze. The wording contrasts with believers promised nearness to prophets and the righteous.

The verse is a general warning, not permission to declare every named person a companion of Hell. Allah knows what evidence reached someone, the state at death, and hidden repentance. Muslims convey categories revealed by Allah and leave individual final judgment to Him unless revelation specifies it.

Fear of Hell should not produce cruelty. Believers may not burn, torture, or imitate divine punishment. Earthly dealings remain governed by law, justice, covenant, and the possibility of repentance.

The signs denied include ethical demands, not only abstract doctrines. A person may praise spirituality while refusing Allah’s right to define food, contracts, or justice. Revelation is a whole covenant.

The contrast with verse 9 makes the choice clear without suggesting equal powers. Allah’s promise and warning both come from one Judge. Satan and idols possess no competing kingdom.

Verse 11 moves from final consequence to remembered rescue. A people intended to stretch their hands against the believers, but Allah restrained them. The memory should produce taqwa and trust rather than arrogance.

=11=

“O believers, remember Allah’s favour upon you when a people intended to stretch out their hands against you, but He restrained their hands from you. Be mindful of Allah, and in Allah let the believers place their trust.”

Reports connect this verse to more than one occasion when opponents planned an attack or assassination and Allah prevented it. Possibilities include a plot against the Prophet and armed encounters in which hostile hands were restrained. The broad wording allows the community to remember divine protection across such events without depending on one uncertain report.

Protection is called ni‘mah, favour. Safety can feel ordinary after danger passes, so revelation commands memory. The believer sees not only the visible guard, negotiation, or changed plan but the Lord who made those means effective.

“Restrained their hands” does not mean believers took no precautions. The Prophet used intelligence, guards, armour, and treaties. Tawakkul is reliance upon Allah through lawful means, not the fantasy that means are unnecessary.

Memory of rescue should reduce revenge. Allah prevented harm and commanded justice in verse 8. Survivors may seek lawful accountability, but they should not invent additional enemies or treat safety as permission to oppress.

The verse also comforts people facing threats beyond their control. The same Allah can block a plan before it reaches them, expose it, or give strength to endure. Trust does not guarantee the exact rescue desired; prophets also suffered. It guarantees that no hostile hand operates outside His decree.

Taqwa comes before tawakkul in the closing. Reliance is not expecting Allah to support disobedient methods. One guards His limits and places the result with Him.

The surah now turns to an earlier covenant. Allah appointed twelve leaders among the Children of Israel and promised His special support if they established prayer, gave zakah, believed and supported His messengers, and spent sincerely.

=12=

“Allah certainly took a covenant from the Children of Israel and appointed twelve leaders among them. Allah said, ‘I am with you. If you establish prayer, give zakah, believe in My messengers and support them, and lend Allah a good loan, I will surely erase your sins and admit you into Gardens beneath which rivers flow. But whoever among you disbelieves after this has truly strayed from the level path.’”

The twelve naqibs represented the tribes and carried responsibility for knowledge, organisation, and covenant. Leadership was not a prize detached from duty. A representative helped his people fulfil what they had promised and answered for betrayal of trust.

“I am with you” is special accompaniment of support and care, conditioned here on obedience. Allah encompasses everyone in knowledge, but this ma‘iyyah promises help to those who establish the covenant.

Its terms resemble the core duties of the final community: prayer, zakah, faith in every messenger, active support for them, and generous spending. True continuity between revelations appears in worship and justice, not only shared ancestry.

“Support My messengers” requires more than claiming their names. It means believing what they actually bring, protecting their mission, and refusing to divide them according to tribe. A person who praises Musa but rejects a later messenger confirmed by revelation has not fulfilled the principle.

A good loan to Allah is wealth spent sincerely in His cause and for people He commands believers to help. Allah owns the wealth already, so “loan” honours the giver with a guarantee of repayment and increase. It must be good—lawfully earned, willingly given, and free of humiliation.

The promised return is both removal and gift: sins erased, then Gardens entered. Religious duty does not merely regulate the world; it prepares eternal companionship.

The “level path” is the balanced, straight road. Disbelief after covenant is described as losing that centre. The warning applies first to those addressed and then instructs Muslims not to repeat their breach.

Verse 13 describes consequences that befell covenant-breakers: distance from mercy, hardened hearts, displaced words, forgotten guidance, and repeated betrayal. Yet it explicitly says “except a few” and commands the Prophet toward pardon and excellence in the setting addressed.

=13=

“For breaking their covenant, We rejected them and hardened their hearts. They distort words from their proper places and forgot a portion of what they had been reminded of. You will continue to discover betrayal from them, except from a few. Yet pardon them and overlook. Surely Allah loves those who act with excellence.”

The causal phrase is essential: consequences followed breach. Ancestry did not produce hardened hearts; repeated choices did. La‘nah is distance from divine mercy, while hardness makes reminders strike without entering.

Distorting words can involve changing wording, removing context, or interpreting revelation to produce what desire already chose. Forgetting a portion is not merely failed memory; it includes neglecting guidance until its influence disappears. Distortion and neglect can reinforce each other.

The address concerns factions encountered by the Prophet, not every Jewish person. “Except a few” preserves moral distinction, and verse 12 itself recalled faithful possibilities within the covenant. Later, verse 5:69 will again judge people by faith and righteousness. Antisemitism and inherited guilt violate Quranic justice.

Betrayal required vigilance; pardon did not mean handing sensitive trusts back to an active traitor without evidence of reform. Fa‘fu wasfah can instruct restraint from retaliation, patient dealing under existing conditions, and leaving matters Allah chose not to prosecute then. The Prophet combined forgiveness with firm action when public safety required it.

Allah loves muhsinin—those who do more beautifully than bare minimum. Excellence toward someone who wronged you is not approval of wrong. It can mean proportional response, an open path to repentance, and refusal to let another person’s betrayal shape your character.

Muslims should read this as warning about their own covenant. Quran can also be pulled from context, inconvenient duties neglected, and institutional betrayal concealed. Condemning an earlier community while repeating the act misses the purpose.

Verse 14 turns to those who called themselves Christians. They too had a covenant, but forgetting part of the reminder allowed enmity and hatred to grow among them until the Day of Resurrection, when Allah will disclose what they made.

=14=

“And from those who say, ‘We are Christians,’ We took their covenant, but they forgot a portion of what they had been reminded of. So We allowed hostility and hatred to arise among them until the Day of Resurrection. Allah will inform them of what they used to do.”

The phrase “those who say” draws attention to a claimed identity measured against covenant. Calling oneself a follower of Christ does not replace following what Isa taught: worship Allah, uphold truth, and receive the messengers of the same Lord.

Forgetting part of revelation creates imbalance. One doctrine is exaggerated, another command disappears, and inherited formulations replace prophetic teaching. The result is not only private error but fractured community.

Christian history includes serious sectarian conflicts, persecution, and wars, though it also includes worshippers, peacemakers, and people acting with justice. The verse diagnoses covenantal division without authorising Muslims to flatten every Christian into hostility. Individuals and eras differ, and the Quran commands fair speech.

Allah “allowed” or cast enmity among them as a consequence. When unity around revealed truth is lost, groups may unite around opposition to each other. Theology becomes identity warfare rather than humble search. The same pattern can afflict Muslims who forget Quranic duties while preserving labels.

“Until the Day of Resurrection” indicates enduring consequences of foundational division, not that every two Christians must personally hate each other. Ecumenical cooperation can reduce worldly hostility without making contradictory creeds all true.

Allah will inform them of what they used to manufacture and practise. Historical narratives written by victors do not settle the final account. Hidden motives and forgotten victims return to complete knowledge.

The purpose is not triumphalism. Muslims are being warned in a surah opening with “fulfil covenants.” Their own sectarian hatred can be evidence of neglected revelation, ego, and injustice.

Verse 15 offers remedy rather than only criticism: the Messenger has come to the People of the Book, clarifying much that was concealed and overlooking much, and from Allah have come light and a clear Book.

=15=

“O People of the Scripture, Our Messenger has come to you, making clear much of what you concealed from the Scripture and overlooking much. There has come to you from Allah a light and a clear Book.”

The Messenger’s clarification restores truths hidden through deliberate concealment, selective teaching, or accumulated tradition. The Quran discusses tawhid, prophethood, law, and histories that correct claims made about Ibrahim, Maryam, Isa, and others.

“He overlooks much” shows revelation is not a catalogue of every textual alteration or private failure. It exposes what guidance requires and leaves many matters without public humiliation. Truth-telling can be selective by wisdom without becoming concealment.

The address remains invitational: “Our Messenger has come to you.” People with earlier scripture are not told that their whole history contained no light. They are called to the one who confirms original revelation and clarifies what human handling obscured.

“Light” has been understood as Muhammad, peace be upon him, the Quran, or the illumination sent through both; the “clear Book” is the Quran. The Book is clear in source and essential message and makes the road clear, even though some details require scholarship.

Light exposes and guides. A person can react to exposure with defensiveness or gratitude. The fact that a hidden matter is revealed may be mercy protecting future generations from inherited error.

Muslims also conceal scripture when they avoid verses that challenge culture, rulers, donors, or family practice. Possessing the final Book does not make selective silence impossible. Teachers must present truth with knowledge, context, and wisdom.

The Quran’s clarity does not authorise crude proof-texting. Language, Sunnah, related verses, and reasons of revelation help the light be received without distortion. Humility is part of seeing.

Verse 16 explains what Allah does through this light and Book: He guides those seeking His pleasure to paths of peace, brings them from layers of darkness into light, and directs them to a straight path.

=16=

“Through it Allah guides those who seek His pleasure to paths of peace, brings them out of darknesses into light by His permission, and guides them to a straight path.”

Guidance is promised to those following ridwan Allah—His pleasure. The Quran is not merely an object to use for proving an inherited opinion. Intention matters: the reader who wants Allah’s approval is prepared to be corrected.

Subul al-salam are paths of peace and safety. They include truthful belief, prayer, lawful family life, economic justice, reconciliation, and ultimately the road to Dar al-Salam, Paradise. The paths are plural in good action while converging upon one straight way.

Darknesses are plural because falsehood fragments: idolatry, ignorance, pride, injustice, despair, and desire each obscure in a different way. Light is singular because truth is coherent. Allah brings people out gradually or suddenly according to wisdom.

“By His permission” prevents worship of the means. A book physically present does not compel guidance, and even the Messenger does not control hearts. Allah opens understanding while requiring the servant to listen, reflect, and act.

The straight path is not simply inner calm. It contains commands that may initially disrupt comfort, such as abandoning interest, returning stolen wealth, or changing inherited theology. Peace with Allah can require conflict with habit before it produces settled wholeness.

Modern readers meet many sources claiming enlightenment. The Quran’s test includes tawhid, moral fruit, coherence, and fidelity to prophetic guidance. Light that flatters every appetite without accountability may be another darkness with attractive branding.

Communities can help or obstruct movement into light. Translation, accessible teaching, patient answers, good character, and protection from abuse make the path visible. Racism, corruption, and humiliation place avoidable shadows in front of seekers.

Verse 17 confronts one central darkness directly: saying Allah is the Messiah son of Maryam. The Quran answers through divine sovereignty—if Allah willed to destroy the Messiah, his mother, and everyone on earth, no one could prevent Him. The created cannot be the Creator who owns it.

=17=

“Those who say, ‘Allah is the Messiah, son of Maryam,’ have certainly disbelieved. Say, ‘Who could prevent Allah in the least if He willed to destroy the Messiah, son of Maryam, his mother, and everyone on earth?’ To Allah belongs the kingdom of the heavens and earth and everything between them. He creates whatever He wills, and Allah is capable of all things.”

The verse addresses direct identification of Allah with Isa. Islamic tawhid rejects incarnation: the eternal Creator does not become a created human, depend upon a mother, eat, sleep, or pass through bodily life. Isa is Messiah and honoured messenger, never Allah.

The argument is about dependence. Isa, Maryam, and every earthly person remain under Allah’s power. The one who can be created, sustained, raised, or taken by another cannot be that sovereign Other. Maryam’s inclusion also blocks transferring divinity through her miraculous motherhood.

“Destroy” here establishes mortality and created vulnerability, not that Allah intends to dishonour them. Both are beloved servants. Their honour exists because He grants it and cannot convert dependency into deity.

“He creates whatever He wills” answers the miracle of fatherless birth. Ordinary creation uses father and mother; Adam had neither; Hawwa came through another manner; Isa had a mother without father. Variation in method displays power rather than producing divine children.

The kingdom belongs to Allah alone. Christian formulations may seek to preserve monotheism while affirming Christ’s divinity, but the Quran judges incarnation incompatible with the distinction between Owner and owned. This disagreement should be expressed accurately and without insult.

The verse does not license mistreatment of Christians. Their lives, churches under covenant, property, and dignity are governed by justice and law. Theological clarity and peaceful coexistence are not opposites.

Muslims should also avoid language that grants saints or leaders powers belonging only to Allah. Love for creation remains inside recognition of dependency.

Verse 18 turns from divine sonship in Christian doctrine to a broader claim reportedly made by Jews and Christians: “We are Allah’s children and beloved.” The Quran tests unconditional spiritual privilege against accountability for sin.

=18=

“The Jews and Christians say, ‘We are Allah’s children and His beloved.’ Say, ‘Then why does He punish you for your sins? No—you are human beings from among those He created. He forgives whom He wills and punishes whom He wills. To Allah belongs the kingdom of the heavens and earth and everything between them, and to Him is the final return.’”

“Children” here can express a claimed special closeness rather than literal biological sonship in every speaker’s belief. The Quran answers the claim of guaranteed beloved status: communities themselves acknowledged divine punishment in their history, so love cannot mean exemption from moral account.

“You are human beings among those He created” restores equality of origin. Revelation may give a community a trust and honour, but chosenness is mission and responsibility, not a different species of soul. A covenant broken can become evidence against the one entrusted.

The statement reports a boast attributed to representatives or doctrines among the communities; it should not be used to claim every contemporary Jew and Christian uses these exact words. Quranic argument addresses the idea of exclusive saving status wherever it appears.

Muslims are equally forbidden to convert “best community” into unconditional immunity. Surah 4:123 already rejected salvation by Muslim wishful thinking or scriptural wishful thinking. Honour depends on faith, obedience, repentance, and Allah’s mercy.

“He forgives whom He wills and punishes whom He wills” is not arbitrary favouritism. His will operates with complete knowledge and justice. He has told people the paths of repentance and warning while retaining sovereignty over final judgment.

Ownership is repeated because no group owns the Judge. Heaven, earth, and the space between belong to Him, and all return to Him. Ancestors and institutions cannot create an alternate court.

The verse should produce humble invitation. Correct theology is not a licence to claim personal acceptance. Every believer asks for forgiveness and fears a bad end.

Verse 19 announces that the Messenger came after a long gap in prophethood so the People of the Book could not say no bearer of good news or warner had reached them. The period of interruption ends in renewed clarity.

=19=

“O People of the Scripture, Our Messenger has come to you making the truth clear after an interval between messengers, so you cannot say, ‘No bearer of good news or warner came to us.’ Now a bearer of good news and a warner has come to you. And Allah is capable of all things.”

Fatrah is an interval in the succession of messengers. Roughly six centuries separated Isa and Muhammad, peace be upon them, though communities retained scriptures and traditions during that period. Human alteration and dispute made final clarification an immense mercy.

The Messenger is both bashir and nadhir. Good news without warning can make people complacent; warning without good news can produce despair. Muhammad announces forgiveness and Paradise and warns of rejection and judgment, continuing the dual mission described in 4:165.

The arrival removes a general excuse after the message is clearly conveyed. It does not authorise believers to assume every individual today received an accurate, understandable presentation. Allah knows whether someone encountered revelation or only a hostile distortion.

“Making clear” includes clarifying concealed scripture, restoring tawhid, explaining lawful and unlawful, and distinguishing Isa’s true honour from slander and divinity. Bayan is a service to people, not merely victory in debate.

Allah’s power is shown by reviving prophethood after a long interval, choosing an unlettered Arab messenger, preserving the final Book, and carrying it beyond its first land. Human institutions did not control where revelation could appear.

Muslims living after the final Messenger bear responsibility to transmit without claiming his infallibility for themselves. Translation, education, conduct, and honest response to questions can convey; coercion and misrepresentation can obscure.

The verse follows theological correction with a historical lesson from Musa. Verse 20 begins his speech to his people: remember Allah’s favour in placing prophets among you, granting autonomy and leadership, and giving your generation blessings not given to others of its time.

=20=

“Remember when Musa said to his people, ‘O my people, remember Allah’s favour upon you when He raised prophets among you, made you people of authority, and gave you what He had not given anyone else in the world at that time.’”

Musa begins with memory before command. The people had emerged from Pharaoh’s enslavement, witnessed deliverance, and received prophetic guidance. Gratitude should prepare them for the difficult instruction to enter the sacred land in the next verse.

Many prophets arose among the Children of Israel, an extraordinary concentration of revelation. This was honour and responsibility. Their stories show that proximity to prophets does not remove the need to obey each new command.

“Made you kings” has been explained as granting actual rulers among them and, more broadly, freedom and household independence after slavery. A person with home, spouse, and servant was described in early explanation as possessing a king-like degree of autonomy. The central contrast is liberation from Pharaoh’s ownership.

“What He had not given anyone else” refers to the worlds or peoples of their time: deliverance through the sea, manna and quails, shade, scripture, and many prophets. It cannot mean no later community could receive another distinction, since the Quran calls Muhammad’s faithful community to its own responsibility.

Remembering favour should not become ethnic pride. Musa ties blessing to Allah, not biological superiority. Gifts call for obedience; they do not guarantee it. The following narrative will show people refusing despite extraordinary signs.

Muslims should read this history as a mirror. They too possess scripture, prophetic teaching, and forms of freedom many others lacked. Gratitude appears through courage and covenant, not merely praising past favour.

Leaders can learn from Musa’s address. Before asking exhausted people to carry responsibility, remind them truthfully of resources, identity, and prior help from Allah. Encouragement should not hide the challenge but place it within remembered grace.

The next file begins with verse 21, where Musa tells his people to enter the sacred land Allah assigned for them and warns them not to turn back and become losers. Their response will reveal how quickly remembered favour can be overcome by fear.
