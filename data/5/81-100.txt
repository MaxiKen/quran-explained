=81=

“Had they truly believed in Allah, the Prophet, and what was revealed to him, they would not have taken those disbelievers as protecting allies. But many of them are defiantly disobedient.”

The verse completes the argument from verse 80. Some among the scriptural community aligned with pagan or hostile forces against the Messenger, even though his tawhid was closer to their prophetic heritage than idolatry. Genuine faith would have restrained that betrayal.

“The Prophet” has been understood as Muhammad, peace be upon him, whom they were called to recognise, while “what was revealed to him” is the Quran. It can also expose inconsistency toward their own claimed prophetic faith: belief in Allah’s revelations cannot coexist comfortably with alliances designed to destroy their confirming message.

Awliya again means protective and strategic allegiance in the context of hostility, not every friendship, trade, treaty, or act of civic cooperation. Al-Ma’idah itself permits the food of the People of the Book and marriage to chaste women among them. The Quran cannot be interpreted as contradicting itself within the same passage.

The problem is using hostile power against believers and truth. A Muslim can likewise betray faith by supporting an oppressor simply because the oppressor offers status or security. Labels do not purify an unjust alliance.

“Many,” not all, are called fasiqun. The Quran preserves individual distinction even during sharp communal criticism. Some People of the Book believed, acted justly, or maintained peaceful covenants. Ethnicity is not the cause of rebellion.

Faith has public consequences. It shapes whose aggression one supports, whose secrets one protects, and whether worldly fear can override moral commitment. Yet ordinary Muslims should judge proved conduct and not claim access to another’s hidden belief.

The next verse compares patterns of response among communities in the Prophet’s setting. It describes severe hostility from particular Jews and polytheists and greater openness among Christians, attributing that openness to devoted priests, monks, and freedom from arrogance. It is a contextual moral description, not a racial law fixing every person forever.

=82=

“You will surely find the most intense in hostility toward the believers to be some Jews and the polytheists, and you will surely find the nearest in affection toward the believers to be those who say, ‘We are Christians.’ That is because among them are priests and monks, and because they are not arrogant.”

The verse describes the Prophet’s experienced landscape and dominant responses around him. Certain Jewish leadership factions in Madinah entered conflict and alliances against the Muslims, while the pagan Quraysh had persecuted them openly. By contrast, Christian figures such as the Negus of Abyssinia and sincere delegations listened with humility.

This is not an eternal racial ranking. The wording concerns observable hostility and affection, and later history has included Jewish people who protected Muslims, Christians who persecuted them, and every other variation. Quranic judgment follows faith and conduct, not DNA.

Nor does the verse erase the covenants and upright individuals among Jews already acknowledged. “Some Jews” in translation conveys the contextual group even where the Arabic communal noun is broad; the Quran elsewhere says they are not all alike (3:113).

The Christian openness is connected to qissisin and ruhban—priests, learned devotees, and monks—and especially to lack of pride. Their disciplined worship and knowledge could prepare them to recognise a messenger rather than view him only as a threat to status.

Islam does not institute monasticism as the normal Muslim path, but it can acknowledge sincerity and humility within another community. The problem in religious leadership is not holding a title; it is arrogance that refuses evidence.

Affection is praised without saying Christian doctrine is fully correct. Verses 72-75 have just rejected deifying Isa and the Trinity. A person can be nearer in manners and openness while still needing theological correction.

Muslims should neither weaponise this verse against Jews nor use it to declare modern Christian political powers automatically trustworthy. Alliances and relations are assessed by actual justice, covenant, and hostility. The moral key offered by the verse is humility.

Verse 83 shows that humility in action. When these listeners hear the Quran, their eyes overflow with tears because they recognise truth, and they ask to be recorded among its witnesses.

=83=

“When they hear what has been revealed to the Messenger, you see their eyes overflowing with tears because they recognise the truth. They say, ‘Our Lord, we believe, so record us among the witnesses.’”

The listeners do more than feel moved by beautiful sound. Tears follow ma‘rifah—recognition of truth. Their scriptural knowledge, humility, and conscience meet the Quran and identify continuity with what Allah genuinely revealed before.

Reports connect these verses to Christians associated with the Negus or a delegation who heard Quran recited. The exact group is less important than the response: listening, recognition, tears, faith, and willingness to testify.

Eyes “overflow” rather than merely moisten, showing emotion that cannot remain contained. Yet tears alone are not proof of guidance. People cry at poetry, memory, and performance. The next words and later conduct reveal what the emotion means.

They address Allah directly: Rabbana, our Lord. Recognition of the Messenger leads not to worship of him but to the Lord who sent him. Their declaration of faith becomes du‘a for acceptance.

“Record us among the witnesses” may mean witnesses to the truth, members of Muhammad’s community that bears witness to humanity, or those testifying that the prophets carried one essential message. Faith asks to join responsibility, not only receive comfort.

A reader should not manufacture tears or judge a dry-eyed listener insincere. Temperaments differ. The essential quality is a heart willing to recognise and submit. Emotion is a gift when it serves that direction.

The verse also models receiving correction from outside one’s inherited circle. These Christians do not treat new revelation as insult to their history; they see its truth as fulfilment. Humility allows identity to grow around evidence.

Verse 84 records their reasoning: why should they not believe in Allah and the truth that reached them while hoping their Lord will admit them among the righteous?

=84=

“‘Why should we not believe in Allah and the truth that has come to us, while we hope that our Lord will admit us among the righteous?’”

Their question exposes the irrationality of refusing recognised truth. Once they know the Quran comes from Allah, social cost, inherited boundary, and fear of leaders cannot justify rejection. Faith becomes the only honest response.

They say “the truth that has come to us,” treating revelation as a gift arriving at their door. Truth is not owned by one ethnicity. The person who receives it is responsible to welcome it, whether or not ancestors did.

Hope for admission among salihin joins creed to community and character. They do not ask merely to win a theological dispute; they want the company of people made sound by obedience. Their former label is less important than their final companionship.

“Why should we not?” can help someone confronting fear of conversion or reform. What actually prevents obedience—evidence, or loss of reputation, family approval, and familiarity? Practical costs may be real and require support, but they do not change truth.

Their hope remains humble. Recognition and belief do not produce a demand that Paradise is owed. They ask their Rabb to admit them, knowing acceptance is mercy.

Muslim communities should make space for such people through education, safety, friendship, and patience with cultural transition. Celebrating conversion publicly without supporting the convert privately can leave serious needs unmet.

The verse applies to Muslims receiving a neglected command too. Once truth becomes clear, “my family has always done otherwise” is not a sufficient answer. The road of the righteous may require changing inherited practice.

Verse 85 gives Allah’s response: because of what they said, He rewards them with eternal Gardens and calls that the reward of those who act with excellence.

=85=

“So Allah rewarded them for what they said with Gardens beneath which rivers flow, remaining there forever. That is the reward of those who act with excellence.”

Their words mattered because they expressed recognised truth, sincere faith, and readiness to join the righteous. Speech arising from the heart and followed by commitment can change eternal destiny.

Gardens and flowing rivers answer the tears of verse 83. Tears shed from recognition in a brief earthly gathering lead to joy without grief in an enduring home. No sacrifice made for faith is forgotten.

“Because of what they said” does not mean a formula earns Paradise regardless of reality. The Quran repeatedly joins statements to belief and action. Allah knows whether the declaration is sincere; the previous passage exposed people who said “we believe” while entering and leaving with disbelief.

They remain khalidin—secure forever. Earthly conversion may cost familiar community or status, but the promised community and home do not expire. This gives courage without denying present hardship.

Muhsinin do what is good with excellence and awareness of Allah. These listeners showed ihsan in honest listening, humility, and response. The label is not reserved for people born inside the Muslim community.

The verse also shows that clear theological correction need not produce hardness. The same surah that strongly rejects Trinity warmly honours Christians who recognise truth. Da‘wah combines conviction, affection for guidance, and reward.

No human can certify a particular person’s final acceptance merely from emotional speech. The promise belongs to those Allah describes, and He alone knows hearts and endings.

Verse 86 gives the opposite outcome in one line: those who disbelieve and deny Allah’s signs become companions of the Blaze. Recognition followed by faith and rejection followed by denial do not lead to the same place.

=86=

“But those who disbelieve and deny Our signs are the companions of the Blaze.”

Kufr is joined to takdhib, active denial of divine signs. This is more than lacking information; it describes a settled rejection of what Allah sent. The general category is clear, while Allah alone knows the proof and final state of individuals.

Ashab al-Jahim are people belonging to the fiercely kindled Fire. The contrast with eternal Gardens is deliberate. The Quran does not treat faith and denial as equally valid routes simply because both can be held sincerely.

The warning follows an account of humble Christians so no group imagines ancestry determines destiny. A person from a criticised community can enter Paradise through truth, and someone surrounded by believers can deny signs and face the Blaze.

Muslims should convey this without delight in punishment. The Prophet grieved over rejection and prayed for guidance. Fear for others should motivate clear, kind invitation; fear for oneself should motivate repentance.

The verse does not authorise earthly harm against people of other beliefs. Their security, property, worship, and civil dealings remain under justice and covenant. Final punishment is Allah’s jurisdiction.

Denial can also appear practically when someone professes Islam but treats clear boundaries as meaningless. That does not allow careless takfir, but it calls the heart to examine whether submission is real.

Verse 87 returns to believers and warns against another kind of religious deviation: forbidding wholesome things Allah made lawful. Piety does not consist of inventing deprivation.

=87=

“O believers, do not forbid the wholesome things Allah has made lawful for you, and do not transgress. Surely Allah does not love transgressors.”

Reports say some companions considered severe ascetic practices—abandoning meat, marriage, sleep, or ordinary comforts—to devote themselves entirely to worship. The Prophet, peace be upon him, rejected this path and taught that he fasted and broke fast, prayed and slept, and married; turning from his balanced Sunnah was not his way.

Only Allah has the right to define halal and haram. A person may voluntarily leave a lawful item for health, discipline, budget, or personal taste, but may not declare it religiously forbidden without evidence. Personal abstention and legislation are different.

Tayyibat are wholesome lawful gifts. Enjoying them with gratitude can be worship. Islam does not treat the body, marriage, good food, or rest as enemies of spirituality. They become harmful through excess, forbidden method, or neglect of duty.

“Do not transgress” guards the other extreme. Permission is not command to consume without limit. Lawful food can be wasted, lawful wealth hoarded, and lawful intimacy pursued unjustly. The balanced believer neither invents prohibition nor turns permission into appetite without restraint.

Vows to avoid something lawful may have legal consequences, but they do not change its ruling for everyone. Oaths are addressed in verse 89, including expiation when a deliberate oath is broken.

Communities should avoid purity competitions in which cultural strictness is presented as superior faith. Caution can be valid in doubtful matters, but another person following a sound permissive opinion should not be shamed.

Allah does not love mu‘tadin, people crossing limits—even when they cross under the banner of devotion. Love of Allah is sought by following His measure rather than performing hardship He did not ask for.

Verse 88 gives the positive rule: eat from what Allah provided as lawful and wholesome, and maintain taqwa toward the One in whom you believe.

=88=

“Eat from the lawful and wholesome things Allah has provided for you, and be mindful of Allah in whom you believe.”

Halal concerns legal permissibility; tayyib adds goodness, cleanliness, safety, and suitability. Something may belong to a lawful category while being spoiled, contaminated, stolen, or harmful in a particular circumstance. Believers seek both qualities.

Provision is attributed to Allah even when earned through work. He created ability, land, rain, markets, and opportunity. Gratitude therefore accompanies effort and blocks the arrogance of imagining oneself the independent provider.

“Eat” is permission and encouragement to receive divine gifts. Religious guilt should not be attached to ordinary lawful enjoyment. The Prophet accepted good food, praised Allah, and shared it without making luxury the purpose of life.

Taqwa governs source, amount, and consequence. Was the income lawful? Were workers treated fairly? Is food wasted while neighbours hunger? Does consumption harm health or prevent worship? The same meal can be gratitude or heedlessness according to context.

The verse does not require every preferred modern ethical standard to be declared legally obligatory. Environmental care, animal welfare, and fair labour are serious concerns, but specific rulings need evidence and practical knowledge. Moral aspiration and halal certification answer related but distinct questions.

Eating together also carries social mercy. Verse 5 allowed food exchange with the People of the Book. Hospitality can build trust without erasing religious boundaries.

Belief is named at the end: the One whose provision is eaten is the One whose limits are feared. Selective gratitude that enjoys gifts while rejecting the Giver’s law is incomplete.

Verse 89 now regulates oaths. Allah does not call people to account for unintentional expressions, but deliberate sworn commitments carry responsibility and, when broken, a structured expiation serving the poor or freeing a person, with fasting for one unable.

=89=

“Allah will not hold you accountable for unintentional oaths, but He will hold you accountable for oaths you deliberately bind. The expiation is to feed ten poor people from the average food you provide your own families, or clothe them, or free an enslaved person. Whoever cannot afford that must fast three days. That is the expiation for your oaths when you have sworn. Guard your oaths. This is how Allah makes His signs clear to you so that you may be grateful.”

Laghw includes expressions that pass on the tongue without deliberate intent to bind, or an oath honestly thought true but mistaken, according to juristic explanations. Allah does not turn ordinary speech habits and genuine error into the same liability as a purposeful covenant.

A mu‘aqqadah oath is consciously tied in Allah’s name to doing or leaving something possible in the future. Breaking it requires kaffarah. The believer does not use Allah’s name casually and then pretend words have no weight.

The first options are feeding ten poor people at the average level used for one’s family, clothing them appropriately, or freeing an enslaved person. “Average” prevents offering contemptible scraps while also not requiring a feast beyond means. Jurists explain quantities and what counts as clothing according to law and custom.

With lawful slavery no longer existing, emancipation is not a presently available option. Feeding or clothing remain. Only someone unable to perform the material options moves to fasting three days; legal schools differ over whether they must be consecutive.

If an oath commits sin, it must not be fulfilled. The Prophet taught that whoever swears an oath and sees something better should do the better thing and expiate. No vow makes family severance, injustice, or neglected obligation righteous.

“Guard your oaths” means avoid frequent swearing, speak truth, fulfil lawful commitments, and expiate responsibly when breaking one. It also means do not let stubborn pride in an oath block a better action.

The expiation turns personal failure into food, clothing, or liberation for others. Mercy repairs covenant through social good, while fasting trains the person without means.

Verse 90 moves to things that corrupt judgment and society more broadly: intoxicants, gambling, idolatrous altars, and divining arrows are filth from Satan’s work and must be avoided for success.

=90=

“O believers, intoxicants, gambling, stone altars, and divining arrows are only filth from Satan’s work, so avoid them completely so that you may succeed.”

Khamr covers what intoxicates and clouds the mind, not only grape wine. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said every intoxicant is khamr and every intoxicant is forbidden. Modern drugs and substances are judged by intoxicating effect, medical use, harm, and necessity—not marketing names.

Maysir is gambling: wealth transferred through games of chance in which gain depends on another’s loss without a productive exchange. It includes many betting systems, lotteries, and casino products. Legitimate prizes funded independently and skill competitions follow different rules and require careful conditions.

Stone altars and divining arrows connect the practices to pagan worship and superstition. Animals were sacrificed at idol sites, and arrows supposedly decided travel, marriage, or responsibility. Islam replaces divination with evidence, consultation, du‘a, and istikharah.

Rijs is moral filth, and “from Satan’s work” identifies the force that decorates it. Intoxicants can appear as relaxation and gambling as quick opportunity, but the attractive beginning hides loss of reason, wealth, family safety, and worship.

“Fajtanibuhu” is stronger than merely “do not drink” or “do not bet”: keep to one side of it. This includes avoiding enabling environments, production or promotion where directly assisting the sin, and triggers that threaten recovery, according to capacity and legal detail.

Addiction requires treatment and compassion alongside moral clarity. A dependent person may need medical detoxification, counselling, peer support, family boundaries, and repeated repentance. Shame without care can drive the illness underground.

Medicines containing alcohol or controlled substances require a separate assessment of intoxicating effect, dose, available alternatives, and genuine medical need. Necessary treatment is not recreational intoxication, but commercial use of the word “medicine” does not automatically make a harmful substance lawful. Qualified clinical and religious advice should work together.

The verse completed the gradual prohibition of alcohol after earlier revelation identified more sin than benefit and barred intoxicated prayer. Mature law now commands avoidance. Verse 91 explains why: Satan uses intoxicants and gambling to create hostility and block remembrance and prayer.

=91=

“Satan only intends to cause hostility and hatred among you through intoxicants and gambling and to turn you away from remembering Allah and from prayer. Will you not then stop?”

The verse exposes both social and spiritual harms. Intoxication lowers restraint and amplifies insult, violence, unsafe behaviour, and family conflict. Gambling produces resentment because one person’s thrill is another’s loss, often followed by debt, concealment, and desperate attempts to recover money.

Satan “intends” these outcomes even when the participant seeks only entertainment. Intention of the user does not erase the structure and predictable harms of the practice. A pleasant first experience can still open the intended road.

Dhikr and salah are specifically targeted. Intoxicants remove awareness; gambling captures attention with odds, scores, and the next wager. Time, money, and heart that should return regularly to Allah become attached to a cycle of craving.

“Will you stop?” is a question demanding decisive response. When companions heard the prohibition, they poured out their wine. They did not ask how close to intoxication they could remain while technically complying.

Abstinence at community level still needs wise policy. Prevention, treatment, enforcement against dangerous conduct, support for families, and lawful recreation all matter. Prohibition stated without alternatives or care may leave vulnerable people isolated.

The verse’s reasoning remains visible in modern data about domestic violence, road deaths, addiction, and gambling debt, but Muslims obey even where an individual claims to avoid these outcomes. Law addresses the substance and practice as well as observed harm.

Hostility can also emerge among those arguing about sinners. Correction should not produce contempt that blocks repentance. Satan would gladly use opposition to wine to create another form of hatred.

Verse 92 commands obedience to Allah and the Messenger, caution against turning away, and reminds believers that the Messenger’s duty is clear conveyance. Accountability now belongs to the hearer.

=92=

“Obey Allah, obey the Messenger, and remain cautious. If you turn away, then know that Our Messenger’s duty is only clear conveyance.”

Obedience to Allah and the Messenger is the natural response to completed prohibition. The Messenger does not merely recommend avoiding wine; his authentic command carries the authority Allah granted.

“Ihdharu”—be cautious—warns against relapse, rationalisation, and the approaches to prohibited practices. A person can accept the rule verbally while keeping every trigger and social pressure unchanged. Caution turns belief into practical safeguards.

If people turn away, they do not defeat the message. The Prophet’s duty is balagh mubin: delivery clear enough that the command, reason, and consequence are understood. He cannot force obedience into the heart.

Clear conveyance also sets the standard for teachers. They should distinguish intoxicating substances from legitimate medicine, gambling from lawful competition, and sin from the person needing help. Confused or exaggerated teaching is not “clear.”

The limitation on the Messenger’s responsibility does not erase his lawful public leadership or enforcement. It means ultimate guidance and personal choice belong to Allah and the individual. Coercive power alone cannot create taqwa.

For families affected by addiction, the verse offers boundaries. They can convey, protect finances and safety, arrange help, and refuse enabling. They cannot control every choice or carry all blame for another adult’s refusal.

Verse 93 addresses concern about what believers consumed before the prohibition. There is no blame for past consumption upon those who believed, acted righteously, and continued growing in taqwa, faith, and excellence once the ruling became known.

=93=

“There is no blame upon those who believe and do righteous deeds for what they consumed in the past, provided they remain mindful of Allah, believe, and do good; then continue in mindfulness and faith; then deepen their mindfulness and excellence. Allah loves those who act with excellence.”

When wine was prohibited, companions worried about believers who had died after drinking it when it was still permitted. The verse removes retroactive guilt. Allah does not punish people for violating a prohibition that had not yet been revealed to them.

“What they consumed” does not create immunity for future drinking. The conditions repeatedly emphasise taqwa, faith, good action, and ihsan—qualities demonstrated by accepting the new ruling. Using the verse to excuse known prohibition reverses its meaning.

The repeated progression has been explained in several ways: guarding oneself regarding the past and present, remaining faithful as new commands arrive, and rising from compliance to excellence. At every stage, taqwa remains alive rather than being a one-time achievement.

Allah loves muhsinin, people who do what is right beautifully. Leaving an addiction may involve more than avoiding a substance: repairing family harm, paying debts, supporting others in recovery, and replacing old habits with beneficial life.

The verse establishes the principle that law is not retroactively sinful before communication, while some acts such as injustice can remain wrong through earlier known revelation and natural conscience. Specific legal application requires knowledge.

It also comforts converts. Lawful marriages, food, and ordinary acts before guidance are not all reopened as guilt. Islam erases prior disbelief, while unresolved human rights still deserve restoration.

Taqwa after revelation matters more than obsessive fear over an innocent past. Satan can use scrupulosity to distract from present obedience.

The surah now returns to ihram. Allah will test pilgrims with game coming within reach of hands and spears, making unseen obedience visible. Deliberate violation brings painful consequence.

=94=

“O believers, Allah will certainly test you with game that comes within reach of your hands and spears, so Allah may make evident those who fear Him unseen. Whoever transgresses after this will have a painful punishment.”

The test concerns pilgrims forbidden to hunt land game while in ihram. Animals became unusually accessible, close enough for small game to be taken by hand and larger game by spear. Ease of violation reveals whether restraint depends only on difficulty.

Allah already knows every heart. “So He may know” means manifesting that knowledge in lived action, establishing the servant’s proof and allowing character to be formed and judged through choice.

Fear “in the unseen” is central. No human guard may notice a quick capture, but the pilgrim is in sacred covenant. Taqwa operates where enforcement ends.

A test often places a desired thing within reach rather than removing it. Money can be unmonitored, a message private, or game abundant. Availability is not permission. The believer asks what Allah said, not what circumstances made easy.

Hunting itself is normally lawful and was permitted after ihram in verse 2. The test is respecting time and state. Sacred discipline trains people not to treat every capability as a right.

“Painful punishment” warns deliberate transgression after clarity. The next verse specifies a worldly expiation for intentionally killing game, while final judgment for contempt or repeated violation remains with Allah.

Modern pilgrims may not hunt, yet they still encounter sanctuary rules about animals, plants, crowds, and conduct. Ihram is not merely clothing; it is a state of restraint from argument, sexual activity, grooming acts, and hunting according to law.

Verse 95 gives the remedy: an equivalent domestic animal judged by two just people and offered at the Ka‘bah, or feeding poor people, or corresponding fasting, so the offender tastes the consequence. Allah pardons what occurred before the ruling.

=95=

“O believers, do not kill game while you are in the sacred state. Whoever among you kills it intentionally must compensate with an equivalent domestic animal, judged by two just people among you, to be delivered as an offering to the Ka‘bah; or make expiation by feeding poor people; or fast an equivalent number of days, so they may taste the consequence of their deed. Allah has pardoned what occurred before. Whoever repeats the offence, Allah will take retribution from them. Allah is Almighty, Owner of Retribution.”

The prohibition concerns wild land game, not ordinary livestock slaughtered lawfully for pilgrims. Intention is specified. Accidents and uncertainty have separate juristic treatment, while deliberate killing triggers the described consequence.

Two just assessors determine an equivalent from grazing livestock. Early companions gave practical comparisons—larger domestic animals for larger game and smaller for smaller—rather than allowing the offender to price his own violation. Fair assessment prevents convenience from defining repentance.

The animal is a hady reaching the Sacred House for sacrifice and distribution. The alternatives involve feeding poor people or fasting corresponding days, with detailed calculation and whether the choices are unrestricted discussed among legal schools. A pilgrim should consult qualified Hajj guidance rather than improvise.

The expiation makes the offender “taste” consequence through property, food, or disciplined hunger. It is corrective rather than permission to purchase hunting rights. Someone planning to violate and simply pay has missed the meaning.

“Allah pardoned what occurred before” protects acts predating the rule. A completed religion does not invent retroactive guilt. Once the command is known, repetition becomes defiance and may receive divine retribution beyond the calculable expiation.

The closing name Dhu Intiqam belongs to Allah, not angry pilgrims. No individual may retaliate against a suspected hunter, seize property, or invent a fine. Authorities and qualified adjudication handle violations.

Conservation appears within worship: animals enjoy special security around a pilgrim whose desires are restrained. The rule teaches that human access to creation is stewardship rather than unrestricted domination.

Verse 96 distinguishes sea from land. Sea catch and food are lawful for pilgrims and travellers, while land game remains prohibited during ihram. All will be gathered before Allah.

=96=

“Lawful to you is the catch of the sea and its food as provision for you and for travellers, but forbidden to you is land game as long as you remain in the sacred state. Be mindful of Allah, to whom you will all be gathered.”

Sayd al-bahr is what is caught alive from the sea, while ta‘amuhu has been understood to include what the sea casts out or preserved seafood used as travel provision. The Sunnah states that the sea’s water is purifying and its dead creatures lawful, with juristic differences over particular species among legal schools.

The allowance serves residents and travellers. Seafood can be caught, carried, dried, and eaten without violating ihram. Divine law narrows one category while leaving another open, showing that sacred discipline is not needless starvation.

Land game remains forbidden “as long as” ihram continues. When the state ends, the ordinary permission returns. A temporary restriction should not become a permanent taboo, and permanent appetite should not erase temporary holiness.

The ruling concerns hunting, not every interaction with land animals. Dangerous animals, domestic livestock, insects, and accidental harm have their own detailed rules. Pilgrims should learn rather than generalise.

Modern questions include commercial fishing, environmental damage, endangered species, and national law. Basic permissibility does not cancel bans needed to prevent ecological harm or theft of public resources. Cooperation in righteousness includes sustainable use.

Taqwa and gathering close the verse. A person may be far from human surveillance on sea or desert, but every act returns to one assembly. Pilgrimage rehearses that gathering through simple clothing and shared movement.

Verse 97 now describes the Ka‘bah, sacred month, offerings, and garlands as supports Allah made for people. Their ordered sacredness demonstrates His knowledge of heaven, earth, and all things.

=97=

“Allah has made the Ka‘bah—the Sacred House—a source of stability for people, along with the sacred month, sacrificial offerings, and garlanded animals. This is so you may know that Allah knows whatever is in the heavens and earth and has perfect knowledge of all things.”

Qiyaman lil-nas means a means by which people stand: religiously, socially, and materially. The Ka‘bah gives one direction to prayer, one centre to pilgrimage, and a sanctuary around which diverse peoples meet under shared rules.

The sacred months create protected time, while offerings and garlands create protected provision visibly dedicated to worship. Arabia’s tribes recognised these signs, allowing travel and trade where ordinary conflict made movement dangerous. Revelation purified and regulated their purpose.

The Ka‘bah supports economy through pilgrimage, but commerce is not its highest reason. Worship, remembrance, equality, and fulfilment of Ibrahim’s call remain central. Exploiting pilgrims through fraud or inaccessible excess contradicts qiyam.

Sacred symbols do not protect wrongdoing automatically. A person cannot harm others and hide behind sanctuary or religious appearance. Law determines how security and accountability coexist.

These institutions display Allah’s knowledge of human need. People require place, time, visible signs, food, security, and communal rhythm. The One who knows heaven and earth designed worship for embodied social creatures.

Knowledge “of all things” includes what pilgrims conceal, the sincerity of offerings, and the authorities managing the sanctuary. Sacred administration is an amanah, not private prestige.

Muslims face the Ka‘bah but worship the Lord of the Ka‘bah. Stone, cloth, and direction are honoured symbols; none receives divine worship. Umar’s statement to the Black Stone—that it could neither harm nor benefit independently—preserved this balance.

Verse 98 tells believers to know both that Allah is severe in punishment and that He is Forgiving and Merciful. Sacred law is guarded by fear and entered through hope.

=98=

“Know that Allah is severe in punishment, and that Allah is also All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

Both truths begin with “know.” Sound faith does not select the divine description that matches temperament. The careless need warning; the broken need hope; every believer needs both.

Severity follows deliberate hunting, corruption, intoxication, and covenant-breaking discussed throughout the surah. Allah’s patience in the world should not be mistaken for inability or approval.

Forgiveness and mercy remain equally stated. The hunter can expiate, the addict repent, the oath-breaker repair, and the former disbeliever believe. Revelation repeatedly opens return after naming punishment.

Fear without hope can become despair, scrupulosity, or a harsh religion projected onto others. Hope without fear can become entitlement and delay. Together they create responsible tawbah.

The order places punishment first in a legal context, then gives the final emotional word to mercy. Yet neither cancels the other. Allah forgives according to wisdom and punishes without injustice.

Teachers should preserve this balance. Using only threats may control outwardly while damaging hearts; using only comfort may leave people in danger. Prophetic teaching diagnosed the listener and conveyed the whole truth.

A victim may find mercy language painful when offenders use it to escape accountability. Divine forgiveness does not automatically erase human rights. A sinner proves hope through restitution and reform.

Verse 99 clarifies the Messenger’s responsibility: only clear delivery rests upon him, while Allah knows everything people reveal and conceal. The hearer cannot blame the Messenger for a chosen response.

=99=

“The Messenger’s duty is only to convey clearly, and Allah knows what you reveal and what you conceal.”

Balagh is not casual mention. The Messenger explains, demonstrates, warns, gives good news, and delivers without concealment. Once that trust is fulfilled, he is not responsible for forcing the result.

“Only” limits responsibility for inner guidance, not his roles as teacher, judge, and leader. It should not be used to erase the Sunnah’s practical authority. It frees him from ownership of hearts.

Allah knows public declaration and private intention. A person may appear obedient while planning violation, or struggle outwardly while hiding sincere faith and effort. Human judgment remains limited to evidence; divine judgment is complete.

The verse encourages callers who blame themselves for every refusal. They should review whether conveyance was accurate and wise, apologise for their own failures, and continue where useful. They cannot make another adult believe.

It also warns callers against manipulation. Since Allah knows concealment, inflated conversion numbers, emotional coercion, and selective presentation cannot produce real success. Clear truth is the duty.

For listeners, the limitation removes excuses. The Messenger’s inability to force belief does not make the message optional. Allah knows what was understood and why it was rejected.

Every Muslim conveys something through conduct. Honesty can clarify the Quran; hypocrisy can obscure it. No ordinary believer becomes a messenger, but all should fear misrepresenting the message.

Verse 100 concludes this file with a standard of value: corrupt and wholesome are not equal, even when the abundance of corruption impresses. People of understanding must practise taqwa to attain true success.

=100=

“Say, ‘The corrupt and the wholesome are not equal, even if the abundance of corruption impresses you.’ So be mindful of Allah, O people of understanding, so that you may succeed.”

Khabith and tayyib cover belief, money, food, speech, people’s deeds, and every morally evaluated thing. Quantity does not erase quality. A large profit from interest is not equal to modest lawful income; widespread vice does not become wholesome through popularity.

Abundance can impress because numbers look like proof. Followers, revenue, votes, luxury, and repetition create social authority. Digital recommendation systems intensify the illusion by showing the most repeated or provocative material until popularity feels like consensus. The Quran teaches ulu al-albab—people of core understanding—to look beneath scale at source and destination.

The verse does not say everything rare is good or everything common evil. Truth is not decided by minority identity either. Revelation and sound evidence determine tayyib, while quantity is morally secondary.

A believer may be tempted to use a corrupt means because “everyone does it” or because lawful alternatives grow slowly. Taqwa accepts less with blessing over more carrying poison. The Hereafter reveals the actual balance sheet.

This principle connects the file’s themes. Sincere tears outweigh proud majorities; lawful food outweighs abundant prohibition; one guarded oath outweighs many dramatic false oaths; a clean pilgrimage outweighs easy game taken in violation.

People of understanding combine intellect with purified judgment. Cleverness that maximises khabith is not Quranic intelligence. Lub is the sound core freed from distraction.

Falah—complete success—comes through taqwa, not merely accumulation. A wholesome small beginning can grow by Allah’s blessing, while corrupt abundance can collapse in one judgment.

The next file begins with verse 101, warning believers not to ask unnecessary questions whose answers would burden them. Sound understanding seeks guidance, but curiosity that manufactures difficulty can turn a complete, balanced religion into self-imposed hardship.
