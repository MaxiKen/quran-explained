=101=

“O believers, do not ask about matters which, if disclosed to you, would trouble you. If you ask about them while the Quran is being revealed, they will be made clear to you. Allah has pardoned what has passed, and Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Forbearing.”

The verse does not condemn learning. The Quran repeatedly tells people to ask those who know when they do not know. It warns against unnecessary questions that manufacture hardship, expose what Allah left concealed, or challenge revelation rather than seek guidance.

Authentic reports mention people asking the Prophet, peace be upon him, provocative personal questions, including a man demanding to know his father, until the Prophet became visibly displeased. Another report links the warning to asking whether Hajj was required every year; repeated insistence could have produced an obligation people could not bear.

During revelation, certain questioning had a unique danger. If people kept demanding a detailed rule, Allah could reveal one and turn open ease into binding duty. Silence in a matter can itself be mercy, not an omission believers must fill with prohibition.

This does not justify avoiding questions needed for worship, safety, consent, finance, or another person’s rights. “Do not ask” should never be used by leaders to block accountability or by families to hide abuse. The distinction is between beneficial inquiry and curiosity that burdens, humiliates, or tests authority without sincere purpose.

Examples include asking for private details that do not affect a ruling, demanding speculative descriptions of the unseen, or pursuing hypothetical edge cases until basic obedience is forgotten. Modern search culture can reward sensational answers while neglecting clear duties.

“If you ask while the Quran is being revealed, they will be clarified” also reminds the first audience that prophetic revelation could answer publicly and decisively. After the Prophet’s death, no new revelation appears; questions are addressed through Quran, Sunnah, evidence, and qualified reasoning.

Allah has pardoned what He left unlegislated or what passed before clarification. Al-Halim does not rush to punish every mistake. Believers should imitate that restraint by not converting every preference into religious law.

Verse 102 points to earlier communities that asked demanding questions and then rejected the answers. The problem was not lack of detail but a heart unwilling to carry the detail once given.

=102=

“People before you asked such questions, then became disbelievers because of the answers.”

Earlier communities sometimes demanded signs, details, and exceptions as conditions for obedience. When the requested clarity came, the answer increased responsibility, and some rejected it. Questioning became a road away from faith rather than toward it.

The Children of Israel’s questions about the cow in Surah al-Baqarah illustrate how repeated narrowing can make a simple command harder. Instead of acting, people can use questions to postpone action until compliance feels impossible.

This does not mean every person who asks many questions resembles them. Scholars grow through precise inquiry, and companions asked thousands of beneficial questions preserved in the Sunnah. Intention, subject, manner, and response to the answer distinguish learning from evasion.

A dangerous pattern appears when someone asks several scholars until one permits what desire already chose. The issue is not comparing evidence or seeking a second opinion; it is treating answers as products and hiding relevant facts until a desired ruling appears.

Another pattern asks for miracles or philosophical certainty while refusing moral truths already understood. New evidence rarely satisfies a person whose real objection is the cost of surrender.

“Kafirin because of them” may mean they rejected the disclosed rulings, mocked them, or turned the questioning itself into disbelief. It is a warning about consequence, not a rule that burdensome curiosity automatically expels a Muslim from faith.

The two verses preserve a religion that is clear without being needlessly invasive. Allah explains what people need, the Prophet teaches it, and responsible scholarship applies it. Humility accepts that not every unseen detail or hypothetical requires an answer.

Verse 103 gives a concrete example of humans inventing religious restrictions Allah never made: pagan categories of livestock declared sacred through superstition. Their false prohibitions were attributed to God without knowledge.

=103=

“Allah has not instituted the bahirah, sa’ibah, wasilah, or ham. Rather, the disbelievers fabricate lies against Allah, and most of them do not understand.”

These were pre-Islamic livestock categories surrounded by invented taboos. A bahirah might be a she-camel whose ear was slit after particular births; a sa’ibah released for an idol; a wasilah protected through a birth pattern; and a ham a male camel exempted after breeding. Exact tribal definitions varied, which reinforces their human origin.

The animals were deprived of normal use in the name of religion, and their supposed sacredness could benefit custodians or idol practices. Allah denies instituting any of them. Cultural age and ritual complexity do not make a rule divine.

The deeper sin is iftira’—fabricating against Allah. A person may choose not to eat an animal for personal reasons, but saying “Allah forbade this” requires evidence. Verse 87 already prohibited believers from forbidding wholesome lawful things.

Most did not use reason because they inherited a system without asking whether revelation supported it. Social repetition made nonsense feel sacred. Understanding in the Quran includes moral courage to test ancestral practice.

Modern equivalents can arise when communities attach divine status to lucky objects, invented food restrictions, ceremonial animals, dates, colours, or family taboos. Not every tradition is forbidden; a neutral custom becomes dangerous when assigned unseen power or falsely attributed to Allah.

Animal welfare is not the target. Islam can restrict use to prevent cruelty or ecological harm through genuine legal principles. The verse rejects superstitious consecration, not responsible care.

Religious teachers should distinguish “haram,” “disliked,” “unwise,” and “not my preference.” Collapsing every category into prohibition trains people to speak about Allah without knowledge.

Verse 104 shows the response when such people are invited to revelation and the Messenger: inherited ancestral practice is enough for them, even if their ancestors lacked knowledge and guidance.

=104=

“When they are told, ‘Come to what Allah has revealed and to the Messenger,’ they reply, ‘What we found our forefathers practising is enough for us.’ Even though their forefathers knew nothing and were not guided?”

Tradition can carry wisdom, language, family connection, and faithful practice. The Quran does not command contempt for ancestors. It rejects making ancestry an authority that can overrule revelation.

Their answer says “enough.” They do not evaluate what Allah sent because inherited familiarity already feels complete. The rhetorical question exposes the flaw: what if the ancestors lacked both knowledge and guidance?

Respecting parents does not require repeating their errors. Ibrahim challenged his father’s idolatry while speaking with dignity. A convert can reject a family creed without becoming cruel to family members.

Muslims can commit the same fault when a Quranic inheritance share, marriage right, or prohibition conflicts with “how our people do it.” An Islamic family name does not transform culture into Sunnah.

Not every innovation in worldly life is blameworthy simply because ancestors lacked it, and not every old practice is wrong. The criterion is evidence and moral fit with revelation, not age in either direction.

Calling people to “Allah and the Messenger” requires accurately representing both. A reformer’s personal preference does not become revelation merely because he opposes culture. Scholarship must distinguish authentic Sunnah, valid juristic diversity, and local custom.

The question also honours reason. Faith is not blind rejection of ancestry for another blind following. People examine source, knowledge, and guidance and then submit where truth becomes clear.

Verse 105 now tells believers to guard themselves. Once they are rightly guided, those who stray cannot ultimately harm their faith. Yet this does not cancel the duty to advise and oppose wrong according to capacity.

=105=

“O believers, you are responsible for your own souls. Those who stray cannot ultimately harm you when you are rightly guided. To Allah you will all return, and He will inform you of what you used to do.”

The verse establishes personal accountability. A believer cannot make his own obedience depend on the crowd’s response. If others reject after clear, wise effort, their choice does not transfer guilt automatically.

Abu Bakr warned people not to misuse this verse. He heard the Prophet say that when people see wrongdoing and fail to change it despite ability, a broad punishment may reach them. Being “rightly guided” includes enjoining good, forbidding wrong, and fulfilling communal responsibilities.

The verse therefore opposes both extremes: obsessive control of other adults and selfish withdrawal from society. Guide yourself, advise with wisdom, protect rights, and recognise where your authority ends.

“Cannot harm you” does not mean misguidance causes no worldly damage. Crime, propaganda, and corruption can injure communities. It means another person cannot destroy the guided soul’s final standing merely by refusing truth, provided the believer has fulfilled his own duties.

Self-care here is spiritual before it is individualistic: faith, prayer, knowledge, lawful earnings, repentance, and protection from corrupt influence. A person busy cataloguing everyone else’s sins can neglect the soul actually entrusted to him.

The common return prevents isolation from becoming indifference. Victim and offender, adviser and advised, will all receive a complete account. Allah will disclose deeds without social media speculation or factional editing.

Parents and callers can find relief here. They should love, teach, set lawful boundaries, and keep doors open, but they are not the creator of guidance. Grief should not turn into manipulation or self-destruction.

Verses 106-108 move into a technical case of testimony over a bequest when death approaches during travel. The law protects estates through witnesses, oaths, and a remedy if the first witnesses are discovered dishonest.

=106=

“O believers, when death approaches one of you while making a bequest, let two just men from among you bear witness, or two others from outside your community if death comes while you are travelling. If you doubt them, detain the witnesses after prayer and let them swear by Allah: ‘We will not sell our testimony for any price, even for a close relative, nor conceal Allah’s testimony. Otherwise we would certainly be sinners.’”

Travel once made death especially difficult: family was absent, property carried by hand, and only strangers might witness final instructions. The Quran provides a procedure rather than leaving heirs dependent upon informal trust.

“From among you” is commonly understood as Muslim witnesses, while “others” can mean non-Muslims available during travel. Some jurists interpret the phrase differently, but the verse at minimum shows practical protection of rights when ideal witnesses are unavailable.

The witnesses must be people of justice where possible. Their task is not to benefit from the estate but to preserve the deceased’s declaration and belongings accurately. A bequest does not override fixed inheritance shares or exceed its lawful limit.

If suspicion arises, the witnesses are held after prayer—a solemn time and public setting—and swear. The oath names three temptations: payment, family favouritism, and concealment. Even a close relative cannot purchase distortion.

“Allah’s testimony” describes entrusted evidence as belonging to Him. A witness does not own the facts and may not trade them. False testimony is not merely private dishonesty but corruption of divine justice.

Modern documentation—signed wills, inventories, secure records, video where lawful, and professional custody—can reduce disputes while fulfilling the verse’s purpose. Digital evidence still requires authentication and protection against coercion.

The testimony concerns a valid bequest and entrusted property, not permission to rewrite the fixed shares revealed in Surah al-Nisa. Funeral expenses and debts are settled, lawful bequests are implemented within their limits, and the remaining estate passes to its heirs. Witnesses preserve that order rather than becoming distributors by personal preference.

This procedure should be applied through qualified legal and inheritance guidance. The verse does not authorise relatives to detain or intimidate witnesses privately. Courts determine doubt and administer oaths.

Verse 107 describes what happens if the witnesses are discovered to have committed breach: two people from those with the strongest claim replace them and swear that their testimony is truer.

=107=

“If it is discovered that the two witnesses were guilty of breach, then let two others from those most directly entitled to the estate take their place and swear by Allah: ‘Our testimony is truer than theirs, and we have not transgressed. Otherwise we would certainly be wrongdoers.’”

The law does not treat the first oath as beyond review. New evidence can expose betrayal, and a procedure allows those whose rights were harmed to answer. Religious solemnity is not a shield against factual correction.

Reports connect these verses to property entrusted during travel, part of which was concealed by two companions of the deceased. When the missing item appeared, the heirs’ claim required a structured oath. The story illustrates cross-community testimony and restoration without making every traveller suspect.

The replacement witnesses come from those awla—closest or strongest in the right, commonly the heirs affected by the breach. They do not simply accuse; they swear to the truth of their counterclaim and deny transgression.

“Our testimony is truer” responds directly to the first witnesses’ account. The second oath is not a popularity vote. It operates after evidence indicates wrongdoing and under adjudication.

The procedure balances finality with correction. Courts need judgments to end disputes, but a fraudulently obtained result should not become sacred merely because an oath was once spoken.

The heirs are also warned. Discovering one lie does not permit exaggerating the loss or punishing beyond their right. Their own oath ends with fear of becoming wrongdoers.

Modern probate systems express the same values through appeals, forensic accounting, asset tracing, and penalties for perjury. Technical tools change, but the covenant of testimony remains.

Verse 108 explains the procedural wisdom: it makes truthful testimony more likely and makes witnesses fear that later oaths will expose and overturn their own.

=108=

“This procedure makes it more likely that they will give true testimony, or fear that other oaths may be taken after theirs. Be mindful of Allah and listen attentively. Allah does not guide defiantly disobedient people.”

Law considers incentives as well as ideals. A witness should tell truth for Allah alone, but the possibility of review and rebuttal discourages someone tempted by hidden profit. Good procedure supports conscience.

The first witnesses know their oath may not be the final word if evidence emerges. That accountability makes careless or false testimony riskier. Transparency and appeal can therefore strengthen, rather than undermine, trust.

“This is more likely” acknowledges human systems do not guarantee perfect outcomes. Revelation provides the best means under limited evidence, while final certainty belongs to Allah. Courts should be humble about what procedure can achieve.

Taqwa is still essential. Rules can be gamed by determined liars, but no one escapes Allah. The command “listen” means receive the law with acceptance and pay attention to testimony rather than approaching a case with a predetermined verdict.

The closing on fisq warns that persistent defiance deprives people of guidance even when procedures are clear. A person who swears falsely is not protected by having followed the outward form.

Families should not treat estate disputes as minor because they occur after death. The deceased can no longer defend instructions, and grief can increase suspicion. Documentation, calm adjudication, and fear of Allah protect kinship.

The surah now shifts from human witnesses to the greatest gathering of witnesses. On the Day Allah assembles the messengers and asks how their peoples responded, they submit that full knowledge belongs to Him, Knower of the unseen.

=109=

“On the Day Allah gathers the messengers and asks, ‘How were you answered?’ they will say, ‘We have no complete knowledge. You alone are the Knower of all unseen things.’”

Allah does not ask because He lacks information. The question publicly establishes testimony and reveals the gravity of how revelation was received. Messengers who were denied, worshipped, or selectively followed stand before their Sender.

Their answer expresses awe and humility. They knew outward responses during their lives, but not every hidden motive, later distortion, or final outcome after death. Compared with Allah’s complete knowledge, their knowledge is as nothing.

Some commentators also describe the terror of the Day temporarily overwhelming ordinary recollection before Allah enables full testimony. Either way, the verse magnifies divine omniscience and prophetic servanthood.

Prophets do not claim control over followers after their missions. Communities may use their names for centuries while teaching what they never authorised. Allah knows the entire chain from first hearing to final alteration.

This corrects worship of messengers. If they defer knowledge of hidden realities to Allah, they cannot be omniscient gods. Their honour is in truthful conveyance and humble witness.

The question also reaches the Muslim community: how was Muhammad answered? Recitation and celebration are not the whole response. Judgment will concern belief, obedience, character, and whether the message was represented faithfully.

Ordinary callers should imitate prophetic humility. They know what people say outwardly but should not narrate hidden motives as fact. Allah alone knows unseen hearts.

Verse 110 begins Allah’s detailed reminder to Isa of favours granted to him and Maryam: support through the Holy Spirit, speech in cradle and maturity, scripture and wisdom, miracles performed repeatedly “by My permission,” and protection from enemies.

=110=

“Remember when Allah will say, ‘O Isa son of Maryam, remember My favour upon you and your mother: how I supported you with the Holy Spirit so you spoke to people in the cradle and in maturity; how I taught you writing, wisdom, the Torah, and the Gospel; how you shaped from clay the form of a bird by My permission, breathed into it, and it became a bird by My permission; how you healed the one born blind and the leper by My permission; how you brought the dead forth by My permission; and how I restrained the Children of Israel from harming you when you came to them with clear proofs and the disbelievers among them said, “This is nothing but obvious magic.”’”

The repeated “My favour” and “by My permission” define every miracle. Isa is a magnificent messenger and sign, but not an independent god. The power belongs to Allah, who grants the act.

Ruh al-Qudus is widely understood as Jibril, who supported Isa in his mission. Speaking in the cradle defended Maryam and announced servanthood; speaking in maturity continued prophetic teaching. The two stages show consistent truth across his life.

Allah taught him writing or scripture, hikmah, Torah, and Gospel. Knowledge is bestowed, not innate omniscience. He confirms earlier revelation and receives his own.

The clay bird begins with Isa shaping material, but life occurs only by permission. Healing congenital blindness and leprosy addressed conditions beyond ordinary medicine of the time. Bringing the dead forth is the clearest possible reminder that even an extraordinary act remains delegated.

Muslims affirm these miracles without forcing them into naturalistic explanations. Miracles are signs of the One who suspends ordinary patterns. At the same time, later miracle claims by religious figures require evidence and never establish divinity.

Allah restrained hostile Israelites, confirming that they did not kill Isa. “The disbelievers among them” preserves distinction; not every Israelite rejected him. Those who did called signs magic because acknowledging them would demand submission.

The repeated qualification “by My permission” is a rule for interpreting miracles. A prophet may perform what ordinary people cannot, yet the act proves his truthfulness as Allah’s messenger rather than independent ownership of divine power. Removing that qualification turns a sign pointing toward Allah into a reason for worshipping the servant who carried it.

The verse honours Maryam alongside her son. Protection of her reputation and the miraculous birth are favours forming part of Isa’s mission.

Verse 111 adds another favour: Allah inspired the disciples to believe in Him and His messenger. They declared faith and asked that their submission be witnessed.

=111=

“And remember when I inspired the disciples: ‘Believe in Me and in My messenger.’ They replied, ‘We believe, so bear witness that we are Muslims—those who submit.’”

The Hawariyyun were Isa’s devoted disciples and helpers. Inspiration here is not necessarily prophetic revelation; Allah can place firm guidance in the hearts of non-prophets, as He inspired Musa’s mother and the bee in different senses.

Their faith has two objects in proper order: Allah and His messenger. Believing Isa means following him as messenger, not making him a partner with the One who sent him.

“We are Muslims” uses the universal meaning of Islam: submission to Allah. The disciples before Muhammad followed the Islam appropriate to Isa’s mission. After the final Messenger, submission includes receiving the completed revelation.

They ask Allah—or Isa as immediate witness—to bear witness to their surrender. Faith wants its inner reality known by Allah and its public covenant clear among people. This differs from hypocrites who use the same language without heart.

The verse shows that disciples’ genuine response was monotheistic. Later theology cannot be projected backward as if Isa explicitly taught them to worship him; the Quran records their submission to Allah.

Inspiration does not remove choice. They respond, “We believe.” Guidance from Allah and responsible human acceptance coexist.

Muslim communities can learn from the disciples’ support. Prophetic work requires helpers who combine belief, service, and testimony without turning loyalty to a leader into worship.

Verse 112 introduces the event giving the surah its name: the disciples ask Isa whether his Lord will send down a table spread from heaven. Isa warns them to fear Allah if they are believers.

=112=

“Remember when the disciples said, ‘O Isa son of Maryam, can your Lord send down to us a table spread with food from heaven?’ He replied, ‘Be mindful of Allah if you are truly believers.’”

Al-Ma’idah is the table or spread of food from which the surah takes its name. The disciples ask for a tangible sign and provision from heaven after already declaring faith.

The wording “can your Lord” can sound like doubt about divine power. Another accepted recitation carries the sense, “Can you ask your Lord?” Commentators also understood the common reading as asking whether He will do it, not denying ability. Their explanation in verse 113 suggests a desire for reassurance rather than atheistic challenge.

Isa nevertheless warns them to have taqwa. Demanding extraordinary signs after evidence is dangerous. A miracle increases certainty but also increases accountability. The people of Musa had seen signs and still disobeyed.

Believers may ask Allah for reassurance, as Ibrahim asked how the dead are revived while already believing (2:260). The manner matters: humble request differs from testing Allah or conditioning obedience on spectacle.

Food from heaven also recalls manna and quails. Physical provision can be a sign, yet gratitude must survive after hunger ends. Miracles do not automate faithful character.

“Isa son of Maryam” again keeps the Messiah within human lineage while they ask him to petition his Lord. He is mediator as messenger making du‘a, not the source of the table.

The verse should make people cautious about chasing miracles, visions, and supernatural claims when Quranic guidance is already clear. Sensational experience can distract from ordinary obedience.

Verse 113 records the disciples’ purposes: to eat, reassure their hearts, know with certainty that Isa told the truth, and become witnesses to the sign.

=113=

“They said, ‘We wish to eat from it, reassure our hearts, know with certainty that you have told us the truth, and become witnesses to it.’”

Their reasons mix bodily need and spiritual reassurance. Wanting to eat is not automatically unworthy; humans need food, and a heavenly meal would be provision as well as sign. Revelation does not pretend bodies are irrelevant to faith.

“Reassure our hearts” resembles Ibrahim’s request for tranquillity. Faith can seek increased certainty without beginning from denial. The disciples have already said “we believe,” yet want direct witnessing to strengthen the heart.

“To know you told us the truth” may mean knowledge by immediate sight added to knowledge based on testimony. Levels of certainty can deepen: hearing reliable news, seeing, and directly experiencing are not psychologically identical.

They also accept responsibility to be witnesses. A sign is not private entertainment. Once seen, they must testify truthfully and live according to what it establishes.

Isa’s warning remains relevant because mixed motives can become dangerous. A person may call curiosity “reassurance” while continually demanding more signs. At some point, faith must act on evidence already sufficient.

The verse allows believers to pray for increased certainty through Quran, reflection on creation, righteous experience, and answered du‘a. It does not guarantee requested spectacles or make their absence a reason to doubt.

Communities should also recognise the relationship between material care and spiritual openness. Feeding people does not replace teaching, but hunger can occupy a heart. The table combines both.

Verse 114 gives Isa’s supplication. He asks Allah to send the table as a recurring celebration for the first and last of his people, a sign, and provision, declaring Allah the best of providers.

=114=

“Isa son of Maryam prayed, ‘O Allah, our Lord, send down to us a table spread from heaven to be a festival for the first and last of us, a sign from You, and provide for us. You are the best of providers.’”

Isa addresses Allah directly: Allahumma Rabbana. The one who asks another for provision is not that Provider. His du‘a itself refutes later deification.

An ‘id is a recurring occasion of return, gathering, and joy. Isa asks that the sign become a celebration for the first witnesses and those after them who remember it. Worshipful festivals are defined by revelation, not invented through every remarkable event.

The table would be ayatan minka—a sign from You. Its value lies not only in food but in what it proves about Allah’s power, generosity, and Isa’s truthful mission. A sign should point beyond itself.

“Provide for us” accepts bodily dependence without shame. Allah is Khayr al-Raziqin, the best provider. Humans provide only from resources He creates and can fail; His treasury and knowledge do not.

The Quran does not specify the menu. Later reports list fish, bread, fruit, or other foods, but none is needed for faith. Concentrating on the menu can repeat the curiosity warned against in verse 101 while missing covenant.

Isa includes himself among “us,” again standing within the needy community. Prophetic honour and dependence coexist.

The du‘a models asking: name Allah, identify lawful need, connect it to worship, and recognise His supreme attribute. It does not command Allah or claim entitlement.

Verse 115 records Allah’s promise to send the table and a uniquely severe warning: whoever disbelieves after this unprecedented sign will receive a punishment unlike that given to others of the world.

=115=

“Allah answered, ‘I will send it down to you. But whoever among you disbelieves afterward, I will punish them with a punishment unlike that given to anyone else in the world.’”

Allah grants the request. The future-form promise is certain, and the mainstream reading understands the table was sent. Some early discussion questioned whether the disciples withdrew the request from fear, but the verse’s direct promise supports fulfilment.

Extraordinary proof creates extraordinary responsibility. A person who eats from a heavenly sign and then knowingly rejects has crossed a boundary different from someone acting amid limited evidence. Punishment corresponds to clarity and betrayal.

The warning does not mean those people are more powerful than every later tyrant. It means a distinctive punishment suited to their uniquely witnessed sign. Allah alone knows its form and application.

This principle appears throughout scripture. Blessing is not only comfort; it is trust. Knowledge, access to prophets, wealth, and answered prayer all increase what will be asked of a person.

Muslims should not demand dramatic signs casually. If Allah answered every test people proposed, their burden might become greater than they understood. The Quran itself is a sufficient enduring sign calling for response.

The verse combines generous provision and severe covenant in one event. The table is mercy to believers and evidence against deliberate rejection. Al-Ma’idah as a surah likewise joins lawful food, covenants, and accountability.

Verse 116 moves to the Day of Judgment, where Allah publicly asks Isa whether he told people to take him and his mother as gods besides Allah. Isa glorifies Allah and denies ever saying what he had no right to say.

=116=

“On the Day Allah will say, ‘O Isa son of Maryam, did you tell the people, “Take me and my mother as gods besides Allah”?’ He will reply, ‘Glory be to You! It was not for me to say what I had no right to say. Had I said it, You would certainly have known it. You know what is within me, while I do not know what is within You. You alone are the Knower of all unseen things.’”

Allah knows Isa never made the claim. The question publicly vindicates the messenger and confronts those who attributed deification to his teaching. Judgment reveals truth before creation.

Mentioning Maryam addresses forms of excessive veneration alongside worship of Isa. It does not require every Christian creed to formally name Mary as a person of the Trinity. Mainstream Christians do not describe her that way, though devotional practices have varied across communities. The Quran’s exact charge is broader and clearer: neither Isa nor Maryam may receive worship besides Allah.

The public question also gives those who deified Isa no opportunity to claim his approval. The very messenger around whose name doctrine developed stands before them denying its source in his teaching.

Isa begins with subhanak—You are utterly above partners and false claims. He does not first defend his reputation; he declares Allah’s transcendence.

“It was not mine to say” combines fact and right. A prophet speaks only what Allah authorises. Deification was never a hidden teaching later disciples were meant to discover.

“If I had said it, You would know” places all speech under divine knowledge. Isa cannot conceal a sentence, let alone a doctrine. His innocence does not depend on missing records.

“You know what is within me; I do not know what is within You” establishes the infinite difference between servant and Lord. Isa’s knowledge is granted and limited; Allah’s knowledge encompasses inner and unseen reality. The language does not imply a physical interior in Allah like creation.

This scene warns Muslims against attributing claims to Muhammad he never made. Loving a prophet means staying within his authentic words, not elevating him through inventions.

Verse 117 continues Isa’s testimony: he commanded only worship of Allah, watched his people while among them, and after Allah took him, Allah alone remained the complete Watcher.

=117=

“‘I told them only what You commanded me: “Worship Allah, my Lord and your Lord.” I was a witness over them while I remained among them. When You took me, You alone were the Watcher over them, and You are Witness over all things.’”

Isa’s message is stated without ambiguity: worship Allah, who is both his Lord and theirs. Shared servanthood joins messenger and audience before one God. No trinitarian or incarnational command appears.

“I told them only” shows prophetic trust. A messenger does not add divinity for himself, conceal Allah’s sole right, or allow admiration to redefine the mission. Revelation delivered is revelation preserved.

While present among his people, Isa witnessed their outward response and taught correction. After Allah mutawaffika—took him—his direct earthly oversight ended. In light of 4:157-158 and authentic hadith, Muslims understand that Allah raised him and that he will later return; the verb does not require that his enemies killed him.

Allah is al-Raqib, the constant Watcher. Communities may change a prophet’s teachings after he departs, but the Lord never leaves, dies, or loses the record. Religious institutions cannot hide alteration behind historical distance.

Isa’s knowledge has boundaries. He does not claim detailed control over centuries of Christians or awareness of every later prayer. This is another proof of servanthood.

Every teacher should recognise the same boundary on a smaller scale. One is responsible for accurate instruction and reasonable care, not permanent control of students after departure. Allah alone watches every outcome.

Verse 118 ends Isa’s submission concerning his followers. If Allah punishes, they are His servants; if He forgives, He remains the Almighty, All-Wise. Isa neither dictates mercy nor objects to justice.

=118=

“‘If You punish them, they are Your servants; and if You forgive them, You alone are the Almighty, All-Wise.’”

Isa returns the entire case to Allah. The people who worshipped him are still Allah’s creation and property; their rebellion did not transfer ownership to Isa or another deity.

“If You punish” acknowledges perfect authority and justice. Isa does not claim his love for followers entitles him to cancel judgment. A true messenger is loyal first to Allah’s truth.

“If You forgive” affirms that mercy is also sovereign. Al-‘Aziz has power to punish and cannot be pressured; al-Hakim places forgiveness and punishment with flawless wisdom. Forgiveness is not weakness or disregard for truth.

The verse does not assert that unrepented shirk at death will in fact be forgiven, since the Quran elsewhere says it will not. It expresses Isa’s complete submission to Allah’s decision and refusal to claim independent intercession or knowledge of every individual case.

Some prophetic reports mention Muhammad reciting this verse through the night while praying and weeping for his own community, reflecting a messenger’s concern without challenging divine judgment. Prophetic mercy directs people to Allah rather than making prophets rival judges.

Believers should avoid using the verse to demand a particular person’s damnation or salvation. They state revealed principles, pray for the living, and trust the Judge who knows access, repentance, and hearts.

The names also comfort victims: forgiveness will never be foolish or unjust, and punishment will never be uncontrolled. Both belong to Might joined with Wisdom.

Verse 119 announces the judgment of the scene: this is the Day when the truthful benefit from their truthfulness. Their reward is eternal Gardens, Allah’s pleasure, and the great triumph.

=119=

“Allah will say, ‘This is the Day when the truthful benefit from their truthfulness. They will have Gardens beneath which rivers flow, remaining there forever. Allah is pleased with them, and they are pleased with Him. That is the great triumph.’”

Isa’s truthfulness stands publicly vindicated, and every truthful servant benefits from a life aligned with reality. Lies can bring temporary safety, but only truth travels safely into this Day.

Sidq includes truthful creed, speech, intention, promise, and conduct. A person can say a technically true sentence for a deceptive purpose; Quranic truthfulness joins tongue to heart and act.

Gardens and rivers provide enduring created delight. Above them is ridwan Allah—Allah’s pleasure. In an authentic hadith, Allah tells the people of Paradise He will grant them something greater: His pleasure and never becoming angry with them afterward.

“They are pleased with Him” means every grief, unanswered question, sacrifice, and decree is understood within complete mercy. No resentment or fear remains. The servant’s heart reaches final contentment.

This pleasure is not earned by flawless performance. Truthful believers repent from failures and enter through mercy. Their truthfulness keeps bringing them back rather than protecting a false image.

“The great triumph” corrects worldly measures. Status gained through concealment, invented religion, or false testimony is loss; a difficult confession that preserves faith can be victory. The surah’s covenants culminate in truth.

The verse also encourages institutional honesty. Communities need not hide errors to protect religion; Islam is protected by truth, while cover-ups protect people and power. Admission and reform can be acts of sidq.

Verse 120 closes Al-Ma’idah with universal ownership and power. Heaven, earth, and everything within belong to Allah, and He is capable of all things. The One who sent food, law, prophets, and judgment owns the complete story.

=120=

“To Allah belongs the kingdom of the heavens and earth and everything within them, and He is capable of all things.”

The surah ends where every covenant begins: Allah owns. Food is His provision, bodies His creation, the Ka‘bah His sanctuary, revelation His speech, and judgment His right. Human authority is stewardship inside His kingdom.

Isa and Maryam are included among “everything within them.” Their honour is immense, but ownership excludes divinity. The closing sentence answers the surah’s theological debate without repeating it: the Messiah belongs to Allah; Allah does not belong to a larger category.

Mulk includes possession, command, and governance. No priest, scholar, ruler, tribe, or market can create a rival moral sovereignty. Their legitimate authority remains delegated and accountable.

Allah’s complete capability explains the table from heaven, Isa’s miracles by permission, resurrection, forgiveness, punishment, and eternal Gardens. None strains Him or requires a partner.

For believers, ownership creates responsibility and relief. They must fulfil contracts because property and speech are trusts, yet they need not control the universe because its King already does. Tawakkul is possible because the Owner is capable.

The final verse is also a warning against selective submission. The One obeyed in prayer is the same One who rules finance, testimony, food, family, alliance, and public justice. Religion cannot be confined to ritual while His kingdom is treated as secular property.

Al-Ma’idah began, “Fulfil your commitments,” and closes with the sovereign before whom every commitment is judged. Between them, believers learned that lawful things must not be forbidden, sacred things must not be violated, enemies must not erase justice, and messengers must not be worshipped. The surah’s table is abundant, but every gift carries covenant.
