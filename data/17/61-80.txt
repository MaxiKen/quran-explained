=61=

The surah now turns to the story of the primordial test, a narrative that explains the root of human struggle with Satan and arrogance. “And remember when We said to the angels, ‘Prostrate before Adam,’ so they all did—but not Iblîs.

” This command was a prostration of honour and greeting, not worship, directed to Adam as a being endowed with knowledge that even the angels did not possess. The angels, being obedient by nature, all fell prostrate, demonstrating their complete submission to Allah’s will.

However, Iblîs, though not an angel but a jinn who had reached their ranks through devotion, refused. His refusal is captured in his protest: “Should I prostrate to the one You have created from mud?” This question reveals the disease of his heart. He looked at the outward material—mud—and deemed it inferior to his own origin of smokeless fire. He compared himself to Adam and saw only the physical, completely ignoring the divine spirit breathed into Adam and the knowledge Allah had granted him. His argument was based on a false sense of superiority, a racist or classist logic that elevates one created element over another.

This is the first sin: pride. Iblîs disobeyed a direct divine command because of arrogance. The verse sets the stage for the enmity between Iblîs and humanity. It teaches us that the test of obedience often lies in accepting what Allah honours, even when our limited perception sees otherwise. Adam’s creation from mud was not a demerit but a divine choice; honour comes from Allah’s command, not from material origin. The prostration of the angels shows that rank in creation is not about substance but about divine will and the knowledge and potential Allah places within.

For believers, the lesson is to eradicate any shred of arrogance from the heart, because it was arrogance that transformed a devout worshipper into an accursed rebel. It also warns against judging people by their origins, race, or wealth, for honour belongs only to the pious.

=62=

Iblîs continues his defiant speech, exposing his envy and his evil plan. He says, “Do you see this one you honoured above me?

” This is the language of contempt. He refers to Adam, whom Allah had honoured with knowledge and the prostration of angels, with scorn. He cannot bear that someone he considers inferior should be elevated.

This is the essence of envy: resenting the favour Allah bestows upon another, desiring its removal, and questioning divine wisdom. Then, instead of repenting, Iblîs makes a brazen request: “If you delay my end until the Day of Judgment, I will certainly take hold of his descendants, except for a few.” He asks for respite, not to mend his ways, but to dedicate his entire existence to leading humanity astray. His statement “I will certainly take hold of his descendants” reveals his single-minded mission.

The word “take hold” implies a fierce, determined grip, leading them off the straight path. He acknowledges that there will be “a few” whom he will not be able to reach—the sincere, chosen servants of Allah, those protected by their pure faith.

This is a chilling declaration of war. It informs every human being that from the moment we enter this world, we have an enemy who has sworn to destroy us. Iblîs is not a myth; he is a real, active force, and his tactics are perfectly tailored to our weaknesses.

However, the verse also contains a profound hope: Iblîs himself admits his inability to overpower the “few.” This means that true sincerity and devotion to Allah create a fortress that Satan cannot breach. The believer is thus forewarned and forearmed. Knowing the enemy’s plan is half the victory.

The verse also illustrates the danger of responding to divine correction with arrogance instead of repentance. Adam and Eve, after their slip, repented and were forgiven, while Iblîs, when given his moment of trial, chose pride and eternal damnation. The choice between these two responses defines the spiritual destiny of every soul.

=63=

Allah’s response to Iblîs’s insolence and challenge is immediate and decisive. “Be gone!” translates “idhhab,” a command of expulsion, driving him away from the divine mercy and from his elevated station. It is not just a physical departure but a spiritual banishment.

The word carries the weight of rejection, disgrace, and the beginning of his status as the accursed. Then Allah pronounces the divine verdict linking the tempter and the tempted: “Whoever of them follows you, Hell will surely be the reward for all of you—an ample reward.” This establishes the principle of shared responsibility and shared punishment. Iblîs will not bear the sins of humanity without them bearing their own; those who follow him willingly are partners in the crime and thus partners in the punishment.

The phrase “all of you” ties them together. The word “reward” is used ironically here, as a fitting recompense for evil deeds. The description of Hell as “an ample reward” indicates that it will be a full, complete, and utterly sufficient punishment, leaving nothing out. Every act of disobedience, every whispered temptation, will be fully compensated with torment. This verse underscores Allah’s perfect justice. No one is forced into Hell; they choose it by following the call of Satan over the call of Allah. Iblîs’s plea for respite was granted, but his ultimate end is sealed.

The verse also provides a powerful deterrent for the believer: every time we are tempted, we should recall that following that temptation links our fate to that of the arch-enemy. The idea of being grouped with Iblîs for an “ample reward” in Hell should send a shudder through the heart and motivate a swift retreat from sin. It teaches that divine justice does not allow anyone to blame their misguidance solely on Satan; personal choice remains central. The expulsion of Iblîs and the warning of a shared doom set the stage for the detailed list of seductions that follow.

=64=

In this verse, Allah describes the extensive arsenal granted to Iblîs in his mission to lead people astray, though always within the bounds of a test. “And incite whoever you can of them with your voice.” The “voice” of Satan includes every whisper, every seductive song, every false call to immorality, every misleading ideology. It is the internal suggestion and the external propaganda. “Mobilize against them all your cavalry and infantry” is a powerful military metaphor, suggesting an all-out, organized war. Satan’s forces include both human and jinn devils, each with specialized roles, attacking from every direction.

The next tactic is even more personal: “manipulate them in their wealth and children.” Satan shares in their wealth by enticing them to earn it through unlawful means, to withhold obligatory charity, or to spend it on sinful pursuits. He shares in their children by encouraging evil upbringing, neglect of religious instruction, or even inspiring the naming of children after idols. He also manipulates through the sorrow of losing children, pushing people to question divine wisdom.

The final tool is “and make them promises.” Satan promises poverty to those who give charity, fame to the immodest, and pleasure without consequence to the sinner. But Allah immediately exposes the reality: “But Satan promises them nothing but delusion.” Every promise of Satan is a mirage. The pleasure is fleeting and followed by guilt; the wealth gained unlawfully carries no blessing; the freedom from divine rules becomes enslavement to desire. Delusion is the total opposite of truth.

This verse reveals the comprehensive nature of spiritual warfare and teaches the believer to be vigilant over voice inputs—what we listen to—and over our financial and parental decisions. Every dirham and every child is a front in this battle. The only shield is awareness, constant remembrance of Allah, and a deep-rooted conviction that whatever Satan promises is a hollow lie, while what Allah promises is eternal truth.

=65=

After detailing the powers given to Iblîs, Allah sets a crucial and comforting boundary: “You will truly have no authority over My faithful servants.” The word “authority” translates “sulṭān,” meaning any kind of compelling power, proof, or control. Satan can whisper, decorate evil, and invite, but he cannot force a true believer to sin. The “faithful servants” are those who are sincere in their devotion to Allah, who constantly turn to Him, and who actively seek refuge from Satan. Their hearts are fortresses that Iblîs cannot conquer. They may still slip, but they do not become his loyal followers.

This is a promise of spiritual protection. It means that no matter how intense the temptation, the ultimate decision rests with the human will, and for the sincere, that will is divinely fortified.

The verse then declares: “And sufficient is your Lord as a Guardian.” Allah is Wakīl, the ultimate Protector and Trustee. When a believer makes Allah their Guardian, they have the strongest ally against whom Satan’s legions are powerless.

The phrase “your Lord” personalizes this care. It is not a distant deity but the nurturing Lord who is sufficient. This verse is a wellspring of hope and confidence for every struggling soul. It reassures us that if we feel overwhelmed by satanic whispers, the fault is not in the strength of the enemy but in the degree of our reliance on Allah. The cure is to strengthen that bond, to become one of the “faithful servants” through sincere worship and constant supplication. It also implies that those who do fall deeply into sin have, at some level, voluntarily given Satan a foothold. The verse, therefore, is both a promise and a challenge: it promises immunity to the sincere, and it challenges us to examine our sincerity and our reliance on Allah as our true Guardian, rather than relying on our own fluctuating willpower.

=66=

From the unseen battle with Satan, the surah shifts attention to the tangible signs of Allah’s power and mercy in the natural world. “It is your Lord Who steers the ships for you through the sea.

” The mention of ships sailing smoothly on the sea, propelled by wind and kept afloat by laws of physics that Allah alone sustains, is a powerful reminder of divine providence. In the Meccan context, the Quraysh were a trading people whose winter and summer journeys depended on sea and land routes. They knew the terror of sea storms and the relief of safe passage. Allah says that it is He who makes the vessels move, who subjects the sea, and who guides them to their destinations. The purpose is “so that you may seek His bounty,” which includes commercial profit, exploration, and the lawful provision that sustains life. This directs the heart to see economic activity not as a purely material pursuit but as a quest for the bounty of a Merciful Lord.

The verse then concludes with the divine name: “Surely He is ever Merciful to you.” The mercy is evident in the very ability to traverse the oceans, which could drown them at any moment if He willed. The ease of modern transportation should not blind us to the fact that every journey we take, every ship that does not sink, is a continuous miracle of divine mercy. This verse connects to the next in a profound psychological sequence. It describes the normal state of reliance on Allah’s law during ease, setting up the contrast with the state of desperation in danger.

For the believer, it fosters gratitude every time we board a vehicle or a ship. It reminds us that the true Captain of every ship is Allah, and recognizing this turns our daily commute into an act of worship. The verse prepares the ground to expose the ingratitude of humankind when they are saved from distress but then forget their Lord.

=67=

This verse unveils a deep psychological truth about human nature and the reality of tawḥīd embedded in the soul. “When you are touched with hardship at sea”—a storm, the threat of drowning, the utter vulnerability—something profound happens. The polytheist who normally calls upon idols, saints, or multiple gods “totally forgets” all those false deities.

The Arabic “ḍalla” means they are lost from memory, completely erased. The only one they remember and call upon with absolute sincerity is Allah. This phenomenon is called the “prayer of the drowning person.” It proves that the innate human disposition, the fiṭrah, recognizes that only the One true God has the power to save. In the grip of existential terror, all artificial theological constructs crumble, and the soul turns purely to its Creator.

The verse then exposes the tragic sequel: “But when He delivers you safely to shore, you turn away.” Once the danger passes, they revert to their old ways, as if they never cried out. This turning away is the height of hypocrisy and ingratitude. The verse pronounces the diagnosis: “Humankind is ever ungrateful. ” The word “kafūr” denotes an intense, habitual ingratitude, a tendency to cover up blessings and deny the very real dependency that was so vividly felt moments before. This pattern is not limited to sea travel; it applies to any severe illness, accident, or crisis where people suddenly turn to Allah, only to forget Him after recovery.

For the believer, this verse serves as a mirror. Do we remember Allah only in times of trouble, or is He constantly in our hearts during ease? It calls for a consistent, truthful devotion that does not fluctuate with circumstances. It also serves as a warning to the pagan Meccans, reminding them of their own experiences at sea, and proving that their idolatry is a superficial layer over a deep-seated monotheistic instinct that they consciously suppress.

=68=

This verse follows the imagery of sea danger with a parallel threat on land, stripping away every false sense of security. “Do you feel secure that He will not cause the land to swallow you up?” After being delivered safely to shore, the disbeliever feels relief and returns to arrogance, thinking the danger is past. Allah immediately challenges this complacency. The land, which seems solid and safe, can become an instrument of divine punishment just as easily as the sea. The earth could split open and swallow them, as it did with Korah (Qārūn). Or Allah could “unleash upon you a storm of stones,” a barrage of rocks from the sky that no one can escape.

The phrase “then you will find none to protect you” emphasizes absolute helplessness. No false god, no idol, no political or military power can shield a person from the Creator’s decree.

The verse teaches the fundamental lesson that there is no haven from Allah except in Allah. Security is not a function of location—land vs. sea—but a function of being in Allah’s protection through faith and obedience. The Quraysh, who prided themselves on the security of Mecca, are reminded that the same land could become their grave. For the modern reader, this verse shatters the illusion of control that technology and infrastructure provide. Earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, and sudden calamities demonstrate that human safety is ultimately a divine gift, not a human right.

The question “Do you feel secure” is a rhetorical device that invites introspection: on what basis do we feel secure? The believer’s security lies not in the absence of danger but in the presence of Allah’s protection. This verse, along with the previous one, forms a complete picture of human vulnerability on sea and land, calling for constant reliance on and gratitude towards the One who alone controls the elements.

=69=

The argument escalates further by addressing another dimension of false security. Having been delivered once, the disbeliever might think, “At least now I am on land, I will not risk the sea again.” Allah says, “Or do you feel secure that He will not send you back to sea once again?” The One who brought you to the sea the first time can bring you back, whether through a new journey, a change of circumstances, or even the great flood on the Day of Judgment.

The repetition emphasizes that you cannot escape His decree. “And send upon you a violent storm” specifically a “qāṣif” which is a storm that shatters ships with a fierce wind. The purpose would be drowning them “for your denial,” as a punishment for their ingratitude and rejection.

The phrase “for your denial” reveals that their drowning is a direct consequence of their kufr, their deliberate covering of the truth they had acknowledged in their hearts during the first hardship. The verse ends with an even sharper warning: “Then you will find none to avenge you against Us.

” The word “avenge” implies that no one can retaliate against Allah on your behalf or seek justice for your destruction. There is no higher power to appeal to, no court where you can sue God.

This is the ultimate expression of divine sovereignty. The verse leaves the arrogant human being utterly without recourse, completely at the mercy of the One they defied.

For the believer, this series of rhetorical questions cultivates a profound state of khawf (fear) combined with love. It is not a fear that paralyzes but a fear that awakens the heart to constant gratitude and obedience, knowing that the same Lord who saved us can, if He wills, change the situation in an instant, and no one can question His decision.

=70=

After the stark warnings, the Quran shifts to a tone of divine honour and love, reminding humanity of their exalted status. “Indeed, We have dignified the children of Adam.

” The word “karramnā” means We have honoured, ennobled, and granted inherent dignity to all human beings. This honour is not based on race, nation, or religion, but is a God-given gift to every descendant of Adam. It includes the upright posture, the intellect, the power of speech, the capacity for knowledge, and the free will that makes moral accountability possible.

The verse then enumerates some specifics of this honour: “carried them on land and sea,” enabling them to traverse the earth and the oceans, mastering transport and exploration. “Granted them good and lawful provisions,” meaning pure, beneficial, and delightful foods and resources that sustain life.

This is a reminder that the wealth of the earth is a divine provision for humanity’s use. Finally, “and privileged them far above many of Our creatures.” Humans are not the only creatures, but they are given a rank above many, including angels in certain respects of knowledge and stewardship, though angels may excel in pure worship. This dignity is not an excuse for arrogance but a call to responsibility. The very fact that we are honoured should make us grateful and obedient, as the previous verses showed that ingratitude turns us into the worst of creatures. This verse is a cornerstone for the Islamic understanding of human rights; every person, simply by being human, carries a sanctity that must be respected. It also balances the fear instilled by the earlier verses. Yes, Allah can destroy, but He has also created us with love and honour, desiring our success, not our doom.

The mention of these favours is a prelude to the Day of Judgment, where this honour will be fully manifested, and each person will be held accountable for how they used these dignified faculties. The believer reads this verse and feels a surge of self-worth rooted in divine affirmation, leading to a life that reflects that dignity through purity, knowledge, and justice.

=71=

The discourse now moves to the ultimate scene of accountability. “Beware of the Day We will summon every people with their leader.” The leader, “imām,” can be interpreted in multiple valid ways: the prophet sent to them, as the people of Muḥammad are called with Muḥammad, the people of Moses with Moses; or the book of deeds; or the leader they followed in this world, whether a righteous guide or a misguiding tyrant. All meanings converge on a single reality: on that Day, no one stands alone; they are associated with their guiding figure, and that association will either be a source of honour or disgrace.

The verse then divides humanity. “So whoever will be given their record in their right hand will read it happily.” The right hand symbolizes success, for the people of the right are the companions of Paradise. They will receive their record with joy, eager to read it, for it contains good deeds and divine mercy.

The phrase “will not be wronged even by the width of the thread of a date stone” uses the smallest imaginable measure to promise perfect justice. A “thread of a date stone,” “fatīl,” is the tiny membrane inside a date pit. Not even that amount of good will be omitted, nor that amount of bad added. This verse provides enormous hope for the believer: every tiny act of kindness, every suppressed tear, every patient moment, is recorded and will be rewarded. It also instills a healthy fear: the leader you follow matters eternally. Following a false leader leads to receiving the record in the left hand. The verse calls us to choose our “imām” wisely—the Prophet Muḥammad as our model, and to ensure our record is one we can read with delight.

=72=

This verse states a direct correlation between one’s spiritual state in this life and their fate in the next. “But whoever is blind to the truth in this world will be blind in the Hereafter.” The blindness is not physical but a blindness of the heart, an inability or refusal to see the clear signs of Allah, to recognise truth when it comes, and to follow the right path. A person who chooses to shut their eyes to guidance, who ignores the revelations, who is indifferent to the divine message, will be resurrected blind on the Day of Judgment. As another verse states, they will ask, “My Lord, why have You raised me blind while I was seeing?” and the answer will be that they were given signs and they forgot them. The blindness in the Hereafter is the inability to see the path to Paradise, to see the light, or to see any good.

The verse then intensifies: “and even far more astray from the Right Way.” Not only are they blind, but they are even further from the correct path than they were in this life. In the world, they had the chance to be guided; in the Hereafter, the misguidance becomes permanent, and they will wander in the darkness of the assembly, unable to find any way out.

This is a terrifying consequence of spiritual negligence. The verse serves as a powerful motivation to cure the blindness of the heart now, through knowledge, reflection, and faith. It teaches that the eyes in the head are merely tools; the real vision is that of the inner heart. A person may be physically blind but spiritually enlightened, like many scholars, and that is better than physical sight with a blind heart. It also comforts the oppressed that those who are “blind” to their wrongdoing in this world will face a harsher blindness in the Hereafter, while those who saw the truth and suffered for it will see their reward clearly.

=73=

This verse reveals a specific incident from the Meccan period, illustrating the intense pressure on the Prophet. “They definitely were about to lure you away from what We have revealed to you.” The pagans of Mecca, seeing the Prophet’s compassion and eagerness for their guidance, attempted to seduce him into a compromise. They proposed that he should worship their idols for a period, and they would worship his God, or that he should modify the Quran to remove criticism of their gods or to soften certain commands.

The Arabic “iftinān” implies a clever, persistent attempt at seduction, a trial meant to shake his resolve. Their hope was that “you would attribute something else to Us falsely,” meaning he would fabricate revelations that pleased them, thereby compromising the purity of the message. The luring was not just theoretical; it was a planned, organized effort. The reward for this betrayal would be worldly: “and then they would have certainly taken you as a close friend. ” The offer of friendship, alliance, and the cessation of persecution was the bait. The leaders of Quraysh promised the Prophet wealth, kingship, and honour if he would just bend a little.

This verse shows how close to the edge of a great trial the Prophet was, not by any flaw in his character, but by the sheer intensity of the pressure, which only the special protection of Allah could withstand. It is a testament to the purity of the Prophet’s mission; he refused every worldly gain to maintain the absolute truth of the Quran.

The verse also serves as a warning to all believers, especially those in leadership and daʿwah, that the world will constantly try to lure them into diluting the truth for the sake of acceptance. Compromise on fundamental principles may bring temporary popularity but leads to divine wrath, as the next verses will show.

=74=

This verse underscores the immense divine grace that shielded the Prophet from error. “Had We not made you steadfast,” the Arabic “thabbitnāka” meaning We gave you firmness, stability, and a rooted stand, “you probably would have inclined to them a little.

” The phrase “a little” is crucial; it indicates that even the slightest inclination towards their demands, even a mere thought of compromise, could have occurred, not out of desire for falsehood, but out of the overwhelming nature of the temptation and the human instinct to seek relief from persecution and to win over the community. This verse does not imply any weakness in the Prophet; rather, it magnifies the perfection of divine support. It teaches the doctrine of ʿiṣmah (infallibility) of prophets—they are protected from error in conveying the message, not by their own power, but by Allah’s continuous reinforcement. The wording “you probably would have inclined” is a hypothetical to highlight the magnitude of the danger. For the ordinary believer, this verse is a humbling reminder: if the Prophet himself, the best of creation, needed Allah’s steadfastness to avoid even a slight inclination, how much more do we need to beg Allah for steadfastness every moment? Our own resolve is brittle; only divine firmness can keep us on the straight path when faced with temptations to compromise our faith for material or social gain. It also reveals the mercy of Allah in actively helping His servants who turn to Him. The lesson is that we must constantly pray, “O Lord, make firm our hearts upon Your religion,” and never rely on our own strength. The trial of near-inclination was averted solely by grace, and that grace is available to every believer who sincerely seeks it.

=75=

This verse completes the hypothetical scenario with the terrifying consequence of even a small compromise. “And then We truly would have made you taste double punishment both in this life and after death.” The principle is that the greater the blessing and status, the greater the accountability. For the Prophet, as the supreme guide, any deviation would have incurred a double penalty: punishment in this world—perhaps loss of peace, exposure, or a bad end—and punishment after death in the grave and the Hereafter. The “double” punishment is because a leader’s error leads countless others astray, magnifying the sin. This underscores the ethical standard for prophets is absolute.

The verse then adds, “and you would have found no helper against Us.” Even the Prophet Muḥammad, the beloved of Allah, would have no one to protect him from divine justice if he strayed.

This is the ultimate declaration of the impartiality of divine law. It instills a profound fear of Allah in the heart of every caller to Islam: the message belongs to Allah, not to us. If we alter it, we expose ourselves to a punishment from which no status, no past service, and no intercessor can save us. For the believers, this verse also reminds us that while we are not prophets, the principle applies proportionally: those who have more knowledge and influence bear a heavier burden. It is a call to sincerity and to the constant plea, “O Allah, protect us from the evil of ourselves.” The hypothetical scenario—though it never occurred—serves as the strongest deterrent against any temptation to distort revelation for worldly gain. It magnifies the Prophet’s triumph; he rejected the lures, not out of human strength, but because he was made steadfast, and he was made aware of the catastrophic consequence of even the slightest inclination.

=76=

The verse shifts from the attempt to seduce to the attempt to intimidate and expel. “They were about to intimidate you to drive you out of the land of Mecca.” The Quraysh, when their negotiations failed, intensified their persecution to the point where they aimed to either kill, imprison, or banish the Prophet.

The word “intimidate” translates “yastafizzūnaka,” meaning to provoke, unsettle, and disturb to make life unbearable, so that he would leave voluntarily. This was a real threat, as they plotted in Dār al-Nadwah.

The verse then delivers a divine verdict on such a course of action: “but then they would not have survived after you had left except for a little while.” This is a prophecy and a law. If the Prophet were driven out unjustly, that community’s destruction would be swift. Indeed, after the Hijrah, the Quraysh were given a short respite; they pursued him, but soon met their fate at Badr and eventually the conquest of Mecca. The “little while” refers to a brief period before divine punishment overtakes them. This verse assured the Prophet that his departure was not a defeat but the prelude to the downfall of the oppressors. For the believers, it contains a general principle: when a community expels its prophets or righteous people, it signs its own death warrant. The presence of the righteous is a mercy that protects a society; their expulsion removes that shield, and punishment becomes imminent.

This is a comfort to all who are persecuted for their faith: the oppressors’ victory is temporary, and their end is near. It also teaches that the land of Mecca itself was sanctified, and driving out the Prophet from it was an act of extreme sin that could not go unpunished. The verse subtly foreshadows the Hijrah, which was a turning point in Islamic history, transforming persecution into triumph.

=77=

This verse generalises the previous warning into a universal divine law. “This has been Our way with the messengers We sent before you.” The law is that when a messenger is driven out by his people, those people are inevitably destroyed or subdued. The Qur’anic narrative is filled with examples: Noah, Lot, Shuʿayb, and others. Their rejection and expulsion led to floods, earthquakes, and sweeping destruction.

This is not a random occurrence but the established Sunnah of Allah. The verse then emphasizes: “And you will never find any change in Our way.” Divine patterns are fixed and immutable. They do not alter for any people, no matter how powerful they think they are. This provides the Prophet and his followers with absolute certainty. The persecution they faced was not a sign of divine neglect; it was exactly the path trodden by all previous messengers, and the outcome was always the triumph of truth and the downfall of the aggressors.

The phrase “never find any change” is a powerful rebuttal to those who might think that the Quraysh, with their custodianship of the Kaʿbah, might be an exception. The law applies without favoritism.

For the believer, this verse builds steadfastness. When injustice seems to win, we recall that it is only a phase within an unchangeable divine pattern. The sunnah of Allah is that after hardship comes ease, after the trial of the messengers comes the destruction of the rejecters. This also serves as a warning to contemporary oppressors: their power does not exempt them from this ancient law. The verse connects the story of the Prophet intimately with the entire prophetic heritage, giving him the comfort of a long line of honoured predecessors who faced the same ordeal and ultimately witnessed divine justice.

=78=

After the heavy discussions of trials and prophetic history, the surah prescribes the most powerful tool of steadfastness: prayer. “Observe the prayer from the decline of the sun until the darkness of the night.” The “decline of the sun” refers to its passing the zenith, indicating the time of the Zuhr and ʿAṣr prayers. The “darkness of the night” refers to the onset of night, bringing the Maghrib and ʿIshāʾ prayers. This beautifully encapsulates the four prayers that span from midday to nightfall. Then it singles out “and the dawn prayer,” Fajr.

The mention of Fajr after the general instruction emphasizes its special status. The reason: “for certainly the dawn prayer is witnessed by angels. ” Authentic hadith detail that the angels of the night and the angels of the day gather at Fajr time. The night angels ascend and the day angels descend, and both witness the prayer.

This is a testament to the honour and importance of this prayer. Reciting the Quran at dawn is especially meritorious, as the heart is fresh and the world is quiet.

This verse establishes the five daily prayers in a concise manner, but its placement here is deeply significant. The Prophet was facing immense psychological pressure, temptations, and threats. The remedy was not political strategy alone but a solid connection with Allah through prayer. Prayer disciplines the soul, provides direct access to divine help, and fortifies the heart against the whisperings of Satan and the lures of enemies. It is the believer’s daily recharge.

The verse also links the rhythm of prayer to the cosmic signs of sun and night mentioned earlier in the surah; the believer aligns their worship with the physical signs of Allah’s creation. For the modern Muslim, this verse is a reminder that no matter how busy or stressed life becomes, the protective shield of the five prayers, especially the dawn prayer witnessed by angels, is the lifeline that ensures we do not succumb to the world’s attempts to lure or intimidate us away from our faith.

=79=

This verse specifically addresses the Prophet with a command that became an emphasised sunnah for the believers: the night vigil, Tahajjud. “And rise at the last part of the night, offering additional prayers.” The last part of the night is a time of intimate privacy with Allah, when the world sleeps and the heart can be fully present. The prayer is described as “nāfilah,” meaning additional, beyond the obligatory. For the Prophet, it was obligatory for a period, then became a highly recommended sunnah. The purpose is magnificent: “so your Lord may raise you to a station of praise.” This refers to “al-Maqām al-Maḥmūd,” the Station of Praise, which is the great intercession on the Day of Judgment. When all humanity is gathered in distress, the Prophet Muḥammad, peace be upon him, will be granted the permission to intercede for the judgment to begin, and all of creation will praise him for this. This station is a direct reward for his night prayer. The connection between the night vigil and the station of intercession is profound. The one who sacrifices sleep and comfort to stand privately before Allah in this life is given the highest honour before all creation in the next.

This verse reveals that true honour comes not through worldly compromise but through sincere, hidden worship. For the believers, Tahajjud is the spiritual ascent. It is the time when duʿāʾ is most accepted, and the nearness to Allah is most felt. The verse invites us to follow the Prophet’s example, even if just with two rakʿahs, to attain a portion of that divine nearness. It shows that the leadership of this ummah and any honour we seek must be rooted in the darkness of the night, in prayer, not in the glitter of the day, in politics or wealth. The “station of praise” is the ultimate goal, and the path to it is paved with night prayers.

The surah provides a comprehensive supplication for the Prophet that encapsulates his entire mission. “And say, ‘My Lord! Grant me an honourable entrance and an honourable exit.’” This was revealed before the Hijrah, as an instruction for the future. The “honourable entrance” refers to the entry into Medina, an entrance that would be truthful, blessed, and victorious, not sneaking or fleeing in disgrace. The “honourable exit” refers to the departure from Mecca, leaving it not as a defeated exile but as a dignified emigrant under Allah’s command, or it refers to every transition in life and ultimately the exit from this world into the Hereafter with honour. The supplication teaches that a believer should seek honour in every transition, not through their own scheming but through Allah’s facilitation.

The second part of the dua is: “and give me a supporting authority from Yourself.” The Prophet asks for “sulṭānan naṣīran,” a clear, compelling authority and power that aids him.

This is not a request for personal glory but for the divine backing needed to establish truth and justice. It is a prayer for the strength to fulfil the mission without compromise. This supplication was perfectly answered; the Prophet’s entry into Medina was triumphant, his exit from Mecca was prophetic, and he was given manifest support through the Quran, the angels at Badr, and the eventual conquest.

For believers, this dua is a sunnah to recite, especially during life transitions—changing jobs, moving homes, or facing challenges. It combines the request for honourable beginnings and endings, covering the whole of an endeavour, and the request for divine authority, recognising that any real success or protection comes solely from Allah. It ties together the themes of the surah: the Night Journey honoured the Prophet, the commands gave him moral authority, and this dua seals it with a direct plea for continuous divine support.
