=1=

The surah opens with the declaration “Glory be to the One Who took His servant by night,” a phrase that immediately fills the heart with awe. The word used for “Glory be” is subḥān, which declares Allah’s absolute perfection and distances Him from any flaw. It is used at moments of tremendous divine action, preparing us for something beyond ordinary human experience. The “servant” here is the Prophet Muḥammad, peace be upon him, and referring to him as His servant in this context of honour teaches a profound lesson: the highest station a human being can attain comes through devoted servitude to Allah. The journey was “by night,” an intimate and quiet time when creation rests, yet the Lord of the worlds remains ever watchful. He transported His beloved servant from “the Sacred Mosque” in Mecca to “the Farthest Mosque” in Jerusalem—Masjid al-Aqsa. This journey is known as al-Isrāʾ. The Sacred Mosque is the first house of worship built for humanity, and the Farthest Mosque is described as “whose surroundings We have blessed. ” That blessed land is the region of Palestine, historically enriched with prophets, revelations, and divine favours.

The phrase “so that We may show him some of Our signs” points to the purpose of the journey. It was not merely a physical relocation but a deeply spiritual experience where the Prophet witnessed extraordinary signs—from the celestial realms to the gathering of prophets in Jerusalem, all the way to the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary during the Miʿrāj. Authentic hadiths, recorded in Bukhari and Muslim, detail that he was carried on al-Burāq, a white steed, led by Jibrīl, and ascended through the heavens, meeting various prophets. The verse closes with two divine names: “Indeed, He alone is the All-Hearing, All-Seeing.

” This is perfectly placed. The Quraysh would soon mock the Prophet’s account, calling it impossible. So Allah affirms that He heard their ridicule and saw their disbelief, and He also heard His Prophet’s truthful words and saw his steadfastness. This reminds every believer that Allah is fully aware of all that is said in support or in rejection of the truth. It comforts the heart that when we face denial, the One who matters most is already listening and watching.

=2=

After the magnificent honour bestowed upon the final Prophet, the Quran turns our attention to Moses, peace be upon him, drawing a parallel between the guidance given to earlier nations and that given to this ummah. “And We gave Moses the Scripture” refers to the Torah, a complete revelation containing laws, wisdom, and light. It was “made a guide for the Children of Israel” specifically, showing them the path to righteousness after their deliverance from Pharaoh. The core message embedded within that Scripture is immediately highlighted: “Do not take besides Me any other Trustee of Affairs. ” The Arabic word translated as Trustee of Affairs is “wakīl,” which carries the sense of the one upon whom you fully rely, entrust all your matters, and turn to in every need.

This command is essentially the heart of tawḥīd in practical life: to make Allah alone your ultimate reliance. The Children of Israel were repeatedly warned against depending on idols, sorcery, or worldly powers.

For the Muslim reader, this verse establishes a vital link between the message of Moses and the message of the Quran; both call humanity to single-minded trust in Allah. It also foreshadows the coming discussion of Israelite history. By mentioning Moses right after the Night Journey, the Quran subtly reminds us that just as Moses was given a grand miracle and a revealed book, Muḥammad, peace be upon him, was also granted a tremendous sign and the Quran.

The emphasis on not taking other trustees speaks directly to the pagan Meccans who placed their trust in idols, and it addresses all of us today: whom do we rely on in moments of anxiety? The verse teaches that true guidance is inseparable from abandoning every false support and placing one’s entire trust in the Lord of the worlds. This sets the stage for the warnings that follow about the consequences of abandoning this principle.

=3=

Allah now addresses the Children of Israel with a profound reminder of their noble lineage, calling them “descendants of those We carried with Noah in the Ark.” This connects them to one of the greatest events of salvation in human history, the flood, during which Noah, peace be upon him, and the believing few were preserved while a corrupt world was washed away. It reminds them that their very existence is a result of that divine rescue; had it not been for that mercy, humanity would have been cut off.

The verse then describes Noah as “a grateful servant. ” This quality of gratitude, “shakūr,” is central to the prophetic character. Despite the monumental trials Noah faced—centuries of daʿwah, mockery, and eventually the flood—he remained deeply thankful to Allah. Being called a grateful servant here serves as a model for the Israelites and for all believers. Gratitude is not merely about uttering thanks; it is about using all of one’s faculties, blessings, and life itself in obedience to the One who bestowed them. The Israelites, having been reminded that they descend from those saved with a grateful prophet, are implicitly asked: will you not emulate his gratitude? The Quran often links the well-being of nations to their thankfulness. Later in this same surah, we see that human beings can be ever ungrateful, as mentioned in verse 67. So here, the mention of Noah’s gratitude stands as a gentle rebuke and an invitation. It also ties the story of Moses to the great patriarch Noah, showing that the chain of guidance and salvation is continuous. For us today, the lesson is that a grateful heart recognizes all blessings as coming from Allah, and that gratitude is the true legacy of the righteous servants carried through history.

=4=

This verse marks the beginning of a divine disclosure that was given to the Children of Israel through their Scripture, meaning the Torah. Allah says, “We warned the Children of Israel in the Scripture,” meaning this was not a casual remark but a recorded, decisive decree from Allah. The warning is precise: “You will certainly cause corruption in the land twice, and you will become extremely arrogant.

” The word for corruption, “fasād,” includes all forms of moral decay, bloodshed, injustice, and violation of divine commands. The phrase “in the land” points to the Holy Land itself, a region repeatedly sanctified and later defiled through transgression.

The mention of “twice” indicates two distinct historical periods of severe moral decline and tyranny. The second part, “you will become extremely arrogant,” adds a dimension of inner disease. The arrogance spoken of is “ʿuluwwan kabīran”—a great, overbearing haughtiness that makes a people feel they are above divine law.

This warning is not a prediction that forces them to act corruptly; rather, it is a foretelling of what they would freely choose to do, demonstrating Allah’s infinite knowledge. Scholars connect these two corruptions to major historical events: the first likely refers to the period when they killed prophets, altered the Torah, and broke the Sabbath, leading to the Babylonian attack and the destruction of the First Temple.

The second corruption refers to the rejection and attempted killing of John the Baptist and their plotting against Jesus, peace be upon them, leading to the Roman destruction of the Second Temple. The pattern is clear: corruption followed by arrogance brought divine punishment.

For the Muslim community, this serves as a mirror. Arrogance and spreading corruption are not sins limited to one nation; whenever a community blessed with guidance turns away and acts with prideful transgression, it walks a path that history shows leads to ruin.

=5=

Continuing the divine warning, Allah describes the consequence of the first corruption. The phrase “when the first of the two warnings would come to pass” means when the time of punishment due arrives, a specific event will unfold. Allah would “send against you some of Our servants of great might.” Note that these aggressors are called “Our servants,” indicating that even mighty tyrants are under Allah’s command and serve His decree, whether they know it or not. The description “of great might” translates “ulī baʾsin shadīd,” meaning they possess overwhelming military power and ruthlessness. They would “ravage your homes,” which in Arabic “jāsū khilāla al-diyār” suggests a thorough searching and ransacking, leaving no corner untouched. This historically aligns with the invasion of the Babylonians under Nebuchadnezzar, who demolished Jerusalem, burned the Temple, and took the Israelites into captivity.

The verse ends with “this would be a warning fulfilled,” emphasizing that what Allah promises must come to pass. The warning was not an empty threat but an established word in the Scripture. This fulfilment also serves as a proof of the truth of revelation, as later history confirmed it.

For the believer, reflecting on this verse inspires a healthy fear of Allah’s justice. When a community is blessed with scripture and guidance, yet persists in corruption, breaking sacred laws and spreading injustice, the arrival of “servants of great might” can be a wake-up call, or a terminal punishment. It dismantles any false sense of security that belonging to a favoured nation automatically protects one from the consequences of evil. The external enemy is merely a tool; the real cause of destruction was internal moral decay and arrogance.

=6=

This verse unveils the mercy that follows sincere repentance. “Then after your repentance” indicates that after the first calamity, the Children of Israel would turn back to Allah in humility and remorse. The wording does not mention their repentance earlier because it is implicit in the change of their condition. When a people truly return to Allah, His response is immediate and generous. “We would give you the upper hand over them” means that Allah would restore their strength and allow them to overcome the very enemies who had ravaged them. Beyond mere military victory, He would “aid you with wealth and offspring, causing you to outnumber them.

” This is a comprehensive restoration: material prosperity through wealth, and demographic strength through many children. The Israelites did experience a return from Babylonian exile, rebuilt the Temple, and regained a measure of political autonomy and prosperity under the Persians.

This verse reveals a profound sunnah of Allah: genuine repentance does not just remove punishment, it brings positive, flourishing blessings. The increase in wealth and children is a signal of divine pleasure and a means to re-establish a righteous society.

However, the gift is also a test—will they remain grateful? The lesson for all believers is that even after suffering the consequences of our sins, the door of repentance is open. When we turn sincerely to Allah, He not only forgives but can restore what was lost and grant even more. It also shows that worldly blessings like wealth and a strong population are not inherently evil; they can be a mark of divine aid when used in obedience. The tragedy of the Israelites, as will be seen, is that they failed to maintain the gratitude and righteousness that brought about this renewal.

=7=

Here, the Quran states a timeless principle: “If you act rightly, it is for your own good, but if you do wrong, it is to your own loss.” Allah does not benefit from our righteousness, nor is He harmed by our evil. All consequences of deeds return to the doer.

This statement sets the stage for the second warning. “And when the second warning would come to pass” signals the next major corruption. The enemies would be sent to “totally disgrace you,” which in Arabic “li-yasūʾū wujūhakum” literally means to make your faces look awful, an expression of extreme humiliation and grief. They would “enter the Temple of Jerusalem as they entered it the first time,” indicating a repeat of the desecration. The “Temple” here is the Masjid or the sacred compound.

Historically, this points to the Romans under Titus in 70 CE, who destroyed the Second Temple and expelled the Jews. They would “utterly destroy whatever would fall into their hands,” meaning complete devastation with no mercy. The verse highlights that punishment is not arbitrary; it is the direct result of wrongdoing. The disgrace and destruction are proportionate to the arrogance and corruption that preceded it. What a sobering lesson for any community: the second chance after repentance was squandered, and the return to sin brought an even more severe consequence. It underscores that divine mercy after the first fall should never be taken for granted, and repeated transgression closes the door of respite. The believer is reminded to maintain righteousness consistently, knowing that slipping back into major sins after being rescued invites a harsher reckoning.

=8=

After the description of the second destruction, Allah opens a window of hope with the word “perhaps,” which in Arabic “ʿasā” when attributed to Allah expresses a near certainty of His mercy. “Perhaps your Lord will have mercy on you” is a gentle invitation to repentance even after such devastation.

However, the mercy is conditional on turning back. The following clause, “but if you return to sin, We will return to punishment,” establishes an unalterable law of divine justice. As long as a people keep returning to transgression, the cycles of punishment will repeat. This verse is not only about the Children of Israel; it applies universally to all nations and individuals. The pattern is clear: sin brings punishment, repentance brings mercy, and relapse into sin brings the return of punishment. Then the verse takes a sharper turn, stating, “And We have made Hell a permanent confinement for the disbelievers. ” This connects earthly punishment with the final consequence in the Hereafter.

The Arabic word “ḥaṣīr” means a prison or an enclosure from which there is no escape. While nations may suffer temporary calamities in this world as a warning and purification, for those who die in a state of disbelief, Hell becomes their ultimate, inescapable prison. This stark warning immediately follows the discussion of repeated sin to teach that persistent wrongdoing, especially when it reaches the level of disbelief, leads to an afterlife punishment far worse than any worldly destruction.

For the Muslim listener, this verse is both a comfort and a caution: comfort in that Allah’s mercy is always near for the repentant, and caution against becoming complacent about sin, because the cycle of punishment is real, and the ultimate prison is a reality for those who reject faith entirely.

=9=

After the long discourse on the Children of Israel, the Quran now refers to itself, asserting its role as the ultimate guidance: “Surely this Quran guides to what is most upright.” The phrase “what is most upright” translates “aḥsan,” meaning the best, the most just, and the most balanced path. It encompasses beliefs, ethics, laws, and personal conduct—everything that leads to human well-being in this life and the next. The guidance of the Quran is not one option among many; it is the standard of what is truly upright. Then the verse moves to the glad tidings: “and gives good news to the believers—who do good—that they will have a mighty reward.” The believers are described as those who “do good,” combining faith with righteous action. The “mighty reward” is generous beyond imagination, encompassing Paradise and the pleasure of Allah. This verse subtly contrasts the Israelites, who received scripture but fell into corruption, with the followers of the Quran, whose scripture is a complete and preserved guide that leads to the straight way.

The mention of “good news” invites every reader to aspire to be among those who not only claim faith but demonstrate it through excellent deeds. The connection to the previous verses is clear: while earlier nations squandered their guidance and faced cycles of punishment, this Quran offers a way out of that destructive pattern. It guides to the most upright, so those who hold fast to it and perform righteous works are assured of a reward that will never be taken away. This verse instills hope and motivation, affirming that the Quran is not merely a set of rules but a roadmap to the highest success.

=10=

The Quran’s message is balanced. After giving glad tidings to the believers, it immediately presents the warning. “And it warns those who do not believe in the Hereafter” highlights a specific category: those who reject the reality of the life to come. Denial of the Hereafter is often the root of moral corruption because when a person thinks there is no ultimate accountability, they are more likely to indulge in oppression and sin. This directly links to the stories of the corrupted Israelites and the arrogant pagans of Mecca, both of whom often acted as if their deeds would have no eternal consequence. The warning is stark: “We have prepared for them a painful punishment.

” The use of “prepared” shows that Hell is not an afterthought; it is a predetermined destination for those who earn it through their disbelief and evil actions. The word “painful” translates “alīm,” conveying intense, ongoing suffering. This verse serves as the necessary counterpart to the previous verse’s promise of reward, completing the picture of divine justice. The Quran guides to the most upright, but it also makes clear that turning away from that guidance leads to a dreadful end. It is an act of mercy that Allah warns us, so that no soul can claim ignorance. Notice that the warning is directed at those who “do not believe in the Hereafter,” not merely those who do wrong in general, underscoring the pivotal role of faith in the last Day. This belief shapes one’s entire worldview and conduct. The verse encourages the listener to reflect on the certainty of the Hereafter and to take the Quran’s warnings seriously, knowing that God’s preparation of punishment is as real as His preparation of reward.

=11=

This verse unveils a profound weakness in human nature. “And humans swiftly pray for evil as they pray for good” points to a common reaction: when a person is angry, impatient, or frustrated, they may curse themselves, their children, or their wealth, calling down destruction or hardship in a moment of emotion, just as eagerly as they would ask for blessings.

The word “swiftly” captures the impulsive haste with which such supplications are made, without considering the consequences. It is narrated that a man once cursed his own mount when it was slow, and the Prophet, peace be upon him, told him not to accompany them, because his curse could actually be answered.

The verse then gives the reason: “For humankind is ever hasty.” This hastiness, “ʿajūl,” is part of human creation. Man wants everything immediately—pleasure, relief, vengeance—and fails to think about the long-term outcomes.

The placement of this verse is brilliant. Previously, the Quran spoke of great rewards and painful punishments, and of nations that fell due to their corruption. Now it reveals that one root cause of many sins is human impatience and rashness. People might wish for evil upon themselves or others without realizing that if Allah were to answer that prayer immediately, they would be destroyed.

This verse teaches restraint in speech, especially in moments of anger. It calls the believer to slow down, to think before speaking, and to avoid invoking harm even in jest. It also links to the earlier warning about the Hereafter: a hasty person wants immediate gratification and may reject the promise of future reward or punishment. The cure for this hastiness is faith, patience, and constant remembrance that Allah’s timing is perfect. A believer learns to supplicate for good with deliberation and to trust that whatever delay occurs is filled with divine wisdom.

=12=

Turning the attention to the natural world, Allah declares, “We made the day and night as two signs.” The alternation of day and night is a cosmic sign pointing to the power and wisdom of the Creator. The “sign of the night” is described as “devoid of light,” which in Arabic “fa-maḥawnā āyata al-layl” suggests the erasure or darkening of light, making the night a time of rest and concealment. The “sign of the day” is made “perfectly bright,” a radiant visibility that enables activity and effort. The purposes are then stated: “so that you may seek the bounty of your Lord” during the daylight through work, trade, study, and all forms of lawful sustenance; and “know the number of years and calculation of time” through the regular cycles of days, months, and years. This means that the day and night are not just functional; they are a precise celestial clock that allows for organization of life, religious observances like prayers and fasting, and historical record. The verse closes, “And We have explained everything in detail,” which is a sweeping statement that in the Quran, everything necessary for guidance has been thoroughly elucidated. The link to the previous verse is clear: humans are hasty, but the measured, precise alternation of night and day teaches patience and order. The one who reflects on the cosmic signs will learn to move in harmony with Allah’s rhythms rather than being driven by impulsive haste. For the modern believer, this verse invites contemplation of the natural world as a source of both spiritual insight and practical discipline. The sun and moon do not rush, and neither should the believer. The detailed explanation mentioned assures us that the Quran leaves no essential matter of faith and life unaddressed.

=13=

This verse moves from the cosmic signs to the individual’s accountability. “We have bound every human’s destiny to their neck.

” The word for “destiny” here is “ṭāʾir,” which originally means bird, but in pre-Islamic Arab culture it was used to refer to omens or fortune. The Quran reclaims the term to signify that every person’s actions, good or bad, are attached to them inseparably, like something hanging around their neck. This imagery is powerful: you cannot shake off your deeds; they go with you wherever you are. Then comes the scene of the Day of Judgment: “And on the Day of Judgment We will bring forth to each person a record which they will find laid open.” This record, the book of deeds, is brought out and spread open, meaning nothing is hidden, nothing is omitted. The register is so comprehensive that the person themselves might have forgotten many deeds, but they are all there, documented by the angels. This verse directly addresses the human tendency to be hasty and forget the long-term consequences of actions. While a person might act in the darkness of night, thinking no one sees, the record being bound to their neck is a constant reminder that everything is being preserved. The record “laid open” implies the ultimate transparency of that Day; there will be no secret compartments. The believer is called to live with the awareness that every word and deed is being attached to them, and one day that record will be presented in plain sight. This motivates careful, conscious living, ensuring that what we attach to ourselves is something we will be pleased to see on that terrifying yet just Day.

=14=

The next verse transports us directly into the dramatic courtroom of the Hereafter. The command “Read your record” is directed at each individual. Imagine the terror and awe when a person is handed a book they never authored but which contains every detail of their life, and they are told to read it themselves. The instruction underscores the absolute justice of that Day; no outside judge needs to dictate charges, for the evidence is in one’s own hands. Then comes the piercing statement: “You alone are sufficient this Day to take account of yourself.” This means that the person’s own conscience, coupled with the open record, will be enough for them to judge themselves. There will be no need for external witnesses or lengthy debate, because deep down, every soul knows what it has done, and the book confirms it.

The phrase “sufficient” implies that the self-reckoning will be so complete and undeniable that the individual will not be able to protest or demand a second opinion. This verse is a powerful call to self-accountability in this life. Umar ibn al-Khattab, may Allah be pleased with him, famously said, “Bring yourselves to account before you are brought to account.” If we learn to read our own actions now—reviewing our day, repenting for sins, and correcting our course—then that final reading will be a moment of relief rather than terror.

The verse also shows Allah’s mercy in giving us the opportunity to audit ourselves before that inevitable day. It strips away all delusions; no one else will bear our burdens, and we cannot hide behind excuses. This self-confrontation is the ultimate consequence of our deeds being bound to our necks.

=15=

This verse establishes four foundational principles of divine justice. First, “Whoever chooses to be guided, it is only for their own good.” Guidance brings peace, purpose, and eternal salvation, and all the benefit of that choice returns to the person. Allah is not in need of our worship; we are in need of Him. Second, “And whoever chooses to stray, it is only to their own loss.” Straying misleads the individual and results in their own destruction; it does not harm Allah in the slightest.

The use of “chooses” emphasizes free will; Allah does not force anyone. Third, “No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another. ” This repudiates concepts of original sin or vicarious atonement. Every person is responsible for their own deeds alone. On the Day of Judgment, a mother will not carry her child’s sins, nor a leader the sins of their followers beyond what they personally committed.

This is a tremendous comfort and a warning: comfort that no one will be wronged by being held accountable for another’s crimes; warning that no intercessor can simply take on your sins without sincere repentance. Fourth, “And We would never punish a people until We have sent a messenger to warn them.” This establishes that divine punishment, whether in this world or the next, is only inflicted after clear warning has been delivered through a prophet or messenger. Those who never received the message in a clear, undistorted form are not held accountable in the same way.

This principle of justice demonstrates Allah’s mercy; He does not hold people liable without first providing them with guidance. The verse ties back to the record and self-accountability, reminding us that the test is fair, the criteria are clear, and the outcome is fully our own making.

=16=

Here the Quran describes the divine pattern when a society reaches the point of no return. “Whenever We intend to destroy a society” refers to a decision in Allah’s eternal knowledge that a people have become irredeemably corrupt. The mechanism of their downfall is fascinating: “We command its elite to obey Allah but they act rebelliously in it.” The “elite” translates “mutrafīhā,” meaning those living in luxury, ease, and power—the trendsetters and leaders. The command comes through messengers, scriptures, and the innate moral sense, urging them to righteousness. But they respond with open transgression, using their wealth and influence to defy divine law. Their rebellion confirms that they are beyond reform. “So the decree of punishment is justified,” meaning the sentence is rightfully passed against them. The destruction that follows is described as “utterly,” leaving nothing behind. This verse explains why seemingly powerful civilizations suddenly collapse. It is not merely due to external invasion or economic failure; it is because their affluent classes became entrenched in sin and rejected all calls to righteousness. The command to the elite is a test—will they use their blessings for justice or oppression? When they choose corruption, they seal their fate. This serves as a warning to every society: the prosperity of the elite is not a sign of divine pleasure if it is accompanied by disobedience. For the individual, it teaches that wealth and comfort can easily become a trap if they lead to heedlessness. The only way to avoid this destructive pattern is for those blessed with means to lead in obedience and humility, recognizing that the test of luxury is to increase in gratitude, not rebellion.

=17=

This verse reinforces the previous warning by calling the listener to reflect on history. “How many peoples We have destroyed after Noah!” Noah’s flood was the first great destruction of a global corrupt civilization, and after him numerous other nations—ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Lot, and Pharaoh’s host—were annihilated for their sins.

The word “how many” is not asking for a number but emphasizing the sheer multitude of destroyed civilizations. The ruins and tales of these peoples were well known to the Arabs, some still visible in their trade routes.

This is a reminder that divine punishment is not an abstract threat but a recurring historical reality. Then the verse shifts to a comforting yet fearsome truth: “And sufficient is your Lord as All-Aware and All-Seeing of the sins of His servants.” Allah’s attributes of being All-Aware (Khabīr) and All-Seeing (Baṣīr) mean that no sin escapes His knowledge, no oppression is hidden from His sight.

This is sufficient for justice. The wrongdoer may escape human authorities, but there is no escape from the One who is fully aware of the innermost secrets. For the Prophet and the believers facing persecution in Mecca, this verse was a powerful reassurance: the Meccan elite’s transgressions are fully documented, and history shows that such arrogant peoples eventually meet their doom. It also personalizes the warning: Allah sees your sins, so do not be deluded that secret misdeeds are forgotten. This dual aspect—historical precedent and present divine surveillance—creates a profound spiritual vigilance in the heart of the believer, encouraging them to purify their private and public actions.

=18=

Now the Quran draws a sharp contrast between two kinds of people. “Whoever desires this fleeting world alone” identifies the one whose ultimate goal, efforts, and concerns are entirely bound to the passing pleasures and gains of this earthly life.

The word “fleeting” translates “ʿājilah,” meaning something hastened away and temporary. For such a person, Allah says, “We hasten in it whatever We please to whoever We will. ” This is a crucial point: not every worldly desirer gets all that they wish for. Allah gives out of His wisdom, some receiving much and others little, as a test and a consequence of His will, not as a reward for their desire. But this hastened portion is limited and temporary. Then comes the devastating conclusion: “then We destine them for Hell, where they will burn, condemned and rejected. ” The word “destine” indicates that Hell is their ultimate abode, an inevitable destination. They will enter it “condemned” (madhmūm) and “rejected” (madḥūr), meaning driven away from all good and mercy.

This is the fate of those who lived solely for this world, ignoring the Hereafter. The verse teaches that worldly success is not a sign of divine approval when it is disconnected from the Hereafter. A person may have wealth, fame, and comfort, yet be heading toward ultimate disgrace.

The phrase “whoever desires this fleeting world alone” emphasizes that the problem is not having worldly things, but making them the exclusive object of one’s desire. A believer may enjoy lawful worldly blessings, but their primary desire is the Hereafter, and they use the world as a means to reach it. This verse awakens a sobering sense of prioritization, urging the listener to examine where their ultimate desire lies.

=19=

In contrast to the previous verse, this one describes the successful. “But whoever desires the Hereafter” means the ultimate goal and longing of the heart is the eternal life, Allah’s pleasure, and Paradise. This desire is not passive; it is followed by “strives for it accordingly.

” The Arabic word “saʿā” implies earnest, diligent effort, going above the bare minimum. The phrase “accordingly” means that the effort matches the magnitude of the goal—seeking the Hereafter demands a striving that is consistent with the greatness of that reward. But striving alone is not enough, for even a non-believer may work hard for a cause.

Therefore, the verse adds “and is a true believer.” Faith is the necessary foundation that makes the striving acceptable. Without īmān, even the most impressive worldly efforts count for nothing in the Hereafter. Then the promise: “it is they whose striving will be appreciated.” Allah, the Most Generous, will recognize, accept, and reward their efforts.

The word “appreciated” (mashkūr) implies that their deeds will be received with gratitude from Allah—an amazing expression of divine generosity, as if the Lord thanks His servant for the good done, though the benefit returns only to the servant. This verse sets a complete formula for success: sincere desire for the Hereafter, vigorous effort that matches that desire, and sound faith. It dispels the idea that mere wishes or shallow claims of belief are sufficient. The believer is invited to constantly align their daily actions with this ultimate desire, so that every hardship, every act of worship, and every moral struggle becomes part of a striving that is seen, valued, and rewarded by the Most Merciful.

=20=

The final verse of this passage ties the two groups together, showing that in worldly provision, Allah is universally generous. “We provide both the former and the latter” meaning both the group that desires only this world and the group that desires the Hereafter receive their sustenance from Allah’s bounty. The sun shines on the righteous and the wicked alike; food, health, and opportunities are distributed by divine wisdom without discrimination in this life.

The phrase “from the bounty of your Lord” reminds us that every good thing we enjoy is a gift, not a right. Then the verse adds a profound closing statement: “And the bounty of your Lord can never be withheld.” No power in the heavens or the earth can prevent Allah from giving.

This statement serves as a reassurance to the believers who might see disbelievers flourishing in worldly terms and feel discouraged. It also warns those who are intoxicated by their wealth that their provision is not a result of their own independent power but of Allah’s bounty, and He could cut it off at any moment if He willed. More deeply, it points to the unlimited nature of Allah’s generosity in the Hereafter. If in this temporary world, the disbeliever is not deprived of provision, what then of the believer’s reward in the eternal life? That bounty is far greater and absolutely certain. This verse beautifully transitions into the next section where Allah will speak about the varying degrees of favour among people in this world, and how the Hereafter is far greater. It leaves the reader with a sense of trust and contentment: the Lord’s bounty is vast, unimpeded, and perfectly distributed according to His knowledge. So we should seek the portion that truly matters.
