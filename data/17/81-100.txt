=81=

After the supplication for honourable entrance and exit, the Prophet is instructed to announce a fundamental divine law. “And declare” is a command to proclaim openly, without fear or hesitation. The declaration itself is a triumphant statement: “The truth has come and falsehood has vanished.

” The word for “truth” is al-ḥaqq, which is one of Allah’s names and the essence of all reality. The truth here refers to Islam, the Quran, and the message of tawḥīd. “Has come” indicates its arrival is a decisive, completed event—the light has dawned and can no longer be ignored. “Falsehood” translates al-bāṭil, meaning all that is futile, untrue, and destined to perish—idolatry, disbelief, oppression, and lies. “Has vanished” uses the Arabic zahūq, implying something that is inherently transient, something that disappears by its very nature, like foam on a wave.

The second part of the verse reinforces this with a universal principle: “Indeed, falsehood is bound to vanish.” The word “bound” underscores that this is an unalterable divine decree; falsehood has no permanent footing. This verse is a powerful injection of confidence. It was proclaimed by the Prophet upon the conquest of Mecca, when he entered the Kaʿbah and destroyed the idols, reciting this verse as he toppled them one by one. The idols, representing decades of entrenched falsehood, crumbled in a matter of hours.

Historically, this verse assured the persecuted believers that the dominance of the Quraysh was an illusion that would soon dissipate. For every believer, it is a timeless source of hope: no matter how powerful falsehood appears—in the form of corrupt systems, dominant ideologies, or personal sins—it carries within itself the seeds of its own destruction. Truth, however small its beginnings, is rooted in the eternal and must grow. The command to “declare” also reminds us that we have a role in speaking truth and challenging falsehood, not through violence but through clear proclamation. The verse is a profound meditation on the nature of reality; only what is connected to Allah, the Ultimate Truth, has lasting existence. Everything else is a fleeting shadow.

=82=

The verse describes the dual effect of the Quran. “We send down the Quran” emphasizes its divine origin; it is not the speech of a man but revelation from above. It is described as “a healing and mercy.

” The word “healing” (shifāʾ) applies to a wide range of ailments: it heals the heart from doubts, ignorance, arrogance, envy, and all spiritual diseases. It heals the mind from confusion and false ideologies. It even provides psychological and, by extension, physical comfort. It is a cure for the disease of shirk, which is the deadliest spiritual illness.

The word “mercy” (raḥmah) encompasses all the good that flows from it: guidance, peace, divine favour, and salvation. However, this healing and mercy are specifically “for the believers.” Only those who approach the Quran with faith, humility, and a willingness to be cured will benefit. They open their hearts, and its verses act as a medicine that removes their inner sickness.

The verse then states the opposite effect: “but it only increases the wrongdoers in loss. ” The “wrongdoers” (ẓālimūn) are those who reject and mock the Quran. When they hear it, instead of being healed, their spiritual disease worsens. Their arrogance increases, their hearts harden further, and their ultimate loss in the Hereafter becomes greater. The same rain that nourishes a fertile garden causes only more barrenness in rocky, unyielding soil.

This is not a defect in the Quran but a manifestation of the differing receptacles. This verse explains the phenomenon of the Meccan leaders; the more they heard the Quran, the more hostile they became, sealing their fate.

For the believer, it is a reminder to always approach the Quran with the heart of a patient seeking cure, not a critic seeking faults. It instills a deep gratitude for the gift of the Quran and a constant awareness that one’s spiritual state determines whether the same divine words become a healing mercy or a cause of increased ruin.

=83=

This verse exposes a recurrent flaw in human nature, particularly among those lacking deep faith. “When We grant people Our favours” refers to any blessing: health, wealth, success, power, or ease. The reaction described is “they turn away, acting arrogantly.” Instead of drawing closer to the Giver and expressing gratitude, they become heedless, as if they earned these blessings by their own merit. Their “turning away” is a physical and spiritual distancing from obedience and remembrance. Their “acting arrogantly” is a display of pride and self-sufficiency, looking down on others and feeling secure from any loss.

This is the story of Qārūn, who said, “I have been given it only because of knowledge I possess. ” The verse then contrasts this with the opposite condition: “But when touched with evil, they lose all hope. ” Evil here refers to harm, illness, poverty, or difficulty. The same person who was arrogantly self-reliant suddenly plunges into utter despair (yaʾūs qanūṭ). They see no way out, forgetting the Lord who granted them the previous blessings, and often falling into depression or ingratitude. This emotional rollercoaster—pride in ease and despair in hardship—is a sign of a heart not anchored in true īmān. The believer, by contrast, is grateful in ease and patient in hardship, knowing both come from Allah. This verse is a mirror for self-reflection: how do we react when we get a promotion versus when we face a setback? The consistent believer remains humble and hopeful. It also serves as a warning to the Meccan pagans, whose arrogance during prosperity was matched by their panic during famines. The verse connects to the next, which explains that everyone acts according to their character, and Allah alone knows who is guided. It teaches that true stability lies in attaching oneself to the unchanging God, not to the changing circumstances of life.

=84=

In response to the varying human reactions described in the previous verse, Allah commands the Prophet to state a profound principle. “Say, O Prophet, ‘Everyone acts in their own way.’” The Arabic “kullun yaʿmalu ʿalā shākilatihi” means each person acts according to their character, disposition, and natural bent. Shākilah refers to one’s inherent nature, formed by intentions, beliefs, and habits.

This is a statement of fact: the believer acts in accordance with faith, the disbeliever in accordance with disbelief, the grateful person according to gratitude, the arrogant according to pride. People will inevitably manifest their inner selves through their deeds.

This is not a commendation of all ways but an acknowledgment of human diversity in moral and spiritual orientations. The Prophet is not to force change but to recognize that people’s actions reflect their inner state.

The verse then concludes: “But your Lord knows best whose way is rightly guided. ” Ultimate judgment belongs to Allah alone. He knows whose path is truly guided—who is sincerely seeking truth and who is stubbornly clinging to falsehood. The external labels people claim for themselves are irrelevant; the reality is known only to the All-Knowing. This verse provides immense comfort to the caller to truth. When you present the message and people react with arrogance or despair, remember that they are merely acting out their inner characters, and you are not responsible for altering their inner makeup. Your duty is to deliver, and Allah will judge. It also instills humility in the believer: we must constantly check our own shākilah. Is our way shaped by sincere faith or by hidden desires? The verse naturally follows the description of contrasting human reactions; it explains that those who turn away arrogantly are acting according to their diseased disposition, while the believers act according to their grateful one. The right way is not a matter of opinion but is known best by the Lord, who will guide whomever sincerely seeks it.

=85=

The Meccan pagans, prompted by the Jews of Medina, sought to test the Prophet’s knowledge by asking about the nature of the rūḥ, the spirit. “They ask you O Prophet about the spirit.

” The word “rūḥ” is a profound mystery that encompasses the life force, the human soul, and the angel Gabriel, who is also called al-Rūḥ. The verse does not specify which meaning they asked about, but the context of human life and resurrection suggests they were asking about the human soul: what is its essence, how does it relate to the body, and how will it be restored after death? They hoped to stump the Prophet and expose his supposed lack of knowledge. The divine response is a masterclass in dealing with questions intended to entrap: “Say, ‘Its nature is known only to my Lord.’” The knowledge of the spirit belongs exclusively to Allah. It is not something that human reason or empirical investigation can fully comprehend. The spirit is a creation of Allah, and its reality is among the matters of the unseen. This response is not an admission of ignorance but a declaration of the limits of human knowledge.

The verse then adds a humbling truth: “and you O humanity have been given but little knowledge.” The word “little” (qalīl) puts all human achievement in perspective. No matter how much science advances, the totality of knowledge given to humanity is minuscule compared to the vast ocean of Allah’s knowledge. This verse established a crucial Islamic epistemological principle: there are limits to what humans can know, and accepting these limits is itself wisdom. It prevents arrogance from intellectual achievements and keeps the door of faith in the unseen wide open. In modern times, when people ask about the nature of consciousness, the soul, or life after death, the same principle applies: we can observe effects, but the essence belongs to Allah.

The verse also subtly answers the question by pointing to the resurrection: the One who created the spirit the first time can easily restore it, even if its nature remains a divine secret.

=86=

This verse and the next one form a powerful pair, highlighting the immensity of Allah’s favour in preserving the Quran. “If We willed, We could have certainly taken away what We have revealed to you O Prophet.” The “what We have revealed” is the Quran, which the Prophet treasured more than anything. Allah reminds him that this revelation is not his by right but a gift that could be withdrawn at any moment. The taking away could mean erasing it from the Prophet’s heart and memory, removing its text from the earth, or causing its blessings to cease. The hypothetical scenario is stark: if Allah willed to take away the Quran, no power could stop Him. Then the verse adds: “then you would find none to guarantee its return from Us.

” The word “guarantee” translates wakīl, meaning a protector or trustee who could secure its restoration. No angel, no jinn, no human being could intercede to bring it back.

This verse teaches the Prophet and the believers not to take the presence of the Quran for granted. It is a continuous mercy that must be cherished with constant gratitude, recitation, and practice. The warning is not just for the Prophet but for the entire ummah: if we neglect the Quran, misuse it, or fail to honour it, Allah could lift it from among us, leaving us in darkness. This has profound contemporary implications; many Muslim communities today have the Quran physically but have lost its spirit and guidance from their lives, which is a form of its being “taken away” in effect.

The verse also underscores that the preservation of the Quran is a miracle in itself; it is not due to human effort but divine will. The fear of losing it should drive us to cling to it more firmly. It connects to the next verse, which explains the sole reason this has not happened: sheer divine mercy and immense favour.

=87=

This verse completes the sentence begun in the previous one, providing the reason why the Quran is not taken away. “Had it not been for the mercy of your Lord.” The entire survival and preservation of the revelation is attributed solely to Allah’s mercy. This mercy is not due to any merit on the part of the recipients but is a pure, unearned gift. The mercy here encompasses the initial sending down of the Quran, its preservation in the Prophet’s heart, and its continuing presence among the believers.

The verse then emphatically states: “Indeed, His favour upon you is immense.” The word “favour” (faḍl) is a great bounty, far beyond what anyone could deserve. The address is to the Prophet, but by extension to the entire community of believers. The Quran itself is the greatest favour bestowed upon humanity, greater than all the material blessings of the world combined. It is the rope of Allah, the light, the guidance, the healing. Recognizing this immensity should fill the believer’s heart with an overwhelming sense of gratitude. Every time we open the mushaf or listen to recitation, we should remember that this is an immense favour that could have been withdrawn, but was left out of mercy. This awareness transforms our relationship with the Quran from casual to deeply reverential. It also humbles any pride that might arise from being a carrier of the Quran; we are not special because we hold it, but we are held by the mercy that gave it. The verse forms a perfect pair with the preceding one: fear of its removal, followed by gratitude for its retention. This combination of khawf and rajāʾ is the essence of a healthy spiritual life. It also refutes any notion that the Prophet could have authored the Quran; if it were his own work, he would not live in fear of it being taken away by a higher power. It is a definitive argument for the divine origin of the revelation.

=88=

This verse is the ultimate challenge to those who doubted the divine origin of the Quran. The Prophet is commanded to issue an open, permanent challenge: “If all humans and jinn were to come together to produce the equivalent of this Quran, they could not produce its equal.” The entire rational and unseen creation—humans with their intellect and language, jinn with their supernatural abilities—are all called to the task. The challenge is not just to produce a book of equal length, but “its equal” in eloquence, wisdom, truth, guidance, legislation, and spiritual impact.

The phrase “no matter how they supported each other” translates “wa law kāna baʿḍuhum li-baʿḍin ẓahīran,” meaning even if they formed a massive cooperative effort, pooling all their resources and genius, they would still fail. This is a bold, unassailable claim. The Quran is inimitable (iʿjāz) in multiple dimensions: its linguistic perfection, its structural coherence, its profound knowledge of the unseen, its comprehensive and just laws, and its transformative power over souls. No human or jinn can produce a text that matches it. This challenge has stood for over fourteen centuries, and not a single successful imitation has been produced; many tried, like Musaylimah, and their attempts were laughable. This verse is a rational proof of the Quran’s divine origin.

For the believer, it is a pillar of certainty: the book we hold is not the product of human genius but the very speech of the Creator. It also invites us to constantly study the Quran to appreciate its inimitability, which strengthens faith. For the skeptic, it is a gauntlet thrown down for all time: if you doubt, try to match it.

The verse also sets the Quran apart from all other scriptures and books; it stands unique in its miraculous nature, a living miracle given to the unlettered Prophet, and it remains as fresh and challenging today as the day it was revealed.

=89=

This verse explains that the Quran contains all the necessary guidance. “And We have truly set forth every kind of lesson for humanity in this Quran.

” The word “ṣarrafnā” means We have turned and diversified the lessons, presenting them in every possible form—parables, stories, commands, prohibitions, arguments, promises, and threats. “Every kind of lesson” translates “kulli mathal,” meaning all essential truths, moral examples, and spiritual teachings are covered. The Quran is a complete guidance; no fundamental human need is left unaddressed.

This is a great mercy, as it provides a comprehensive manual for life. Yet, despite this exhaustive clarity and variety, “yet most people persist in disbelief.

” The word “abā” means they refuse stubbornly and arrogantly. The phrase “most people” indicates that the majority, across all times, reject the truth, not because of any lack of evidence, but because of their own obstinate natures.

This is a comfort to the Prophet and to all believers: if people reject the message, it is not due to insufficient presentation but due to their own choice. The Quran’s abundance of lessons, far from satisfying them, becomes a burden they wish to avoid, because accepting it would mean changing their lifestyles and acknowledging accountability.

This verse also calls believers to deeply explore the Quran, recognizing that it contains every lesson we need for our personal and collective lives. We should never feel that the Quran is lacking in guidance for any modern problem; it is we who fail to extract and apply its lessons. The universality and completeness of the Quran are highlighted, and our task is to turn to it fully, rather than seeking other ideologies. The verse connects to the next set of verses, where the pagans demand specific, unreasonable signs, exemplifying their refusal despite the comprehensive nature of the Quranic message.

=90=

This verse begins a series of demands made by the Quraysh, illustrating their arrogance and absurdity. “They challenge the Prophet” comes from “wa qālū,” they said to him, but the context is one of defiant challenge, not sincere inquiry. Their statement “We will never believe in you” is a categorical refusal that sets the stage. They make their belief conditional upon tangible, supernatural acts: “until you cause a spring to gush forth from the earth for us. ” Mecca was a dry, rocky valley, and a perpetual, gushing spring would transform it into a garden. This demand was not about seeking truth but about worldly benefit and testing the Prophet’s power. They wanted a personal miracle that would serve their material comfort. It reveals their mindset: they saw prophethood as a kind of sorcery that could produce wonders on demand, not as a moral and spiritual call. The specific request for a spring echoes the story of Moses striking the rock, but the Quraysh were not like the Israelites; they were not following a prophet and then becoming thirsty, they were asking for a sign to satisfy their skepticism. The verse exposes the pattern of demanding signs as a pretext for disbelief. If the spring were produced, they would likely dismiss it as magic, exactly as Pharaoh did.

For the believer, this verse highlights the danger of setting conditions for faith. Faith is not a transaction; it is a submission of the heart once the truth is clear. Waiting for a “personal miracle” often masks a desire to avoid the moral obligations of religion. The Prophet’s response will come later, emphasizing that he is only a human messenger. The demand also reflects a materialistic worldview: they wanted a sign that adds to their worldly wealth, not one that transforms their souls. The series of demands becomes increasingly extravagant, revealing their insincerity.

=91=

The second demand follows the first: “or until you have a garden of palm trees and vineyards, and cause rivers to flow abundantly in it.” This request is a step up from the spring; it is a personal, lush, fertile estate. In the barren landscape of Mecca, a garden of date palms and grapevines, with rivers freely flowing through it, was the epitome of luxury and success. By asking for this, they were essentially challenging the Prophet to prove his claim by displaying personal wealth and material power. They wanted a visible, material proof of divine favour, confusing prophethood with worldly kingship. Their mindset was: if Allah truly favoured you, He would give you such a garden.

This is a fundamental misunderstanding of the nature of prophethood; prophets are not sent to amass worldly wealth but to deliver a message, and their miracles are given to establish truth, not to satisfy personal whims. The demand for a personal garden also revealed their envy and skepticism. If he could produce it, they would say it was magic; if he could not, they would say he was a fraud.

This is a common tactic of disbelievers: setting impossible or irrelevant standards that were never the criteria for prophethood. The believer learns from this that the absence of worldly wealth in the lives of the righteous is not a sign of their falsehood. The Prophet lived humbly, and his miracle was the Quran, not a private estate.

The verse also highlights the human tendency to measure truth by material success, a fallacy that the Quran repeatedly refutes. The righteous may be tested with poverty, and the wicked may be granted gardens, as a test. True success is the garden of Paradise, not a garden on earth.

=92=

The demands escalate to the realm of the cosmic and the directly visible divine. “Or cause the sky to fall upon us in pieces, as you have claimed.” They referred to the Quranic warnings of divine punishment, where a fragment of the sky could fall as a sign of doom. They mockingly say: you threaten us with this; go ahead, make it happen now. This was not a request for guidance but a suicidal dare, a challenge rooted in arrogance. They thought they were safe, not believing in the possibility. “Or bring Allah and the angels before us, face to face.

” This is the ultimate audacity: a direct demand to see God and the angels physically, in plain sight, to have a face-to-face encounter. This reveals their profound misunderstanding of the divine. They could not comprehend that the majestic and transcendent Lord is beyond such physical manifestation in this world. Even Prophet Moses, who spoke to Allah, was told he could not see Him. To demand a visible, physical encounter with Allah is to ignore the very nature of God as described by revelation. This request is similar to the Israelites who said, “Show us Allah outright.” The demand for angels “face to face” shows they wanted intermediaries in a form they could physically verify, rejecting the idea of an unseen message. The verse exposes the absurd depths to which the disbelievers will go to avoid accepting the truth. They set conditions that are inherently impossible or inappropriate, all to justify their rejection. It teaches the believer that the demand for empirical, sensory proof for every aspect of faith is a category error; spiritual truths are accepted through signs and sound reasoning, not by making the unseen fully visible on human terms.

=93=

The final set of demands culminates in a grand, materialistic ultimatum. “Or until you have a house of gold”—a palace of pure gold, a symbol of unimaginable wealth and power. They wanted visible, tangible evidence of superhuman status. “Or you ascend into heaven,” meaning physically rise to the sky in their presence. Even this, they claim, would not suffice: “and even then we will not believe in your ascension until you bring down to us a book that we can read.” They preemptively reject the very thing that the Prophet had already experienced—the Miʿrāj—and demand a physical token, a readable book from the heavens, as proof. Their skepticism is absolute; nothing short of a tangible object they can hold and read would convince them, and even then, the subtext is they would likely find an excuse. The Prophet is commanded to respond with a clear, dignified reply: “Say, ‘Glory be to my Lord! Am I not only a human messenger?’” The first part, “Subḥāna rabbī,” is an exclamation of glorification, dismissing their absurd demands and declaring Allah far above such human tests.

The second part, “Am I not only a human messenger?” is the crux. He acknowledges his humanity; he does not claim to be a lord or an angel. His role is to convey the message, not to perform arbitrary miracles on demand. Miracles are given by Allah for a purpose, not to entertain skeptics.

This is the perfect response. It reminds that the essence of prophethood is the message, not the messenger’s personal material status or ability to perform tricks.

The verse teaches that faith is not built on satisfying endless, shifting demands; it is built on recognizing the truth of the message itself. The Prophet’s humility and clarity in this response show that the real miracle was the Quran, and those who cannot be guided by it would not be guided by any other sign.

=94=

This verse identifies the core intellectual objection of the disbelievers, an objection that recurs throughout history. “And nothing has prevented people from believing when guidance comes to them” establishes that the guidance is clear and sufficient; the problem lies entirely in the hearts of the rejecters. The only thing that stops them is their “protest” or amazement: “Has Allah sent a human as a messenger?” They found it inconceivable that a human being, who eats, walks in the markets, and has a family, could be a divinely chosen messenger. Their false assumption was that a messenger must be an angel, a being of a higher order, not a mere mortal. They said, “What is this messenger that he eats food and walks in the markets?” This objection stems from pride and a failure to understand the wisdom of Allah. A human messenger is the most effective guide for humans; he shares their nature, experiences their struggles, and serves as a tangible role model. An angel would be alien, and they would have objected, “We cannot relate to him. ” Moreover, prophethood is an honour bestowed by Allah, not a rank earned by one’s nature. The Meccan pagans’ protest was not a sincere inquiry but an excuse to justify their rejection. The verse unpacks the psychology of disbelief: people often reject truth because it comes through a vehicle they consider ordinary or beneath them. The same objection is made against the Quran: they wanted an angel, but Allah sent a man with a book.

This verse teaches the believer to appreciate the profound wisdom of human messengers. It also reminds us that the value of a message is not determined by the worldly status of its bearer. The Prophet Muḥammad’s humanity is a mercy, making his life an accessible pattern for us to follow, and his human struggles a source of comfort for us in our own trials.

=95=

Allah instructs the Prophet to respond to their objection with a profound logical argument. “Say, O Prophet, ‘Had there been angels walking the earth, well settled…’” The hypothetical scenario presents the fundamental principle of divine communication: the messenger must be of the same kind as the recipients. If the inhabitants of the earth were angels, settled and living in a stable manner, then the messenger sent to them would logically be an angel from heaven.

This is because communication, example, and leadership require commonality. An angelic messenger for human beings would create a disconnection; humans could not emulate him or fully comprehend his nature. The verse exposes the folly of their demand. They asked for an angel, but the very fact that they are humans means that the messenger must also be human. If an angel were sent, it would either have to appear in human form, which would negate their demand, or remain in angelic form, making interaction impossible. This response also subtly hints at a deeper truth: the earth is a testing ground for humans and jinn, not for angels. The angels are already obedient; the test is for beings with free will, and thus the guide must be from among them.

This verse establishes the methodology of divine sending: every prophet was from his own people, speaking their language. It is a universal law. The wisdom of this is that the messenger is a living proof; if an angel were sent, the disbelievers would still object, claiming the angel had an unfair advantage.

For the believer, this verse reinforces the perfect wisdom of Allah in sending human prophets. It dismantles any lingering doubt about why our Prophet was a man. It also teaches us to accept Allah’s choices without imposing our limited, often ignorant, preferences. The argument is simple, clear, and unbeatable, revealing the unreasonableness of the pagan demand.

=96=

After dismantling their objections with reason, the Prophet is instructed to conclude the debate with a powerful declaration of divine witness. “Say, ‘Sufficient is Allah as a Witness between me and you.’” When all arguments are presented and the disbelievers still refuse, the matter is entrusted to Allah. The Prophet declares that he does not need their acceptance to validate his truthfulness; Allah’s testimony is enough. Allah is the witness who knows the truth of the Prophet’s message and the falsehood of their rejection. This witness will be fully manifested on the Day of Judgment, but it is a reality even now.

The verse then emphasizes two attributes of this Witness: “He is certainly All-Knowing, All-Seeing of His servants.” All-Knowing (Khabīr) indicates His complete awareness of inner intentions, secret plots, and the sincerity of the Prophet. All-Seeing (Baṣīr) indicates His observation of outward actions, the efforts of the Prophet, and the stubbornness of the disbelievers. Nothing is hidden from Him. This declaration is a source of immense peace for the believer. When we are engaged in daʿwah or facing rejection, we must know that our ultimate validation is not from people but from Allah. If we have Allah as our witness, we need no other. It is also a warning to the rejecters: the All-Knowing, All-Seeing is recording everything, and His witness will be the strongest evidence against them.

The verse shifts the focus from human judgment to divine judgment, relieving the Prophet of the emotional burden of their rejection. It teaches us to adopt the same attitude: when we have sincerely conveyed the truth and people still argue and reject, we say, “Allah is sufficient as a witness,” and move forward, leaving the outcome to Him.

This is the ultimate psychological and spiritual closure to any conflict with disbelievers.

=97=

This verse draws a stark conclusion about guidance and misguidance. “Whoever Allah guides is truly guided.” True guidance is exclusively from Allah. A person may have all the rational proofs, all the prophets, all the scriptures, but without the divine light cast into the heart, they will not be guided. This guidance is a gift for those who sincerely seek it. “And whoever He leaves to stray, you will find no guardians for them besides Him.” Those whom Allah, in His wisdom, allows to stray due to their persistent rejection and arrogance, have no one to guide or protect them. The Prophet himself cannot guide them; no human power can override Allah’s decree.

This is a statement of fact that both humbles the caller and instills reliance on Allah alone for guidance. The verse then paints a terrifying picture of their end: “And We will drag them on their faces on the Day of Judgment—deaf, dumb, and blind.” They will be gathered on their faces, a position of utter humiliation, dragged into Hell. They will be “deaf” to any good words, “dumb” to utter any excuse, and “blind” to see any path of escape.

This is the culmination of their spiritual state in this world, where they willfully deafened their ears to truth, silenced their tongues from speaking it, and blinded their eyes to its signs. “Hell will be their home.” It is not a temporary detention but a permanent abode. And the torment is relentless: “Whenever it dies down, We will flare it up for them. ” Just as the fire subsides, it is rekindled with fresh intensity, ensuring the punishment never lightens.

This is a graphic, awe-inspiring warning. It reinforces the earlier theme of the blindness in the Hereafter.

For the believer, it creates a profound gratitude for guidance and a constant prayer for steadfastness, recognizing that guidance is a mercy that must be cherished and renewed daily.

=98=

This verse links the severe punishment described directly to two specific crimes of the disbelievers. “That is their reward” refers to the dragging on faces and the eternal hellfire. It is the fitting recompense for two things. First, “rejecting Our signs. ” The signs include the verses of the Quran, the natural signs in creation, and the miracles. Their rejection was not based on lack of clarity but on arrogance and willful denial. Second, and equally damning, they are punished for “asking mockingly, ‘When we are reduced to bones and ashes, will we really be raised as a new creation?’” This question was posed in verse 49 of this same surah. The Quran records their mockery and then later reveals that this very mockery becomes a cause for their eternal doom. The resurrection, which they ridiculed, becomes the undeniable reality they are forced to face. Their sarcasm, which seemed clever and harmless to them in the world, is registered as a grave sin because it mocks a core tenet of faith that all prophets proclaimed. This teaches us that mocking the truths of religion is no small matter; it reflects a deep-seated contempt for divine wisdom. The verse is a warning against trivializing or ridiculing any aspect of the unseen. It also beautifully wraps up the theme of resurrection that ran through the earlier parts of the surah. The punishment fits the crime: they denied being raised as a new creation, so they are raised—but to a creation of torment. The verse serves as a powerful deterrent, making the believer take every word seriously, especially concerning matters of faith. We must guard our tongues from any sarcasm or dismissive speech about the afterlife, for such words come from a heart that doubts, and they will be presented as evidence against us.

=99=

After stating their punishment, the Quran returns to the rational argument for resurrection, questioning their lack of realization. “Have they not realized” is a rhetorical question expressing astonishment at their intellectual blindness. The logic is irrefutable: “Allah, Who created the heavens and the earth, can easily re-create them.” The heavens and the earth are the greatest imaginable creations, vast and complex beyond human comprehension. The One who originated this entire universe from non-existence without any model or difficulty certainly has the power to reassemble human bones and ashes.

This is an argument from the greater to the lesser. If someone can build a palace, can they not rebuild a small room?

The word “easily” is implied in the Arabic structure; the recreation is trivial for such power. Then the verse adds another element: “He has already set for them a time, about which there is no doubt.” The resurrection is not just a possibility; it is an appointed certainty. The exact time is fixed in Allah’s knowledge, and it will occur without fail. This answers the question “When will that be?” posed earlier. It will come at its preordained moment, leaving no room for escape. Despite this clear argument, “But the wrongdoers persist in denial.

” The word “wrongdoers” (ẓālimūn) identifies their denial as an act of injustice, a transgression against truth and against their own souls. They choose to deny despite the evidence because they do not wish to face the implications of accountability. This verse is a masterclass in rational daʿwah: directing the mind to the observable creation to infer the unseen recreation. It calls every skeptic to ponder the universe; its very existence is proof of the Creator’s ability to resurrect. The surah concludes this section with a psychological insight into the nature of the disbelievers, and humans in general. “Say to them, O Prophet, ‘Even if you were to possess the infinite treasuries of my Lord’s mercy…’” The “treasuries of mercy” encompass all forms of provision, blessings, and bounties that Allah bestows—wealth, rain, knowledge, guidance, and more. The scenario is hypothetical: if these miserly, demanding people were given control over Allah’s unlimited treasures, what would they do?

The answer: “then you would certainly withhold them, fearing they would run out. ” Their stinginess is so deeply ingrained that even with infinite resources, they would hoard and fear depletion. They would not distribute generously because their mindset is one of scarcity and selfishness. This exposes their hypocrisy: they demanded miracles and material wealth from the Prophet, but if they themselves were given the keys to divine mercy, they would not share it. Their demands were not about seeking truth but about their own greed and desire for control.

The verse ends with a universal diagnosis: “for humankind is ever stingy!” The word “qatūran” indicates an intense, habitual stinginess. This does not describe every single human but the predominant characteristic of the unrefined human soul when not illuminated by faith. Faith purifies the soul from stinginess and replaces it with generosity. The Quraysh were known for their miserliness in some respects, despite occasional hospitality. The verse is a profound warning against the love of wealth and the fear of poverty that drives stinginess. It teaches that the root of many of their rejections was not intellectual but moral—a selfish attachment to worldly possessions.

For the believer, it is a call to self-examination: do we hoard what Allah has given us, fearing we will not have enough? True trust in Allah’s mercy eliminates stinginess and opens the hand to give. The verse connects back to the commands earlier in the surah about spending and not being tight-fisted.
