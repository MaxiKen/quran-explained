=101=

This verse draws a parallel between the Prophet Muḥammad and his predecessor Moses, peace be upon them both, while also serving as a rebuttal to the Meccan pagans who demanded physical miracles. “We surely gave Moses nine clear signs.” The “nine clear signs” refer to the tangible proofs given to Moses to establish the truth of his prophethood before Pharaoh and the Egyptians. Although the Quran does not list them all in one place, scholars have compiled them from various verses: the staff turning into a serpent, the shining white hand, the years of drought, the shortage of crops, the flood, the locusts, the lice, the frogs, and the blood. Each of these was a distinct, unmistakable miracle, yet Pharaoh and his chiefs remained obstinate.

The phrase “clear signs” translates “āyātin bayyināt,” meaning signs that were manifest and left no rational room for denial. By reminding the Prophet of this history, Allah is comforting him: your people’s rejection is not new; even with nine powerful signs, Pharaoh refused to believe.

The verse then says, “You, O Prophet, can ask the Children of Israel. ” The Children of Israel in Medina were well aware of these signs and acknowledged them in their scriptures. The Prophet could ask them as witnesses to confirm this account, which also served as a proof that he was receiving revelation from the same God who sent Moses. This was a challenge to the Jews of Medina as well.

The verse then recounts: “When Moses came to them, Pharaoh said to him, ‘I really think that you, O Moses, are bewitched.’” Notice the accusation: Pharaoh did not deny the signs; he attributed them to sorcery. The word “bewitched” (masḥūr) is the exact same accusation the Quraysh hurled at the Prophet Muḥammad, as mentioned earlier in this surah. The parallelism is deliberate; the disbelievers’ playbook is the same across history. They cannot refute the truth, so they slander the messenger.

This verse teaches the believer that the repetition of accusations against prophets is a sign of truth, not falsehood. It also serves as a warning that witnessing miracles does not guarantee faith; the heart must be receptive. The nine clear signs were given, but Pharaoh’s arrogance rendered them useless for his guidance. The verse prepares for the following account of his downfall.

=102=

Moses, peace be upon him, responds to Pharaoh’s accusation with calm, reasoned confidence. “You know well that none has sent these signs down except the Lord of the heavens and the earth as insights.” Moses appeals to Pharaoh’s inner knowledge. Despite his outward arrogance, Pharaoh deep down recognized the divine origin of the signs.

The phrase “you know well” indicates that the truth was clear in Pharaoh’s conscience, but he suppressed it out of pride and political interest. The signs were not sent as mere magic tricks; they were “insights” (baṣāʾir), meaning they were meant to open the eyes of the heart to the truth of monotheism and the falsehood of Pharaoh’s claim to divinity. They were proofs that guided the sincere and left the obstinate without excuse.

This is exactly the nature of the Quranic signs; they are insights, not mere entertainment. Moses then delivers a blunt verdict: “And I really think that you, O Pharaoh, are doomed.

” The Arabic “mathbūran” means utterly destroyed, cut off from all good, and heading towards perdition. Moses did not say this out of anger or speculation but as a prophetic warning inspired by Allah. He saw that despite the clear signs, Pharaoh was only increasing in tyranny and arrogance, sealing his fate for destruction. This response teaches the balance of a dāʿī: Moses combined logical appeal with a firm warning. He told the truth both about the divine origin of the message and about the consequence of rejecting it. For the Prophet Muḥammad, this was a model. He too was called bewitched, and he too could respond by pointing to the clear nature of his signs—the Quran—and warning the Quraysh of their impending doom if they persisted.

The verse also reveals that the ultimate fate of a tyrant is not a mystery; it is evident to the eye of faith. The believer should not be intimidated by the power of the oppressor but should see through to his inevitable end.

The phrase “Lord of the heavens and the earth” emphasizes that the sender of the signs is the universal Creator, not a tribal deity, making Pharaoh’s self-deification utterly absurd.

=103=

After Moses presented the signs and delivered the warning, Pharaoh’s reaction was not repentance but intensified aggression. “So Pharaoh wanted to scare the Israelites out of the land of Egypt.

” The phrase “scare them out” translates “fa-arāda an yastafizzahum min al-arḍ,” indicating a planned campaign of intimidation, persecution, and possibly expulsion or extermination. Pharaoh sought to uproot the Children of Israel, to terrorize them into submission or drive them out entirely. He saw them as a demographic and ideological threat to his regime, especially after the humiliation of his magicians who believed in Moses. His plan was not just to suppress them but to eliminate their presence from the land.

However, the verse immediately reveals the divine counter-plan: “but We drowned him and all of those with him.” The contrast is sharp and powerful. While Pharaoh plotted to remove the Israelites, Allah removed Pharaoh and his army entirely, by drowning them in the Red Sea. The “all of those with him” emphasizes that his chiefs, soldiers, and supporters shared his fate. Not a single one escaped. This was the ultimate failure of a tyrant’s scheme. The verse highlights the law of divine retribution that has been a central theme of this surah: those who drive out the righteous are themselves destroyed. Earlier in verse 76, the Quraysh were warned that if they drove out the Prophet, they would not survive except for a little while. The story of Pharaoh serves as the archetypal example of this sunnah. Pharaoh’s arrogance, his rejection of the nine signs, and his plan to expel the believers led directly to his annihilation. For the believers facing persecution in Mecca, this was an immensely reassuring message. The power of the oppressor is temporary, and the plan of Allah is always greater. The drowning of Pharaoh is one of the most frequently recounted stories in the Quran for this very reason; it is a timeless symbol of the triumph of truth over falsehood.

The verse teaches that we should never fear the plots of tyrants, for Allah’s plan encompasses and overrides all human scheming.

=104=

After the destruction of Pharaoh, the Children of Israel inherited the land. “And We said to the Children of Israel after Pharaoh, ‘Reside in the land.’” The land here refers to Egypt, or more broadly the blessed land that Allah had decreed for them. After the exodus, they were given the opportunity to settle and live in security and prosperity, fulfilling the divine promise to them. The command to “reside” (uskunū) implies a settled, peaceful life, enjoying the blessings of the land.

However, the verse immediately attaches a condition and a reminder of the ultimate reality: “but when the promise of the Hereafter comes to pass, We will bring you all together.” This promise of the Hereafter (al-ākhirah) could refer to the Day of Judgment specifically, or to the ultimate fulfillment of all divine promises. On that Day, Allah will gather all of humanity—the oppressed and the oppressor, the faithful and the unfaithful, including the Children of Israel and Pharaoh’s people—for the final judgment.

This reminder was meant to keep them focused: the worldly residence in the land is temporary; the real gathering is yet to come, and with it comes accountability. This verse also serves as a bridge from the story of Moses to the affirmation of the Quran. The Children of Israel were given scripture, land, and prophets, yet they often strayed, as earlier verses in this surah detailed.

The mention of the Hereafter gathering connects to the Quran’s role as a warner about that very Day. For the Meccan pagans, this verse is an indirect warning: just as the Israelites were given blessings and then held accountable, so too will you. The land you now enjoy is a test; the true meeting with your Lord is inevitable. For the believers, it reinforces the concept that all worldly nations, no matter how favoured, will ultimately be gathered for judgment. This prevents any sense of false entitlement and keeps the focus on righteousness. The verse masterfully ties together the historical lesson with the eschatological reality that is the Quran’s central theme.

=105=

After recounting the story of Moses and the signs, the surah returns to the primary subject: the Quran itself. “We have sent down the Quran in truth, and with the truth it has come down.

” The repetition of “truth” underscores that the Quran is entirely and exclusively truth from its source, its content, its commands, and its descent. It is not a fabrication, not a poetry, not a sorcery, but the absolute truth from the Lord of the worlds.

The phrase “in truth” (bi-al-ḥaqq) indicates the purpose and content, while “with the truth it has come down” indicates the manner of its revelation—it descended accompanied by justice, right guidance, and the angel Gabriel faithfully conveying it. There is no falsehood in it, from before it or behind it. This verse powerfully refutes all the accusations hurled at the Prophet by the Quraysh. It reasserts the divine origin of the Quran, which is the ultimate sign, greater than the nine signs given to Moses. Then the verse clarifies the Prophet’s mission: “We have sent you O Prophet only as a deliverer of good news and a warner.” The “only” is crucial; his role is purely to convey the message with glad tidings for the believers and warnings for the disbelievers. He is not responsible for forcing faith, nor is he a keeper over them, as mentioned earlier.

This is a restatement of the prophetic job description. The good news (bashīr) is of Paradise, mercy, and divine pleasure for those who believe and do good. The warning (nadhīr) is of Hell, punishment, and loss for those who reject. The balance of hope and fear is essential for spiritual health. This verse, placed here, serves as a summary of the entire surah: the Quran is the truth, and the Prophet’s duty is clear. The stories of Moses, the commands, the arguments against idolatry, and the scenes of judgment all fall under this dual function of glad tidings and warning.

For the believer, it inspires confidence in the book and clarifies the role of the messenger, preventing any exaggerated veneration while honouring his mission.

=106=

This verse explains the wisdom behind the gradual revelation of the Quran. “It is a Quran We have revealed in stages” translates “wa qurʾānan faraqnāhu,” meaning We have divided and separated it into portions over time. The Quran was not revealed as a single book all at once; rather, it was sent down piecemeal over twenty-three years. The purpose is explicitly stated: “so that you may recite it to people at a deliberate pace. ” The deliberate pace (ʿalā mukthin) ensures that the message can be absorbed, memorized, understood, and implemented gradually. This gradual process was essential for the transformation of a deeply entrenched pagan society. If the entire legal code and theology had come at once, it would have overwhelmed the early Muslims. Instead, verses addressing specific situations, questions, and needs were revealed, allowing the community to grow organically in faith and practice.

The verse also says, “And We have sent it down in successive revelations,” translating “nazzalnāhu tanzīlan,” emphasizing a deliberate, sequential sending. This gradual revelation also served as a continuous miracle, constantly answering the objections of the disbelievers and providing fresh guidance and comfort to the Prophet and the believers.

In contrast to the nine signs of Moses, which were given at once and then ended, the Quranic revelation was an ongoing sign that accompanied the community throughout its formation. For modern believers, this verse reminds us to approach the Quran with patience. We should not rush through its recitation without reflection, nor expect instant mastery of its meanings. The deliberate pace prescribed for the Prophet is a model for our own engagement with the Quran: reading, pondering, and gradually applying its teachings. It also assures us that the apparent fragmentation of topics in the Quran has a divine wisdom; the arrangement and the piecemeal revelation both serve the goal of effective guidance. The surah’s own structure, moving between various themes, reflects this deliberate, pedagogical method.

=107=

The Prophet is instructed to address the disbelievers with a statement that reflects both the independence of the truth and the recognition it receives from genuine seekers. “Say, O Prophet, ‘Believe in this Quran, or do not.’” This is not an expression of indifference but a declaration that the truth of the Quran does not depend on their acceptance. Their belief benefits only themselves, and their disbelief harms only themselves. It also demonstrates the Prophet’s complete trust in Allah and his detachment from their rejection. Then the verse makes a powerful claim: “Indeed, when it is recited to those who were gifted with knowledge before it was revealed, they fall upon their faces in prostration.” The “gifted with knowledge” refers to the sincere scholars among the People of the Book—the Jews and Christians—who recognized the truth of the Quran. They had studied their own scriptures and found the descriptions of a final prophet, and when they heard the Quran, its truth resonated deeply with their prior knowledge. Their immediate reaction was not skepticism but prostration, an act of physical and spiritual submission. This scene is in stark contrast to the arrogance of the Quraysh, who turned their backs. It highlights that those who truly possess knowledge from previous revelations embrace the Quran when they encounter it. Historical examples include the Negus of Abyssinia, who wept when he heard verses about Jesus, and certain Jewish rabbis and Christian monks who converted upon hearing the Quran’s truth. This verse is a call to the disbelievers: if the learned among the People of the Book recognize this as truth, what stops you? It also serves as a permanent encouragement for dāʿīs; sincere seekers will recognise the truth, even if the masses reject it. The prostration mentioned here is so moving that it is one of the verses where a prostration of recitation (sajdah tilāwah) is performed, uniting the reciter with that humble reaction.

=108=

This verse continues the description of the reaction of the knowledgeable People of the Book upon hearing the Quran. After falling into prostration, they speak words of deep reverence and acknowledgment: “Glory be to our Lord!

” This is a declaration of Allah’s absolute perfection, a spontaneous outburst of praise that arises from witnessing the truth and beauty of the Quran. It is an expression of amazement that such a perfect revelation could descend, and an implicit confession that Allah is far above the false claims made by the polytheists. Then they say, “Surely the promise of our Lord has been fulfilled.” This refers to the promises found in their own scriptures—the Torah and the Gospel—regarding the advent of a final prophet and the sending of a final revelation. For years, they read those promises and waited. When they heard the Quran, they recognized it as the very fulfillment they had been awaiting.

This is a powerful testimony from the People of the Book themselves, used here to counter the Meccan pagans’ rejection. The Meccans had no scripture, but the Jews and Christians did, and their sincere ones confirmed the Quran.

The phrase “our Lord” shows that these were true believers in Allah who had not fallen into the polytheism of the Meccans or the trinitarian deviations. They now see the unity of revelation. For the Prophet, this was a divine reassurance: the truth of your message is testified not only by your immediate followers but by the learned across history.

For believers today, this verse enhances our appreciation of the Quran as the fulfillment of a grand divine plan. It also encourages interfaith outreach; many sincere People of the Book, when hearing the Quran without prejudice, find it resonates with their own scriptural heritage. The words “Glory be to our Lord” remind us that the proper response to the greatness of the Quran is to glorify Allah, and the recognition that His promises are always true strengthens our own faith in the promises yet to be fulfilled in the Hereafter.

=109=

The verse adds a profound physical and emotional dimension to the reaction of the knowledgeable ones. “And they fall down upon their faces weeping.” The prostration is not a mere ritual; it is accompanied by tears. The weeping is a sign of a deeply moved heart—tears of joy at finding the truth, tears of awe at the majesty of Allah’s words, tears of regret for any previous misguidance, and tears of gratitude for being guided. The “faces” emphasize that this is not a superficial gesture but a complete, face-on-the-ground submission, the posture of ultimate humility.

The verse then reveals the spiritual effect: “and it increases them in humility.” The word “khushūʿan” refers to a state of deep, heart-felt submissiveness, concentration, and quiet reverence. The more they engage with the Quran, the more their humility grows.

This is the hallmark of true knowledge; it does not produce arrogance but profound humility. Their prior knowledge of the scriptures had prepared the ground, and the Quran waters that ground, yielding a harvest of tearful, humble devotion. This stands in stark contrast to the Quraysh, who, when they heard the Quran, increased in defiance and aversion, as stated in verse 60. The same rain that nourishes a fertile garden hardens barren clay. The verse provides a beautiful standard for believers: when we recite or listen to the Quran, does it make us weep? Does it increase our inner humility, our khushūʿ in prayer and in life? If our engagement with the Quran is dry and merely intellectual, we are missing its transformative power. This weeping is not a sign of weakness but of a living heart.

The verse also serves as a rebuke to those who treat the Quran as a text to be critiqued rather than a message to be submitted to. The reaction of these pious predecessors sets the model for every Muslim: approach the Quran with a heart ready to be broken and reshaped, to weep out of love and awe, and to have one’s humility deepened with every verse.

=110=

This verse addresses two issues that arose in Mecca. First, the pagans criticized the Prophet when he supplicated using the name “Allah” and also when he used “al-Raḥmān” (the Most Compassionate). They were familiar with the name Allah for the Supreme Being but found “al-Raḥmān” strange, as it was not commonly used among them. Some even thought he was calling upon a different deity. The verse responds: “Call upon Allah or call upon the Most Compassionate—whichever you call, He has the Most Beautiful Names.” Both names refer to the same One God. All His names are beautiful and perfect, and you may invoke Him by any of them. This establishes the permissibility and indeed the excellence of calling upon Allah by His various names, each reflecting a different attribute of His perfection.

The second issue relates to the volume of recitation in prayer. The early Muslims were either praying too loudly, which provoked the disbelievers to insult and attack, or too silently, which prevented fellow believers from benefiting. The verse instructs: “Do not recite your prayers too loudly or silently, but seek a way between.

” This is a balanced, moderate approach. The prayer should be audible enough for oneself and those nearby to hear and benefit, but not so loud as to attract unwarranted hostility or show off. This middle way reflects the comprehensive moderation of Islam. It also teaches adaptability: in times of persecution, a lower voice is wise; in times of security, a louder recitation in the night prayer may be more beneficial. The command is not just about decibels but about intention and context. The verse connects the etiquette of supplication and prayer with the nature of Allah; because He is al-Raḥmān, the Most Compassionate, and possesses all beautiful names, we can approach Him with any of those names, and we should do so with the proper reverence and wisdom, neither ostentatiously loud nor neglectfully silent.

For the believer, it encourages using all of Allah’s beautiful names in duʿāʾ, and to maintain a balanced spiritual practice that considers both inner devotion and external circumstances.

=111=

The surah concludes with a magnificent doxology that encapsulates the core of tawḥīd and glorifies Allah in the most comprehensive terms. “And say, ‘All praise is for Allah.’” This declaration of universal praise acknowledges that every perfection and every blessing originates from Allah alone. The praise is absolute, not limited to any specific favour but encompassing all that He is and all that He does. Then the verse negates three fundamental falsehoods that had been addressed throughout the surah. First, “Who has never had any offspring.” This refutes the pagan claim that angels are Allah’s daughters, the Christian claim of Jesus as the son of God, and any notion that Allah has a child. Allah’s perfection precludes any need for offspring; He is the Eternal, the Self-Sufficient. Second, “nor does He have a partner in governing the kingdom. ” This refutes the polytheistic belief that other deities share in the dominion of the universe. The “kingdom” (mulk) is the entire creation, and its governance is solely in Allah’s hands. No saint, angel, or idol has any share in running the affairs of the heavens and the earth. Third, “nor is He pathetic, needing a protector. ” The Arabic “waliyyun mina al-dhull” literally means a protecting friend from lowliness or humiliation. Allah is not weak or in need of any ally to defend Him or raise Him from a state of lowliness. This refutes any notion that Allah requires helpers, which would imply a deficiency. He is al-ʿAzīz, the Almighty, and al-Qawī, the Strong.

The verse ends with the command: “And revere Him immensely.” The word “kabbirhu takbīran” means to declare His greatness in the most emphatic and repeated manner.

This is the takbīr: Allāhu Akbar. The surah that began with “Glory be to the One” (subḥān) ends with “revere Him immensely” (kabbirhu), creating a perfect envelope of glorification and magnification. It sums up the entire message: Allah is One, He has no partners or offspring, He is in no need, and all praise and greatness belong to Him. The believer is left with the heart’s compass firmly oriented towards the glorification and magnification of Allah in all aspects of life.
