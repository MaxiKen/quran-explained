=21=

The discourse now invites reflection on a visible reality: “See how We have favoured some over others in this life.” Human beings are not all equal in their worldly provisions. Some are wealthy, others poor; some are strong, others weak; some enjoy high status, while others struggle in obscurity. These disparities are part of Allah’s deliberate design, a test to see how the favoured will behave—with gratitude and generosity or with arrogance and stinginess—and how the less favoured will respond—with patience and trust or with envy and despair.

The word “favoured” translates “faḍḍalnā,” indicating that every gift a person possesses is a deliberate bestowal, not a random occurrence. By saying “See how,” the Quran invites us to observe, ponder, and learn from these differences rather than resent them. Then the verse immediately redirects the heart towards the ultimate truth: “but the Hereafter is certainly far greater in rank and in favour.

” This statement is profoundly comforting and reorienting. The differences of this world, however vast they may seem—a king and a beggar, a billionaire and a labourer—pale into insignificance when compared to the vastness of the Hereafter. In Paradise, the ranks will be determined not by wealth or lineage but by taqwa and righteous deeds. The “favour” of the Hereafter is not just quantitatively larger but qualitatively different: eternal, pure, and free from all the anxieties that accompany worldly gifts. This verse connects perfectly to the previous section where Allah spoke of providing both the desirer of this world and the desirer of the Hereafter. Now He clarifies that the real competition should not be over the temporary favours of this life, but over the eternal ranks of the next. It instills contentment: the poor believer is not deprived, for their rank in the Hereafter may far surpass that of the wealthy heedless person. And it warns the privileged that their worldly advantage is a fleeting test, while the rank that truly matters is yet to be determined. This perspective heals the heart from jealousy and fosters a healthy ambition for everlasting excellence.

=22=

This verse marks the opening of a majestic set of divine commandments that form the ethical core of this surah. The first and most fundamental command is: “Do not set up any other god with Allah.

” This is the bedrock of tawḥīd, the uncompromised oneness of the Creator in worship, love, fear, and reliance. The prohibition of shirk—associating partners with Allah—is the central message of every prophet. Here, it is presented as the first and greatest condition for a life of righteousness. The wording “any other god” includes idols, human beings, angels, desires, or any entity to which one attributes divine authority or unconditional devotion. The consequence of violating this command is devastating: “or you will end up condemned, abandoned.

” The word “condemned” means blameworthy, disgraced, deserving of rebuke. “Abandoned” suggests being left alone, cut off from divine help and mercy, without any supporter.

This is the state of the one who commits shirk in this life and the next: they lose the inner peace that comes from relying solely on the One true God, and in the Hereafter they will find no intercessor. The phrasing “end up” indicates the final outcome, a permanent condition. This verse is immediately addressed to the polytheists of Mecca, but its warning extends to all believers. Shirk is not only bowing to an idol; it can be the hidden shirk of showing off in worship, loving wealth more than Allah, or obeying created beings in defiance of the Creator. The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned his companions that what he feared most for them was the minor shirk, riya.

Thus, the commandment calls us to constant purification of our intentions. It is placed first among the commandments because if tawḥīd is sound, everything else can be corrected; if it is corrupted, all other deeds become worthless. The believer’s greatest security lies in ensuring that nothing, absolutely nothing, is set up as a rival to Allah.

=23=

The verse begins by reiterating the foundation: “For your Lord has decreed that you worship none but Him.” The word “decreed” (qaḍā) indicates a firm, binding command, an unalterable divine ruling. Worship is exclusive to Allah; no act of devotion, be it prayer, supplication, or sacrifice, may be directed to anyone else. Immediately and remarkably, this command is paired with: “And honour your parents. ” The conjunction is powerful; after the right of the Creator, the Quran elevates the right of parents, making kindness to them a core element of faith.

The word translated as “honour” is iḥsān, which means excellence, goodness, and the most beautiful conduct. It is far more than mere tolerance; it is proactive, loving, and generous treatment.

The verse then zooms into a particularly vulnerable stage of life: “If one or both of them reach old age in your care. ” The phrase “in your care” (ʿindaka) emphasizes that they are now dependent, perhaps frail or demanding. The command is exquisitely detailed: “never say to them even ‘ugh.’” This is the smallest expression of annoyance, a mere exhale of frustration, and it is forbidden. The instruction goes further: “nor yell at them.

” The Arabic “tanharhumā” conveys scolding, shouting, or rebuffing them harshly. Instead, the believer is commanded, “Rather, address them respectfully.

” The word “respectfully” translates “qawlan karīman,” a noble, generous, and dignified speech. This means choosing the best words, speaking gently, and honouring them in conversation. The shift from the majestic decree of worship to the intimate care of aging parents teaches that true piety is demonstrated in the closest relationships. It is easy to be religious outwardly but irritable at home. This verse installs a profound guard over the tongue, making the care of elderly parents a spiritual act of worship. The ageing parents, who once cared for us with patience, are now entitled to our patient, grateful service, and the smallest sign of irritation is a major spiritual offence.

=24=

Building on the previous command of respectful speech, this verse describes the inner posture and the ongoing spiritual duty towards parents. “And be humble with them out of mercy.

” The Arabic word for “humble” is “dhull,” which conveys a lowering of the wing of humility, a tender, voluntary submission. This humility is not due to weakness but springs “out of mercy” (raḥmah). The child is moved by compassion as they witness the vulnerability of those who were once their protectors. This beautiful phrasing shows that the ideal conduct toward parents is not a cold fulfilment of duty but a warm, merciful tenderness that mirrors the mercy they showed us when we were helpless. Then the verse instructs the believer to make duʿāʾ for them: “My Lord! Be merciful to them as they raised me when I was young. ” This prayer is a masterpiece of emotional depth. It acknowledges that our parents’ greatest gift was not just provision, but tarbiyah—raising, nurturing, and caring for us with mercy during our most dependent years. By saying “as they raised me when I was young,” the child remembers the countless sleepless nights, the sacrifices, the patience with childish mistakes. This remembrance fuels the prayer for Allah’s mercy upon them, especially if they have passed away, as a continuous charity. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that when a person dies, their deeds cease except for three, one of which is a righteous child who prays for them. This verse gives us the exact words of that life-giving prayer. It also teaches that even after a parent’s death, the relationship of birr (righteousness) continues. The believer never outgrows the need to pray for their parents.

The sequence is profound: first, the prohibition of any harsh word; then, the command of positive, noble speech; next, the inward state of merciful humility; and finally, the constant supplication. Together, they form a comprehensive code of parental care that elevates it to one of the highest stations of devotion after tawḥīd.

=25=

After the lofty commands concerning worship and parents, Allah addresses the human heart’s inner reality. “Your Lord knows best what is within yourselves.” This refers to the intentions, the hidden feelings, the occasional irritation that may arise despite one’s efforts, and the genuine struggles in fulfilling these commands perfectly. Perhaps a child feels a fleeting sense of annoyance with an elderly parent but suppresses it and continues to serve with kindness. Allah, being fully aware of inner states, does not hold a person accountable for involuntary emotions, but He knows the sincerity of the struggle. This verse brings immense comfort, as it acknowledges that human beings are not angels; they experience internal challenges while striving to do what is right. The condition of the heart is fully known to the Lord, so one should not despair over momentary weaknesses.

The verse continues: “If you are righteous, He is certainly All-Forgiving to those who constantly turn to Him. ” “Righteous” here implies having a general state of taqwa and goodness, striving to obey and avoid major sins. The emphasis, however, is on “those who constantly turn to Him”—the awwābīn. This word comes from a root meaning to return, and it describes people who, whenever they slip, immediately turn back to Allah in repentance. They do not persist in error. The promise for such people is that Allah is All-Forgiving (Ghafūr).

This is a vital balance: the law is strict, but the path to forgiveness is wide open for the penitent. The verse thus prevents two extremes: on one hand, using human weakness as an excuse to neglect duties, and on the other, despairing of Allah’s mercy because one cannot achieve perfection. The Lord who knows our inner selves is also the One who eagerly forgives those who constantly return to Him. This divine understanding should inspire us to keep striving, to renew our repentance, and to maintain a hopeful, realistic spirituality that acknowledges our fragility while trusting in Allah’s vast mercy.

=26=

This verse introduces the financial and social commandments that form the next segment of this moral code. “Give to close relatives their due” establishes the priority of family ties in charity and support. The “due” (ḥaqq) implies that relatives have a moral and legal right to assistance, maintaining kinship, and generous treatment. In many Islamic legal systems, this includes obligatory maintenance for needy relatives.

The verse then expands the circle: “as well as the poor and needy travellers.” The poor (miskīn) are those who have little or no wealth, while the needy traveller (ibn al-sabīl) is someone who is stranded away from home, even if they are wealthy in their homeland, because they lack access to their resources. This tripartite categorization shows that Islam’s vision of social justice is both familial and communal. It is not enough to pray and fast; one must actively circulate wealth to those who have a right to it, beginning with the nearest kin and radiating outward. Immediately, the Quran adds a crucial caution: “And do not spend wastefully.

” The word for wasteful is “tabdhīr,” which means scattering wealth in useless or sinful pursuits, even in extravagant charity that leaves one’s dependents unsupported. The believer is to give generously but wisely, not haphazardly. There is a beautiful balance here: you must give to relatives and the needy, but not in a manner that is itself destructive or reckless. The spending must be purposeful, meeting needs and earning Allah’s pleasure, not squandering resources on vain display or excess. This injunction corrects both the miser who refuses to give and the spendthrift who gives or spends without sense. The believer’s wealth is a trust from Allah, and using it according to His guidance means fulfilling the rights of others while preserving the remainder for one’s own lawful needs. The verse thus lays the foundation for a responsible, compassionate, and sustainable economic ethic.

=27=

This verse provides the spiritual diagnosis of wastefulness. “Surely the wasteful are like brothers to the devils.

” The phrase “brothers” indicates a deep affinity, a likeness in character and action. Those who squander wealth, whether in sin, extravagance, or reckless spending, are mirroring the behaviour of the devils, who themselves misuse the resources and abilities given to them.

This is a severe condemnation. Wastefulness is not just a minor financial mistake; it is a satanic trait. The connection is explained immediately: “And the Devil is ever ungrateful to his Lord.

” The Arabic word for ungrateful is “kafūr,” from the same root as kufr, meaning to cover up and deny blessings. Satan’s rebellion began with ingratitude and arrogance. He refused to acknowledge the wisdom of Allah in commanding him to prostrate to Adam. Similarly, a wasteful person displays ingratitude by not valuing the blessings of wealth appropriately, using them in ways that displease the Provider. Instead of channelling wealth into obedience and gratitude, the squanderer follows satanic impulses. The comparison also implies that wastefulness often opens the door to other evils, just as a person who keeps company with a devil will be led astray. The Prophet, peace be upon him, emphasized moderation and condemned extravagance even in permissible things, like overeating or excessive luxury. This verse shocks the conscience of a believer: every time one is tempted to waste money on useless items, ostentatious displays, or harmful entertainment, they should remember that such behaviour aligns them with the devils. It is a powerful motivator to cultivate gratitude, which naturally leads to using blessings with wisdom and restraint. Gratitude is the opposite of satanic ingratitude; the grateful servant sees every coin as a divine trust and spends it in a way that would please the Giver.

=28=

Real life often presents a situation where a believer genuinely wishes to help those in need but simply lacks the means. This verse provides the beautiful solution. “But if you must turn them down because you lack the means to give” acknowledges that financial inability is a reality.

The phrase “turn them down” (tuʿriḍanna) indicates a situation where you must avoid engaging in giving because you have nothing to offer. However, this turning away is paired with an internal state: “while hoping to receive your Lord’s bounty.” This means that even as you say no, your heart is full of hope that Allah will soon provide you with the means to be able to give. This hope transforms the act of refusal from an awkward dismissal into a spiritually engaged moment of longing for the chance to be generous. It also indicates that one should not be content with poverty as an excuse to permanently ignore the needy, but should actively look forward to the time when they can contribute. Then comes the divine instruction: “then at least give them a kind word.” The “kind word” translates “qawlan maysūran,” which literally means an easy, gentle word. This includes a polite apology, a warm prayer for their well-being, or words of encouragement that comfort their heart. In some interpretations, it also refers to promising to give later when Allah grants ease, coupled with a sincere intention.

The emphasis on “at least” shows that when material help is impossible, the spiritual and emotional support of a kind word is never out of reach. This prevents the needy from feeling humiliated or rejected, and it preserves the dignity of both parties. The verse beautifully teaches that the doors of charity are not closed by poverty; the believer always possesses the currency of good speech and sincere duʿāʾ. It instills a culture of emotional generosity that complements material generosity, ensuring that even a poor person can be a giver in their own way.

=29=

After forbidding wastefulness and encouraging measured generosity, this verse directly addresses the balanced approach to spending. “Do not be so tight-fisted” translates a powerful Arabic expression, “lā tajʿal yadaka maghlūlatan ilā ʿunuqik,” which literally means “Do not make your hand chained to your neck.” This vivid imagery depicts the extreme miser who cannot extend his hand to give, as if it were shackled. The consequence of such miserliness is that “you will be blameworthy. ” People will criticize the miser, and in the Hereafter, such stinginess brings severe rebuke. The miser not only harms the needy but also degrades his own soul, becoming a prisoner of his wealth. The opposite extreme is equally condemned: “nor so open-handed, for you will end up in poverty.

” The phrase “open-handed” here means extending the hand so completely that one gives away everything, leaving nothing for one’s own needs or dependents, thus becoming destitute. The word translated “end up in poverty” can also mean to sit down in regret and blame, having exhausted resources.

This is not a prohibition of generosity, but of imprudent spending that leads to one’s own ruin. The Prophet, peace be upon him, praised the one who gives charity while maintaining enough to avoid becoming needy themselves. The Quranic principle is the golden mean: a hand that is neither chained nor recklessly open, but freely gives in due proportion. This verse is a foundation of Islamic economics, promoting circulation of wealth without self-destruction. It acknowledges that a believer must also safeguard their own and their family’s well-being. This balanced approach reflects the beauty of a religion that does not demand monastic renunciation nor permit heartless hoarding. The believer’s hand is controlled by wisdom and taqwa, giving generously where required, withholding where necessary, and always aiming for the pleasure of Allah in both giving and retaining.

=30=

This verse provides the theological foundation for the balanced spending commanded in the previous verse. It explains that differences in wealth are not random but are directly from Allah: “Surely your Lord gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills.

” The Arabic words for “abundant” (yabsuṭ) and “limited” (yaqdir) convey expansion and restriction. The source of all provision is Allah alone, and He distributes it according to His infinite wisdom. Some people He tests with abundance, to see if they will be grateful and spend correctly. Others He tests with restriction, to see if they will be patient and avoid unlawful means or envy.

The verse adds, “He is certainly All-Aware, All-Seeing of His servants.” Allah’s knowledge encompasses every detail of our circumstances, our inner characters, our capacities, and our reactions. He knows who will thrive with wealth and who would be corrupted by it. He sees who struggles in poverty and remains faithful, and who would transgress if given more. This awareness is deeply comforting. When a believer faces financial difficulty, they can rest assured that the Lord who sees them and knows their situation has not made a mistake. The restriction is not a sign of abandonment but a tailor-made test from the All-Aware. Conversely, the one given abundance must not become arrogant, for it is a trust from the All-Seeing who will question how it was spent.

This verse also prohibits envy and dissatisfaction with one’s lot. Since the distribution is from a Lord who is All-Aware and All-Seeing, to resent His decree is to question His wisdom. The believer’s response to any financial state is contentment with Allah’s choice, coupled with effort within lawful means. It closes the small circle of financial ethics by rooting all spending and earning in the recognition of Allah’s sovereign control and perfect knowledge, leading to a heart at peace regardless of the balance in one’s account.

=31=

This verse confronts one of the most horrifying practices of pre-Islamic Arabia: the killing of children, particularly daughters, due to fear of poverty or shame. Allah commands, “Do not kill your children for fear of poverty.

” The phrase “for fear of poverty” translates “khashyata imlāq,” pinpointing the economic anxiety that drove some parents to bury their infant daughters alive. The verse first addresses the misguided logic by providing a divine guarantee: “We provide for them and for you. ” Notice that the provision for the children is mentioned first.

This is a profound rhetorical shift. The parent’s fear was that they could not afford to feed another mouth. Allah responds by saying, in essence, do not worry about providing for them out of your limited means, because We will provide for them—and for you as well. This establishes that the sustenance of every living being is solely in Allah’s hands, and the arrival of a child does not diminish the parent’s portion; rather, the child brings its own provision decreed by Allah.

The verse then delivers its judgment: “Surely killing them is a heinous sin.” The Arabic word “khiṭʾan kabīran” means a great sin, a tremendous crime. It is an act that violates the most sacred trust, the taking of an innocent life that Allah created. The verse transformed the Meccan society, which gradually abandoned this practice under the influence of the Quran and the Prophet’s teachings. For today’s world, the command extends beyond literal infanticide to all forms of ending a child’s life out of fear of financial or social hardship, including, in many scholars’ view, abortion motivated by economic anxiety. The principle is that economic hardship can never justify the taking of an innocent life. It also teaches reliance upon Allah in the matter of provision, encouraging parents to trust that when Allah grants a child, He also grants the means for that child’s sustenance. This verse instills a deep respect for life and a profound trust in the Provider.

=32=

This verse delivers the command regarding sexual morality. “Do not go near adultery” is a uniquely emphatic prohibition. It does not simply say “Do not commit adultery,” but warns against even approaching it. This means that any step, circumstance, glance, conversation, or relationship that could potentially lead to adultery is forbidden. The goal is to block all paths that might culminate in this sin. This preventive approach is one of the great wisdoms of Islamic law.

The verse then states: “It is truly a shameful deed.” The word “fāḥishah” denotes an act of extreme indecency, something that is manifestly vile and abhorrent to sound human nature. It corrupts individuals, destroys families, and spreads moral chaos in society. The addition of “and an evil way” emphasizes that adultery is not just an isolated act; it is a path, a lifestyle, a trajectory that leads to numerous other evils—lies, betrayal, broken trust, illegitimate children, and the breakdown of the marital bond which is the foundation of a stable community.

The word “way” (sabīl) suggests that once a person embarks on this path, it becomes increasingly difficult to retreat. The prohibition in this verse forms a cornerstone of Islamic sexual ethics, which channel human desires into the sacred bond of marriage. The wisdom behind prohibiting even the precursors to adultery is that human desire is powerful, and casual proximity between non-mahram men and women can easily ignite what the Prophet, peace be upon him, described as the “zaan” of the eyes, ears, and heart leading to the ultimate act.

Therefore, lowering the gaze, modest dress, and avoiding seclusion are all applications of “do not go near.” The verse is a call to protect the purity of the soul and the integrity of the family unit. It reminds believers that chastity is not just about refraining from the final act, but about guarding every interaction, maintaining clear boundaries, and cherishing the safety of distance from anything that could disgrace one in this life and the next.

=33=

This verse establishes the sanctity of human life, the most precious gift after faith. “Do not take a human life—made sacred by Allah—except with legal right.” The life of every human being is inviolable.

The phrase “made sacred by Allah” indicates that it is Allah who has declared life sacrosanct; it is not a human convention. The only exceptions—legal right—refer to specific situations prescribed by Islamic law, such as just retribution for murder, or punishment for certain severe crimes that threaten the social order, as determined by a legitimate legal authority.

This is not a license for vigilantism. Then the verse addresses the case of murder: “If anyone is killed unjustly, We have given their heirs the authority.” This means that the victim’s family, the walī al-damm, is granted the right to seek justice. They have three options under Islamic law: exact equal retaliation (qiṣāṣ), accept blood money (diyah), or forgive entirely. This empowerment of the heirs is a unique feature that combines justice with mercy.

However, the verse immediately issues a warning: “but do not let them exceed limits in retaliation.” The heirs must not go beyond the killer to harm his family or tribe, nor mutilate the body, nor kill more than one person for one life, as was the custom in pre-Islamic vendettas.

The phrase “exceed limits” curbs the human tendency toward uncontrolled vengeance. The verse closes with reassurance: “for they are already supported by law.” The heirs are not left alone; the law supports them in achieving justice, so they should trust the system rather than taking matters into their own hands destructively. This legal framework transforms retaliation from a cycle of tribal blood feuds into a regulated, merciful, and divinely sanctioned avenue for healing. It safeguards both the sanctity of life and the rights of victims, while placing strong boundaries against the escalation of violence.

=34=

The Quran now turns to the protection of the most vulnerable members of society. “Do not come near the wealth of the orphan” again uses the language of “do not come near,” indicating that one must maintain a safe distance from any misuse. An orphan, who has lost their father and protector, is extremely vulnerable to exploitation. The wealth they inherit is their only means of future security, and it is sacred. The guardian is commanded not to meddle with it.

However, the exception is given: “unless intending to enhance it.” This means the guardian may invest or manage the orphan’s property if it is clearly for the orphan’s benefit, such as through trade, development, or prudent safekeeping that yields increase. The intention must be purely to enhance and protect, not to consume. This stipulation is a mercy, allowing responsible stewardship while erecting a high wall against embezzlement. The time limit is “until they attain maturity,” meaning when the orphan reaches physical and mental maturity (rushd), at which point their wealth must be handed over to them fully.

The verse then shifts to another solemn matter: “And honour your pledges, for you will surely be accountable for them.” The word “pledges” translates “ʿahd,” which includes all covenants, promises, treaties, and oaths. Honouring them is a hallmark of a true believer. The warning “you will surely be accountable for them” reminds that every promise we make, whether to God or to people, will be questioned on the Day of Judgment. The connection between the two parts of the verse is subtle but profound: the guardian of the orphan is essentially fulfilling a sacred pledge of trust, and breaking that trust is both a financial crime and a breach of covenant. This verse therefore builds a society where the weak are protected and every word a person utters carries weight and moral responsibility, knowing that Allah will hold them accountable for their commitments.

=35=

This verse addresses commercial ethics with divine precision. “Give in full when you measure” refers to any transaction where goods are measured by volume, such as grains or liquids. The command is to give the full, exact measure, without shortchanging the buyer. “And weigh with an even balance” extends the same principle to items sold by weight.

The word “even” translates “mustaqīm,” meaning straight, just, and perfectly balanced. This is not merely a suggestion but a divine command that makes fair dealing a religious obligation. The early Meccan pagans, particularly those in trade, were notorious for manipulating scales, a practice condemned in many earlier surahs. Here, within this comprehensive set of commandments, fair measure is elevated to a core moral duty.

The verse then gives the rationale: “That is fairest and best in the end.” The word “fairest” (khayr) means it is good and virtuous in itself, reflecting honesty and integrity. But the phrase “in the end” (taʾwīlan) points to the ultimate consequence, both in worldly reputation and in the Hereafter. A business built on honesty may seem less profitable in the short term, but its long-term blessing and the eternal reward are immeasurable. Cheating may bring quick gains, but it destroys trust, invites societal decay, and earns Allah’s wrath. This verse, placed among commands about worship, parents, and killing, demonstrates the holistic nature of Islam, where the mundane act of weighing wheat is connected to the fear of God. It shows that religion is not restricted to the mosque but extends to the marketplace. The believer is called to be scrupulously just in every transaction, knowing that the balance they use will one day be mirrored in the scales of the Day of Judgment. This builds a community where mutual trust and fairness are the foundations of economic life.

=36=

This profound verse lays down a principle of epistemology and moral conduct: “Do not follow what you have no sure knowledge of.” The word “follow” (taqfu) means to pursue, trail after, or act upon. The prohibition covers every realm—speech, belief, accusation, and decision. One must not assert something, spread a rumour, or base a judgment on mere conjecture, hearsay, or unverified assumptions. “Sure knowledge” translates “ʿilm,” meaning verified, certain knowledge.

This command fights against one of the greatest social evils: speaking without evidence, which leads to slander, false beliefs, and broken relationships. The verse then emphasizes accountability: “Indeed, all will be called to account for their hearing, sight, and intellect.” The three faculties mentioned—hearing, sight, and the heart (intellect)—are the primary tools of perception and knowledge. Every sound we listen to, every sight we behold, and every thought or belief we form in our hearts will be scrutinized on the Day of Judgment. We are responsible for how we used these gifts. The ear that listened to gossip, the eye that lingered on forbidden scenes, the mind that entertained baseless suspicions—all will testify for or against their owner. This verse thus instills a deep sense of responsibility over one’s information intake and output. In the age of social media, where unverified news, rumours, and half-truths spread in seconds, this Quranic command is urgently relevant. A believer must verify before sharing, speak only with knowledge, and refrain from filling their hearing and sight with falsehood. The verse encourages intellectual humility: admitting that there are many things we do not know, and it is safer and more righteous to remain silent than to pretend certainty. It is a call to a life of truthfulness, where every word is weighed and every belief is grounded in genuine knowledge.

=37=

After addressing inner beliefs and interpersonal dealings, the Quran now corrects the external behaviour and posture. “And do not walk on the earth arrogantly.

” The word “arrogantly” translates “maraḥan,” which denotes a swaggering, boastful gait, the walk of someone who feels superior to others and to the very earth beneath their feet. This prohibition is not merely about the physical walk but the attitude of pride and self-exaltation it reflects. The manner of walking is an outward expression of an inner state; the believer’s walk should be humble, moderate, and dignified, as described elsewhere as the walk of the servants of the Most Merciful who walk on the earth with ease and humility.

The verse then provides a humbling logical argument: “Surely you can neither crack the earth nor stretch to the height of the mountains.” This is a direct rebuke to human arrogance. No matter how proudly a person walks, their feet will never split the ground open; they remain a tiny, fragile creature on a vast planet. And however tall they hold their head, they will never reach the mountain peaks, let alone surpass them. The physical insignificance of the human being compared to the earth and mountains is a powerful cure for pride. The one who walks with arrogance is living in an illusion of grandeur. This verse echoes the wisdom of Luqmān’s advice to his son, where he similarly warns against the proud gait. It reminds us that true greatness belongs only to Allah, the Creator of the earth and mountains.

For the believer, every step is an opportunity to practice humility, to remember that they are from dust and will return to dust, and that any status or achievement they have is a gift from Allah, not a reason to strut with self-importance. This simple yet profound instruction beautifies the character and harmonizes the individual’s relationship with the earth they tread upon.

=38=

After listing a series of commands beginning with the prohibition of shirk and encompassing parents, charity, spending, infanticide, adultery, murder, orphans’ wealth, fair trade, false knowledge, and arrogance, this verse delivers a sweeping judgment: “The violation of any of these commandments is detestable to your Lord.” The word translated as “violation” comes from “sayyiʾuhu,” meaning the evil or bad aspect of these matters—essentially, the transgression of the limits set. “Detestable” translates “makrūhan,” which means something deeply disliked, hateful, and odious in the sight of Allah. This means that every single one of these forbidden acts is not a light matter but is abhorrent to the Creator. The verse serves as a powerful summary, reinforcing that these are not merely suggestions or cultural norms, but divine boundaries whose violation provokes divine displeasure. There is a profound psychological effect in grouping them all under this conclusion: the believer cannot pick and choose. The one who worships Allah alone but mistreats parents has violated something detestable. The one who avoids adultery but cheats in business has done something makrūh in the sight of Allah. This holistic presentation binds moral, social, and spiritual obligations together.

The phrase “to your Lord” personalizes the matter; your own Lord, the One who nourishes and sustains you, finds these actions hateful. This should be enough to deter any heart that has love and fear of Allah.

The verse also implies that what is detestable to Allah should naturally become detestable to the believer, who strives to align his likes and dislikes with those of his Master. Thus, the verse cultivates a God-conscious moral sensibility where one’s conscience is trained to be repulsed by these evils, not just out of fear of punishment, but out of desire to be beloved to the Lord.

=39=

Having completed the list of core commandments, the Quran now affirms their source and status: “This is part of the wisdom which your Lord has revealed to you O Prophet.” The word “wisdom” (ḥikmah) elevates these instructions beyond mere rules; they represent divine wisdom that encompasses knowledge of human nature, societal harmony, and eternal salvation. The address to the Prophet, peace be upon him, assures him that these are not his own words but revealed wisdom from the Lord. The commandments thus carry the weight of revelation, and they form the essence of all prophetic teachings. Then the verse returns to the very first commandment, repeating the prohibition of shirk: “And do not set up any other god with Allah O humanity.” This repetition is deliberate and strategic. It serves as a bracket that encloses the entire set of commandments, reminding that the foundation and the capstone of all morality is tawḥīd. If one associates partners with Allah, then adherence to the other commands, while possibly beneficial in this world, will ultimately be nullified in the Hereafter without the essential ground of pure faith. The consequence of shirk is described vividly: “or you will be cast into Hell, blameworthy, rejected.” Being “cast” (mulqan) suggests a violent, humiliating tossing. “Blameworthy” means you will be deserving of all condemnation, with no excuse accepted. “Rejected” (madḥūran) means driven away from all good, utterly forsaken. This stark warning against shirk after detailing the moral code teaches that ethical living without correct belief is incomplete. It is a powerful reminder that the ultimate purpose of all these commandments is to serve and draw near to the One God alone. For the listener, it ties the entire passage together, ensuring that the magnificent ethical system is never detached from its theological core. The fear of Hell and the hope for Paradise remain the driving forces that make adherence to these commands a matter of life and death, spiritually.

The focus now shifts to refuting a specific pagan belief. The verse rhetorically asks, “Has your Lord favoured you pagans with sons and taken angels as His daughters?” This question is dripping with irony and condemnation. The Meccan polytheists considered themselves blessed with male offspring, whom they preferred, and yet they attributed daughters—the angels—to Allah. They held a contradictory and insulting belief: for themselves, they desired sons and despised daughters, yet for Allah, they assigned daughters, whom they themselves viewed as inferior.

The question “Has your Lord favoured you” exposes their arrogance; they imagined Allah gave them the “better” gender (sons) while taking the “lesser” (daughters) for Himself. In reality, Allah is far above having children of any kind, and the angels are honourable servants, not offspring.

The verse then delivers the verdict: “You are truly making an outrageous claim. ” The word “outrageous” translates “ʿaẓīman,” meaning a tremendous, monstrous, and terrible statement. This claim is outrageous in multiple ways: it insults Allah by attributing to Him the weakness of needing offspring; it degrades angels by calling them female children of God; it contradicts their own preferences; and it is a blatant lie without any evidence. This verse transitions the surah from the ethical commands to a broader critique of polytheistic beliefs, which will continue in the following verses about the glorification of Allah by all creation. It serves as a direct challenge to the Quraysh, dismantling one of their core falsehoods with simple logic.

For the believer, it reinforces the principle that any belief about Allah must be based on revelation and sound reason, not on cultural prejudice or self-serving myth. The outrageousness of such claims should make every Muslim vigilant in maintaining the purity of tawḥīd and in rejecting all forms of assigning partners or offspring to the Divine.
