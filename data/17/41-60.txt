=41=

The Quran here addresses a painful reality: “We have surely varied the signs in this Quran.” This means Allah has presented His revelations in diverse, repeated, and multifaceted ways—through stories, parables, warnings, promises, and logical proofs—all to appeal to human understanding and stir the conscience. The purpose is stated: “so perhaps they may be mindful.

” The Arabic “yadhdhakkarūn” implies a heartfelt remembrance and reflection that leads to change. Allah desires that people listen, ponder, and turn back to the truth.

However, the verse then describes the tragic opposite reaction: “but it only drives them farther away.” For those with diseased hearts, the very same signs that should guide them become a cause for increased aversion.

This is not a flaw in the Quran but in the disposition of the listener. When a person approaches the truth with arrogance, they perceive each new proof as a threat, and their flight from it intensifies. This verse captures the psychological law that truth does not force acceptance; it either softens or hardens the heart depending on one’s receptivity.

In the context of the Meccan pagans, they heard the Quran’s powerful arguments against idolatry and its demand for moral accountability, yet instead of submitting, they mocked and turned away, asking for different signs or accusing the Prophet of sorcery. This variation of signs was a great mercy, for it offered every type of mind a path to understanding, but stubborn rejection turned that mercy into a curse.

For the believer, it is a warning: continuous exposure to guidance without humble acceptance can lead to spiritual insensitivity. It also comforts the dāʿī that rejection is not due to a lack of clarity but to the condition of hearts, which only Allah can turn. The verse reflects a pattern seen throughout history: the more Pharaoh saw clear signs, the more tyrannical he became. So the Quran’s rich variety serves as a test; it brings the sincere closer and pushes the obstinate further away.

=42=

The Quran now presents a compelling logical argument against polytheism. The Prophet is instructed to say: “Had there been other gods besides Him—as they claim—then they would have certainly sought a way to challenge the Lord of the Throne.” The argument is simple yet devastating. If the universe had multiple deities, each with independent will and power, then conflict and competition would be inevitable. Each god would seek to assert dominance, to challenge the authority of the Lord of the Throne, and to seize control for itself. This would lead to cosmic chaos.

The phrase “Lord of the Throne” refers to Allah’s supreme sovereignty over all creation. The very stability, order, and harmony of the universe testify that there is one singular will governing it. The famous verse in Surah Al-Anbiyāʾ explains this further: “Had there been within the heavens and earth gods besides Allah, they both would have been ruined.” The argument here, however, highlights the natural tendency of a sovereign: any true god would not accept subordination; they would attempt to overpower the Supreme Ruler. Since we observe no such struggle and no rival challenger, it logically follows that no other gods exist. This argument was particularly striking to the Quraysh, who believed in a supreme God but also in lesser deities as intercessors.

The verse shows that if these lesser gods were real, they would not peacefully coexist; they would fight for ultimate authority. The use of “as they claim” reminds us that the whole structure of polytheism rests on baseless assumptions. The argument appeals to reason, which is a hallmark of the Quranic refutation of shirk.

For the believer, it strengthens tawḥīd by demonstrating that monotheism is not just a dogma but a rational necessity for the existence of an ordered universe, leaving no room for any partner with Allah.

=43=

This brief yet powerful verse serves as a divine declaration of transcendence. After refuting the logical possibility of other gods, Allah proclaims: “Glorified and Highly Exalted is He above what they claim!

” The word “Glorified” translates “subḥānahu,” meaning He is far removed from all imperfections, and “Highly Exalted” adds a dimension of supreme elevation in His essence, attributes, and authority. The phrase “above what they claim” refers to all the falsehoods the polytheists attribute to Allah—partners, children, daughters, limitations, or any likeness to creation. Whatever evil or deficiency they imagine, Allah is infinitely beyond it. This verse is a refrain that punctuates the Quranic discourse, calling the heart to pause and magnify the Creator. It reminds us that human conceptions, when not guided by revelation, inevitably fall short and often slander the Divine. The pagans claimed that angels were Allah’s daughters, that idols could intercede, that He had consorts. All these are projections of human ignorance onto the Divine Being. The verse cuts through these delusions with a clear, absolute negation. It also comforts the Prophet and the believers, who might feel disheartened by the outrageous claims of the disbelievers. Allah Himself takes charge of declaring His own purity, so they need not be overly anxious; the truth about God is secure. This glorification also serves as a model for the believer’s response to falsehood: instead of being consumed by anger, one should immediately declare the transcendence and perfection of Allah, thereby purifying their own heart from any stain of doubt. The verse, though short, is a complete act of worship, and the Prophet recommended reciting such glorifications frequently. It perfectly follows the logical argument, moving from rational demonstration to a direct, awe-filled affirmation of divine majesty.

=44=

This verse expands the glorification of Allah beyond the human realm to the entire cosmos. “The seven heavens, the earth, and all those in them glorify Him.” The “seven heavens” are a creation known fully only to Allah, layered in perfection. Everything within them and the earth, including angels, humans, jinn, animals, plants, and inanimate objects, are engaged in a continuous, real act of tasbīḥ—declaring Allah’s transcendence and purity.

The verse then emphasizes this universality: “There is not a single thing that does not glorify His praises.” The word “thing” includes the smallest particle to the largest galaxy. Every leaf’s rustle, every stone’s existence, every star’s orbit is a hymn of praise.

This is not metaphorical; it is a literal, though mostly incomprehensible, reality. The phrase “but you simply cannot comprehend their glorification” acknowledges human limitation. While we might not hear the pebbles or understand the birds’ specific songs of worship, they are nonetheless genuine expressions of praise. The Prophet, peace be upon him, heard the tasbīḥ of gravel in his hand and the trunk of a palm tree moaning. This reveals that creation is in constant worship, while many humans are heedless.

The verse then closes with two divine names: “He is indeed Most Forbearing, All-Forgiving.” This is remarkable. Despite the fact that the whole universe glorifies Him, humans—the ones equipped with intellect—often fail to do so, and many even insult Him with shirk.

Yet Allah is Most Forbearing, not rushing to punish, and All-Forgiving for those who repent. The connection is profound: your refusal to glorify Him does not diminish His majesty, for everything else does it, and He, in His forbearance, still offers you forgiveness. This inspires immense hope and shame, motivating the believer to join the cosmic chorus of worship willingly.

=45=

The verse addresses a specific protection granted to the Prophet. “When you O Prophet recite the Quran, We put a hidden barrier between you and those who do not believe in the Hereafter.” The “hidden barrier” translates “ḥijāban mastūran,” an invisible veil or partition that prevented the disbelievers from physically harming the Prophet or from truly understanding the Quran in a way that would make them believers, as a punishment for their arrogance.

This is a divine response to their rejection. When the Quraysh leaders persistently turned away and mocked, Allah cast a veil that separated them from the guidance. This barrier was spiritual and sometimes physical; it is reported that the leaders would pass by the Prophet while he recited and not see him, as if blinded. The purpose was to safeguard the Prophet from their plots and to demonstrate that guidance is ultimately Allah’s gift. The specification “those who do not believe in the Hereafter” is significant; denial of the afterlife is the root cause of their heedlessness. Without a sense of ultimate accountability, their hearts were locked against the Quran’s message.

This verse also explains a psychological and spiritual phenomenon: when a person persistently rejects truth, a veil gradually forms over their perception, making the truth seem alien and incomprehensible. For the believer, this is a warning to cherish and act upon the guidance while the heart is receptive, lest one’s continuous neglect leads to such a barrier. For the Prophet, it was a reassurance that the hostility of the disbelievers could not touch him except by Allah’s will, and that their failure to believe was not due to his deficiency but due to a divinely placed consequence of their own chosen blindness.

=46=

This verse explains the inner state of the disbelievers that necessitated the hidden barrier. “We have cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend it—and deafness in their ears.” The “veils” are coverings that prevent the heart from grasping the truth. The heart, which is the seat of understanding, is shrouded so that the Quran’s wisdom cannot penetrate. Similarly, “deafness in their ears” means they cannot truly hear the message with a receptive, believing hearing, even though they physically hear the words.

This is a divine punishment that matches their attitude. Because they persistently turned away, mocked, and refused to listen with sincerity, Allah sealed their faculties. The verse does not describe an arbitrary cruelty but the natural consequence of their free choices.

The phrase “when you mention your Lord alone” pinpoints their specific allergy: tawḥīd. As long as the Prophet spoke of general morality or even acknowledged their gods, they might tolerate it, but the moment he declared the exclusive oneness of Allah without partners, “they turn their backs in aversion.” This aversion was so intense that they physically spun around and walked away, unable to bear the message that stripped their idols of divinity. This describes the Meccan elite’s reaction vividly. For the contemporary reader, it serves as a mirror: how does one react when pure tawḥīd is emphasized? Is there a desire to run away, to water it down, or to embrace it fully?

The verse also underscores that the Quran’s core message is the absolute oneness of Allah, and any message that compromises this is not the Quran. It reminds believers that the rejection they face may be due to the message’s power, not its weakness, and that the hearts of the rejecters are veiled by their own doing, leaving the matter to Allah.

=47=

This verse offers comfort to the Prophet by revealing Allah’s encompassing knowledge. “We know best how they listen to your recitation.” Allah is fully aware of the exact manner in which the disbelievers listen—their secret motives, their whispered conversations, their pretense of attentiveness while plotting mockery. Nothing escapes His knowledge.

This is a great consolation for the Prophet: he is not unseen, his effort is noted, and the enemy’s schemes are fully exposed. Then the verse reveals their private words: “when the wrongdoers say, ‘You would only be following a bewitched man.’” They would tell one another and potential followers that the Prophet was not divinely inspired but rather afflicted by magic, and thus anyone who followed him was following a man whose mind was clouded. This was a deliberate propaganda tactic to isolate the Prophet and discourage converts.

The term “bewitched” (masḥūr) implies that his speech was not his own but the product of a spell, a clever reframe of the Quran’s eloquence as sorcery. The Quran exposes this lie, but does so in a way that shows Allah hears even their most private councils.

For the believer, this verse is a reminder that no conspiracy against the truth is hidden from Allah. When people today mock religious commitment or label devout individuals as backward or deceived, Allah is aware.

The verse also subtly instructs the Prophet and his followers not to be consumed by worry over what is said in secret; the All-Knowing is sufficient as a witness and judge. This knowledge is also a threat to the wrongdoers; their sneers are recorded, and they will be held accountable for every word uttered in the darkness of their private gatherings. It fosters a deep sense of divine surveillance that deters evil and reassures the righteous.

=48=

After revealing their private talk, the Quran now addresses the Prophet directly with a rhetorical exclamation: “See how they call you names O Prophet!” This refers to the various insulting labels the Quraysh attached to the Prophet, peace be upon him. They called him a sorcerer, a soothsayer, a madman, a poet, and a liar. Each label was an attempt to discredit his message and discourage people from listening. The verse says “See how,” as if to express astonishment at the absurdity and creativity of their slander.

This is not a command to dwell on insults but to observe the lengths to which people will go to reject the truth. Then the verse draws the logical conclusion: “So they have gone so far astray that they cannot find the Right Way.” The very act of calling the Prophet such contradictory names demonstrates their confusion and misguidance. They are so lost that they cannot even agree on a single consistent lie. More profoundly, their hearts are so far from the Straight Path that they cannot recognize the man of truth standing before them, the very one they formerly called al-Amīn, the trustworthy. This verse is a powerful lesson in dealing with personal attacks. It shows that insults against a caller to truth are a sign not of the caller’s deficiency but of the attacker’s deep misguidance. The believer should not be shaken by name-calling; instead, they should pity those who are so lost they cannot see the Way even when it is clear.

The verse also contains a hidden comfort: the one who is insulted for Allah’s sake is in good company, for all prophets were called names. The key is to remain steadfast, knowing that true guidance is with Allah, and those who resort to mockery have already shut the door to it themselves.

=49=

This verse introduces one of the central objections of the Meccan disbelievers: the denial of bodily resurrection. “And they say mockingly” sets the tone; their question is not a sincere inquiry but a sarcastic dismissal.

The phrase “when we are reduced to bones and ashes” reflects their materialist outlook. They saw death as the final disintegration of the body. Bones may last a while, but eventually turn to dust; ashes refer to cremation or complete decay. From their perspective, a reconstitution of such scattered, transformed elements was scientifically impossible.

The question “will we really be raised as a new creation?” drips with incredulity. The word “really” underscores their absolute disbelief. This objection was frequently raised against the Prophet. They could not conceive of a power that could reverse decay. The Quran will answer this in the following verses, but here it just presents the objection, letting its weakness echo. The mockery reveals their arrogance and lack of imagination about divine capability. It also shows that their denial of the Hereafter was not based on evidence but on a feeling that it was too extraordinary.

For the believer, this verse highlights a timeless struggle: faith in the unseen versus the materialist worldview that insists only what is observable is real. The argument from “bones and ashes” is still used today in different language by those who deny afterlife. The Quran prepares to answer this by pointing to the original creation. If Allah created them from nothing the first time, re-creation is easier. The mockery also teaches that falsehood often laughs at truth, but that laughter will turn to horror.

The verse reminds us to hold firmly to the resurrection, even when it seems impossible to the disbelieving mind, because the One who does it is the All-Powerful Creator.

=50=

The response to the mockery begins with a divine command: “Say, O Prophet.” This instructs the Prophet to answer confidently, not with hesitation.

The answer is a direct and startling affirmation of resurrection, but it takes the argument to an even higher level. The verse begins “Yes,” confirming that resurrection will indeed happen. Then it adds an extreme hypothetical: “even if you become stones, or iron.” The pagans thought bones and ashes were too decayed to be revived. Allah responds by saying that even if their bodies turned into materials much harder and more resistant to change—stones, like rocks, or iron, a solid metal—He would still be able to resurrect them.

This is a powerful rhetorical escalation. If Allah can bring life from hard, lifeless substances, then bringing life from decomposed bones is not difficult at all. Stones and iron are further from life than bones. The argument shifts the focus from the state of the remains to the absolute power of the Creator. It also serves as a subtle proof: stones and iron are inorganic, yet if Allah willed, He could give them life, just as He first created life from clay.

The verse also connects to the following one, building a cumulative case. The pagans’ objection was based on material transformation; the Quran demolishes it by saying no matter how transformed, hard, or scattered, the original Creator can reassemble it.

This is a logical and theological response that removes every excuse. For the believer, this verse expands their view of Allah’s capability—nothing is beyond His power. It reassures the heart that the promise of resurrection is not dependent on the current state of bodies; Allah can recreate from anything. This should inspire a deep trust in the Lord who can bring the dead stones of the earth to life and will certainly bring the dead humans back for judgment.

=51=

This verse continues and concludes the powerful argument. It begins with “or whatever you think is harder to bring to life!

” This is a comprehensive challenge. The pagans are invited to imagine the most impossible, the most dead, the most resistant substance they can conceive of—and Allah can still resurrect it. This shifts the argument from the physical to the conceptual: there is no material condition that lies outside Allah’s power. Then the verse anticipates their next question: “Then they will ask you, ‘Who will bring us back to life?’” The question is either incredulous or a last-ditch attempt to stump the Prophet.

The answer is immediate and conclusive: “Say, ‘The One Who created you the first time.’” This is the Quran’s core rational proof for resurrection. The original creation—bringing living, conscious human beings from non-existence—is the greater feat. Re-creation is logically easier for any rational mind. The One who did the first can certainly do it again. The pagans had no answer to this, so they resorted to ridicule: “They will then shake their heads at you and ask, ‘When will that be?’” Shaking heads is a gesture of amazement and mocking denial. They change the subject from ‘how’ to ‘when,’ hoping to escape the logical force. The response is divinely crafted: “Say, ‘Perhaps it is soon!’” The timing is not disclosed, but the possibility that it is near is enough to create urgency. This prevents procrastination.

The use of “perhaps” is for human understanding, not uncertainty on Allah’s part. For the believer, this verse provides a complete method of dawah: answer doubts with reason, refer to original creation, and instil urgency without claiming knowledge of the exact time. It also prepares the next verse about the Day of Resurrection itself, which will feel incredibly near when it comes.

=52=

This verse paints a vivid scene of the Resurrection. “On the Day He will call you” refers to the call of Isrāfīl blowing the Trumpet or Allah’s direct command for the dead to rise. The response is immediate and involuntary: “you will instantly respond by praising Him.

” The Arabic “bi-ḥamdihi” indicates that upon resurrection, all human beings, the righteous and the wicked, will instinctively praise Allah, acknowledging His greatness. No one will be able to deny or delay. The rebellion, arrogance, and argumentation will vanish; everyone will become fully aware of their absolute dependence on the Creator. Then the verse adds a profound psychological detail: “and you will think you had remained in the world only for a little while.” The entire earthly life, with all its decades, struggles, pleasures, and pains, will seem like a fleeting moment—a day or part of a day.

This is a common Quranic theme. The immense reality of the Hereafter shrinks the perceived duration of the dunya to almost nothing. This feeling of brevity will be a source of immense regret for the disbelievers, who wasted that short opportunity, and a source of comfort for the believers, who endured patiently for a moment that has now ended. The verse serves as a powerful reminder to not be deceived by the length of worldly life. When death comes, or on the Day of Judgment, a full lifespan will feel like a brief nap. This perspective is meant to energise the believer for obedience and to make the sacrifices of this world bearable. It also answers the mockery of the pagans who thought resurrection was too far-fetched and far-off. In reality, the waiting period between death and resurrection will feel like nothing. This verse thus ties together the argument for resurrection with its emotional and spiritual impact, urging immediate preparation.

=53=

This verse provides a vital code of conduct for the Muslim community, especially in the face of provocation. “Tell My believing servants to say only what is best.” The Prophet is instructed to command the believers that their speech, especially in dialogue with opponents and even among themselves, should be the best possible.

The word “best” (aḥsan) means the most excellent, most courteous, most truthful, and most effective. In an environment where they were constantly insulted, accused of following a madman, and mocked, the natural human reaction would be to respond with harsh words. But Allah orders restraint and the highest standard of speech.

This is because the enemy’s goal is exactly the opposite: “Satan certainly seeks to sow discord among them.” An angry, insulting reply would ignite further conflict, divide the community, and give Satan exactly what he wants—fitna. The believers are warned of the enemy’s tactics; Satan waits for moments of anger to exploit.

The verse concludes with a fundamental truth: “Satan is indeed a sworn enemy to humankind.” This statement is a call to never forget the underlying reality: our battle is not just against human opponents but against a spiritual enemy who uses words and emotions as weapons.

Therefore, the believer must always choose the speech that builds, reconciles, and invites to truth, not the speech that destroys. This command applied first to the Muslims’ interactions with the Quraysh, but it is a universal principle for all daʿwah and internal disputes. The best word may be a gentle explanation, a firm but polite refusal, or even silence when speech would cause harm. This verse is the foundation of Islamic conversational ethics. It requires emotional intelligence and self-control, recognizing that every word spoken is either a blow against Satan or a tool for him.

The phrase “My servants” adds a sense of honour and belonging, motivating believers to represent their Lord in the best manner.

=54=

This verse addresses both the Prophet and, through him, the believers, while also speaking to the disbelievers. “Your Lord knows you best” is an all-encompassing statement. Allah’s knowledge encompasses every person’s inner state, intentions, and ultimate destiny. For the Prophet, it is a comfort: the constant rejection is fully known to Allah, and He is the One who judges.

For the disbelievers, it is a warning: their secret plots and hidden disbelief are not hidden from the Lord. Then the verse says, “He may have mercy on you if He wills, or punish you if He wills.” This underscores that mercy and punishment are entirely in Allah’s hands and are based on His infinite wisdom. No one can demand or predict them. This should instil fear and hope simultaneously. The disbelievers should not be complacent that their current freedom means Allah’s approval; He may delay punishment, but it will come if they persist. The believers should not despair of Allah’s mercy, nor should they feel self-righteous, for it is Allah who accepts repentance. Then the verse directly addresses the Prophet: “We have not sent you O Prophet as a keeper over them. ” The Prophet’s duty is only to convey the message clearly; he is not responsible for forcing people to believe, nor is he a guardian who will be held accountable for their rejection.

This is a significant relief for the Prophet, who was deeply grieved by their disbelief. It also establishes a fundamental principle: no human being can control the faith of another. This frees the dāʿī from the burden of results. They must deliver the message in the best way, and the outcome is with Allah. For the community, it prevents the compulsion of others in religion and ensures that the prophetic mission remains one of invitation, not enforcement. The verse beautifully balances divine sovereignty and human free will.

=55=

This verse expands the scope of divine knowledge and prophetic favour. “Your Lord knows best all those in the heavens and the earth.” This reassures the Prophet and the believers that Allah’s knowledge is absolute. He knows every angel in the heavens and every human and creature on earth. He knows their ranks, their sincerity, and their struggles.

In the context of the Meccan rejection, this is a reminder that Allah is fully aware of the believers’ small number and weakness compared to the powerful disbelievers, and He will deal with each justly. Then the verse states: “And We have surely favoured some prophets above others.

” This is an important theological point. While all prophets are maʿṣūm (infallible) and worthy of honour, they are not all equal in rank. The Quran mentions that some were given particular gifts: Abraham was a friend of Allah, Moses was spoken to directly, and Muḥammad, peace be upon him, received the Quran and the Night Journey. This hierarchy is by divine choice and wisdom. It also serves as a retort to the Jews and Christians who claimed exclusive virtue for their prophets while rejecting others. The verse specifically adds, “and to David We gave the Psalms.” David is singled out here for mention of the Zabūr, a book of wisdom and praise. His mention, along with the reference to the Psalms, connects to the broader theme of scripture and guidance. It shows that divine favour includes the giving of revealed books, and that the Quran is part of a chain. For the Prophet, this mention of David also foreshadows the argument against shirk in the next verse; even a great prophet-king like David worshipped Allah alone. The verse instructs the believer to respect all prophets while understanding the special status of the final Prophet. It grounds the concept of prophetic hierarchy in Allah’s perfect knowledge, preventing human argument about ranks based on nationalism or prejudice.

=56=

The surah returns to the refutation of shirk with a direct challenge. The Prophet is commanded to tell the polytheists: “Invoke those you claim to be divine besides Him.

” This is a daring call for them to test their false gods. The challenge is to call upon those idols, angels, or saints they worship in times of distress, and see if they can respond. The verse immediately exposes their powerlessness: “they do not have the power to undo harm from you or transfer it to someone else.” This covers two fundamental human needs: the removal of existing affliction and the prevention of future harm. A real god would have the power to lift a calamity—sickness, poverty, grief—or at least shift it to another. But these false deities can do neither.

The phrase “transfer it” means to move a harm from you onto another, which a true sovereign could do. Their utter impotence is laid bare. This argument was extremely powerful in a society that turned to idols for relief. The verse invites rational introspection: if these beings cannot avert even a minor worldly harm, how can they be worshipped as providers of ultimate benefit or intercessors before Allah? This challenge also implicitly highlights Allah’s power, for He alone removes harm and controls destiny. The verse is an application of the earlier logic about other gods seeking a way to the Throne; here, the test is practical, not theoretical.

For the believer, this reinforces the complete reliance on Allah in every difficulty. Calling upon dead saints, idols, or any created being is futile and illogical.

This verse also serves as a timeless response to all forms of intercessory worship, reminding that only the Creator can remove harm and that true tawḥīd demands directing all supplication to Him alone.

=57=

This verse further dismantles the polytheists’ justification for idol worship. They used to claim that they worshipped angels, Jesus, or saintly figures only to bring them closer to Allah. The Quran responds: “Even the closest to Allah of those invoked would be seeking a way to their Lord.” The beings they call upon—like the angels or prophets—are themselves engaged in a continuous effort to get nearer to Allah, hoping for His mercy and living in fear of His punishment.

The phrase “the closest” emphasizes that if the most honoured, purest creatures are in a state of humble servitude and need, then what possible power can they exert for others? They themselves seek a “wasīlah,” a means of approach, through obedience and worship. They hope for Allah’s mercy, because even the righteous are not self-sufficient; they dread His punishment, because no creature is secure from it except by divine grace. This demolishes the idea of independent intercessors.

The verse then ends with a warning: “Indeed, your Lord’s torment is fearsome.” This refers to the punishment that the disbelievers, and even the worshipped ones fear. It is a threat to the idolaters that the very torment their false gods fear will befall them if they persist in shirk.

The verse also implicitly includes a beautiful comfort: the beings they worship are themselves devoted servants, so why not worship the Master directly instead of the servants? For the believer, this verse instills a profound sense of humility. If the closest beings to Allah are in constant hope and fear, what should be the state of an ordinary believer? It encourages a life of balanced worship between love, hope, and fear, and utterly rejects any form of saint worship or excessive veneration that shades into shirk. It is a call to direct, unmediated devotion to the One whom even the greatest creatures worship.

=58=

This verse establishes a divine law concerning societies that persist in wickedness. “There is not a wicked society that We will not destroy or punish with a severe torment before the Day of Judgment.

” The word for “society” is “qaryah,” meaning a township or a civilization. The condition is that they are “wicked,” meaning oppressive, unjust, and entrenched in disbelief and moral decay. The decree is that every such society will meet either complete destruction or a severe torment of some kind before the final Hour. This could be a natural disaster, plague, war, or subjugation. This divine law gives hope to the oppressed and warning to the oppressor. It means no unjust power will last forever; divine justice will inevitably intervene in this world to some degree, even if the ultimate recompense is in the Hereafter.

The verse then adds: “That is written in the Record.” This means it is a fixed decree in the Preserved Tablet (al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ), an unalterable part of Allah’s plan. This recording emphasizes certainty and preordination. For the Meccan persecutors, this verse was a direct threat: their oppression of the believers would not go unpunished, and their society’s fate was already sealed unless they repented. For the believers, it was a powerful reassurance that their suffering was temporary and that justice was guaranteed. It also teaches that the laws of history are not random; there is a moral order. When a society crosses the line, punishment becomes unavoidable. This verse calls believers to hold on to their faith even when evil seems dominant, and it serves as a sobering reminder to nations today that corruption, tyranny, and collective sin set them on a course to divine retribution, which no human power can avert once the decree unfolds.

=59=

The pagans often demanded miraculous signs from the Prophet, like turning the Safa mountain into gold or bringing Allah and the angels face to face. This verse answers why such demands were not met. “Nothing keeps Us from sending the demanded signs except that they had already been denied by earlier peoples.” The principle is that when a people stubbornly demand a specific tangible miracle as a condition for belief, and they are given it, if they then reject it, they are immediately destroyed.

The earlier nations, like Thamūd, were given exactly what they asked for—a living she-camel emerging from a rock—yet they still disbelieved and hamstrung it, leading to their annihilation. In His mercy, Allah withholds such signs from the Meccans because their pattern of disbelief would only lead to their immediate destruction, and Allah willed to keep the door of repentance open longer, especially for later generations. The example of Thamūd is cited: “We gave Thamūd the she-camel as a clear sign, but they wrongfully rejected it.” The sign was clear and undeniable, a test of their obedience. They failed, and were crushed. Then the verse clarifies the nature of signs: “We only send the signs as a warning.” Their purpose is to warn and evoke fear of punishment, not to compel belief or entertain curiosity. The Quranic signs themselves are sufficient for those with sound hearts.

This verse teaches the wisdom behind not granting every demand of skeptics. Today, people ask for physical miracles, but the pattern of history shows they rarely cause lasting faith. The Quran thus directs attention to the intellectual and spiritual signs already present in the universe and in the revelation.

For the believer, it cultivates contentment with the signs of the Quran, avoiding the trap of demanding that Allah conform to human tests. It also reveals a profound mercy: the withholding of spectacular signs was to spare the Meccans from the sure destruction that follows their rejection, allowing a window for some to believe.

=60=

This verse directly links to the Night Journey and the Meccan reaction to it. “And remember, O Prophet, when We told you, ‘Certainly your Lord encompasses the people.’” This is a message of security; Allah surrounds and controls all of humanity, so the Prophet is completely safe under His protection, and the disbelievers are entirely within His grasp. Then the verse speaks of two specific tests: “And We have made what We brought you to see as well as the cursed tree mentioned in the Quran only as a test for the people.” “What We brought you to see” refers to the great visions during the Night Journey and Ascension (al-Isrāʾ wa al-Miʿrāj). The Prophet was shown wondrous signs that night. “The cursed tree” is the tree of Zaqqūm, which grows in Hell, mentioned elsewhere in the Quran. When the Prophet reported these unseen realities, the Meccans ridiculed him. For instance, they found the idea of a tree growing in fire absurd, and they mocked the Night Journey. Allah explains that these revelations are deliberately a test to distinguish the believers from the disbelievers. The believers accept them based on the Prophet’s truthfulness, while the disbelievers reject them, thereby sealing their fate.

The verse concludes: “We keep warning them, but it only increases them greatly in defiance.” Despite repeated warnings, their transgression and stubbornness only grow.

This is the same pattern noted in verse 41. The Night Journey was a test of faith, and many faltered, some even apostatizing, while Abu Bakr earned the title al-Ṣiddīq for his immediate belief.

For the believer, this verse highlights the reality that some revealed truths, especially concerning the unseen, are meant to test the purity of our faith. Will we trust the truthful Prophet, or will we impose our limited reason on the infinite capacities of Allah? It also comforts those who convey truth that increased defiance is not a sign of failure but a manifestation of the test described by Allah Himself.
