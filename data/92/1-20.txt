=1=

The surah opens with a solemn oath that immediately draws the heart into the rhythm of creation: “By the night when it covers.” The night is described in its act of covering. When darkness descends, it wraps the world in a blanket of stillness, hiding the vivid details of the landscape, muffling activity, and calling all living things to rest. This covering is a divine mercy, not a punishment. It is a sign of the Creator’s wisdom that human beings are granted a natural curtain behind which they can retreat from the labour of the day.

Yet the word “covers” also evokes a deeper spiritual reality. The night conceals; it can hide deeds done in secret, both righteous charity and sinful mischief. The oath thus reminds us that the alternation of night and day is not accidental—it is a deliberate design that brings life into balance and tests human behaviour in the seen and the unseen. By swearing by the night in its covering state, Allah draws attention to the contrast that will follow. The night’s darkness prepares for the day’s shining; the hidden prepares for the revealed. The oath also resonates with the surah’s theme of diverse human strivings. Just as the night covers and the day exposes, so will every soul’s inner motivations be uncovered on the Day of Judgment.

In the context of the revelation in Mecca, the believers were suffering under a metaphorical night of persecution. The oath assured them that this night of oppression would not last forever; a day of justice would dawn. The Prophet, peace be upon him, often pondered these natural signs that called humanity to reflect. So the surah begins not with a command but with an image that speaks to the deepest human experience: we all know the night, and we all await the morning, pointing to a cosmic order governed by One who alternates the shades and the light with perfect precision.

=2=

The second oath completes the pair: “and the day when it shines!” The word “shines” translates a verb that conveys full, brilliant illumination, the time when the sun is high and the world is bathed in light, making everything clearly visible. This stands in vivid contrast to the covering night. The day does not merely exist; it actively shines, dispelling darkness and providing the energy for life to stir, for work to be done, and for sights to be seen. On a spiritual level, the day’s shining symbolises revelation, guidance, and the uncovering of truth. The Qur’an itself is described as a light, and the Prophet as a lamp spreading illumination. By swearing by the day in its shining, Allah draws our focus to the blessing of clarity. Human beings are not left in a perpetual night of ignorance; the day of divine guidance has risen, and those who open their eyes can see the path.

The pairing of night and day in the oath introduces the foundational idea of duality that runs through the surah: darkness and light, concealment and exposure, male and female, ease and hardship, charity and stinginess, faith and denial. Each pair is not an accident but a sign pointing to the Creator who established these contrasting realities. The day’s shining also highlights responsibility. Actions performed in the daylight are witnessed; they are public and recorded. So the believer is reminded that just as the day reveals the face of the earth, a coming Day will reveal all deeds. The oath connects seamlessly to the next, moving from the cosmic to the human, because the same God who brings the day to shine after the night’s covering is the One who created the fundamental human distinction of male and female, establishing order in human life just as He established it in the cosmos.

=3=

The third oath rises from the creation of time to the creation of humankind: “And by the One Who created male and female!” Here Allah swears by Himself, the Creator of the genders. This shift from swearing by created things to swearing by the Creator of such a profound pair signals the utmost solemnity. The creation of male and female is not a random evolutionary outcome; it is a deliberate act of a wise Maker. The two sexes are complementary in anatomy, psychology, and role, and through their union, human life continues and families are formed. The oath highlights that the diversity in humanity—the simple, universal fact that every person is either male or female—points directly to a single Originator. Each gender has unique strengths, and together they complete the human tapestry.

In the context of Meccan society, which often devalued females to the point of burying infant girls alive, this oath was also a powerful corrective, implicitly honouring both genders as deliberate creations of Allah. Moreover, the pairing of male and female foreshadows the surah’s central message about the diversity of human striving. Just as the night and day are opposites that serve a unified purpose, so are male and female, and so are the divergent paths that humans take—some towards ease, others towards hardship. The oath by the One who created male and female directly connects to the next verse, which declares the diversity of human efforts. The implied logic is this: the God who could create such a foundational distinction in human nature is certainly able to distinguish between the outcomes of different moral choices, and He will reward or punish accordingly. The oath also reminds every soul that its identity as male or female is a gift and a test from the One who fashioned it.

=4=

With the oaths complete, the surah now delivers its central thesis: “Surely the ends you strive for are diverse.” The word “ends” refers to the goals, aims, and ultimate destinations that people work towards through their efforts and choices. The original language stresses that human strivings are scattered and varied—pointing in completely different directions. Every day, every human being exerts themselves, spending their energy, time, and resources, but the purpose behind that exertion differs radically from person to person. Some strive for worldly wealth, power, and pleasure; others strive for the pleasure of Allah and the peace of the Hereafter. Some toil in service of their ego, while others toil in service of the oppressed. The verse is a statement of fact, but it is also a warning and an invitation. It warns that not all striving leads to the same outcome; the destination is defined by the intention and the path taken. It invites the listener to pause and examine: what exactly am I striving for? Where is my daily effort leading me? The diversity of ends is rooted in the free will and the moral inspiration that Allah placed in every soul, as detailed in the previous surah. The night and day oaths now find their moral parallel: just as the night covers and the day shines, human lives are either heading towards a covering darkness of loss or towards the bright shining of eternal success. The verse serves as a hinge, moving from the cosmic signs to the moral classification of humanity that immediately follows. It also comforts the Prophet and the believers who were striving in the path of Allah while the disbelievers were striving to oppose him. The apparent success of the worldly strivers does not mean their end is good; it only means their striving is of a different, ultimately ruinous kind.

Thus the verse calls everyone to align their struggle with the destination that truly matters.

=5=

Having established that human strivings lead to diverse ends, the surah now begins to describe the first of two fundamental types of people. “As for the one who is charitable, mindful of Allah.

” The word “charitable” translates a verb meaning to give freely from one’s wealth to those in need. This is the outward action. But charity alone is not sufficient if it is not rooted in a proper inward state, so the verse immediately adds “mindful of Allah,” which translates the quality of taqwa—God-consciousness, a reverent fear that leads one to obey Allah and avoid His displeasure.

The pairing is crucial: the charitable person is not merely a philanthropist seeking praise; they give because they are mindful of their Lord, knowing that wealth is a trust and that the needy have a right in it. This verse and the next two paint a portrait of the believer whose striving is directed toward the finest reward.

The description also consciously echoes and contrasts with the behaviour of the boasting disbeliever in Surah Al-Balad who “wasted enormous wealth” in enmity. Here, the wealth is spent in charity, with a heart filled with mindful awareness of Allah. The verse thus establishes that striving for the right end must combine outward generosity with inward piety. The historical context of Mecca saw many examples of both types. The wealthy Quraysh chiefs who hoarded their riches and refused to support the poor embodied the opposite type that will be described shortly. Meanwhile, the early believers like Abu Bakr and others were known for their generosity, freeing slaves and feeding the hungry, all while trembling in awe of their Lord. This verse provides the first half of a clear-cut moral test: look at how you use your wealth and what your heart harbours, and you will know which path you are on.

=6=

The description of the first type continues: “and firmly believes in the finest reward.” The “finest reward” translates the Arabic al-husna, which refers to the ultimate good, the beautiful reward of Paradise, or more broadly, the best outcome that Allah has promised to the righteous. It encompasses the certainty of resurrection, accountability, and the eternal bliss that awaits those who believe.

The word “firmly believes” underscores that this is not a casual, wavering opinion, but a deep-rooted conviction that shapes behaviour. A person might be charitable and appear mindful, but if they doubt the Hereafter, their striving is for a worldly end, not the divine finest reward. This belief in the finest reward is what transforms charity from a social virtue into an act of worship destined for eternal consequence. It links the outward deed to the unseen world. The disbelievers of Mecca often dismissed the idea of resurrection as a fable; their worldly striving was therefore unmoored from ultimate purpose.

The surah teaches that true success requires a firm anchor in the unseen reality of the Hereafter. The “finest reward” is not merely a neutral exchange—it is described as beautiful, delightful, and perfect. The believer’s faith in that reward makes the sacrifices of charity and God-consciousness feel light.

This verse also forms a logical sequence with the previous one: charitable deeds spring from mindfulness, and both are sustained and validated by firm belief in the ultimate good. The three qualities together—generosity, piety, and eschatological faith—form the character of the one whose striving is directed toward the Way of Ease. It is a holistic picture that refuses to separate action from creed. The believers are thus defined not by abstract theology alone, but by a life that flows from theology into tangible kindness, all aimed at the beautiful promise of their Lord.

=7=

The surah now announces the divine response to such a person: “We will facilitate for them the Way of Ease.” The phrase “the Way of Ease” translates a word that suggests a path that is smooth, gentle, and lacking in severe hardship. This does not mean that the believer will face no difficulties in life—the Qur’an repeatedly states that believers will be tested—but that Allah will make the journey to the Hereafter easy in specific, profound ways. The ease includes granting the ability to perform good deeds without excessive internal resistance, opening the heart to faith, providing inner contentment during trials, and making the crossing into Paradise a gentle experience after death. The verb “facilitate” indicates that this ease is not a natural human state; it is a divine gift bestowed on those who commit themselves to generosity, mindfulness, and firm belief. The verse echoes the principle that good deeds lead to further goodness. When a person takes the first step of charity and taqwa, Allah smooths the path ahead, removing obstacles and inclining the soul toward righteousness. This facilitation may come in the form of encountering helpful people, finding opportunities to give, feeling joy in worship, and ultimately dying in a state of faith. The promise also serves as immense encouragement for the struggling believer. The steep path described in Surah Al-Balad is indeed challenging, but Allah does not leave the climber alone. He facilitates the ascent. Conversely, the one who chooses stinginess and denial will find the opposite. The verse is thus a window into Allah’s merciful nature: He rewards the first steps of sincere striving by making the subsequent steps easier, a truth that the Prophet himself embodied when he taught that whoever draws near to Allah by a handspan, Allah draws near by an arm’s length. The Way of Ease is the destination of the first type of striver, and it is described with a beauty that invites every listener to aspire toward it.

=8=

The surah now presents the stark contrast: “And as for the one who is stingy, indifferent to Allah.” The word “stingy” refers to someone who hoards wealth, refusing to give the charity that is due, clinging to possessions out of greed and a lack of trust in the Provider.

This is the outward symptom of a diseased heart. The stingy person sees his wealth as the fruit of his own effort alone and fears poverty if he shares it. But the stinginess is not merely about money; it reflects a deeper spiritual miserliness, a refusal to extend any goodness to others. Paired with this is “indifferent to Allah,” which translates the concept of istighna—a feeling of self-sufficiency that makes a person consider themselves above needing Allah. The indifferent one thinks they are rich, powerful, and beyond any divine command. They act as if they can manage life on their own, without gratitude or obedience to their Creator. This attitude was epitomised by the wealthy Quraysh chieftains who saw the Prophet’s call to worship one God as an affront to their independence and status. The verse thus identifies the second type of striver: one whose life is oriented around hoarding and self-conceit. The contrast with the charitable and mindful believer is absolute. Where the first type opens their hand and heart, the second type clenches both. The surah does not merely critique outward behaviour; it traces stinginess back to its root: a heart that feels no need for God. This diagnosis is timeless. In every age, individuals and even entire civilisations can fall into the trap of thinking that their material abundance makes them self-sufficient, freeing them from the duty to give and from the humility of worship. The verse serves as a mirror, prompting each listener to check their own soul for traces of this deadly indifference.

=9=

The description of the second type deepens: “and staunchly denies the finest reward.” Denial of the finest reward—the ultimate good of Paradise and the reality of the Hereafter—is the intellectual and creedal dimension of their corruption. The adverb “staunchly” emphasises that this is not a doubt born of honest questioning; it is a hardened, stubborn rejection. The disbeliever does not merely fail to believe; they actively call the promise of the Hereafter a lie. This denial is the natural outcome of their indifference to Allah. Because they feel self-sufficient, they do not want to hear about a Day of Judgment where their wealth and status will count for nothing. The denial of the finest reward conveniently removes any future accountability, allowing them to enjoy their stinginess and arrogance without anxiety. But the surah links this denial directly to their moral behaviour. A person who truly believes that they will stand before Allah and be rewarded for charity will be motivated to give. A person who denies that ultimate good has no compelling reason to sacrifice their wealth for an unseen future.

Thus belief and behaviour are two sides of the same coin. The Quraysh elite mocked the idea of resurrection, saying it was an ancient myth; their mockery served to protect their comfortable, unjust lifestyle. The verse strips away their pretensions and shows that their denial is not intellectually noble but morally convenient. It also warns believers that faith in the finest reward cannot be compartmentalised; if it is real, it will necessarily transform one’s relationship with money and with the needy. The one who claims to believe in Paradise but remains consistently stingy and indifferent is living a contradiction. The verse thus poses a searching question to the heart: do I truly, firmly believe in the finest reward, or do I live as a practical denier?

=10=

The inevitable divine response to the stingy, indifferent deniers is announced with sober finality: “We will facilitate for them the path of hardship.” Just as the generous believer is granted the Way of Ease, the miserly denier is steered toward the path of hardship. This hardship manifests in multiple dimensions. In this life, the stingy person’s heart remains constricted; they are perpetually anxious about losing wealth, never satisfied with what they have, and plagued by the fear of poverty. Their relationships suffer, their communities do not love them, and their soul is starved of the joy that comes from giving. In the grave and the Hereafter, the hardship becomes excruciatingly literal: a painful punishment, a narrow tomb, and a Fire that encloses them. The verb “facilitate” is profoundly significant. It suggests that Allah makes the path to damnation easy for such a person by removing the obstacles that might have led them to repent. Their own choices set a direction, and then divine facilitation seals them on that path. Doors of goodness become heavy for them, while opportunities for sin appear smooth and attractive.

This is a terrifying dimension of divine justice: when a person persistently chooses evil and denies the truth, Allah does not force them to change; instead, He facilitates their chosen road toward its ultimate, dreadful end. The verse does not mean that Allah creates evil in them, but that He withdraws further guidance and light, allowing their own corruption to pull them downward with increasing speed. The surah is thus describing a spiritual law as certain as the law of gravity. The contrast between the Way of Ease and the path of hardship forms the moral heart of the surah. Every soul is moving in one of these two directions, and the everyday choices about wealth, faith, and God-consciousness are the signposts indicating which way the journey is heading.

=11=

The surah closes the description of the second type with a crushing final note: “And their wealth will be of no benefit to them when they tumble into Hell.” The very wealth that the stingy person hoarded and that made them feel self-sufficient will prove utterly useless at the moment of death and judgment.

The word “tumble” vividly portrays the sudden, helpless fall of the disbeliever into the abyss of Hell, as if all their worldly stability vanishes in an instant. They had clung to their riches as a source of security, believing that money could buy anything, including protection from calamity. But on that Day, wealth has no purchasing power. No ransom is accepted, no bribe can turn away the flames. This verse directly refutes the boast of the man in Surah Al-Balad who proclaimed, “I have wasted enormous wealth!” That wasted wealth, which could have been a means of salvation had it been spent in charity, becomes a witness against its owner.

The verse also serves as a powerful psychological check. It asks the listener: is it rational to devote your entire life to accumulating something that will abandon you at the moment you need it most? The only wealth that truly benefits a person is the wealth they send ahead in the form of charity, the purification of the soul, and the seeking of Allah’s pleasure. This verse transitions the surah toward its concluding reassurance that guidance is from Allah and that the ultimate outcomes are in His hands. By stating that the wealth of the wicked will not avail them when they fall, the surah reinforces that the criteria for success and failure are entirely different from the world’s standards. The believer is thus trained to look at the rich and the poor not by their bank balances but by the direction of their striving and the final destination they are tumbling toward.

=12=

After presenting the two contrasting human types and their respective destinations, the surah grounds the entire discussion in divine responsibility and mercy: “It is certainly upon Us alone to show the way to guidance.” This statement is a direct assurance from Allah that He has taken it upon Himself to provide clear direction. The “way to guidance” is the path that leads to Him—the revelation, the prophets, the innate human disposition, the signs in creation, and the scriptures.

The emphasis on “upon Us alone” indicates that clarifying the truth is a divine commitment; Allah does not leave humanity to wander in the dark guessing what might please Him. He sends messengers, reveals books, and illuminates the conscience. This verse answers a potential question that might arise from the previous verses: how can a person be blamed for taking the path of hardship if they do not know the way?

The answer is that the way has been shown, thoroughly and unmistakably. The responsibility for showing guidance is Allah’s, and He has fulfilled it.

The verse also comforted the Prophet, who was deeply pained by his people’s rejection. It reminded him that his task was to convey the clear guidance that Allah had provided; the ultimate outcome of each soul was tied to how they responded to that shown path. For the believers, the verse reinforces gratitude and humility: the guidance they enjoy is not a product of their own intelligence or merit; it is a gift from Allah who took it upon Himself to show the way. This eliminates spiritual arrogance and plants the constant prayer for steadfastness. The statement also serves as a bridge to the next verse, which expands the scope of divine ownership, making it clear that both guidance and the final judgment belong to the Lord alone.

=13=

The surah now expands the declaration of divine ownership: “And surely to Us alone belong this life and the next.” This is the ultimate assertion of sovereignty. Everything that exists in the present world—the wealth, the power, the nights and days, the male and female—and everything that will exist in the Hereafter—the Paradise, the Fire, the scales of justice—all belong exclusively to Allah. No human being truly owns anything; they are temporary trustees. The wealthy Quraysh who acted as if their riches were theirs by absolute right were living in a delusion that this verse shatters. Life on earth is a brief loan; the next life is the permanent reality, and both are in the hands of the One who created them. This comprehensive ownership means that Allah can give and take, elevate and humble, reward and punish, without any one questioning His authority. It also means that the “finest reward” promised to the believer is from the Owner of the next life, so it is absolutely secure. The verse ties together the surah’s themes: the diversity of human striving takes place on a stage that belongs to Allah, and the outcomes—ease or hardship—are distributed by the Owner with perfect wisdom. Because the Hereafter belongs to Him, He alone determines what leads there and what bars the way.

This verse also encourages asceticism and trust. When a believer fully internalises that this life and the next belong to Allah, the stinginess of the miser seems foolish and the charity of the generous seems natural—both are merely handling God’s property. The verse paves the way for the direct warning that follows, because the One who owns both abodes has the full right to warn of the Fire that He has prepared for those who misuse His trust.

=14=

With divine ownership established, the surah delivers a direct warning: “And so I have warned you of a raging Fire.” The speaker here is Allah, or by implication the Prophet conveying Allah’s message. The warning is personal and immediate: “I have warned you.” This brings the listener face to face with the consequence of choosing the path of hardship. The Fire is described as “raging,” translating an Arabic word that suggests a blaze that is intensely kindled, fiercely burning, leaping with flames that consume everything in their path.

This is not a distant, weak flicker; it is a violent inferno prepared for those who denied the finest reward and hoarded their wealth. The warning follows logically from the previous verses. Since Allah has shown guidance, since both lives belong to Him, since human strivings are diverse and lead to different ends, it is an act of mercy to clearly inform humanity of what awaits those who take the wrong road. The warning is an expression of divine compassion, not divine cruelty. By informing us of the raging Fire, Allah gives us the chance to avoid it.

The phrase also reminds the Prophet to continue his mission of warning, however much the disbelievers mock. In Mecca, the Quraysh would laugh at the idea of a blazing Hell, yet the verse insists on its reality.

The image of the raging Fire contrasts sharply with the Way of Ease described earlier; one is a smooth path to Paradise, the other leads to infernal torment. The surah is now building to a climax that will define precisely who will enter that Fire and who will be spared, moving from the general categories of the charitable and the stingy to the specific eschatological verdicts in the following verses.

=15=

The surah now narrows the scope of the Fire’s inhabitants: “in which none will burn except the most wretched.” The term “the most wretched” translates a superlative form that indicates those who have reached the peak of spiritual misery and ruin. This does not mean that the Fire will only contain a tiny handful; it means that those who enter it will have merited that status through their own choices. Their wretchedness is defined by what follows in the next verse: they are the ones who deny and turn away. The exclusionary phrase “none will burn except” serves as both a warning and a source of hope for the listeners. It warns that the Fire is not a random punishment but a precise justice; only those who have made themselves worthy of it will enter. And it implies that those who have some measure of faith, however imperfect, may eventually be spared or delivered.

In the broader context of the Qur’an, the “most wretched” are contrasted with the “foremost” in Paradise. This verse does not encourage complacency, but it clarifies that the ultimate horror of the blazing Fire is reserved for those who utterly reject the truth after it has been clearly shown. The Meccan disbelievers who were actively persecuting the believers and mocking the Prophet were heading toward this very description. By labeling them as “the most wretched,” the surah overturns their worldly self-image as the noble and the honoured. They prided themselves on their lineage and wealth, but in the divine register, they were the truly wretched.

The verse also connects back to the earlier description of the one who is stingy, indifferent, and denies the finest reward; that person is heading toward the Fire as one of the most wretched. So the sequence is logical and devastating.

=16=

The surah defines the most wretched with two concise, interrelated actions: “who deny and turn away.” Denial refers to the intellectual and creedal rejection of the truth—they call the revelation a lie, they reject the oneness of Allah, and they disbelieve in the Hereafter. Turning away refers to the behavioural and volitional rejection—they physically and emotionally distance themselves from guidance. They do not merely have doubts; they actively refuse to listen, they turn their backs, and they walk away from the Prophet’s call. This pairing of denial and turning away shows that disbelief is rarely a neutral, passive state. It is a combination of rejecting the heart and shunning with the limbs. The most wretched are not people who never heard the message; they are people who heard it, recognised its signs, and then wilfully denied and turned away out of arrogance, just as the Thamud did in the previous surah.

The verse also illustrates that salvation and damnation are tied to a person’s response to divine guidance. The one who embraces the truth draws near; the one who denies and turns away becomes increasingly distant, and that distance hardens into eternal separation.

In the context of the Prophet’s mission in Mecca, this verse was a clear description of Abu Jahl, Abu Lahab, and their like. They heard the Qur’an’s unmatched eloquence, they knew the Prophet’s truthfulness, yet they denied and turned others away. The warning is timeless: any person in any era who is shown the truth and then refuses it and turns their back falls under this devastating label. The verse leaves no room for neutrality. There is no middle ground between accepting and turning away; the act of turning is itself a choice that leads to the raging Fire. This stark description is then immediately balanced by the portrait of the righteous in the next verse, maintaining the surah’s rhythmic pattern of contrasts.

=17=

The surah pivots with a powerful, hope-filled contrast: “But the righteous will be spared from it.” The “righteous” are those who possess taqwa—God-consciousness, piety, and a constant awareness of their Lord that leads them to obey and avoid sin. They are the exact opposite of the most wretched who denied and turned away. The verb “spared” indicates that they will be kept far from the raging Fire, given safety and distance from its horror.

This is not because they were sinless or perfect, but because their overall orientation was toward faith, charity, and the pleasure of Allah. The righteous will experience the ultimate success: deliverance from the Fire and entry into Paradise. This verse echoes the promise given earlier that the charitable, mindful believer will be facilitated on the Way of Ease. The sparing is the final outcome of that facilitation. The verse serves as a powerful motivator and a source of deep consolation. For the early Muslims in Mecca who were being tortured and impoverished, the promise that they would be spared the Fire while their arrogant persecutors would be its fuel was a balm for wounded hearts. It reminded them that the scales of ultimate justice would tip completely in their favour. The description of “the righteous” is intentionally broad, but the next verse will specify a defining characteristic that relates to wealth and sincerity, picking up the earlier theme of charity. The righteous are not merely people who hold correct beliefs in private; their righteousness manifests in their financial behaviour and their inner motivations. The verse thus bridges the general category of “righteous” and the specific portrait of those who give to purify themselves, seamlessly tying the surah’s ethical teaching together.

=18=

The surah now fleshes out the character of the righteous: “who donate some of their wealth only to purify themselves.” This is the action that corresponds directly to the charity mentioned at the beginning. The verb “donate” implies a consistent practice of giving, not a one-time gesture. They give “some of their wealth,” which indicates that they do not impoverish themselves recklessly, nor do they give so little that it is meaningless; they give a portion that is significant and helpful. The purpose is explicitly stated: “only to purify themselves.” This purification is not a self-righteous, prideful cleansing in the eyes of others, but a sincere effort to cleanse the soul from the stains of greed, selfishness, and attachment to the material world.

The word tazkiyah, purification, is the same as in Surah Ash-Shams: successful is the one who purifies the soul. Here, wealth is a tool for that purification. By giving it away for Allah’s sake, the believer sheds a layer of worldly clinging and allows the soul to grow toward spiritual purity. The addition of “only” (or “no one being owed a favour” as the next verse elaborates) underscores that this act is done with absolute sincerity, not to repay a past kindness or to place someone under obligation. The verse directly counters the behaviour of the stingy, indifferent denier who hoards wealth and whose soul remains unpurified, caked in the dirt of avarice. It also establishes a beautiful principle: wealth is not inherently evil; it is a test. When given correctly, it becomes a purifying agent. When hoarded and loved for its own sake, it becomes a cause of spiritual ruin. The righteous are thus defined by their economic ethics, showing that true taqwa has a direct impact on the wallet and on the treatment of the poor and needy.

=19=

The description of the righteous giver continues with a crucial clarification of motive: “not in return for someone’s favours.” The Arabic wording negates any idea that the charity is given to repay a previous good turn or to balance a social debt. The righteous person is not keeping a ledger of favours, doling out charity only to those who have helped them in the past, or expecting the recipient to return the favour in the future. This purity of intention is the hallmark of the believer whose deeds are directed solely toward the face of Allah. In the tribal society of Mecca, gift-giving and financial assistance were often deeply entangled with networks of obligation, loyalty, and pride. A wealthy man might support a poorer relative or ally, but that support came with strings attached—it demanded political allegiance, public gratitude, and reinforced the giver’s superior status. The Qur’an shatters this transactional mentality. True charity, the charity that purifies the soul and leads to being spared from the Fire, is free from any expectation of worldly return. The verse implicitly criticises the hypocrisy of those who give to be seen, to win followers, or to lay others under obligation. The righteous person’s heart is so focused on the Hereafter that earthly reciprocation does not even register as a motivation.

This teaching is foundational to Islamic ethics. The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught that one of the seven types of people shaded under the Throne on the Day of Judgment is the one who gives charity so secretly that the left hand does not know what the right hand has spent. The anonymity and pure intention described here are the spiritual antidote to the stingy, boastful spending condemned in Surah Al-Balad. The righteous person has converted their struggle from showing off to sincere purification.

=20=

The verse completes the portrait of the giver’s motive with a sublime expression: “but seeking the pleasure of their Lord, the Most High.” The only reason the righteous donate their wealth is to seek the face of their Lord, yearning for His approval, His smile, His pleasure. The divine name “the Most High” adds an element of transcendent majesty. The Lord they seek is not a distant, indifferent force; He is the Exalted One, far above any need for their charity, yet He honours them by accepting it and being pleased with them. The believer understands that true honour, true security, and true satisfaction lie not in accumulating wealth or winning the approval of fellow humans, but in attaining the good pleasure of the Most High. This motive transforms mundane financial transactions into acts of profound worship.

The verse also brings the entire moral argument of the surah to a beautiful resolution. The diversity of human striving boils down to this: some people strive for the material, the temporary, the ego, and end up in hardship; others strive for the face of their Lord, the Most High, and are guided to ease and to Paradise. The “finest reward” that the believer firmly believes in is, in its essence, the pleasure of Allah, which is the greatest bliss. This verse, coupled with the next and final verse of the surah, promises that the one who seeks this pleasure will find exactly what they seek. The Lord they yearned for will satisfy them completely. The surah thus ends on a note of profound hope, showing that a life directed purely toward the Most High is not a life of loss but a life that culminates in the ultimate fulfilment.

The mention of “Most High” reminds the believer that no matter how low their worldly position may be, their aspiration reaches the loftiest summit, and that Summit will respond with pleasure and contentment.
