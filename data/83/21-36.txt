=21=

The description of the sealed fate of the virtuous continues in verse twenty-one, adding a dimension of exalted witness: “witnessed by those nearest to Allah.” The Arabic yashhaduhu al-muqarrabūn refers to the angels who are brought near to Allah, the highest-ranking celestial beings who are closest to the divine presence.

This statement indicates that the register of the righteous, their kitāb marqūm placed in ‘Illiyūn, is not a hidden, obscure document. It is an open record witnessed by the most noble of creation, the angels of the highest heavens.

The word muqarrabūn comes from qaruba, to be near, and denotes those who have attained the supreme station of nearness to Allah. They include the great angels like Jibrīl, Mīkā’īl, Isrāfīl, and the bearers of the Throne. The fact that they witness the record of the abrār means that the honour of the virtuous is celebrated in the highest assembly. Their good deeds are not just recorded and stored; they are presented, acknowledged, and appreciated in the heavens.

This is an immense source of motivation for the believer. The small, honest act of giving full measure in a dusty market is seen and witnessed by the angels who are closest to the Lord of all worlds. It completely re-frames the value of righteous action. What seemed insignificant in the eyes of the defrauders is of such great worth that the muqarrabūn are called to witness it. This also reinforces the concept of the communion of the righteous: the believers on earth are linked to the heavenly assembly, and their deeds rise to be witnessed by the purest beings.

The verse also implicitly contrasts the two records: the wicked’s record is in the deep, dark Sijjīn, isolated and cursed, while the virtuous’ record is in the elevated ‘Illiyūn, surrounded by the light of the nearest angels. It is a powerful image of honour, transparency, and the ultimate recognition that awaits those who are faithful in the smallest of matters. The believer should strive to have their name mentioned in that noble gathering, witnessed by those nearest to Allah.

=22=

Having established the honoured place of their record, the sūrah now directly states their condition in verse twenty-two: “Surely the virtuous will be in bliss.” This is the same declaration found in Sūrah al-Infiṭār, and it serves as a repeated, comforting refrain.

The Arabic inna al-abrāra la-fī na‘īm uses the emphatic particles inna and la- to leave no doubt. The abrār, the same righteous ones whose record is in ‘Illiyūn, will be immersed in na‘īm, which is pure, unadulterated bliss and delight. This word encompasses every form of pleasure, joy, and comfort. It is not merely the absence of pain but a positive, overflowing state of happiness. The verse functions as a summary statement of the destiny of the virtuous.

In the context of the surah, it provides the starkest contrast with the fate of the wicked. The defrauders who took a little extra in the scales and denied the Judgment will be burning in Hell, while those who were honest and mindful will be in bliss. The present tense “will be” indicates the certainty of this future state, as if it is already a reality. For the early Meccan Muslims who faced economic boycott and cheating at the hands of the pagan defrauders, this was a profound consolation. Their patience and honesty were not going unnoticed; they were purchasing an eternal bliss that no amount of worldly profit could compare to.

The verse also speaks to the universal human desire for happiness. Everyone seeks bliss, but many seek it in the wrong places—in wealth, status, or fleeting pleasures. The verse redirects the seeker to the true and lasting bliss, which is only attained through faith and righteousness. It is a call to re-evaluate one’s definition of success. The believer, upon reading this, should feel a deep sense of peace and a renewed commitment to the path of the abrār, knowing that their destination is na‘īm.

=23=

The bliss of the virtuous is given a vivid, sensory dimension in verse twenty-three: “seated on canopied couches, gazing around.” The Arabic ‘alā al-arā’iki yanẓurūn paints a picture of serene luxury and contemplative enjoyment.

The word arā’ik is the plural of arīkah, which refers to a throne-like couch that is raised and covered with a canopy. It is a seat of honour, comfort, and privacy. The believers in Paradise will recline on these magnificent couches, experiencing a state of complete relaxation and regal ease.

The second part, “gazing around” (yanẓurūn), is a beautiful detail. They will look around at the delights of Paradise, at the beautiful gardens, at the rivers flowing, at the wonderful companions, and, most supremely, at the countenance of their Lord. The gaze is not one of anxiety or searching but of leisurely, joyful contemplation.

This is the reward for the believers who in the world would look at the signs of Allah with reflection and who would gaze upon the plight of the poor with compassion. Their sight in Paradise will be a source of continuous delight.

The mention of canopied couches also evokes a sense of permanence and security. They are not merely sitting on the ground; they are enthroned, shaded, and honoured.

The word “seated” implies that the toil and struggle of the world are over. Now is the time of rest and enjoyment.

This verse also implicitly contrasts with the description of the wicked, who will be burning in Hell with no comfort. The believers will be gazing around in wonder, while the disbelievers will be veiled from their Lord, unable to see anything that brings them joy.

For the believer, this verse creates a powerful visual anchor. When the trials of life weigh heavily, one can recall this image of reclining on a canopied couch in Paradise, gazing at the endless beauty that Allah has prepared. It instills hope and patience, transforming the hardships of the world into stepping stones to that eternal seat of honour.

=24=

Verse twenty-four adds another sensory detail that reveals the inner state of the believers in Paradise: “You will recognize on their faces the glow of delight.” The Arabic ta‘rifu fī wujūhihim naḍrata al-na‘īm uses the verb ta‘rifu, “you will recognize” or “you will know.” The “you” here addresses the Prophet (ﷺ) and every onlooker, emphasising that the joy of the believers is not hidden; it radiates visibly.

The phrase naḍrata al-na‘īm is rich in meaning. Naḍrah refers to a radiant glow, a bright freshness, a lustre and beauty that shines from the skin. It is the visible effect of inner happiness, peace, and well-being. Al-na‘īm is the bliss mentioned in the previous verse, the source of this glow. So their faces will shine with the very bliss they are experiencing.

This is the opposite of the dusty, gloomy faces of the wicked described in Sūrah ‘Abasa. In this world, a person’s face may be marked by worry, fatigue, sadness, or the consequences of sin. In Paradise, the faces of the righteous will be illuminated with a light that reflects the eternal joy within. This verse connects outward beauty with inward purity. The believers spent their lives purifying their hearts from the stains of sin, as earlier mentioned, and now that inner purity manifests as an outer radiance. It also indicates that in Paradise, there is no envy, no hidden sorrow, no masks. The joy is so complete that it shines through every face. For the believers who were mocked by the disbelievers, as the later verses will describe, this verse is a powerful reassurance. The same faces that were looked upon with scorn in the world will glow with a delight that their mockers can only look upon with envy and regret. This verse encourages the believer to seek that inner light now, through sincere worship and good character, so that it may be perfected in the Hereafter. It is a vision of beauty that makes the fleeting pleasures of sin fade into insignificance.

=25=

The description of the pleasures of Paradise continues with a specific gift in verse twenty-five: “They will be given a drink of sealed, pure wine.” The Arabic yusqawna min raḥīqin makhtūm indicates that the believers in Paradise will be served a special drink.

The word raḥīq refers to the purest, most refined wine, free from any impurity. It is described as “sealed” (makhtūm), meaning that it is kept in sealed containers, a sign of its preciousness and purity. In this world, valuable drinks and perfumes are sealed to preserve their quality and prevent tampering. The seal guarantees that the drink is untouched and of the highest grade. The believers in Paradise will be served this exquisite wine directly, as a mark of honour and hospitality from Allah. It is crucial to note that the wine of Paradise is entirely different from the intoxicating wine of this world. The Qur’ān explicitly states elsewhere that the wine of Paradise does not cause headache, intoxication, or vain talk. It is pure pleasure without any negative consequence.

The word “sealed” also carries a connotation of exclusivity. This is a drink reserved for the honoured guests of the Most Merciful. Some scholars have even interpreted the seal as musk, as the following verse will mention the musk-like aroma at the final sip. The act of being given this drink signifies that the believers are in a state of celebration, a divine banquet where they are served by the best of hosts. For the early Muslims who were forbidden from drinking the intoxicating wine of this world, this verse offered a far superior alternative, one that they could anticipate with eagerness. It teaches the principle of delayed gratification: giving up a prohibited, harmful pleasure in this life for a pure, exquisite pleasure in the eternal life. The believer is trained to look beyond immediate gratification and to trust that what Allah has in store is infinitely better.

=26=

The excellence of this paradisiacal wine is further described in verse twenty-six: “whose last sip will smell like musk.” The Arabic khitāmuhu misk refers to the seal or the final impression of the drink. When the drink is finished, the last drop or the lingering scent in the cup and on the breath will be that of pure musk. Musk is an intensely fragrant, precious substance used in perfumes, highly valued in Arabian culture. Instead of the foul smell associated with worldly alcohol, the heavenly wine leaves behind a delightful perfume. This sensory detail adds to the overall atmosphere of purity and refinement. Some scholars also interpret khitām as the seal of the container itself, meaning the wine is sealed with musk, indicating its supreme value. Both interpretations point to the exquisite nature of the reward.

The verse then adds a powerful call to action: “So let whoever aspires to this strive diligently.” The Arabic wa fī dhālika fa-l-yatanāfasi al-mutanāfisūn means “and for that, let the competitors compete.

” The word yatanāfasi comes from nafāsah, which implies a competition in something precious, a rivalry in seeking something highly desirable. The verse transforms the description of bliss into a motivation for effort. The delights of Paradise are not obtained by wishful thinking; they require striving. This striving is both internal—against the desires of the ego—and external—in acts of worship and honesty. The believer is called to compete not for the fleeting gains of this world, as the defrauders do, but for the eternal delights of the next.

The word “strive diligently” captures the sense of a race, a determined and focused effort. The verse connects back to the opening of the surah: while the defrauders compete to steal a few ounces in the marketplace, the believers should compete to earn the wine of Paradise sealed with musk.

This is a profound redirection of human ambition. Ambition is not condemned; it is simply redirected to its proper, eternal object. The believer should be a fierce competitor for goodness, not for the trinkets of the world. This verse serves as a timeless motto for the spiritual life.

=27=

The source of the exquisite drink is revealed in verse twenty-seven: “And this drink’s flavour will come from Tasnîm.” The Arabic wa mizājuhu min tasnīm indicates that the sealed wine is blended with water from Tasnîm, a special spring in Paradise.

The word mizāj means mixture, blend, or the component that gives the drink its distinctive flavour. Tasnīm is the name of a specific spring, derived from the root sanama, which means to be high, elevated. It is a spring that flows from the highest heights of Paradise. The fact that the wine of the abrār is mixed with water from Tasnīm shows that even their drink has a connection to the most elevated realms. The flavour and quality of a drink often come from the water source used. Tasnīm provides the purest, most delightful water, enhancing the raḥīq to a level of perfection.

The mention of Tasnīm introduces a hierarchy within the pleasures of Paradise. While the virtuous enjoy this wine mixed with Tasnīm, the following verse will explain that those nearest to Allah drink from Tasnīm directly. This indicates that the abrār, the general righteous, receive a taste of the higher blessings through mixture, while the muqarrabūn, the foremost, receive them in their purest form. It is a beautiful picture of interconnected bliss and graded reward. The believers are all in bliss, but their ranks vary according to their striving. The verse motivates the believer to not just seek entry into Paradise but to aspire for the highest ranks, to be among those who drink directly from Tasnīm.

The name Tasnīm itself, with its connotation of height and elevation, reminds the believer that the path to this spring is through raising one’s spiritual station in this world. The verse adds yet another dimension to the reward, making the heart yearn for that blessed water and the effort required to reach it.

=28=

The full status of Tasnīm is clarified in verse twenty-eight: “a spring from which those nearest to Allah will drink.” The Arabic ‘aynan yashrabu bihā al-muqarrabūn identifies Tasnīm as a spring reserved primarily for the muqarrabūn, those brought near to Allah.

This is the same group mentioned in verse twenty-one, the highest-ranking angels who witness the record of the righteous, and, as used here, the highest class of believers—the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the most righteous. They will drink directly and purely from Tasnīm, without it being mixed with anything. The abrār, the general righteous, will have their wine blended with it, receiving an overflow of the blessings granted to the muqarrabūn. This beautiful hierarchy shows the interconnectedness of the inhabitants of Paradise. The higher ranks receive the purest essence, and the lower ranks partake of its flavour through the generosity of Allah. It is not a source of envy but of harmony and shared delight.

The verse also teaches that nearness to Allah is the ultimate achievement. The muqarrabūn are not necessarily those with the most wealth or fame in the world; they are those who excelled in faith, sincerity, and good deeds. They drew near to Allah through their devotion, and their reward is to drink from the spring of nearness.

For the believer, this verse is both a comfort and a challenge. It comforts by showing the abundance of Allah’s mercy, where even the general righteous taste Tasnīm. It challenges by setting a high bar: strive to be muqarrab, strive to drink directly.

The mention of those nearest to Allah also brings the surah’s theme of spiritual vision full circle. The wicked were veiled from their Lord, while the muqarrabūn, who drink from Tasnīm, enjoy the highest degree of nearness and, as other verses indicate, the vision of the divine countenance.

The verse concludes the description of the rewards, leaving the listener with a profound longing for the upper reaches of Paradise, and the understanding that the path begins with the small choices of integrity and faith in the marketplace.

=29=

With the rewards and punishments fully described, the sūrah now turns to a specific historical scene, the moral psychology of which provides deep satisfaction. Verse twenty-nine begins: “Indeed, the wicked used to laugh at the believers.

” The Arabic inna alladhīna ajramū kānū min alladhīna āmanū yaḍḥakūn uses the past tense “used to” (kānū), indicating a habitual action in the worldly life. The “wicked” are the mujrimūn, the criminals, those who committed the crimes of disbelief, fraud, and mockery. Their crime included laughing at the believers, ridiculing their faith, their poverty, and their commitment to honesty. This verse references the experience of the early Muslims in Mecca, where the powerful pagan elite would mock the weak and oppressed who embraced Islam. Figures like Bilāl, ‘Ammār, and Ṣuhayb were laughed at by the nobles of Quraysh for abandoning the idols and following an unlettered prophet. The laughter was a weapon of humiliation, designed to make the believers feel small and foolish. The verse acknowledges the pain of that mockery by recording it in the divine revelation. It validates the believers’ suffering and tells them that their Lord has seen and heard everything. The choice of “the wicked” (alladhīna ajramū) instead of simply “the disbelievers” links their mockery to their criminal nature. Mocking the faithful is a crime against dignity, a sin that will be answered.

This verse also reveals a timeless pattern: the people of falsehood often use ridicule as their tool when they cannot defeat the truth with reason. The believer is thus forewarned and comforted. The laughter of the wicked is not a sign of their strength but of their arrogance, and it will be answered.

The use of “Indeed” (inna) assures the listeners that this is a factual account, a record that will be presented on the Day of Judgment, where the roles will be reversed.

=30=

The description of the mockery becomes more specific and vivid in verse thirty: “wink to one another whenever they passed by.” The Arabic wa idhā marrū bihim yataghāmazūn uses the verb yataghāmazūn, which means to wink, gesture with the eyes, or nudge each other in a secretive, mocking manner. As the believers would walk by, the wicked would catch each other’s eyes and exchange knowing, contemptuous glances.

This is a form of subtle, psychological bullying. The wink conveys a message: “Look at these fools. How can they believe in resurrection? How can they give up the pleasures of life for some imaginary paradise?” It is a gesture designed to belittle and isolate the believers, to make them feel like outcasts in their own city. The verse captures the arrogance and the group mentality of the wicked. They reinforce each other’s mockery through these non-verbal cues, creating a hostile social environment.

The phrase “whenever they passed by” shows that this was a continuous, relentless behaviour. Every encounter was an opportunity for mockery. This historical detail would resonate deeply with the early Muslims, who endured this silent torture daily. By recording this wink in the Qur’ān, Allah exposes the hidden meanness of the disbelievers. That secret wink, which they thought no one of consequence noticed, is seen by the Lord of all worlds and is written in the eternal record. The verse serves as a powerful reminder that no act of cruelty, no matter how small or subtle, escapes divine notice. The wink that was meant to shame the believer will shame the winker on the Day of Judgment.

For believers in any age who face similar quiet mockery—the raised eyebrow, the sarcastic glance, the condescending smirk—this verse provides immense comfort. The One who sees all will bring justice, and the winks will be answered.

=31=

The pattern of the wicked’s behaviour continues in verse thirty-one: “and muse over these exploits upon returning to their own people.” The Arabic wa idhā inqalabū ilā ahlihimu inqalabū fakihīn describes the wicked returning to their families and friends after their encounters with the believers.

The word inqalabū means they turned back, they returned. The word fakihīn (also read as fakihīn) means jesting, amusing themselves, being in a state of playful, mocking delight. So they would go home and recount their day’s “exploits”—how they mocked this believer, how they winked at that one, how they cleverly cheated in a trade, how they laughed at the idea of resurrection. The mockery was not just a public performance; it was a source of private entertainment and family bonding. They would sit with their wives and children and turn the believers into objects of ridicule, laughing together at the “fools” who had abandoned the religion of their ancestors.

This verse reveals the depth of their heedlessness. Their social life was built around the mockery of the truth.

The word fakihīn can also imply a sense of self-satisfaction and complacency. They returned home pleased with themselves, convinced of their own cleverness and the stupidity of the believers. The verse sets up the ultimate reversal. The home, which was the site of their arrogant laughter, will be replaced by the Fire. The family, with whom they shared their cruel jokes, will be of no help. The believer, who may have been the subject of those evening mockery sessions, can find solace in knowing that the divine record preserves every word and every laugh.

This verse also serves as a warning to believers about the type of entertainment they enjoy. Are our gatherings filled with mockery of others, or with remembrance of Allah? The wicked’s “musing” led to their doom; the believer’s gatherings should be led to nearness to the Lord.

=32=

The verbal form of their mockery is recorded in verse thirty-two: “And when they saw the faithful, they would say, ‘These people are truly astray.’” The Arabic wa idhā ra’awhum qālū inna hā’ulā’i la-ḍāllūn uses the emphatic la- to stress their claim of misguidance. The “faithful” are the believers, those who had embraced Islam. The wicked, looking at the believers, pronounced them to be profoundly lost.

This is the classic inversion of reality by the arrogant. The believers had found the Straight Way, the path to eternal bliss and ‘Illiyūn, yet the disbelievers called them astray. The believers had abandoned idol worship, cheating in trade, and the immoral practices of the pre-Islamic society. From the perspective of the Quraysh elite, this abandonment was a deviation from the norm, a loss of social standing and economic advantage. So they labelled it “ḍalāl,” misguidance. This verse captures the loneliness of the truth-bearer in a corrupt society. When the majority has normalized falsehood, the one who adheres to the truth appears strange and lost. The believers were called astray because they refused to defraud, because they believed in the resurrection, because they worshipped one God. The verse consoles the believers that this accusation is a badge of honour. The prophets before them were called the same. It also exposes the psychology of the wicked: they must convince themselves and others that the righteous are astray in order to justify their own sinful lifestyle. If the believers are astray, then the wicked do not have to examine their own hearts. This accusation is a shield against the call of truth.

For the believer today, this verse is a reminder that being called “astray” by a world that has lost its moral compass is a sign that one might be exactly on the Straight Path. The final reversal in the surah will show who is truly astray when the ultimate reality is unveiled.

=33=

The verse immediately comments on the absurdity of the disbelievers’ judgmental stance: “even though they were not sent as keepers over the believers.” The Arabic wa mā ursilū ‘alayhim ḥāfiẓīn states that the wicked were never appointed as guardians, watchers, or keepers over the believers.

The word ḥāfiẓīn means those who guard, watch over, and hold responsibility. The disbelievers had no divine mandate to judge the spiritual state of the believers. Their pronouncement of “These people are astray” was not based on any authority or knowledge; it was pure arrogance and presumption. They were not prophets, nor angels, nor anyone entrusted with the guidance of others. They were simply fellow human beings, and sinful ones at that. This verse strips them of their assumed role as judges. It implies that the real judge is Allah, and He has already declared the believers to be on the Straight Path and the wicked to be in error. By mocking and judging the believers, the wicked were usurping a role that belongs to Allah alone.

This is a profound lesson for all believers. We are not to be overly concerned with the judgments and mockery of people who have no authority to judge. Our worth is not determined by the winks and whispers of those who reject the truth. The only opinion that ultimately matters is that of the Lord of all worlds.

Furthermore, the verse hints that the disbelievers, by acting as self-appointed keepers, were actually exposing their own insecurity. They needed to put the believers down to feel secure in their own path. The believer is liberated from the tyranny of public opinion by this verse. It does not matter that the world calls you astray; what matters is that Allah calls you guided. The disbelievers were not sent as keepers, so their judgment is null and void. The only Keeper whose judgment counts will be revealed on the tremendous Day.

=34=

Now comes the great reversal, the moment of divine justice that forms the climax of the sūrah. Verse thirty-four declares: “But on that Day the believers will be laughing at the disbelievers.

” The Arabic fa-al-yawma alladhīna āmanū min al-kuffāri yaḍḥakūn uses “that Day” (al-yawma) to refer emphatically to the Day of Judgment. The particle fa- (but/so) connects the past mockery to the future recompense. The very believers who were laughed at, winked at, and called astray will be the ones laughing. This laughter is not the cruel, mocking laughter of the wicked in the world; it is a laughter of relief, of justice, of witnessing the truth they held onto being vindicated. The believers will look upon the disbelievers in their state of punishment—burning, veiled, humiliated—and they will laugh.

This is not an act of petty revenge but a divinely sanctioned response that restores the moral balance. The ones who were humiliated for their faith are now honoured, and they are given the joy of seeing their oppressors receive their just deserts. This verse provides immense comfort to the oppressed faithful. It assures them that the roles will be completely reversed. The laughter of the believers is part of their bliss. It is not that they are devoid of mercy; rather, they are rejoicing in the perfection of divine justice. The disbelievers laughed in arrogant denial; the believers laugh in grateful witness to the truth of Allah’s promise.

The verse also serves as a warning to those who mock the believers today: your laughter is temporary, and it is being recorded. A day is coming when you will be the object of laughter, and that laughter will echo for eternity. The believer should not despair at the mockery they face but should look forward to that Day with patient hope, knowing that the final laugh belongs to those who held onto the truth.

=35=

The scene of the believers’ laughter is placed in its full, majestic context in verse thirty-five: “as they sit on canopied couches, looking on.” The Arabic ‘alā al-arā’iki yanẓurūn is the exact same phrase used in verse twenty-three to describe the believers’ state in Paradise. Here, it is repeated to connect the laughter with the bliss. The believers are not standing in a crowd laughing; they are reclining on their thrones of honour, under canopies, in a position of absolute security and comfort. From this elevated station, they will “look on” (yanẓurūn) at the disbelievers. The verb yanẓurūn means to gaze, to observe, to contemplate. Their gaze is not a nervous glance but a steady, regal observation. They will see the hellfire, the punishment, the humiliation of those who wronged them, and this sight will increase their gratitude for the mercy of Allah. The canopied couches emphasise the permanence of their state. They are not standing in a temporary court; they are already in their eternal abode, and they look out from Paradise to witness the fate of the wicked.

This is the ultimate vindication. The same couches that symbolise their rest and reward become the seats from which they witness the fulfillment of divine justice.

The repetition of the phrase “canopied couches” creates a structural ring in the surah, linking the description of bliss with the description of the final triumph. The believer is meant to visualise this scene: the poor, oppressed, mocked Muslim of Mecca, now seated on a throne of unimaginable beauty, looking at Abu Jahl or Abu Lahab in the Fire. It is a vision that makes every worldly sacrifice seem trivial. The verse is a powerful motivator to endure hardship with patience, knowing that the couch is prepared and the day of looking on is certain.

=36=

The sūrah concludes with the final, satisfying note of justice in verse thirty-six: “The believers will be asked, ‘Have the disbelievers not been paid back for what they used to do?’” The Arabic hal thuwwiba al-kuffāru mā kānū yaf‘alūn is a rhetorical question posed to the believers. The passive “will be asked” implies that Allah, or the angels by His command, will direct this question to the believers, drawing their attention to the perfect recompense that has been delivered. The verb thuwwiba means to be paid back, to be rewarded or requited, with a full and appropriate compensation.

The question “Have they not been paid back?” expects an affirmative answer: yes, indeed, they have been paid in full. Every wink, every laugh, every act of fraud, every denial—all of it has been compensated with exact justice.

The phrase “for what they used to do” (mā kānū yaf‘alūn) encompasses the entire catalogue of their crimes described in the surah: the defrauding in the scales, the denial of Judgment, the staining of hearts, the mockery of the believers. Nothing has been overlooked. This final question is a source of profound psychological relief for the believers. It is as if Allah is personally reassuring them that the account is settled, that their pain has been witnessed and avenged, not out of pettiness but out of perfect justice.

The question also implicitly contains a warning for any who might repeat the behaviour of the Meccan disbelievers. The sūrah, which began with “Woe to the defrauders,” ends with the confirmation that the woe has been executed, and the believers are invited to witness and affirm it. The believer, seated on the canopied couch, looking on, will answer from the depth of their heart: “Yes, they have been paid back, and all praise is due to the Lord of all worlds.”
