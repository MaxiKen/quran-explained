=1=

The sūrah opens with a devastating declaration: “Woe to the defrauders!” The word “Woe” translates the Arabic wayl, a severe expression of threat, punishment, and destruction. In the Qur’ān, wayl is used to warn of an impending, terrible chastisement. It is as if a valley of suffering in Hell is designated for these specific sinners.

The term “defrauders” translates al-muṭaffifīn, from the root ṭaffafa, which refers to something small, slight, or marginal. The muṭaffif is the one who deals in the tiny, barely noticeable fractions when measuring or weighing, but those tiny fractions are stolen from others.

This is the sin of cheating in trade, of giving less than due in a way that is easily overlooked. The sūrah is Meccan, and while its immediate context addresses the dishonest commercial practices prevalent in Mecca, the condemnation is universal and timeless. The one who shortchanges others, whether in the market, in the workplace, or in any trust, falls under this devastating “woe.” What makes the verse so powerful is that it begins with the threat without any introductory phrase. It plunges directly into the warning, showing the severity of the crime. The defrauder thinks his small manipulation goes unnoticed, but the Lord of all worlds opens a sūrah with a curse directed squarely at him. This teaches a profound lesson: social justice and honesty in trade are not peripheral matters in Islam; they are central to faith. A person may pray, fast, and perform pilgrimage, but if he habitually cheats others in his business dealings, he is inviting wayl upon himself.

The verse also implicitly answers those who deny the Judgment. The defrauder cheats because he does not truly believe he will be held to account. The surah will go on to connect the cheating with the denial of the Day of Reckoning, showing that economic injustice is ultimately a spiritual disease.

=2=

Verse two precisely defines the defrauders by describing their behaviour: “Those who take full measure when they buy from people.” The Arabic phrase is alladhīna idhā iktālū ‘alā al-nāsi yastawfūn. The verb iktālū means they measure for themselves, taking by a measuring container. When they are the recipients, when they are buying and it is their turn to receive the goods, they demand full and exact measure.

The phrase ‘alā al-nāsi indicates that this measuring is to their own advantage and at the expense of others. The word yastawfūn means they take in full, they exact their right completely, they make sure not a grain is missed. This verse paints a picture of a person with a double standard. When it is their right to receive, they are meticulous, demanding, and exacting. They ensure the scale is tipped in their favour down to the last ounce. There is no thought of generosity or leniency. This behaviour, on its own, is not necessarily criminal—people have a right to receive what they paid for. The crime is revealed in the next verse, where their behaviour when they are the givers is described.

However, this verse already hints at the selfishness and the absence of God-consciousness that characterises the defrauder. His entire mindset is built on maximising personal gain.

The use of the phrase “from people” (al-nās) generalises the victims to all of humanity, indicating that the defrauder’s greed is indiscriminate. He does not care who the buyer is; his only concern is his own profit.

This emphasis on the meticulousness of receiving serves to magnify the ugliness of the contrasting behaviour in the next verse. It sets up the psychological profile of the cheater: a person whose scales have two different standards, one for himself and one for others.

This is the essence of injustice, and it stems from a heart that does not fear the ultimate accountability.

=3=

The crime is fully exposed in verse three: “but give less when they measure or weigh for buyers.” The Arabic wa idhā kālūhum aw wazanūhum yukhsirūn uses the verbs kālū (they measure) and wazanū (they weigh) to cover all forms of trade. The key word is yukhsirūn, which means they cause loss, they diminish, they give short. When the defrauder is the seller, and it is his turn to give the goods, he tips the balance the other way. He skimps, he shaves off a little from the measure, he manipulates the scale so that the buyer receives less than what they paid for. The loss may be so small that the buyer does not notice, but it is theft nonetheless.

This is the core of taṭfīf: stealing through tiny, almost imperceptible adjustments. The contrast with the previous verse is glaring. For himself, he takes full measure; for others, he gives short.

This is the hypocritical character of the unrepentant cheater. The verse reveals that his heart is not governed by a sense of justice or by the fear of Allah, but by a raw, calculating greed. The fact that the sūrah opens with such a specific social and economic sin shows the profound concern of Islam for fair dealing. The marketplace is not a zone of moral exemption; it is a place where faith is tested and demonstrated. The Prophet (ﷺ) himself was known before his prophethood as al-Amīn, the trustworthy, in his trade dealings. He would later say that the honest trader will be with the prophets, the truthful, and the martyrs on the Day of Judgment. Conversely, the one who cheats is called out with wayl.

The verse also addresses a psychological reality: the defrauder often minimises his sin, thinking, “It’s just a little bit.” The Qur’ān calls it wayl, a tremendous destruction, because it is not about the quantity stolen but about the corruption of the soul, the violation of trust, and the denial of the coming Judgment. The entire social fabric unravels when such behaviour becomes widespread, which is why the warning is so severe.

=4=

After describing the crime, the sūrah immediately links it to its root cause in verse four: “Do such people not think that they will be resurrected?” The question is rhetorical and deeply probing.

The Arabic alā yaẓunnu ulā’ika annahum mab‘ūthūn uses the verb yaẓunnu, which can mean to think, to consider, to have an opinion, or to be certain. Here, it carries the sense of a firm conviction that should be present. The defrauders act as if they will never be brought back to life and held accountable. Their cheating is a practical denial of the resurrection. If they truly believed that they would stand before the Lord of all worlds and answer for every grain they shortchanged, they would not dare. The verse points to the direct relationship between belief in the Hereafter and ethical conduct. When belief in the resurrection weakens, corruption flourishes. When a person is certain that there is a Day of absolute justice where the tiny scales of this world will be re-weighed on the divine balance, then cheating loses its appeal. The profit gained by fraud is incredibly short-term; the punishment is eternal.

The question “Do they not think?” is a challenge to the intellect. It asks whether the defrauder has really considered the logical end of his existence. Has he thought about the graves spilling out, about standing alone with his record, about the scale that will weigh every atom of good and evil? The verse is an invitation to reflection, a call to connect the mundane act of measuring grain with the cosmic reality of the resurrection. It teaches that faith is not an abstract concept confined to the mosque; it is the invisible hand that should weigh every transaction. The defrauder’s failure to think is not a lack of intelligence but a suppression of the truth, a deliberate turning away from the obvious conclusion so that he can continue his unjust gain.

The following verses will describe the Day that he refuses to think about.

=5=

The Day that the defrauders fail to consider is now named in verse five: “for a tremendous Day.” The Arabic li-yawmin ‘aẓīm uses the adjective ‘aẓīm, which means great, tremendous, huge, awful, and of immense significance.

This is the Day of Judgment. The word “tremendous” captures both the scale of the events that will unfold and the weight of the accountability that will take place. The cheating that seemed so small and clever in the marketplace will be re-examined on a Day so vast that the heavens will split and the earth will be changed. The verse emphasises the total disparity between the petty crime and the cosmic setting of its reckoning. The defrauder tampered with a few ounces for a few coins of profit. He will face that action on the Day whose terror makes children grey-haired and whose length is fifty thousand years.

The word ‘aẓīm also conveys that the judgment of that Day is not a trivial matter. It will be conducted by the Lord of all worlds, and its outcome will be eternal bliss or eternal fire.

The use of “tremendous” serves as a direct rebuttal to the defrauder’s mindset. He was concerned with tremendous profits in this world. The verse tells him that the real tremendous thing is the Day he is ignoring. The description creates a sense of awe and fear, meant to shake the believer into rectifying his dealings and to warn the cheater of the impending disaster. The very fact that the Day is described as “tremendous” after the question about thinking implies that anyone who reflects on the nature of that Day cannot possibly continue in fraud. The tremendous Day dwarfs all worldly gains and makes them utterly worthless. The believer is called to live with the ‘aẓīm constantly in mind, letting its grandeur restructure his priorities and his conduct in even the smallest of daily transactions.

=6=

Verse six provides the defining feature of this tremendous Day: “the Day all people will stand before the Lord of all worlds.” The Arabic yawma yaqūmu al-nāsu li-Rabbi al-‘ālamīn describes a universal standing. The verb yaqūmu means to stand, to rise, and to remain standing. All of humanity, from the first to the last, will be raised from their graves and made to stand in the court of the Almighty. This standing is not a momentary one; it will last for a prolonged period while each person’s record is scrutinised, while the scales are balanced, and while the final decree is issued.

The phrase “all people” (al-nās) includes the defrauders and their victims, the powerful and the weak, the rich and the poor, all in a single row, with no distinctions of worldly rank. The title “Lord of all worlds” (Rabbi al-‘ālamīn) emphasises the absolute sovereignty of the Judge. He is the Lord of every world, every realm, every creation. His authority is not limited to a specific tribe, nation, or planet. The defrauder who acted as if he were a little lord over his own scale will now stand before the true Lord, the One who owns the very materials he weighed, the air he breathed, and the hands he used to cheat. This standing is the ultimate exposure. In the world, the cheater could hide behind his stall, manipulate in secret, and smile at his customers. On that Day, he stands exposed, his entire record laid open, with no ability to cheat the divine scale. The verse connects the specific crime of defrauding to the universal Lordship of Allah. To cheat a human being is to rebel against the Lord of that human being and to violate the trust given by the Lord of all worlds. The verse is a powerful expansion of perspective. It pulls the listener out of the narrow confines of the market and places him in the vast, terrifying court of the Day of Judgment, forcing the ultimate question: if you must stand before the Lord of all worlds, how will you weigh your actions today?

=7=

The sūrah now transitions from the general warning to the specific fate of the wicked, beginning with verse seven: “But no! The wicked are certainly bound for Sijjîn in the depths of Hell.

” The opening “But no!” (kallā) is a strong negation, as if to say, “The matter is not as they think; they will not escape accountability.” “The wicked” translates al-fujjār, the same term used in the previous sūrahs for those who break moral boundaries. They are now identified with the defrauders and those who deny the Judgment. Their destination is “Sijjîn,” a mysterious and ominous word. The verse itself explains it as being in the depths of Hell.

Linguistically, Sijjîn comes from the root sijn, meaning prison. It is a place of confinement, a narrow, oppressive dungeon within the Fire. It is the opposite of ‘Illiyūn, the elevated paradise of the righteous, which will be described later.

The phrase “certainly bound for” translates la-fī, “indeed in,” with the particle la- giving emphasis. The record of the wicked is placed in Sijjîn, meaning their deeds are stored in this lowly, confined register, and they themselves will be cast there.

The verse reveals that the defrauding and denial are not random acts; they are symptoms of a deep-rooted wickedness that has a specific, designated punishment. The mention of Sijjîn creates a sense of dread and curiosity. What is this Sijjîn? The listener’s mind is captured.

The following verse will heighten the mystery by asking a rhetorical question. The verse also establishes a clear moral geography of the Hereafter: there is an elevated paradise and a deep, deep prison. The defrauders, who thought they were cleverly climbing the ladder of wealth, are in reality descending into the deepest confinement. The stark contrast between their worldly arrogance and their eternal humiliation is a key theme of this sūrah.

=8=

The terror of Sijjîn is amplified by the rhetorical question in verse eight: “and what will make you realize what Sijjîn is?” The Arabic wa mā adrāka mā Sijjīn is the same powerful device used in the previous sūrah for the Day of Judgment. It indicates that Sijjîn is a reality so severe, so beyond normal human comprehension, that words cannot fully convey its horror.

The question is addressed to the Prophet (ﷺ) and through him to all listeners, drawing them into a state of awe and apprehension. It is as if the Qur’ān is saying, “Whatever you might imagine Sijjîn to be, the reality is far worse.

” The question also serves a rhetorical purpose: it pauses the description, allowing the dread to sink in before the answer is given. The word Sijjīn itself has been interpreted by scholars in several complementary ways. Some say it is the name of the lowest earth, the seventh and deepest level of Hell. Others say it is a rock or a well in Hell.

Yet others say it is the register or book in which the deeds of the wicked are recorded, a book that is placed in a confined, despicable place. All these meanings share the concept of extreme lowness, confinement, and disgrace.

The question “What will make you realize?” implies that no human teacher, no book, no description can fully make one realise; only witnessing it firsthand would suffice, and by then it is too late. The believer is thus urged to take the warning seriously, to live in such a way as to never have to find out what Sijjîn is. The verse is a masterpiece of psychological impact, using suspense and the limits of language to instil a deep-seated fear of the destination of the wicked, motivating the heart to flee from the sins that lead there.

=9=

The answer to the suspenseful question comes in the brief, ominous statement of verse nine: “a fate already sealed.” The Arabic kitābun marqūm literally means “a written book, inscribed, marked.” This refers to the record of the wicked that is placed in Sijjîn.

The word kitāb means a book or a written record. The word marqūm from the root raqama means something that is written in a clear, marked, and indelible manner. It is not a draft; it is a final, sealed document. Every deed of the fujjār is inscribed in this book, a record that cannot be altered, erased, or appealed. The “fate already sealed” indicates the finality of the judgment. The defrauders may have thought their clever manipulations went unrecorded, but they were being marked down with absolute precision by the honourable angel-scribes mentioned in the previous sūrah. The book in Sijjîn is the compilation of their deeds, and it testifies against them. Some scholars also interpreted kitābun marqūm as meaning that the fate of the wicked is written and decreed by Allah’s knowledge, or that Sijjîn itself is a place where their punishment is written and prescribed. The overall meaning is one of inescapable destiny. The defrauder’s life, his cheating, his denial, all of it has been sealed into a record that will determine his eternal abode. This verse is deeply sobering. It teaches that every action is being recorded, and the final “sealing” of the record at death marks the end of the opportunity to change. While alive, the book is still being written, and repentance can erase sins and replace them with good deeds. But once the book is sealed, there is no return. The sealed fate of the wicked is a powerful call to ensure that one’s own book is being filled with deeds worthy of ‘Illiyūn rather than Sijjīn.

=10=

The theme of warning returns with renewed intensity in verse ten: “Woe on that Day to the deniers.” The word “Woe” (wayl) echoes the opening of the sūrah, creating a structural ring. The defrauders were given wayl at the beginning, and now the deniers are given wayl specifically “on that Day.

” The phrase “on that Day” (yawma’idhin) anchors the threat to the Day of Judgment, the tremendous Day when people stand before the Lord of all worlds. The “deniers” (al-mukadhdhibīn) are those who persistently deny the truth of the revelation, the resurrection, and the judgment. This verse explicitly connects the social crime of defrauding with the theological crime of denial. The defrauders are also deniers, as the earlier rhetorical question indicated: they do not think they will be resurrected. Their cheating is a form of practical denial. So the woe that was announced for their fraud is now confirmed for their denial on the Day when denial becomes impossible. The verse serves as a refrain, punctuating the description of the wicked’s fate. It also shifts the temporal focus: the first wayl was a present warning, but this wayl is located on the Day itself, when the threat becomes reality. The deniers who laughed at the warnings will experience the woe firsthand.

The repetition of “woe” is a rhetorical device to keep the listener’s heart in a state of fear and reflection. It is as if the sūrah is saying: do not forget the central message; the cheater, the denier, the scoffer will all face the same devastating cry of destruction on that Day.

For the believer, the repeated “woe” is a shield, a reminder to check one’s own faith and conduct so as not to fall among those who earn this terrible exclamation of punishment.

=11=

Verse eleven defines the deniers more precisely: “those who deny Judgment Day!” The Arabic alladhīna yukadhdhibūna bi-yawmi al-dīn identifies the specific object of their denial: the Day of Recompense.

This is the same yawm al-dīn that was emphasised in the previous sūrah. The deniers reject not just any doctrine but the core reality that gives moral weight to human actions. By denying Judgment Day, they render all their worldly dealings a game without ultimate consequences.

This is the root that allows the defrauder to cheat: if there is no final court, no divine scale, no Hellfire, then the only smart thing to do is to maximise worldly gain by any means. The verse exposes the ideological foundation of corruption. The Meccan pagans did not deny the existence of Allah; many of them acknowledged a supreme Creator. But they denied the resurrection and the judgment because it imposed moral accountability on their desires and commercial practices.

The verse shows that belief in the Last Day is not an optional extra in faith; it is the pillar that upholds justice. Without it, the entire edifice of ethics collapses. The description “those who deny Judgment Day” is presented as an explanation for their state as “deniers” in the previous verse. It is as if the sūrah answers the question: what are they denying? They are denying the very Day that this sūrah has been describing, the Day of standing before the Lord of all worlds.

The verse also implies the absurdity of their position. They deny a Day whose signs are manifest in creation and whose necessity is written in the human conscience. Their denial is not based on evidence but on a willful rejection of the truth that would force them to change their fraudulent ways. The believer, upon reading this, should feel a renewed conviction in the reality of the Judgment and a renewed commitment to living each day in its light.

=12=

Verse twelve provides a profound psychological and moral analysis of the denier: “None would deny it except every evildoing transgressor.” The Arabic wa mā yukadhdhibu bihī illā kullu mu‘tadin athīm uses a structure of exclusivity. The denial of Judgment Day is not an innocent intellectual error; it is the specific symptom of a corrupted character. The verse lists two qualities: mu‘tadin, meaning a transgressor, one who goes beyond the bounds, who is aggressive and unjust; and athīm, meaning a habitual sinner, one deeply immersed in evil.

The word “every” (kullu) indicates that these traits are universal among the deniers. This is a devastating diagnosis. The reason a person denies the Day of Reckoning is that he wants to continue his transgression without facing the consequences. Denial is a cover for sin. The human intellect, left to its pure nature, recognises the need for ultimate justice and the logic of resurrection. But when a person indulges in persistent sin, he begins to hate the idea of judgment. He convinces himself it is not real so that he can enjoy his evil in peace. The verse thus flips the denier’s supposed intellectual superiority on its head. The denier prides himself on being rational, free from superstition. The Qur’ān declares that his denial is actually the product of his moral failure. He is a mu‘tad, one who crosses limits, and an athīm, one steeped in sin.

This connection between vice and disbelief is a crucial Qur’ānic theme. It teaches that a sound heart leads to sound belief, and a corrupt heart leads to the rejection of truth.

For the believer, this verse serves as a warning to guard one’s character. Persistent sin, if not repented from, can darken the heart and eventually lead to doubts and denial. It is also a call to see through the facade of the disbeliever’s intellectual objections and recognise the moral struggle underneath. The verse thus links the earlier commercial fraud to a general lifestyle of transgression, showing that all of it flows from the same poisoned well: the refusal to accept accountability.

=13=

Verse thirteen gives a concrete example of the deniers’ attitude: “Whenever Our revelations are recited to them, they say, ‘Ancient fables!’” The Arabic asāṭīru al-awwalīn means the tales, myths, or writings of the ancient people. This was a common Meccan accusation against the Qur’ān. They claimed it was nothing new but merely a collection of old stories from previous civilisations that the Prophet (ﷺ) had learned and was passing off as divine revelation.

The use of “Whenever” (idhā) indicates this was their habitual, knee-jerk reaction. Whenever the powerful verses about resurrection, judgment, Paradise, and Hell were recited, they immediately dismissed them as fairy tales. This dismissal served a dual purpose for them: it relieved them of the duty to reflect seriously on the message, and it protected their egos from admitting that an unlettered man from their own midst could bring a truth they had not discovered.

The phrase “Our revelations” (āyātunā) personalises the offense. They were not rejecting the words of a human being; they were mocking the signs of Allah. The accusation of “ancient fables” is a rejection of history as a source of wisdom. The Qur’ān frequently recounts the stories of past prophets and destroyed nations as lessons. The deniers tried to neutralise these lessons by relegating them to the status of folklore. The verse exposes the arrogance and intellectual dishonesty of the deniers. Instead of engaging with the content, they resorted to name-calling.

This is a pattern that repeats in every age: when people find the divine message too demanding, they try to discredit it with labels—“myth,” “fairy tale,” “outdated.” The believer is comforted by this verse, knowing that the Prophet faced the same mockery. The appropriate response is not to despair but to continue conveying the message, knowing that the accusation of “ancient fables” is itself an ancient fable of the deniers across time.

The next verse will reveal the true internal reason for this dismissive reaction.

=14=

The sūrah now reveals the hidden reality behind the deniers’ mockery in verse fourteen: “But no! In fact, their hearts have been stained by all the evil they used to commit!

” The opening “But no!” (kallā) again forcefully rejects their claim. The Qur’ān is not ancient fables; the real problem lies within themselves.

The Arabic word rāna means a covering, a stain, a rust that coats and envelops something. The phrase bal rāna ‘alā qulūbihim mā kānū yaksibūn means that their hearts have been covered, rusted, and stained by the sins they used to earn.

This is one of the most profound psychological insights of the Qur’ān. Sins are not just external actions; they leave a residue on the heart. Over time, as sins accumulate without repentance, this residue forms a thick layer that blocks the light of truth. The heart becomes insensitive, unable to perceive the divine message as anything other than a stale myth. The Prophet (ﷺ) elaborated on this concept in a well-known ḥadīth: “When a believer commits a sin, a black dot appears on his heart. If he repents, gives up the sin, and seeks forgiveness, his heart is polished clean. But if he increases in sin, the dot increases until it covers his entire heart. That is the rān which Allah mentions.” This verse thus explains why two people can hear the same Qur’ānic recitation and react completely differently. One heart is polished and receptive; the other is stained and repelled. The deniers’ mockery is not a sign of their intellectual superiority but of their spiritual sickness. The verse is a powerful call to self-purification. Every believer must constantly examine their heart for the stains of sin and polish it with repentance.

The phrase “all the evil they used to commit” indicates that the staining is a gradual, cumulative process. No one becomes a denier overnight; it is the result of repeated sinful choices that slowly darken the soul. This awareness should inspire a vigilant and proactive approach to spiritual hygiene, ensuring that the heart remains a clear mirror for the light of revelation.

=15=

The consequence of the heart’s staining is revealed in verse fifteen: “Undoubtedly, they will be sealed off from their Lord on that Day.” The Arabic kallā innahum ‘an Rabbihim yawma’idhin la-maḥjūbūn uses multiple emphasis particles (inna, la) to make the statement absolutely certain.

The word maḥjūbūn comes from ḥajaba, meaning to veil, to screen off, to prevent access. On the Day of Judgment, the deniers will be veiled, blocked, and prevented from seeing their Lord.

This is the ultimate deprivation. In contrast, the believers will have the supreme joy of looking upon their Lord in Paradise. The deniers, who in this world turned away from the remembrance of Allah, will find themselves turned away from His mercy and His vision on that Day.

The phrase “from their Lord” is particularly poignant. He is their Lord, their Creator, their Sustainer, but they rejected Him. Now the relationship is one of separation and veil. This verse is one of the primary scriptural proofs for the doctrine that the believers will see Allah in the Hereafter. The fact that the wicked are explicitly veiled indicates that the righteous are not veiled; they are granted the vision. The scholars of the Salaf (the pious predecessors) were unanimous on this point.

The verse also connects back to the stained hearts. The stain that covered their hearts in this world becomes a veil between them and their Lord in the next. The moral and spiritual blindness they cultivated by their sins becomes a literal screening off from the source of light and mercy.

This is the greatest loss imaginable. For the believer, this verse inspires a profound longing to be among those who are not veiled, to purify the heart so that it may one day witness the Countenance of the Most Generous. It also serves as a sobering warning against the sins that stain the heart, for those stains may become eternal barriers.

=16=

The deprivation of the vision of the Lord is not the only punishment. Verse sixteen adds: “Moreover, they will surely burn in Hell.

” The Arabic thumma innahum la-ṣālū al-jaḥīm uses thumma to indicate an additional, sequential punishment. First, they are veiled from their Lord, and then they are made to enter and burn in the blazing Fire.

The word “burn” (ṣālū) means to enter into and be roasted by the fire. It is a direct, physical punishment that accompanies the spiritual punishment of being veiled. The Fire is al-jaḥīm, the intensely hot, raging Fire.

The use of “surely” (la-) again emphasises the certainty of this outcome. There is no escape, no alternative, no last-minute reprieve. The verse makes clear that the deniers will experience both the external torment of Hell and the internal torment of separation from Allah. Neither will be lightened. This dual punishment is the just recompense for a life spent in denial and defrauding others. In the world, they sought to maximise their physical pleasure and wealth at the expense of their spiritual well-being.

In the Hereafter, they will have nothing but physical pain and spiritual deprivation. The verse also serves to further refute the deniers’ claim that the Qur’ān is ancient fables. The Fire they mocked will become their undeniable reality. The description is blunt and unadorned, leaving no room for reinterpretation.

For the believer, this verse intensifies the motivation to avoid Hell at all costs. It also provides a sense of justice: the defrauders who seemed to get away with their cheating, and the deniers who seemed to enjoy their mockery, will face a punishment that perfectly matches their crimes. The burning in Hell is the final destination for those whose hearts were stained and who refused to believe in the Day of Reckoning.

=17=

The punishment is made complete by the verbal rebuke in verse seventeen: “and then be told, ‘This is what you used to deny.’” As the deniers are burning in Hell and veiled from their Lord, they will hear these words of excruciating reproach. The phrase “this is what you used to deny” connects the present suffering directly to their past attitude. The Hellfire that they are experiencing is the very thing they mocked and called “ancient fables.” The verse uses their own denial as a weapon against them. There is no argument they can make, no appeal they can file, because they were warned repeatedly and they chose to scoff. The passive “be told” implies that angels, or Allah Himself, will deliver this statement, adding to their humiliation.

This is the ultimate psychological torment layered on top of the physical burning: the crushing regret of knowing that they could have avoided this by simply believing. In the world, they demanded proof. Now the proof is surrounding them, consuming them, and the memory of their former arrogance burns hotter than the flames.

The verse also serves a profound purpose for the living. It warns that the words we speak carelessly today—our denials, our mockery, our dismissals of the truth—may be played back to us on a Day when silence will be impossible. The believer should learn from this to guard the tongue and to never mock any aspect of the religion. The verse is also a powerful tool for da‘wah. It invites the listener to imagine that very moment, to hear those words in their mind, and to choose to believe and reform before it is too late. The surah thus completes the portrait of the wicked’s fate with this final, piercing note of regret.

The next section will shift dramatically to the opposite fate, the bliss of the virtuous, providing the full picture of divine justice.

=18=

With the same “But no!” (kallā) that introduced the wicked’s fate, the sūrah now turns to the destiny of the righteous in verse eighteen: “But no! The virtuous are certainly bound for ‘Illiyūn in elevated Gardens.” This parallel structure is deliberate and beautiful. Just as the fujjār are in Sijjīn, the abrār are in ‘Illiyūn.

The word “‘Illiyūn” comes from the root ‘alā, meaning to be high, elevated, sublime. It is the exact opposite of Sijjīn. If Sijjīn is the lowest of the low, the deep dungeon of Hell, then ‘Illiyūn is the highest of the high, the loftiest station in Paradise. The verse itself explains it as being “in elevated Gardens,” meaning the highest levels of Paradise.

The phrase “certainly bound for” (la-fī) again uses emphasis, making it as certain as the fate of the wicked. The “virtuous” (al-abrār) are the righteous, the pious, those who fulfilled their duties to Allah and to their fellow human beings. They are the opposite of the defrauders and the deniers. Instead of cheating in trade, they were honest; instead of denying the Judgment, they lived in its light; instead of staining their hearts with sin, they polished them with obedience. Their book of deeds is placed in ‘Illiyūn, a record of honour and elevation. The contrast between Sijjīn and ‘Illiyūn represents the ultimate separation of humanity based on their deeds. The believer, upon reading this, is filled with hope and aspiration. The way to ‘Illiyūn is open, and it is paved with faith, honesty, and righteousness.

The verse also serves as a powerful argument for justice: the same divine system that casts the wicked into the deepest prison elevates the virtuous to the highest honour. The structural mirroring of the sūrah teaches that every human being is heading towards one of these two destinations.

=19=

The same rhetorical device used for Sijjīn is used for ‘Illiyūn in verse nineteen: “and what will make you realize what ‘Illiyūn is?” The Arabic wa mā adrāka mā ‘Illiyūn creates a sense of wonder and majesty. Just as the horrors of Sijjīn were beyond full human comprehension, so too are the delights and honours of ‘Illiyūn beyond what any eye has seen, any ear has heard, or any heart has imagined.

The question draws the listener into a state of eager anticipation. The use of the same question for both destinations highlights the perfect balance of the sūrah. The listener is made to ponder both extremes, to feel the dread of one and the longing for the other.

The word ‘Illiyūn itself has been interpreted by scholars as the highest place in Paradise, the abode of the prophets, the martyrs, and the most righteous. Some say it is the name of the register where the deeds of the pious are recorded, kept in the highest heavens, witnessed by the angels nearest to Allah. Others say it is the seventh heaven, or directly beneath the Throne. All these interpretations converge on the idea of supreme elevation, honour, and proximity to Allah.

The question “What will make you realize?” reminds the believer that no description in this world can truly capture the reality of ‘Illiyūn. The pleasure of Paradise is a secret known only to Allah and those who enter it. This unknowability is part of the motivation; the believer strives not for a fully comprehended reward but for a reward that is promised to be beyond all expectations.

The question serves to elevate the listener’s gaze from the dusty scales of the marketplace to the sublime heights of the unseen, where the honest trader’s record rests in a place of unimaginable honour.

=20=

The parallel with the description of Sijjīn continues in verse twenty: “a fate already sealed.” The Arabic is exactly the same phrase used for the wicked: kitābun marqūm. But the context transforms its meaning. For the virtuous, the sealed book is not a record of doom but a record of honour, a document of confirmed salvation. Their deeds are written, sealed, and preserved in the highest register. The “sealing” here implies finalisation and security. It means that the reward of the abrār is guaranteed, fixed, and cannot be lost or diminished. While the sealing of the wicked’s book brought despair, the sealing of the righteous’ book brings eternal reassurance.

The word kitāb can also refer to the entire decree of their blessed fate. It is inscribed, marked, and witnessed. This verse, like its counterpart for the wicked, emphasises the precision and finality of divine justice. Every honest transaction, every act of patience, every prayer, every tear of repentance is marked in that elevated book. Nothing is lost. The defrauder’s clever manipulations were also marked, but in the book of Sijjīn. The believer should find immense comfort in this. The small, unseen acts of goodness, the secret charity, the honest word when a lie would have been profitable, the extra effort to give full measure—all of it is written in a book that resides in ‘Illiyūn. The verse serves as a powerful motivator to fill one’s book with deeds that deserve such a sublime placement. It also provides a satisfying narrative closure: the defrauder’s double standard in measuring is answered by the divine double standard in recording—a despicable record in the deep prison or an honoured record in the highest paradise.
