=41=

Having moved through the parables of light and darkness, the surah now turns the believer’s gaze outward to the entire cosmos, revealing that the very existence of all things is a continuous act of worship. The question “Do you not see…” is not about physical eyesight alone; it is an invitation to insight and reflection. Allah declares that He “is glorified by all those in the heavens and the earth.” The verb yusabbiḥu means to declare Allah’s perfection, to exalt Him above every flaw and deficiency. Every single being—angels, humans, jinn, animals, plants, and even inanimate objects—participates in this glorification, each in its own mode of being. The verse singles out “even the birds as they soar” (ṣāffāt). Birds are a daily, visible sign; when they spread their wings and glide through the sky, their very flight is a tasbīḥ. Some commentators say that the act of flight itself glorifies Allah by obeying the laws He placed in their nature. Other scholars mention that when birds cry out, their sounds are glorifications that other creatures may not understand.

The next statement, “Each ˹instinctively˺ knows their manner of prayer and glorification,” contains the Arabic phrase qad ʿalima ṣalātahu wa tasbīḥahu. Allah has taught every species its specific form of worship. The bee glorifies Allah through its honey-making, the tree through its yielding of fruit, the sun through its rising and setting. None of them deviate; they are in constant, perfect submission.

This is a profound lesson for the believer: if the entire creation is in a state of worship, then the human being, who has been given free will and intellect, should be the most eager and consistent in glorifying Allah. The verse ends with “And Allah has perfect knowledge of all they do.” No act of worship, however silent or hidden, escapes His knowledge. The hypocrites who thought their false proclamations of faith were unnoticed will be judged by the One who knows even the praises uttered by a bird in the sky.

This verse also links back to the men of light in the mosques who glorify Allah morning and evening; they are joining a cosmic chorus that never ceases. The believer who prays is not alone; he is synchronising his heart with the entire universe. This realisation instils humility and a sense of belonging to a vast, worshipping creation. The surah thus expands the sphere of mindfulness from the home and the mosque to the entire heavens and earth.

=42=

The cosmic glorification described in the previous verse is anchored in a fundamental truth: sovereignty belongs entirely to Allah. “To Allah alone belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth.

” The Arabic word mulk denotes absolute dominion, ownership, and authority. Everything in the skies and on the earth, from the soaring birds to the deep-sea darkness, is under His rule. No human king, no hypocrite’s plot, no slanderer’s scheme holds any real power independent of His will. This declaration directly challenges the arrogance of those who spread corruption, thinking they can act with impunity. If the kingdom belongs to Allah, then His laws, revealed in this surah, are the only legitimate basis for human conduct.

The verse then states, “And to Allah is the final return. ” The word maṣīr is the destination, the place of return. This short phrase redirects every worldly action to its ultimate conclusion. The believer, the hypocrite, the slanderer, the chaste woman, the fornicator—all will return to the Owner of the kingdom. This truth is both a comfort and a warning. For those wronged by slander, like ʿĀʾishah, the final return means ultimate justice.

For those who escaped worldly punishment, the final return is an appointment they cannot miss. The pairing of mulk and maṣīr is a powerful theological anchor: because the kingdom is His, the return is to Him.

The verse also serves as a transition from the general glorification of creation to the specific discussion of hypocrites and believers in the following verses. Before dissecting the behaviour of those who pretend to believe, Allah reminds the Prophet and the community that He is the Sovereign and the Judge. The hypocrites may have temporarily evaded exposure, but they are heading toward the One who owns them entirely. This verse thus reinforces the principle of accountability that underpins every legal and moral instruction in the surah.

For the believer, meditating on this verse cultivates detachment from worldly power and status. No one should envy a sinner’s temporary comfort, for the entire kingdom belongs to Allah, and He grants portions of it as a test. The truly successful are those who return to Him with a heart that glorified Him in the world, joining the universal chorus that never ceased to praise its Sovereign.

=43=

After establishing Allah’s ownership of the entire kingdom, the surah provides a breathtaking close-up of His control over one of the most common yet miraculous phenomena: rain. The rhetorical question “Do you not see…” again calls for reflective observation. “Allah gently drives the clouds” (yuzjī saḥāban) uses a verb that suggests a gentle, purposeful herding. The clouds do not move by random chance; they are driven by the command of the Almighty. Then He “joins them together,” merging scattered wisps into a unified mass, and “piling them up into masses,” stacking layer upon layer until they become thick and heavy. The observer then sees “raindrops come forth” from within them.

The word wadaq describes the clear, fresh droplets emerging from the seemingly solid cloud mass. This is a sign of resurrection, as water brings life to dead earth.

The verse then introduces a more intimidating aspect: “And He sends down from the sky mountains of clouds loaded with hail.” The clouds are described as mountains (jibāl) due to their towering size, containing hail (barad). Allah pours the hail “on whoever He wills and averts it from whoever He wills.” This reminds humanity that even a weather phenomenon is a direct instrument of divine decree; hail can destroy crops and harm, or it can be averted by mercy. The verse climaxes with “The flash of the clouds’ lightning nearly takes away eyesight.” The brilliant, blinding flash (sanā barqihi) illustrates the overwhelming power contained in the clouds, a power that could snatch away sight. All these operations—the driving, joining, piling, releasing rain, forming hail, and flashing lightning—are under the precise command of the All-Wise. This passage functions as a magnificent parable for the Quranic revelation: just as Allah drives clouds and brings down life-giving water, He drives the verses of the Quran and brings down faith into hearts, reviving dead souls. And just as He sends hail to punish or avert it in mercy, His revelation can be a cure or a source of hardness for those who reject it. The lightning nearly taking away eyesight parallels the overwhelming truth of the Quran that exposes the hypocrites’ darkness. The verse beautifully connects the physical world to the spiritual reality, reinforcing that the same Lord who controls the rain controls the guidance of hearts. The believers are thus encouraged to see the natural world as an ongoing sign of the very truths being revealed in the surah.

=44=

From the detailed portrait of cloud and rain, the surah shifts to one of the most fundamental rhythms of existence: the alternation of day and night. “Allah alternates the day and night” (yuqallibu al-layla wa-n-nahār). The verb yuqallibu means to turn, rotate, and cause to succeed one another in a perfectly balanced cycle. The day does not encroach permanently on the night, nor does the night overrun the day; each follows the other with flawless precision. This alternation is not a mechanical clockwork independent of Allah; it is He who actively flips them, moment by moment.

The verse then declares, “Surely in this is a lesson for people of insight.” The word ʿibrah (lesson) derives from crossing over; an insight that allows one to cross from the seen phenomenon to the unseen reality. The people of insight (ulī al-abṣār) are those who possess deep perception, not just physical eyes but enlightened hearts that perceive the fingerprints of the Creator. The alternation of day and night teaches multiple lessons. It demonstrates Allah’s absolute power, as no force in the universe can resist the onset of night after day. It teaches the transience of this world; just as day turns to night, power, youth, and wealth turn to weakness, old age, and poverty. It teaches the certainty of the Hereafter; the death of night followed by the resurrection of dawn is a daily proof that Allah can bring the dead back to life. It also teaches the importance of order and balance; the lives of believers should follow a rhythm of worship and halal rest, mirroring the cosmic pattern.

In the context of the surah, this verse serves as a bridge between the physical signs and the coming exposure of the hypocrites. Those with true insight recognise the divine patterns and do not demand worldly proofs for every claim; they see the alternation of night and day and trust that Allah, who manages this vast cosmos so perfectly, is also managing the affairs of the small community in Madinah. The hypocrites, on the other hand, lack this insight; they are blind to the signs and thus fail to see the light of the revelation. The verse is a call to develop inner vision, so that the soul perceives the truth as clearly as the eyes perceive the sunrise.

=45=

The signs continue to unfold, moving from the sky to the very essence of life on earth. “And Allah has created from water every living creature.

” The Arabic phrase kulla dābbatin min māʾ indicates that water is the fundamental component of all animal life. Modern science confirms that water constitutes the major portion of every living body and that life originated in water. The Quran’s statement, made in a time of no microscopes, is a profound sign in itself.

The verse then classifies creatures by their mode of movement: “Some of them crawl on their bellies,” like snakes, fish, and worms; “some walk on two legs,” like humans and birds; “and some walk on four,” like cattle and most mammals. This simple yet comprehensive classification covers the major forms of locomotion in the animal kingdom. The diversity of form emerging from a common origin—water—demonstrates the limitless creativity of the Creator.

The verse then states, “Allah creates whatever He wills.” This is a direct rebuttal to any notion that nature or evolution operates independently of Allah’s will. Every new species, every variation, every individual creature is a product of His deliberate creation. He is not bound by any pattern; He invents, diversifies, and designs according to His infinite wisdom. The conclusion, “Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything,” reassures the believer that the same Power that crafted this dazzling array of life can also bring the dead to life, can grant victory to the oppressed, and can expose the secrets of the hypocrites.

In the context of the surah, this verse serves a subtle argument: the One who created all living beings from water and gave them their diverse forms is the One who legislates the rules of modesty and chastity. Human beings, as part of the “creatures walking on two legs,” are not left without guidance; the same Creator who shaped their bodies also shaped their moral code.

Moreover, the mention of diverse forms of movement after the alternation of night and day reinforces the idea that the universe is full of purposeful variety, all pointing to the One Designer. The believer who contemplates the crawling, walking, and running creatures around him is reminded of Allah’s power and the gratitude he owes for being created in the most upright form, with a soul capable of receiving the divine light.

=46=

After the expansive cosmic signs, the surah returns to the core message: the revelation itself is the ultimate clarifying light. “We have indeed sent down revelations clarifying the truth.

” The word āyātin mubayyinātin means verses that explain, make manifest, and distinguish truth from falsehood with perfect clarity. All the proofs in the universe and the detailed laws in the surah are part of this clarification. No one can claim ignorance or confusion after this.

The verse then immediately introduces a crucial limitation: “But Allah only guides whoever He wills to the Straight Path.” This declaration balances the clarity of the message with the reality of divine will. The revelation is clear, but guidance—the actual act of embracing and following it—is a gift that Allah bestows upon whom He wills. This does not mean that Allah is arbitrary; His will is always in accordance with His perfect wisdom. He guides those who sincerely seek truth, those who humble themselves, and those whose hearts are free from arrogance and stubbornness. Conversely, He withholds guidance from those who persistently reject, mock, and turn away. The verse serves as a response to the Prophet’s grief over those who refused to believe; the clarity of the Quran does not compel hearts, because guidance is a divine affair.

This principle is crucial for understanding the following discussion of the hypocrites. They heard the same revelations, witnessed the same signs of nature, and even observed the Prophet’s character, yet they remained unguided because their hearts were diseased. Their sickness was a barrier that Allah, in His wisdom, did not override. For the sincere believers, this verse cultivates humility and gratitude; they acknowledge that their own faith is not their personal achievement but a gift of guidance from Allah. It also instills reliance on Allah; the daʿwah is to convey the clear revelations, and the results are in Allah’s hands. The “Straight Path” (ṣirāṭ mustaqīm) is the comprehensive way of life that encompasses the beliefs, laws, and ethics detailed throughout this surah—a path of purity, honesty, and submission to divine judgement. The coming verses will contrast those who are guided with those who only pretend to be, revealing the true nature of hypocrisy.

=47=

The surah now pivots to expose the inner reality of the hypocrites, whose duplicity is a major theme of the Medinan revelation. The verse opens with their verbal declaration: “We believe in Allah and the Messenger, and we obey.” These are the exact words that a sincere believer would say, and the hypocrites had mastered the language of faith. They attended the mosque, swore allegiance, and publicly affirmed the pillars.

However, their words were hollow shells. The proof of their falsehood lies in their conduct: “Then a group of them turns away soon after that.” When a matter required real sacrifice, or when the Prophet’s judgement went against their personal interests, they would withdraw, find excuses, and abandon their proclaimed obedience.

The phrase “soon after that” indicates that their reversal was not after a long period of doubt but almost immediately when their faith was tested. The Quranic diagnosis is sharp and final: “These are not true believers.” Despite their claims, the reality of faith (īmān) had not entered their hearts.

This verse establishes a fundamental principle: faith is not a mere linguistic affirmation; it is a commitment that must manifest in action, especially when it conflicts with personal desire. The hypocrites’ turning away is a direct contrast to the “men of light” described earlier, who are not distracted from Allah’s remembrance by any transaction. The historical context likely includes specific incidents where the hypocrites, led by ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy, refused to accept the Prophet’s decisions in disputes or failed to participate in expeditions after pledging to do so. The verse alerts the community that verbal Islam is not sufficient; it must be verified by consistent obedience. The wording “a group of them” shows that not all of them turned away at once; factions within the hypocrites would peel off depending on the difficulty, revealing the fragmented and insincere nature of their commitment. This serves as a permanent caution for all believers: to check their own hearts when they find themselves resisting a clear command after having professed faith, for that resistance may be a symptom of the same disease.

=48=

The previous verse spoke of general turning away; this verse provides a concrete scenario that exposes the hypocrite’s real stance. “And as soon as they are called to Allah and His Messenger so he may judge between them, a group of them turns away.” The call is to the judgement of the Quran and the Prophet’s Sunnah in a dispute. For a sincere believer, such a call is a source of relief and justice. For the hypocrite, it is a threat. If they suspected the truth was against them, they would refuse the tribunal of the Prophet and would instead seek judgement from other sources—perhaps a tribal chief, a soothsayer, or simply force.

The Arabic verb yuʿraḍū indicates an active, physical turning away with aversion, showing their disdain. This is a specific litmus test of faith. The verse indicates that they had already accepted Islam outwardly, and yet when a personal matter required submitting to divine law, they recoiled.

Historically, this was revealed concerning a dispute over water rights or a case of murder between a Jewish tribe and a group of hypocrites who sought a biased verdict. The hypocrites were willing to accept Islam as a social label, but they reserved the right to reject its rulings whenever they clashed with their interests. The verse does not say they openly rejected the Prophet’s authority; that would have exposed their disbelief. Instead, they would find pretexts to avoid the judgement—procrastination, slander against the other party, or secret agreements. Their turning away is a disease of the heart that makes them loathe submitting to a higher authority. For the believers, this verse highlights the virtue of absolute submission to divine and prophetic judgement. It is a theme that will be reinforced in verses 51 and 52. The lesson for the community is to examine whether they wholeheartedly seek the judgement of Islam in their conflicts, or whether they try to circumvent it when it is inconvenient. The turning away described here is the very antithesis of the “we hear and obey” that characterises true believers.

=49=

This verse completes the portrait of hypocrisy by revealing the flip side of their behaviour. “But if the truth is in their favour, they come to him, fully submitting.” The hypocrites are not consistently opposed to the Prophet’s judgement; they are opportunistic. When they know or strongly believe that the objective truth of the matter aligns with their desires—that they are legally in the right—they rush to the Prophet’s court. In such cases, they present themselves as model believers, “fully submitting” (mudhʿinīn), showing exaggerated obedience and respect. They want the moral and social legitimacy that comes from a prophetic verdict in their favour, while also knowing the Prophet would rule justly and they would win their case. Their submission is not out of love for Allah’s law, but out of self-interest.

The Arabic word mudhʿinīn conveys a sense of compliance that is prompt and visibly humble, but in their case, it is a performance. This exposes the essence of their disease: they do not object to the principle of divine judgement, but they only accept it when it serves them. They treat the Shariah as a tool for personal victory, not as an absolute standard of right and wrong. If the ruling goes against them, they reject the process; if it goes for them, they champion it. This selective submission is one of the clearest signs of a sick heart. The Quran, in its psychological depth, lays bare the calculations of the hypocrite, leaving them no place to hide. For the sincere, this verse is a stark warning: a person must desire the truth and justice even when it is against their own self or their loved ones. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “None of you truly believes until his desires are in accordance with what I have brought.” The hypocrite’s love of the truth is conditional; the believer’s love is unconditional. The juxtaposition of their turning away in verse 48 and their running toward in verse 49 paints a complete picture of their transactional relationship with faith. The community is being taught to identify this pattern and to guard their own souls from falling into such a state.

=50=

After exposing their contradictory behaviour, the Quran diagnoses the underlying causes through a series of rhetorical questions. “Is there a sickness in their hearts?

” The first and primary cause is maraḍ, a disease that corrupts the heart’s ability to perceive truth and love submission. This sickness includes envy of the Prophet and the believers, arrogance, and hatred of divine law. A diseased heart finds the sweet taste of obedience repulsive and the bitterness of rebellion sweet. “Or are they in doubt?

” The second possible cause is shakk, uncertainty about the truthfulness of the Prophet or the reality of the Hereafter. If a person doubts the very source of the judgement, they will not trust it. Hypocrites are characterised by perpetual wavering; they never attain the firm certainty of īmān. “Or do they fear that Allah and His Messenger will be unjust to them?

” The third cause is a suspicion of injustice. Do they think that Allah’s law or the Prophet’s ruling would oppress them? This question is almost sarcastic, because the Prophet was known as the most just of men. The mere suggestion that they might fear injustice shows a perverse misunderstanding or a projection of their own crookedness onto the straight path.

The verse then delivers the verdict: “In fact, it is they who are the true wrongdoers.” The word ẓālimūn means oppressors, those who put things out of place. Their rejection of divine judgement is not a justified fear of injustice; it is a gross act of injustice against their own souls, against the truth, and against the community. They are the ones who wrong themselves by choosing misguidance. This verse provides a comprehensive psychological profile of the hypocrite: diseased heart, wavering doubt, and paranoid suspicion. It also absolves the Prophet (ﷺ) of any imperfection in his judgement. The cure for these ailments is to seek the light described in verse 35, to purify the heart through repentance and sincere submission. The questions also serve as a mirror for every believer: when feeling resistance to a Quranic ruling, one should ask, “Is there a sickness in my heart? Am I in doubt? Do I suspect injustice in Allah’s command? ” More often than not, the problem lies within the self, not in the law.

=51=

The surah now presents the diametric opposite of the hypocritical attitude, holding up the mirror of true faith. “The only response of the true believers, when they are called to Allah and His Messenger so he may judge between them, is to say, ‘We hear and obey.’” The Arabic phrase samiʿnā wa aṭaʿnā is not a mere passive listening; it is an active, wholehearted hearing that is immediately followed by obedience. It leaves no gap between the command and the compliance. This response was the hallmark of the companions. When the verses of prohibition against alcohol, usury, or hijab were revealed, they did not ask for exceptions or delay; they immediately implemented. The verse emphasises that this is “the only response”—kāna qawl al-muʾminīn. Any other response, such as questioning, hesitating, or turning away, is incompatible with the very definition of a believer. The condition of being “called to Allah and His Messenger so he may judge between them” ties directly to the scenario of the hypocrites. The believers love the judgement of Allah; they see it as a mercy, not a burden.

The verse then declares the outcome: “It is they who will truly succeed.” Al-mufliḥūn are the successful ones who attain everything good and are saved from everything evil.

The word “truly” (ulāʾika hum) emphasises exclusivity: only those with this attitude are the real success stories, regardless of their worldly status. The hypocrites may think they are winning by escaping unfavourable rulings, but they are the ultimate losers.

This verse also links back to the opening of the surah, which stated that the chapter was revealed so that you may be mindful. “Hearing and obeying” is the practical manifestation of mindfulness. The verse provides a constant self-check for every believer: when a verse of the Quran or an authentic hadith is presented, is the heart’s immediate reaction “we hear and obey,” or does it produce excuses and resistance? The success promised here is both in this world—a life of peace, justice, and dignity—and in the Hereafter—eternal Paradise. This verse, simple yet profound, is one of the core definitions of faith in the Quran.

=52=

Expanding on the definition of success, this verse clarifies that the “hearing and obeying” is part of a broader spiritual station. “For whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger, and fears Allah and is mindful of Him…” The verse combines three elements: ṭāʿah (obedience), khashyah (fear), and taqwā (mindfulness/God-consciousness). Obedience is the outward action; fear (khashyah) is an inner awe based on knowledge of Allah’s greatness, which prevents one from disobedience; and taqwā is the comprehensive state of guarding oneself against Allah’s displeasure by fulfilling commands and avoiding prohibitions.

The pairing shows that outward compliance without inner reverence can degenerate into mechanical or hypocritical behaviour, while inner reverence without outward action is meaningless. The true believer’s obedience is infused with a profound consciousness of Allah. The result is “then it is they who will truly triumph.

” The word fāʾizūn denotes those who obtain the ultimate prize. It is a different word from mufliḥūn (successful) used in the previous verse, but similar in meaning; triumph implies winning the greatest reward and escaping the greatest loss. The verse answers the unspoken question: “Why should we obey in every matter?” Because this is the path to eternal triumph. The hypocrites seek triumph through deceit and evasion, but this verse guarantees that real triumph is only in this comprehensive obedience rooted in God-consciousness.

The mention of fearing Allah adds an important dimension: the believers are not merely obeying a set of rules; they have a living, emotional relationship with Allah characterised by awe. This khashyah is what makes the believer hasten to the judgement of Allah and His Messenger rather than turn away; they fear the consequences of disobedience more than the loss of worldly gain.

The verse also subtly echoes the opening of the surah (“so that you may be mindful”) and the many references to taqwā throughout the chapter. It provides a complete formula for success: hearing and obeying outwardly, fearing and being mindful inwardly.

This is the station of the men of light, those who are not distracted by commerce, and those who lower their gaze. The triumph is absolute and final, encompassing the illumination of the grave, the ease of the reckoning, and the entrance into Paradise.

=53=

The surah now returns to the hypocrites, exposing their exaggerated oaths as a cover for their empty promises. “They swear by Allah their most solemn oaths…” The hypocrites would use the heaviest oaths, invoking Allah’s name with emphasis, to convince the Prophet (ﷺ) of their sincerity. Their oath was that if the Prophet were to command them to march forth (for jihad or any difficult task), they would certainly do so without hesitation. This was likely in the context of the Battle of Tabuk or other expeditions where the hypocrites made excuses to stay behind. Their mouths overflowed with pledges, but their hearts were absent. Allah instructs the Prophet to reply: “Say, ‘You do not have to swear; your obedience is well known!’” This response is layered. On the surface, it means that their past behaviour already reveals the truth, making their oaths unnecessary. Their obedience is “well known”—known to be false, or known to be conditional and weak.

The phrase can also be understood as “your obedience is known to be merely verbal,” a well-known fact among the believers. There is no need to add perjury to hypocrisy. The verse closes with “Surely Allah is All-Aware of what you do.

” This is the ultimate check. Human beings might be impressed by the solemn oaths, but Allah knows the hidden reality of their deeds. He knows that when the time comes, they will find excuses and desert. This verse condemns the culture of making grand promises without follow-through, a behaviour that erodes trust in any community. The believers are taught that actions speak louder than oaths; a sincere person’s record of obedience is their testimony, not their verbal flourishes. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “The signs of a hypocrite are three: when he speaks he lies, when he promises he breaks it, and when he is entrusted he betrays.” The oaths in this verse are the “speaking” part, and their subsequent turning away is the broken promise.

The verse also serves as a warning against using religious language to mask a lack of real commitment. In the modern context, one may proclaim loyalty to Islamic values in grand terms, but the test is in the actual sacrifice of time, wealth, and desires for the sake of Allah.

The verse teaches that Allah is not impressed by our declarations but by the reality of our deeds, which are fully transparent to Him.

=54=

After exposing the hypocrites’ empty oaths, the Prophet is given a clear, balanced declaration to make. “Say, ‘Obey Allah and obey the Messenger.’” This concise command reaffirms the central theme. Obedience to the Messenger is obedience to Allah, for he does not speak of his own desire. The command is absolute, leaving no room for selective submission.

The verse then immediately addresses the possibility of rejection: “But if you turn away, then he is only responsible for his duty and you are responsible for yours.” The Prophet’s duty is to convey the message clearly and to lead by example. If people reject after that, the harm falls on themselves. They will bear the burden of their own sins. This removes any pressure from the Prophet; his task is not to compel hearts but to deliver.

This principle is a source of comfort for all callers to truth: guidance is from Allah, and the caller is only accountable for clear communication. The verse continues, “And if you obey him, you will be rightly guided.

” This is the positive promise. Obedience to the Messenger is the exclusive path to guidance (ihtidāʾ). There is no alternative route; anyone seeking guidance must submit to his teachings.

The final sentence reiterates the limit of the Messenger’s duty: “The Messenger’s duty is only to deliver the message clearly.” The word balāgh mubīn is a clear, unambiguous delivery that leaves no excuse for misunderstanding. The Prophet fulfilled this to perfection. This verse serves as a summary of the entire discussion on the response to divine judgement. The hypocrites wanted to escape the Prophet’s authority; the verse tells them that turning away only harms themselves, while obedience is the sole key to guidance. The clarity of the message leaves them with no valid excuse. The believers are reminded that their salvation is tied to a single condition: obeying the Messenger.

The verse also establishes the principle of individual responsibility; no soul can blame another for its misguidance when the message was made crystal clear. The surah, having established the light of faith and the darkness of hypocrisy, now moves toward a grand promise for those who combine faith and good deeds.

=55=

After the tests of slander, the threats of the hypocrites, and the hardships of Madinah, Allah reveals a magnificent promise of empowerment that resonates with hope and conditions. “Allah has promised those of you who believe and do good…” The promise is for those who combine īmān and righteous action (ṣāliḥāt).

The first component of the promise is “He will certainly make them successors in the land, as He did with those before them.” The word yastakhlifannahum means to grant them authority, stewardship, and succession.

This is a promise of political and social empowerment—that the oppressed, faithful community will eventually become the leaders of the land, just as Allah granted dominion to Prophet Dāwūd and Sulaymān and the righteous nations before. The second component: “and will surely establish for them their faith which He has chosen for them.” This means He will make Islam firm and stable, removing the fear of its eradication. The believers will be able to practice their religion openly and comprehensively.

The third component: “and will indeed change their fear into security.” The state of constant anxiety, persecution, and threat that the early Muslims endured would be replaced by a state of safety and tranquillity. This was fulfilled remarkably within the Prophet’s lifetime, as Makkah was conquered, the Arabian Peninsula submitted to Islam, and the believers lived in security. The condition for this threefold promise is embedded in the clause: “provided that they worship Me, associating nothing with Me. ” The empowerment is not an unconditional entitlement; it hinges on pure tawḥīd. As soon as the community turns to shirk or abandons sincere worship, the promise is forfeited.

The verse ends with a warning: “But whoever disbelieves after this promise, it is they who will be the rebellious.” Those who taste the blessing of security and empowerment and then turn to disbelief are the ultimate ingrates and transgressors (fāsiqūn). This verse is a charter for the Muslim ummah: it teaches that the path to collective strength and stability is faith, righteous deeds, and pure monotheism. It also serves as an explanation for the decline of Muslim societies throughout history; when shirk and sins crept in, the divine khilāfah was weakened. The verse connects perfectly to the surah’s themes; the men of light, who are chaste and obedient, are the ones who will be granted this succession. The slanderers and hypocrites are excluded. It provides both a vision and a responsibility.

=56=

After the grand promise of succession, the surah immediately prescribes the practical pillars that maintain that station. “Moreover, establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and obey the Messenger…” These three commands encapsulate the entirety of the religion: the rights of Allah (prayer), the rights of creation (zakāh), and the comprehensive guidance of the Prophet which covers all aspects of life.

The word aqīmū (establish) for prayer indicates performing it perfectly, consistently, and with outward and inward propriety. Zakāh purifies wealth and soul. Obedience to the Messenger is the overarching principle that ensures the implementation of all other divine commands. The three are presented as the path to the mercy mentioned in the promise: “so you may be shown mercy.” The mercy (raḥmah) here encompasses the fulfilment of the promise—succession, establishment of faith, and security—as well as the ultimate mercy of Paradise. The verse is a summary of the practical program that follows from sincere faith. It also connects to the earlier description of the men of light who are not distracted from prayer and zakāh by commerce (verse 37). The hypocrites are those who are distracted and who fail to establish these pillars.

Thus, verse 55 provides the promise, and verse 56 provides the methodology. The believers cannot simply claim the promise; they must actively uphold the deeds that attract divine mercy.

The sequence also teaches that political and social empowerment is not an end in itself; it is a means to establish prayer and zakāh across the land. When a community becomes the successor in the land, its first duty is to ensure the houses of worship are raised and the pillars of Islam are established, as described in verse 36. This verse is a timeless reminder that the strength and survival of the Muslim ummah depend not on numbers or technology, but on the sincere and correct performance of these fundamentals.

=57=

After addressing the believers with hope and instruction, the surah turns to the disbelievers with a severe warning that also comforts the Prophet. “Do not think O Prophet that the disbelievers can escape in the land.” The verb yuʿjizū means to frustrate, to render helpless, or to escape the power of. The Prophet is told not to imagine that the disbelievers can ever escape Allah’s grip or thwart His plan, no matter how powerful they appear. Their seeming freedom of movement and their temporary worldly success are not a sign of their escape from divine justice. The verse follows the promise of succession to reassure the believers that the present power of the disbelievers is an illusion destined to collapse. The statement “The Fire will be their home” asserts the ultimate reality. Jahanam is their permanent abode.

The phrase “Indeed, what an evil destination!” (biʾsa al-maṣīr) is a forceful exclamation, emphasising the horror of their end. This verse balances the promise of security for the believers by declaring the inevitable punishment for the rejecters.

In the context of the surah, this applies to the ringleaders of slander, the hypocrites who obstruct justice, and all those who wage war against the light of faith. While the believers might suffer temporarily, the final outcome is never in doubt. The command “do not think” also addresses the psychological strain on the Prophet and the believers; it is natural to wonder why the wicked seem to prosper. This verse cuts through that wonder by revealing the end state. The disbelievers may escape human justice, but they will never escape Allah. This assurance allows the believers to remain steadfast, knowing that their patience is not in vain and that the oppressors are heading toward an inevitable, terrible destination.

The verse also links back to the mirage parable in verse 39; the disbelievers’ deeds are a mirage, and their final reality is the Fire. The surah thus maintains the balance of hope and fear, addressing both the believers and their antagonists, leaving no room for complacency or despair.

=58=

The surah returns to the theme of household privacy, now adding specific details for servants and children within the home. The address “O believers” again frames these rules as a mark of faith. The verse commands that “those bondspeople in your possession and those of you who are still under age” must seek permission to enter at three specific times. The bondspeople include male and female slaves, and the under-age children are those who have not yet reached puberty. The three times are: “before dawn prayer”—when people are sleeping or getting up in their night clothes; “when you take off your outer clothes at noon”—the midday siesta time when people may rest and dress lightly; and “after the late evening prayer”—the night time when people go to sleep. These are called “three times of privacy for you” (ʿawrāt).

The word ʿawrah here means a time when people are likely to be in a state of undress or intimate privacy. During these periods, even young children and household servants must not enter without permission, so that they do not inadvertently see something inappropriate.

The verse then provides relief: “Other than these times, there is no blame on you or them to move freely, attending to one another.” Outside these three periods, children and servants can come and go without permission, as it would be overly burdensome to require it all day.

The phrase “attending to one another” (ṭawwāfūna ʿalaykum) reflects the natural, frequent interactions within a household. The verse concludes, “This is how Allah makes the revelations clear to you, for Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise. ” The detailed instruction is a manifestation of divine knowledge and wisdom; Allah knows the needs of human modesty and the practical demands of daily life, so He legislates accordingly. This verse demonstrates Islam’s comprehensive care for privacy, extending it even to the inner workings of a household, and training children from a young age to respect boundaries. It also implicitly safeguards the dignity of the servants. The rules are not meant to isolate but to ensure that the home remains a haven of modesty, aligning with the earlier command to lower gazes and the rules of hijab.

=59=

The previous verse gave a concession for young children to enter without permission outside the three times of privacy. This verse introduces the natural escalation: puberty. “And when your children reach the age of puberty, let them seek permission to come in, as their seniors do.” Once a child becomes bāligh, they are considered full adults in terms of legal responsibility and potential awareness of intimate matters.

Consequently, they must seek permission at all times, not just during the three privacy periods. The phrase “as their seniors do” refers to the general rule established in verse 27 for all believers: do not enter any house until you have asked for permission and greeted. The adolescents now join the adult world of full istiʾdhān. This rule applies even inside their own family home; they cannot simply walk into their parents’ or siblings’ rooms unannounced at any time. The training in childhood during the three times was a preparation for this comprehensive rule. The verse repeats the refrain, “This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, for Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise,” emphasising that this progression is part of the divine educational curriculum. The All-Knowing understands the developmental stages of children; a pre-pubescent child can be given leniency, but the post-pubescent youth must learn full respect for privacy. The All-Wise places these rules to prevent any awkward situations that could lead to temptation or family discomfort. This verse reinforces the surah’s holistic approach to modesty: it begins with the gaze, moves to dress, regulates public and private entry, and now trains the next generation in these habits from childhood through adolescence. It cultivates a society where the sanctity of personal space is woven into the fabric of upbringing.

The repetition of “Allah makes His revelations clear” also reminds the believers that these are not trivial customs; they are divine revelations equal in importance to the creedal and legal verses, deserving of full attention and implementation.

=60=

The surah now addresses a specific demographic, providing a relaxation within the bounds of modesty, demonstrating the practicality and mercy of Islamic law. “As for elderly women past the age of marriage…” The Arabic word qawāʿid refers to women who are advanced in age and no longer menstruate, and who have lost the desire for marriage and the physical attractiveness that normally draws attention. For such women, “there is no blame on them if they take off their outer garments, without revealing their adornments.” The outer garments (thiyāb) refer to the overcoat like the jilbāb or abaya, which is worn over the regular clothes when going out or in the presence of non-maḥrams. The concession allows them to lay aside this outer covering within the house or in similar safe contexts, because the natural attraction that the hijab rules protect against is no longer present in their case.

However, the relaxation comes with a strict condition: “without revealing their adornments.” They may remove the outer layer, but they must not display their hidden adornments (zīnah), meaning the hair, neck, arms, or any other beauty that the general rule of hijab conceals. Their underclothes must still be modest and covering. The removal is only of the additional outer wrapping, not a licence to dress revealingly.

The verse then adds, “But it is better for them if they avoid this altogether.” Even though it is permissible, exercising the full hijab is more virtuous and safer, as it guards against any inadvertent exposure and maintains the highest standard of modesty. This encouragement reflects the principle that the closer one adheres to the ideal, the purer for the soul.

The verse concludes, “And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.” He hears the discussions about the ruling and knows the intentions of the elderly women and the circumstances of each. This assures that the law is based on perfect knowledge of human nature and society. This verse beautifully balances ease and idealism; Islam does not burden the old with what is irrelevant to their state, yet it elevates the spirit by recommending a higher path. It also teaches younger women that the hijab is fundamentally linked to the reality of attraction and social protection, and when that reason diminishes naturally, a partial concession is given, while the overall spirit of modesty remains.
