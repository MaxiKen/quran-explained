=61=

This verse concludes the social legislation of the surah by removing a number of burdensome scruples that had arisen among some early Muslims, while simultaneously reinforcing the etiquettes of mutual love and greeting. The opening statement, “There is no restriction on the blind, or the disabled, or the sick,” addresses a misunderstanding that had caused some believers to avoid eating with people who had physical limitations. Some felt that a blind person might not be able to choose the best food, that a disabled person might struggle and cause discomfort, or that a sick person might have a contagious illness or an offensive odour. Others hesitated out of a misplaced sense of piety, thinking that sharing a meal with someone who could not earn equally might involve consuming charity inadvertently. Allah removes all such restrictions with the word ḥaraj, meaning no sin, no blame, and no difficulty. The community is taught that disability and illness do not diminish human dignity, and that social inclusion at the dining table is part of the spirit of Islam.

The verse then extends the removal of hardship to the act of eating from specific houses: “Nor on yourselves if you eat from your homes, or the homes of your fathers…” and the list continues to include mothers, brothers, sisters, paternal and maternal uncles and aunts, homes entrusted to one’s care, and the homes of friends. In pre-Islamic custom and even into the early Islamic period, some people felt they could not eat at the houses of relatives or friends unless a formal, explicit invitation was given, and that eating without invitation was somehow a violation or a burden on the host.

The verse clarifies that these close relationships implicitly carry permission, provided one knows the host does not mind. The mention of “the homes in your trust” refers to properties one manages on behalf of others, such as an orphan’s estate or a business partner’s premises; one may eat reasonably from those homes without it being misappropriation. The inclusion of “the homes of your friends” is particularly beautiful, as it elevates sincere friendship to a bond akin to kinship, where formalities can be relaxed out of mutual love.

However, scholars caution that this leniency is conditioned on the absence of any known objection from the owner and on moderation. Then the verse addresses another scruple: “There is no blame on you eating together or separately.” Some early Muslims felt that eating alone might be sinful because they were not sharing, while others felt that eating in a group when someone was left out might be blameworthy. The verse liberates them from such unnecessary worries; both are permissible. Having cleared these social anxieties, the verse returns to the overarching theme of manners when entering homes: “However, when you enter houses, greet one another with a greeting of peace from Allah, blessed and good. ” This is the Islamic salutation, as-salāmu ʿalaykum, a greeting of peace that is described as “from Allah”—divinely taught—and as “blessed and good,” bringing barakah and wholesomeness into the interaction. The command is to greet one another, meaning even when entering your own home, greet your family so that peace descends upon the dwelling.

The verse concludes with the familiar refrain, “This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, so perhaps you will understand,” linking these social norms to deep spiritual comprehension. The surah is teaching that true understanding of the religion includes not just the grand penal laws but also the seemingly small courtesies of daily life—sharing food, removing awkwardness, and spreading the greeting of peace. In a contemporary context, this verse calls for inclusive communities where those with disabilities are not shunned, where relatives and friends feel welcome without artificial barriers, and where the greeting of peace is a living practice that knits hearts together. It removes the false guilt that can creep into social interactions and replaces it with a secure, divinely guided framework of mutual love and dignity.

The phrase “so perhaps you will understand” is the final note, urging the believer to see that these instructions are not trivial; they are windows into the wisdom and mercy of the All-Knowing Lord, who cares for every detail of human life.

=62=

After legislating for household and social interactions, the surah returns to the relationship between the community and its leader, defining one of the key signs of genuine faith. “The true believers are only those who believe in Allah and His Messenger, and when they are with him on a public matter, they do not leave without his permission.

” The word “public matter” translates amrin jāmiʿ, which refers to a collective affair that gathers the believers—such as a consultative meeting, the Friday prayer, a military expedition, or any communal undertaking led by the Prophet (ﷺ). In such a setting, a true believer does not simply walk away when he feels like it. His presence is part of the collective strength, and his departure could weaken the unity or leave a gap. The demand for permission is not merely a rule of discipline; it is an expression of respect for the Prophet and recognition of the importance of communal cohesion. The hypocrites, as described earlier in the surah, used to slip away secretly, showing their contempt for the community’s affairs.

By contrast, the sincere believers stay until the matter is concluded, and if they have a legitimate need to leave, they formally request permission. The verse then affirms: “Indeed, those who ask your permission O Prophet are the ones who truly believe in Allah and His Messenger.” This beautifully equates the act of properly seeking leave with the truth of faith. It teaches that the small courtesies of collective life are directly tied to the health of one’s īmān. The hypocrite leaves without a word; the believer approaches with humility and asks. This etiquette was not limited to the Prophet’s lifetime; scholars apply it to any legitimate communal leader or gathering where a person’s presence is expected and departure could be disruptive.

The next instruction is directed to the Prophet: “So when they ask your permission for a private matter, grant permission to whoever you wish and ask Allah’s forgiveness for them.” This shows the balance in Islamic leadership. The leader has the authority to accept or decline the request depending on the circumstances and the sincerity of the excuse. He is not required to grant permission to everyone, because the public interest may require certain individuals to remain.

Yet, if he grants leave to someone with a genuine personal need, he should also seek Allah’s forgiveness for that person—perhaps for any shortcoming in not staying, or simply as a blessing. The verse closes with “Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful,” reminding both the leader and the followers that the ultimate arbiter of intentions is Allah, and His mercy covers those who act with sincerity. In modern terms, this verse inculcates a profound sense of responsibility toward communal obligations. Attending a gathering for a good cause, a meeting for community work, or a study circle carries a weight of commitment. Leaving without due notice is not just a breach of etiquette; it betrays a weakness in faith.

The verse also teaches leaders to be approachable, to hear excuses, and to be a source of forgiveness and prayer for their people, not harsh taskmasters. The ethos of the entire surah—mindfulness, obedience, mutual respect—culminates in this picture of a disciplined yet merciful community, bound by the thread of faith in Allah and His Messenger.

=63=

This verse delivers one of the sternest warnings of the surah, addressing a specific form of disrespect toward the Prophet (ﷺ) that reveals the inner condition of hearts. “Do not treat the Messenger’s summons to you as lightly as your summons to one another.

” The Arabic construction prohibits making the call of the Messenger equal to the ordinary calls exchanged between people. When the Prophet called the believers for a collective matter—whether prayer, consultation, or battle—that call was not a casual invitation that one could ignore or postpone as one might do with a friend’s invitation. It was a divine summons, an obligation that required immediate and full response. Some companions, or more likely the hypocrites among them, would respond with laxity, saying things like “I’ll come later” or “Let me finish my business,” treating the Prophet’s call as if it were merely a social appointment. Others would use informal language to address the Prophet, calling him by name without due respect. The verse puts an end to this attitude, elevating the status of the prophetic command to its rightful, supreme place.

The next sentence exposes the hidden behaviour of the hypocrites: “Allah certainly knows those of you who slip away, hiding behind others.” They would try to sneak out of the gathering without being noticed, using the crowd as cover, thinking they could avoid the Prophet’s sight and the community’s notice. But the verse reminds them—and all—that Allah sees them perfectly.

The word yaʿlamu (knows) is in the present tense, indicating continuous, encompassing knowledge. No stealthy exit escapes Him.

The verse then delivers a direct and terrifying warning: “So let those who disobey his orders beware, for an affliction may befall them, or a painful torment may overtake them.” The “affliction” (fitnah) can refer to a trial in this world—such as hypocrisy taking root in the heart, civil strife, or defeat—or it can refer to a punishment in the grave and the Hereafter. The “painful torment” (ʿadhāb alīm) unmistakably points to the Hellfire.

This warning is not merely for the hypocrites; it applies to anyone who deliberately disregards the Prophet’s commands and the Shariah he brought. The orders referred to include his religious commands as well as his administrative directives as the head of the community. The severity of the warning underlines the central message of the surah: submission to the Messenger is not optional; it is the very definition of faith. Scholars explain that this verse also protects the community from anarchy. If everyone treated the leader’s call as optional, the collective unity would collapse. For later generations, the “Messenger’s summons” is the call of the Quran and the authentic Sunnah. Treating these with casual indifference, or prioritizing personal opinions and cultural norms over clear prophetic guidance, falls under the spirit of this warning. The verse is a powerful call to examine one’s attitude toward religious obligations: do we respond to the adhān with the same seriousness as we respond to a business call? Do we read a hadith and immediately implement it, or do we slip away behind excuses? The warning of fitnah is particularly poignant; the consequence of persistent, casual disobedience can be a hardening of the heart and a deprivation of guidance, a living death far worse than physical calamity. This verse, like the lightning flash mentioned earlier, nearly takes away eyesight with its intensity, and it stands as a permanent sentinel guarding the boundary between faith and hypocrisy.

=64=

The surah that began with the revelation of a light-filled book now reaches its conclusion with a majestic seal that reiterates the absolute sovereignty and knowledge of Allah. “Surely to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth.

” This is the foundational truth upon which every law and command in the surah is built. Because the entire cosmos is His property, He alone has the right to command, prohibit, reward, and punish. The slanderers, the fornicators, the hypocrites, and those who violate privacy—all are living in a kingdom that belongs to the One whose rules they are breaking. This short sentence reorients the heart, reminding the believer that nothing in existence is outside Allah’s dominion.

The verse then turns to a penetrating statement: “He knows well what you stand for.” The Arabic phrase mā antum ʿalayhi can mean “the state you are upon” or “the path you are following. ” Allah knows the exact condition of every soul—whether it is standing on faith and obedience, or on hypocrisy and sin.

The word “knows” here is ʿalīm, indicating constant and complete awareness of inner convictions and outward deeds. After an entire surah of detailed laws, this verse tells the believers and hypocrites alike that their true position is not hidden from the Lord. He knows who lowered his gaze out of conviction and who only pretended; He knows who forgave Mistah out of love for divine forgiveness and who held a grudge; He knows who entered homes with permission and who spied. Then the verse points to the Day of Accountability: “And on the Day all will be returned to Him, He will inform them of what they did.” The return (yurjaʿūna) is inescapable. On that Day, all the concealed actions—the secret glances, the whispered slander, the sneaked departures—will be brought to light. Allah will inform each person of their deeds, not as a recounting from an external scroll but as a direct, undeniable confrontation with their own record, made by the One who witnessed everything.

The final sentence seals the surah with the comprehensive divine name: “For Allah has perfect knowledge of all things.” This attribute, ʿalīm bi-kulli shayʾ, encompasses every subject discussed in Sūrah An-Nūr—the rulings of fornication, the testimonies of liʿān, the intricacies of light, the darkness of the deep sea, the flight of birds, the alternation of night and day, and the secrets of human hearts. Nothing, not even an atom’s weight, escapes His knowledge. This conclusion provides immense comfort to the victims of injustice, such as ʿĀʾishah, the falsely accused, and those whose honour was attacked: their innocence is known to the All-Knowing, and He will vindicate them on the Day of Return. It also serves as a final, terrifying warning to the unrepentant slanderers and hypocrites: the secrets they thought were buried will be fully exposed by the One who knows all things. The surah thus ends exactly where it began—with the reminder that this is a revealed chapter, made obligatory, containing clear signs, so that you may be mindful. Mindfulness of Allah’s ownership, knowledge, and the inevitable return is the thread that ties together every verse from the punishment of fornication to the greeting of peace. The listener is left in a state of reflection, awe, and a renewed commitment to live under the watchful eye of the All-Knowing Lord, whose light illuminates the heavens and the earth, and whose mercy embraces those who turn to Him in sincerity.
