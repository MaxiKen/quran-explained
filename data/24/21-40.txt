=21=

The surah now moves from the painful incident of the slander to a universal warning about the root cause of such sins: the subtle influence of Satan. The address “O believers” draws the faithful back to their core identity and reminds them that the battle against gossip, slander, and immorality is ultimately a spiritual battle against an unseen enemy. The command “Do not follow the footsteps of Satan” uses a profoundly insightful image. Satan does not usually lead a person into a massive sin in one leap; he takes him step by step, footprint by footprint. A glance here, a whisper there, an entertained suspicion, a half-spoken word, a repeated rumour—each step seems small and harmless until the person finds himself deep in a major transgression.

This is exactly what happened during the Ifk; otherwise good believers followed the footsteps of idle talk, and step by step they became part of a heinous slander. The Prophet (ﷺ) explained this reality in a hadith: “Beware of the small sins, for they gather upon a person until they destroy him,” and he likened it to people who each drop a small stick until they collect a large fire.

Thus, the believer must be vigilant about the first step, blocking Satan’s entrance before the door is open. The verse then clarifies why Satan must be avoided: “Whoever follows Satan’s footsteps, then ˹let them know that˺ he surely bids ˹all to˺ immorality and wickedness.

” The word fāḥishah denotes extreme indecency, and munkar is every evil that sound nature and divine law reject. Satan’s ultimate goal is to corrupt society through sexual immorality and to tear apart the honour of believers through slander, because these destroy families and communities. He beautifies these vices and makes them appear trivial or even liberating. When a believer follows these footsteps, he is being shepherded toward the worst outcomes. Then the verse strikes a note of deep humility and gratitude: “Had it not been for Allah’s grace and mercy upon you, none of you would have ever been purified.

” The word zakā means to purify and grow. This acknowledges that even the best of believers cannot purify themselves by their own power. Without Allah’s grace in sending revelation, providing clear commands, and accepting repentance, all would remain spiritually impure. The declaration “But Allah purifies whoever He wills” affirms that purification is a divine gift. Allah chooses to purify those who turn to Him, providing them the means and the will to avoid Satan’s footsteps. This prevents arrogance in the righteous and despair in the sinner.

The verse concludes “And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing,” meaning He hears all the whispers of gossip, the covert slander, and the secret plots of Satan, and He knows the hidden intentions of hearts. No scheme escapes Him, and His legislation is perfectly informed. This verse thus bridges the specific trial of the Ifk to the ongoing spiritual struggle, teaching the believers never to underestimate small sins and to rely solely on Allah’s grace for purity.

=22=

This verse descended as a direct response to an emotional reaction from one of the noblest companions, Abū Bakr aṣ-Ṣiddīq. After the slander against his daughter ʿĀʾishah, it came to light that his relative Mistah ibn Uthātha, a poor emigrant whom Abū Bakr had been supporting financially, had participated in spreading the rumour. Deeply hurt, Abū Bakr swore an oath that he would never spend on Mistah again. The verse does not condemn the feeling of hurt—it is natural—but it redirects the response toward something higher. The address “Do not let the people of virtue and affluence among you swear to suspend donations” uses the specific description “people of virtue and affluence” as a gentle recognition of their goodness and status, while forbidding them from using their oaths to cut off charity.

The phrase “to their relatives, the needy, and the emigrants in the cause of Allah” shows that the aid was for multiple categories, all deserving. The command then comes: “Let them pardon and forgive.

” The Arabic word yaʿfū means to efface the fault, and yaṣfaḥū means to turn the page and not even hold a grudge in the heart. This is the highest moral standard—not just continuing the financial support (which is legally revocable), but doing so with a cleansed heart, as if the offence never happened. Immediately, Allah appeals to the deepest longing of every believer: “Do you not love to be forgiven by Allah?” This rhetorical question links human forgiveness to divine forgiveness. If Abū Bakr wished to be among those forgiven by Allah, he must forgive the one who wronged him. The hadith records that upon hearing this, Abū Bakr wept and said, “Yes, by Allah, I love that Allah forgives me.” He restored the charity to Mistah and increased it. The verse closes with “And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful,” promising that those who emulate these attributes will receive them in full. The lessons are immense: personal hurt, even legitimate pain, must not lead to a permanent break in kinship ties or the withholding of good. The incident also shows that the righteous can slip, but the community should embrace their repentance. This verse stands as an eternal call to transcend the ego, to answer evil with good, and to recognise that unforgiveness blocks the very mercy we all desperately need from Allah. It is a shining example of how Quranic ethics rebuild relationships torn by slander, and it remains a guiding light in every family and community conflict.

=23=

After the specific instruction to forgive, the surah returns to the divine verdict on slanderers, now broadening it into a severe universal warning. The verse opens with “Surely those who accuse…” emphasising the certainty of the judgement. The accusation here is of fāḥishah, sexual indecency, and the target is defined by three powerful adjectives: “chaste” (muḥṣanāt), meaning they have guarded their private parts and are known for purity; “unsuspecting” (ghāfilāt), meaning their minds are so innocent of evil that they do not even imagine such filthy things, let alone commit them; and “believing” (mu’mināt), adding the honour of faith. The combination depicts women who are at the pinnacle of moral and spiritual integrity, completely oblivious to the depravity projected onto them. To slander such a woman is to attack the very fabric of a believing society. The verdict is “cursed in this life and the Hereafter.” A curse (laʿnah) means expulsion from Allah’s mercy and invoking divine rejection. That it applies in this life indicates the legal punishment of eighty lashes and the permanent loss of credibility as a witness, a social and spiritual death. The curse extends to the Hereafter, meaning they will be driven away from Allah’s mercy on the Day of Judgement.

The phrase “And they will suffer a tremendous punishment” adds that in addition to the curse, there is a specific painful torment awaiting them, which scholars interpret as the Hellfire unless they repent. Some may think that mere words cannot warrant such severity, but the destruction of a person’s honour can ruin marriages, break families, and push the slandered into lifelong trauma. Islam considers the dignity of a chaste woman so sacred that a single false word can cause incalculable harm; hence the penalty is proportionately severe.

This verse also serves as a reassurance to ʿĀʾishah and all chaste believing women that their honour is protected by Allah Himself. The fact that the slanderers are cursed in both worlds demonstrates that this is not a sin that goes unnoticed; it is a crime against the social order and against the commands of Allah.

The use of “unsuspecting” carries a subtle rebuke: such women do not even imagine evil, which makes the slander not only false but also a violation of their innocence. The verse thus places an impenetrable fence around the reputation of believing women.

=24=

The previous verse declared the curse and punishment for slanderers; this verse pulls back the curtain on one of the most terrifying scenes of the Hereafter, revealing how that punishment will be established. “on the Day their tongues, hands, and feet will testify against them for what they used to do.” The body parts that were the instruments of sin will be given speech by Allah and will bear witness. The slanderer used his tongue to utter the false accusation, passed the rumour along with his hands perhaps by gesturing or writing, and walked with his feet to spread the gossip. On the Day of Judgement, these very limbs will break their silence and confess the truth, leaving the slanderer no room for denial.

This is consistent with other Quranic verses, such as Sūrah Yā-Sīn (36:65) where Allah says, “Today We will seal their mouths, and their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will testify to what they used to earn.” The specific mention of the tongue first in this verse highlights the primary tool of slander. The Prophet (ﷺ) once asked the companions, “Do you know who the bankrupt is?” and explained that it is the one who brings prayers and fasting but has slandered others, and his good deeds are taken to compensate his victims.

However, here the scene is even more immediate: the sinner’s own body turns state’s witness. The wisdom is that human beings can hide their crimes behind sweet words and denials, but their limbs are imprinted with their actions, and on that Day, the truth will be manifest. This should cause every believer to reflect deeply on how they use their tongue, hands, and feet today. Are these limbs being employed in spreading truth and goodness, or are they storing up testimony that will condemn their owner?

The verse also delivers a powerful psychological deterrent: the knowledge that one’s own tongue will speak against them is more terrifying than any external witness, because there is no escape. It also demonstrates the perfect justice of Allah; no external witnesses are needed when a person’s own body confesses.

The mention of “for what they used to do” indicates habitual action, not a single slip, pointing to the fact that slanderers usually make a pattern of such behaviour. For those who repent, however, the testimony may be replaced by the recording of good deeds, as Allah may transform their sins into virtues. The verse thus concludes the legal ruling with an eschatological enforcement that anchors the prohibition in the absolute certainty of accountability.

=25=

After the body parts testify, the actual recompense is meted out. “On that Day, Allah will give them their just penalty in full…” The word din refers to recompense or requital, and haqq means the true, deserved, and fair. So the punishment they receive will be precisely what they earned, neither increased nor diminished. There will be no oppression, no error in the divine court.

This is a direct response to those who might think the hadd of eighty lashes in this world is sufficient or too harsh; the real full settlement is on the Day of Judgement. The slanderers who escaped worldly punishment by concealing their crime will not escape this just penalty.

The verse continues, “and they will ˹come to˺ know that Allah ˹alone˺ is the Ultimate Truth.” In the life of this world, the slanderers may have thought their lies were the truth, or that they could manipulate the narrative. They may have considered their accusations as a means to elevate themselves or harm others. On that Day, all falsehoods will vanish, and they will be confronted with the undeniable reality that Allah is Al-Ḥaqq al-Mubīn, the Ultimate Truth whose very being is Truth, whose promises and threats are True, and whose judgement is the final truth.

This is not an intellectual knowing but an experiential, earth-shattering realisation that will come too late for their own good. The phrase “Allah ˹alone˺ is the Ultimate Truth” also implies that every other supposed truth—rumours, slanders, false ideologies—is utterly baseless. The slanderers had relied on the power of the spoken word to create a false reality, but on that Day, only one Reality remains.

This verse also links back to the exoneration of ʿĀʾishah; the truth of her innocence was from Allah, and those who denied it will face the Truth Himself as their judge. For the believers, this verse strengthens tawakkul: when they are wronged and the world believes a lie, they can rest assured that a Day is coming when Allah will establish the ultimate truth and give full justice. The combination of just penalty and revelation of divine truth provides both a warning to the wrongdoers and a profound comfort to the victims of slander, assuring them that their honour is safe with Allah.

=26=

This verse delivers a powerful general principle that simultaneously serves as the final word on the slander against ʿĀʾishah. “Wicked women are for wicked men, and wicked men are for wicked women.

” The word khabīth denotes something filthy, corrupt, and impure. The principle is that in Allah’s wise ordering of the world, like is paired with like. A person of corrupt nature is naturally drawn to another of similar corruption, and in the divine decree, they end up together. Conversely, “And virtuous women are for virtuous men, and virtuous men are for virtuous women.

” The word ṭayyib means good, pure, and wholesome. So the pure and chaste are matched with their counterparts.

In the context of the slander, the implication is unmistakable: the Messenger of Allah (ﷺ) is the purest of all men, the most virtuous, and the very pinnacle of goodness. How could his wife be wicked? If ʿĀʾishah were a khabīthah, she would not be the spouse of the Ṭayyib. Her being the wife of the Prophet is itself the greatest testimony to her purity and virtue. The verse thus silences the slander with an unassailable logical and theological argument.

The next sentence states, “The virtuous are innocent of what the wicked say.” Those described as ṭayyibūn are declared completely free from the foul accusations uttered by the evildoers. The slander does not stick to them; it simply slides off their pure image. This declaration gave immense relief to ʿĀʾishah and her family, as it came directly from the heavens.

The verse then promises, “They will have forgiveness and an honourable provision.” Forgiveness (maghfirah) covers any minor human shortcomings, and “honourable provision” (rizq karīm) refers to the bountiful, dignified sustenance in Paradise.

Thus, not only are they cleared in this world, but they are guaranteed the highest honour in the Hereafter. The verse also serves as a timeless guide for marriage: a believer should seek a spouse of matching virtue, and one can gauge a person’s inner state by looking at the company they keep or the partner they are content with.

However, scholars note that this is not an absolute deterministic law; sometimes a good person marries a wicked one as a test, and then the believer is expected to be patient and call the other to goodness. Yet the general pattern and the ideal is that hearts gravitate toward their own kind.

The verse concludes the theme of chastity and slander with an assurance of divine provision and purity, moving the surah into new realms of spiritual light.

=27=

With this verse, the surah shifts gracefully from the protection of moral honour to the protection of domestic privacy, establishing the Islamic etiquette of seeking permission (istiʾdhān). The address “O believers” again roots the command in the identity of faith. “Do not enter any house other than your own” is a sweeping prohibition against walking into someone’s dwelling unannounced. A person’s home is their sanctuary, a private space where they may be in a state of undress or engaged in intimate family matters. The two conditions for entry are “until you have asked for permission and greeted its occupants.” The seeking of permission usually involves a phrase like “Assalāmu ʿalaykum, may I enter?” and the practice of the Prophet (ﷺ) was to stand at the side of the door, not directly facing it, and to ask up to three times. If no permission is granted after the third request, one must leave. The greeting (taslīm) is an essential part; it announces the visitor’s peaceful intentions and invokes blessings upon the household. The order in the verse puts seeking permission before the greeting, but in practice they are often combined, and some scholars say the greeting comes first as a way of seeking permission. The wisdom is clear: unannounced entry could lead to seeing something that should be covered, causing embarrassment or even suspicion. It preserves the sanctity of the home and the modesty of both the visitor and the residents.

The verse adds, “This is best for you, so perhaps you will be mindful. ” The instruction is not meant to be a burden but a source of goodness, purity, and social harmony. The phrase “so perhaps you will be mindful” connects this etiquette to taqwā, God-consciousness, teaching that respecting the privacy of others is a part of one’s mindfulness of Allah. The pre-Islamic Arabs would often enter houses without warning, and this revelation came to civilise those interactions. In a broader sense, this verse lays the foundation for the concept of privacy in Islam, a value that later verses will develop further regarding intra-household permissions and the behaviour of children and servants. The mindfulness mentioned is a call to internalise that every command, even one as social as knocking on a door, is an act of worship when done for Allah’s sake.

=28=

This verse continues the instruction on seeking permission by addressing two additional scenarios. The first scenario: “If you find no one at home, do not enter it until you have been given permission.” When a person knocks and no one answers, it is forbidden to simply walk in, even if the door is open. The absence of a response means permission has not been granted. Entering an empty house without the owner’s consent is a violation of trust and can lead to suspicion if something is later found missing. The Shariah protects both the owner’s property and the visitor’s reputation.

The second scenario is even more delicate: “And if you are asked to leave, then leave.” Sometimes a person is granted initial entry but then the host, for whatever personal reason, requests that the guest depart. Perhaps the family needs privacy, or a private matter has arisen. The command is to leave immediately and without resentment. This can be psychologically difficult; a person might feel offended or slighted.

Yet Allah declares, “That is purer for you. ” It is purer for the visitor’s heart, cleansing it from the urge to pry or take offence, and purer for the host’s private life. This training of the ego is a mark of a refined believer who does not let personal pride override divine command.

The verse concludes, “And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of what you do.” This serves as both a comfort and a warning. If the host’s request to leave was based on a valid reason known only to them, Allah knows it. If the visitor leaves gracefully for Allah’s sake, Allah records that good deed. Conversely, if someone enters uninvited or lingers resentfully, Allah is aware of that transgression.

The emphasis on divine knowledge throughout the surah reminds believers that privacy laws are not just social conventions but religious obligations enforced by the All-Knowing. The hadith literature provides practical examples: the Prophet (ﷺ) said, “If a man invites you and you are asked to leave, then leave.” Some companions would even avoid looking inside a house when seeking permission. All these etiquettes built a society of mutual respect, where the sanctity of the home was inviolable.

=29=

After establishing the strict rule for private homes, the Quran provides a reasonable exception to avoid undue hardship. “There is no blame on you if you enter public places where there is something of benefit for you.

” The phrase “public places” translates buyūtan ghayra maskūnah, literally “uninhabited houses” or places not used as private dwellings. Scholars give examples such as inns, guest houses, shops, public baths, mosques, libraries, and ruins. These are spaces built for public access or not designated as someone’s private living quarters. The condition “where there is something of benefit for you” indicates that the entry must be for a legitimate purpose—seeking shelter, conducting trade, resting, or using facilities—not for loitering or mischief. The exemption demonstrates the practicality of Islamic law; it does not demand impossible standards but recognises genuine human needs. The subsequent phrase, “And Allah knows what you reveal and what you conceal,” adds a layer of moral accountability. Even in public spaces, where legal permission is relaxed, one’s intentions and conduct remain under divine scrutiny. A person might enter a shop with the concealed intention of stealing, or enter a public bath while lustfully gazing at others. The legal ruling removes “blame” in terms of the technical requirement of asking permission, but it does not remove moral responsibility. Allah knows the outward actions and the hidden motives.

Thus, the heart of the believer remains vigilant. This verse also subtly connects back to the preceding theme of slander and modesty; entering a public house is not a licence to abandon the lowering of the gaze or to engage in gossip. The overarching objective of the surah is to create a community where even permissible freedoms are exercised with God-consciousness.

The mention of Allah’s knowledge serves to fuse the external law with internal purity, ensuring that the relaxation of one rule does not become a loophole for sin. This balance between ease and mindfulness is a hallmark of Quranic legislation.

=30=

With the principles of home privacy established, the surah now directs attention inward, to the most intimate guardian of modesty: the human eye. The command is addressed to the Prophet to “Tell the believing men to lower their gaze.

” The Arabic ghadd al-baṣar means to reduce, restrain, or cast down the gaze. It does not mean walking with eyes permanently fixed on the ground, but rather avoiding the intentional, lingering look at that which is forbidden, particularly the unrelated opposite sex and the private areas of others.

The first glance, which happens unintentionally and without desire, is forgiven, as the Prophet (ﷺ) instructed ʿAlī, “O ʿAlī, do not follow one glance with another; the first is for you, but the second is against you.” The command to lower the gaze is proactive; it cuts off the very first pathway of zinā. The eyes are the scouts of desire, and a gaze can plant a seed of temptation in the heart that grows into an obsession. So the divine wisdom blocks the disease at its inception. Coupled with this is “and guard their chastity,” meaning to protect the private parts from all unlawful sexual activity. Guarding chastity is the ultimate goal, and lowering the gaze is the indispensable means.

The verse then adds, “That is purer for them.” The word azkā connects to tazkiyah, the purification of the soul. Restraining the gaze purifies the heart from the stain of lust, preserves the dignity of the man, protects the honour of women, and fosters a society free from the constant sparks of temptation. It is an act of spiritual hygiene.

The verse ends with “Surely Allah is All-Aware of what they do.” This is a profound reminder that even the most secret glance, which no human may detect, is fully known to Allah. The eye that steals a look in the dark is still seen by the All-Aware. This consciousness transforms the command from a social rule into a deeply personal act of worship and self-discipline. The instruction is given to men first, not because women are exempt, but because the gaze is often the first point of contact. The very next verse will address women, showing that the obligation is mutual and forms a complete system of communal modesty. The man who lowers his gaze in obedience to Allah is promised the sweetness of faith and the light of discernment in his heart.

=31=

This verse forms the comprehensive charter of modesty for Muslim women. It begins with the same command given to men: “lower their gaze and guard their chastity.” The believing woman must also restrain her eyes from looking at forbidden things and protect her private parts. Then come the instructions unique to women, given the greater emphasis on concealment of beauty. “and not to reveal their adornments except what normally appears.” Adornments (zīnah) include both the natural beauty of the body and the added ornaments like jewellery, makeup, and fine clothing. “Except what normally appears” is a clause that scholars have interpreted differently; the most sound view, supported by Ibn ʿAbbās and others, is that this refers to the face and hands, which are necessarily visible during daily activities, and the outer garment like the jilbāb.

The next command, “Let them draw their veils over their chests,” corrects a pre-Islamic practice where women would wear a headscarf (khimār) that hung down behind the back, leaving the neck and bosom exposed. The word khumur is plural of khimār, and the instruction yadribna means to cast or draw it over the juyūb (the chest openings of the garment). So the veil must cover the hair, neck, and chest, establishing the obligation of hijab.

The verse then lists the maḥārim—the close relatives before whom a woman may uncover her hidden adornments, which include her hair, neck, arms, and legs. The list includes husband, fathers, fathers-in-law, sons, stepsons, brothers, and nephews, along with fellow Muslim women, slaves, male attendants devoid of sexual desire, and children who have not yet reached an awareness of sexuality. The father-in-law is included because his respect and permanent relation remove any fear of attraction. The “male attendants with no desire” refers to those who are physically or mentally incapable of sexual desire, or elderly servants who are completely trustworthy.

The verse then adds a subtle prohibition: “Let them not stomp their feet, drawing attention to their hidden adornments.” In pre-Islamic times, women would strike their feet to make their anklets jingle, thus attracting attention. The prohibition extends to any action that deliberately draws attention to hidden beauty, such as heavy perfume in public or certain walking styles.

Finally, the verse concludes with a sweeping call to repentance: “Turn to Allah in repentance all together, O believers, so that you may be successful.” This acknowledges that perfection in modesty is difficult and that past lapses can be forgiven. Success (falāḥ) is tied to heeding these commands and continually returning to Allah.

This verse establishes the outer and inner dimensions of hijab, making modesty a collective act of worship that leads to spiritual triumph.

=32=

After legislating the means of guarding chastity through gaze and dress, the surah now addresses the positive institution that channels natural desires into a blessed union: marriage. The command “Marry off the ˹free˺ singles among you” is addressed to the guardians and the community at large, urging them to facilitate marriage for those who are unmarried.

The word ayāmā includes both men and women who are single, whether never married, divorced, or widowed. The community has a collective responsibility to help its members find spouses and to remove the barriers that make marriage difficult. The command extends to “the righteous of your bondmen and bondwomen,” showing that the slaves, too, have a right to marry and that their piety should be the primary criterion, not their social status. The verse anticipates a major objection—poverty—and sweeps it aside with a divine promise: “If they are poor, Allah will enrich them out of His bounty.” Fear of financial burden often prevents young people from marrying or parents from consenting. Allah guarantees that He will provide for them from His limitless bounty. This does not mean that a person should be reckless, but that sincere trust in Allah’s provision is part of faith. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “There are three whom Allah has taken upon Himself to help: the one who marries seeking chastity, the slave who wants to buy his freedom, and the warrior in the path of Allah. ” The verse then grounds the promise in two divine names: “For Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing.” Al-Wāsiʿ means His bounty is vast, encompassing all needs; Al-ʿAlīm means He knows the circumstances, intentions, and the best timing for each person. This reassures the believers that the command to marry is not a burden but a gateway to divine enrichment. The historical context is also important: many early Muslims were poor emigrants, and the community was being taught to transcend material calculations. Marriage is thus framed as an act of worship that invites Allah’s material and spiritual blessings.

The verse also implies that remaining single without a valid reason while having the means is discouraged, because marriage is the prophetic sunnah and a completion of half the religion, as stated in a hadith.

=33=

This verse addresses those who are not yet able to marry, providing guidance that balances realism with hope. “And let those who do not have the means to marry keep themselves chaste until Allah enriches them out of His bounty.” The verb yastaʿfif means to seek and maintain chastity through self-restraint.

This is an active, spiritual struggle, not a passive state. The person who lacks financial ability, physical health, or a suitable partner is commanded to guard themselves through fasting, lowering the gaze, avoiding triggers, and making duʿāʾ, while placing trust that Allah will provide a way out. The promise “until Allah enriches them out of His bounty” reinforces that the state of hardship is temporary and that divine relief will come. This cultivates patience and hope.

The verse then shifts to the institution of slavery, laying down laws that lead to its gradual eradication. “And if any of those bondspeople in your possession desires a deed of emancipation, make it possible for them, if you find goodness in them.

” This is the contract of mukātabah: a slave may request a written agreement to purchase his freedom by paying a certain sum in instalments. The master is commanded to accept this contract if he sees in the slave the ability to earn and the character of honesty and religiosity.

The verse adds, “And give them some of Allah’s wealth which He has granted you,” encouraging masters to help the slave financially, either by reducing the agreed sum or giving them money from zakah or charity. This effectively accelerates freedom. Then comes a stern prohibition: “Do not force your slave girls into prostitution for your own worldly gains while they wish to remain chaste.” This was revealed concerning a specific hypocrite, ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy, who forced his slave women into prostitution and took their earnings. Islam completely prohibited this exploitation. The dignity of the slave woman is protected; if she desires chastity, she cannot be compelled into sin.

The final sentence, “And if someone coerces them, then after such a coercion Allah is certainly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful ˹to them˺,” is a profound declaration of divine justice. The sin of coercion falls entirely on the coercer, and the coerced woman is forgiven because she acted under compulsion. Allah, in His mercy, does not hold the powerless accountable for what they are forced to do.

This principle extends to all forms of duress in Islamic law. The verse thus combines personal chastity with social justice, showing Islam’s transformative care for the vulnerable and its rejection of all forms of sexual exploitation.

=34=

This verse serves as a transitional hinge in the surah, recapitulating the purpose of the preceding legal and ethical instructions and preparing the ground for the magnificent parables that follow. “Indeed, We have sent down to you clear revelations…” The word āyātin bayyinātin means signs or verses that are luminous, self-evident, and leaving no ambiguity. All the commands regarding fornication, slander, liʿān, hijab, and social etiquette are not obscure cultural norms but clear divine guidance meant to be understood and implemented.

The verse then mentions, “along with examples of those who had gone before you. ” The term mathal (examples) includes the parables that will immediately follow—the Light verse, the mirage, the deep-sea darkness—but it also includes the stories of previous nations mentioned throughout the Quran. These examples are not mere stories; they are vivid illustrations that make abstract truths tangible.

The mention of “those who had gone before you” reminds the believers that the trials of slander, the struggle for chastity, and the battle against hypocrisy are not unique to them. Previous nations faced similar tests, and their fates serve as warnings or inspirations.

Finally, the purpose is “and a lesson to the God-fearing.” The word mawʿiẓah is an admonition that penetrates the heart and stirs it to change. The qualification “to the God-fearing” indicates that only those who have taqwā, a living consciousness of Allah, will truly benefit from these revelations. The disbelievers and the heedless may read or hear the same verses, but they will not extract the lesson. This closing of the section ties neatly to the opening of the surah, which declared that the chapter is revealed so that “you may be mindful.” The God-fearing are those who have internalised that mindfulness.

Thus, verse 34 acts as both a summary and a gatekeeper; it tells the reader that the forthcoming parables about light and darkness are not ornamental but are part of the same pedagogical project, offering vivid imagery to the hearts that are ready to receive it. The believers are now invited to ascend from the detailed laws to the contemplation of the very source of guidance: Allah, the Light of the heavens and the earth.

=35=

This is the renowned Light Verse, one of the most profound and spiritually charged passages of the Quran. It opens with the statement “Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth.” This does not mean Allah is a physical light, for nothing is like Him. Rather, He is the source of all light: the physical light of the sun, moon, and stars; the light of guidance, knowledge, and faith in the hearts; and the light by which the entire cosmos is ordered and illuminated. He is the One who brings everything out of the darkness of non-existence into the light of being, and out of the darkness of misguidance into the light of belief.

The verse then presents a parable: “His light is like a niche in which there is a lamp…” The niche (mishkāt) is a recess in the wall designed to hold and reflect the lamp’s light efficiently. The lamp (miṣbāḥ) is the source of illumination, placed inside a crystal (zujājah). The crystal is described as being “like a shining star,” transparent, pure, and radiant. The lamp is lit from the oil of a blessed olive tree, “neither to the east nor the west,” meaning it is planted in an open, sun-drenched location that receives sunlight all day long, making its oil the purest. This oil is so refined that “it would almost glow, even without being touched by fire.” When the fire touches it, it becomes “Light upon light!” The scholars of tafsir have offered layers of interpretation. The niche is often understood as the chest of the believer; the crystal as the purified heart; the lamp as the light of faith and the Quran; the oil as the innate, uncorrupted fiṭrah (primordial disposition); the blessed tree as the revelation and the prophets. The oil almost glowing by itself refers to the pure human nature that almost recognises the truth on its own, but when the fire of divine revelation touches it, it blazes into a radiant, multi-layered illumination. “Light upon light” captures the accumulation of guidance—fiṭrah plus revelation, knowledge plus practice, the Quran plus the Sunnah. The statement “Allah guides whoever He wills to His light” indicates that this light is a gift; no one can attain it through mere intellect without divine guidance.

The verse ends by asserting that Allah sets forth parables and that He has perfect knowledge of all things. This verse is the heart of the surah, connecting the outer purity legislated earlier to the inner illumination of the soul. The one who guards their gaze and chastity, who respects privacy, and who repents sincerely, is the one whose heart becomes a niche for this divine light. Conversely, the darkness of slander and immorality extinguishes this light. The parable elevates the believer’s aspiration from merely following rules to becoming a vessel of divine radiance.

=36=

The divine light described in the previous verse is not abstract or remote; it shines concretely in specific places on earth. “That light shines through houses of worship which Allah has ordered to be raised…” The phrase fī buyūtin connects back to the light; the niche and the lamp are located within these houses. The “houses” are the mosques, the places of prostration. Allah has commanded that they be “raised,” meaning both physically built and spiritually honoured and elevated in status. Mosques should be kept clean, maintained, and respected, never desecrated. They are the centres where the Name of Allah is mentioned abundantly—through the recitation of the Quran, the calls to prayer, the five daily ṣalāh, dhikr circles, and teaching. The light of guidance and faith is kindled and sustained in these houses, and from them it radiates out into the community.

The verse continues, “He is glorified there morning and evening. ” Glorification (tasbīḥ) involves declaring Allah’s perfection and transcendence above all flaws. The specific mention of morning and evening brackets the entire day, with the morning referring to the Fajr prayer and the evening to the ʿAṣr and Maghrib prayers, or more generally all the prayers. The mosques are alive with worship from dawn to dusk. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “Whoever builds a mosque for Allah, even like a sparrow’s nest, Allah will build for him a house in Paradise. ” This verse reveals the outward dimension of the light: the individual heart of the believer is the inward niche, and the mosque is the outward niche that houses the collective light of the ummah. The connection between inner purity and the mosque is essential; the mosques are populated by those who have guarded their chastity and lowered their gaze, and in turn, the mosque reinforces that purity.

The verse also subtly refers back to the hypocrites who built the Ḍirār mosque to cause harm; only the mosques raised for Allah’s pleasure are the true houses of light. For the faithful, the mosque is a refuge from the darkness of worldly distractions, a place where the oil of fiṭrah is ignited by communal worship. The daily rhythm of morning and evening tasbīḥ frames the believer’s entire worldly activity within a cycle of light, ensuring that the lamp never goes out.

This description of the mosques leads naturally into the description of the men who populate them, which follows in the next verse, completing the picture of where and in whom the divine light resides.

=37=

The light shining in the mosques is not an impersonal force; it is carried by specific human beings. The verse says “by men” (rijāl), and the use of the plural emphasizes that the carriers of this light are real, living believers whose lives embody the guidance. The primary quality of these men is that they “are not distracted—either by buying or selling—from Allah’s remembrance, or performing prayer, or paying alms-tax.” Commerce and trade, represented by buying and selling, are the most common worldly activities that can consume a person’s mind and time. The verse does not condemn trade; the Prophet (ﷺ) himself was a trader, and many companions were merchants. It condemns being so engrossed in commerce that it distracts from the primary duties to Allah.

The word tulhīhim means to divert or preoccupy to the point of forgetting. These men engage in business, but when the time for ṣalāh arrives or the call to charity sounds, they leave their transactions and turn to Allah without hesitation. Their hearts are anchored in remembrance (dhikr), which includes both the ritual prayers and the constant awareness of Allah.

The mention of paying zakāh alongside prayer shows that their light is not just vertical (worship) but also horizontal (social responsibility). Their wealth does not harden their hearts; they purify it through alms.

The verse then reveals the engine driving this devotion: “They fear a Day when hearts and eyes will tremble. ” The Day of Judgement is described with the graphic image of hearts and eyes being violently overturned and agitated in sheer terror. This fear is not a paralysing dread but a motivating awe that reorders priorities. Because they truly believe in that Day, the temporary gains of a business deal lose their power to distract. The heart’s trembling at the thought of divine judgement keeps the heart steady in obedience. The companions exemplified this; many were wealthy traders, but when the adhān was called, they would close their shops and hasten to the mosque. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf, one of the richest companions, would walk as if his head was a flag, yet his heart was so detached that he donated entire caravans.

The verse teaches that true masculinity in Islam (rijāl) is not measured by physical strength but by the ability to control one’s priorities under the pull of dunyā. These men are the ones who truly benefit from the light of the mosques and whose inner lamps burn brightly, undistracted by the shadows of worldly commerce.

=38=

The previous verse described the men of light as driven by fear of the Day of Judgement; this verse balances that fear with the positive, forward-looking motivation of hope. They perform their devotions “hoping that Allah may reward them according to the best of their deeds.

” The phrase “the best of their deeds” (aḥsana mā ʿamilū) is deeply significant. Allah, in His infinite generosity, does not take the average of a believer’s deeds or punish them for the mixed quality of their actions. Instead, He examines their record and rewards them based on the very best they did—the most sincere prayer, the most generous charity, the most patient forbearance. The shortcomings and sins that are repented from are overlooked, and the peaks of their righteousness become the standard for their recompense.

This is a manifestation of divine grace that should fill the believer with hope. The verse then adds, “and increase them out of His grace. ” The reward is not limited to strict merit; Allah gives an additional, unearned increase—aḍʿāfan muḍāʿafah—multiples upon multiples, far beyond what deeds alone could justify. This increase includes the joys of Paradise that no eye has seen and no ear has heard.

The verse concludes, “And Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit.” The word bi-ghayri ḥisāb means “without reckoning” or “without measure. ” Allah’s provision in the Hereafter, and in this world for that matter, is not constrained by any budget or calculation. He gives to the men of light in a manner that defies human accounting.

This promise addresses the concern that spending in charity might diminish wealth; the same divine attribute that provides Paradise without limit can enrich a poor person who marries or a merchant who leaves his trade for prayer. The verse creates a beautiful cycle: these men are not distracted by commerce because they fear the Day and hope for Allah’s reward; and that hope is grounded in the certainty that Allah’s provision is infinite. The light within them—the product of revelation and pure fiṭrah—illuminates their path, showing them that the profit of the Hereafter is incomparably greater than any worldly transaction.

Thus, verses 36–38 together paint a complete portrait of the ideal believer: rooted in the mosque, untethered from worldly distractions, balanced between fear and hope, and trusting in the limitless bounty of the Lord of Light.

=39=

After the luminous portrait of the believer, the surah presents a stark contrast with two parables of utter darkness and deception. The first parable is of the mirage. “As for the disbelievers, their deeds are like a mirage in a desert.

” The Arabic word sarāb refers to the optical illusion that appears on a hot, barren plain, shimmering like a body of water. A desperately thirsty person runs toward it with hope, only to find absolutely nothing there to quench his thirst.

This is the exact condition of a disbeliever who performs good deeds in this world—charity, kindness, justice—without the foundation of faith in Allah and the Last Day. On the Day of Judgement, he will approach these deeds expecting them to quench his need for salvation, only to find them utterly void and without weight. They will have evaporated like the mirage, because the condition of acceptance—īmān—was absent. The Quran elsewhere states, “And We will regard what they have done of deeds and make them as scattered dust” (25:23).

The second part of the verse delivers a chilling surprise: “Instead, they find Allah there ready to settle their account.” When the disbeliever reaches the point where he expected relief, he meets not emptiness, but the All-Compelling Lord. He had denied Allah, ignored His messengers, and lived as if there were no meeting with Him. Now, at the very spot of his false hope, he finds Allah waiting to hold him to account for every deed, word, and belief. The “swift in reckoning” (sarīʿ al-ḥisāb) indicates that Allah’s judgement is fast, effortless, and all-encompassing. He does not need time to calculate; in an instant, the entire record is assessed and the verdict delivered. This parable serves as a dire warning: a life full of worldly achievements, bereft of the light of faith, is a trek across a desert chasing a mirage. The disbeliever’s deeds may have been applauded by people, but in the ultimate reality of the Hereafter, they are worthless.

The verse also links back to the slanderers and hypocrites of the surah, who thought their schemes were effective, but they were merely building on mirages. The only deeds that hold substance are those performed by the men of light, whose hearts are illuminated by the niche of faith.

=40=

The second parable for the disbelievers’ state takes us from the desert’s blinding illusion to the abyss of the ocean’s impenetrable darkness. “Or like the darkness in a deep sea, covered by waves upon waves, topped by dark clouds.” The imagery is multilayered and suffocating. It begins with a deep, dark sea (baḥr lujjī) far below the surface where no sunlight penetrates. Above this sea are waves, then upon those waves are another set of waves, and above all that are thick, dark clouds blocking any remaining light from the sky.

This is “Darkness upon darkness!”—layers of obscurity piled one upon another, each reinforcing the total absence of light. The result is that “If one stretches out their hand, they can hardly see it.” The hand is right in front of the face, yet it remains invisible; this is the ultimate disorientation. The scholars explain that this represents the darkness of disbelief, compounded by the darkness of false doctrines, ignorance, hardened hearts, and sinful deeds. The deep sea is the heart drowned in worldly pursuits; the waves are the successive distractions, doubts, and lusts that crash upon it; the clouds are the veils of arrogance and rejection of truth. The disbeliever who has turned away from the light of revelation lives in this state of spiritual blindness, unable to see the truth even when it is right before him. The parable concludes with a definitive statement: “And whoever Allah does not bless with light will have no light. ” The source of all true light is Allah alone. If He, in His wisdom and justice, withholds guidance from a person due to that person’s persistent rejection and arrogance, then no alternative source exists. No human philosophy, no material success, no intellectual prowess can substitute for the divine light. The person remains in complete darkness, both in this world, groping for meaning, and in the Hereafter, stumbling toward the Fire. This verse forms a powerful contrast with verse 35, where the believer’s heart is described as “light upon light.” The believer is bathed in clarity upon clarity; the disbeliever is sunk in darkness upon darkness. The two parables together, the mirage and the deep-sea darkness, capture the two aspects of disbelief: the deceptive illusion of benefit and the crushing reality of utter loss. The surah’s message is clear: only through the divine guidance revealed in this very surah can a person move from the darkness of sin, slander, and denial into the radiant light of faith and purity.
