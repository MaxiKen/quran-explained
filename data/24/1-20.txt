=1=

This opening verse of Sūrah An-Nūr, a Medinan chapter, immediately establishes the divine origin and binding nature of the guidance that follows. The phrase “˹This is˺ a sûrah” points to a distinct, self-contained unit of revelation, and the emphasis “which We have revealed” reminds every listener that the Prophet Muḥammad (ﷺ) did not compose these words; they come directly from Allah, the Most High.

This is crucial because the surah will address deeply sensitive social and legal matters—sexual misconduct, false accusations, domestic privacy, and the purity of the community—and only the Creator has the right and wisdom to legislate on such intimate affairs. The declaration “and made ˹its rulings˺ obligatory” translates the Arabic faradnāhā, meaning that the specific commands, prohibitions and penal laws contained within this chapter are not optional pieces of advice; they are divine obligations that the Muslim community must implement. Some scholars note that the pronoun “it” can refer to the surah itself, meaning Allah has made the chapter as a whole obligatory to follow, or it can refer to the rulings explicitly detailed within it. Either way, the message is that there is no room for selective adherence when it comes to the laws of chastity, slander and hijab that follow. Then Allah adds, “and revealed in it clear commandments”—āyātin bayyināt.

The word “āyāt” here can mean verses, signs, or proofs, and “bayyināt” means clear, manifest, and self-evident. So the surah contains luminous proofs that distinguish truth from falsehood, right from wrong. These clarities are meant to cut through the confusion and moral ambiguity that human beings often face in matters of desire and honour.

Finally, the purpose of this entire revelation is summed up in “so that you may be mindful”, or tadhakkarūn. This mindfulness, tadhakkur, is a deep, continuous awareness of Allah that leads to obedience and the avoidance of sin. The verse thus frames the harsh penalties and strict moral codes not as arbitrary rules, but as a mercy and a reminder from a Lord who wants to protect the purity, dignity and cohesion of the believing community. As the surah unfolds, this mindful awareness will be tested—especially when the believers were faced with the vicious slander against the Prophet’s wife ʿĀʾishah—and the legal injunctions will serve as a manifestation of that very mindfulness. This foundational verse ties the whole chapter together: it is a revealed obligation containing clear signs so that hearts may be filled with God-consciousness.

=2=

After the introductory declaration of obligation, the surah immediately presents one of its most important penal rulings: the punishment for zinā, unlawful sexual intercourse. The verse begins with “As for female and male fornicators”—al-zāniyah wa az-zānī—placing the female first, which some commentators explain as a linguistic nuance that gives due attention to the gravity of the act when it involves women, whose chastity is the bedrock of family honour and lineage, and also because the context of the surah revolves around the slander against a woman. The command is unequivocal: “give each of them one hundred lashes.

” The Arabic word jaldah refers to a strike with a whip or a strap, not a whip that cuts the skin like a punitive flogging, but one that causes pain and public disgrace while preserving life. This is a prescribed ḥadd punishment, a fixed penalty that cannot be increased or decreased by any human authority. Crucially, the verse then issues a stern warning: “and do not let pity for them make you lenient in ˹enforcing˺ the law of Allah.” The natural human inclination to feel compassion for the offender—especially if the fornicator is young, respectable, or tearfully repentant—must not override obedience to the divine command. Emotional mercy that prevents the execution of Allah’s law is a false mercy that leads to the corruption of society. The Prophet (ﷺ) firmly set this principle when a woman from the Makhzūm tribe committed theft and some interceded on her behalf; he became angry and said, “By Allah, if Fāṭimah the daughter of Muḥammad were to steal, I would cut off her hand” (al-Bukhārī, Muslim). Similarly, in the matter of zinā, no social status or personal feeling can suspend the punishment. The condition “if you ˹truly˺ believe in Allah and the Last Day” makes the enforcement of the ḥadd a marker of genuine faith. Those who truly believe in accountability on the Day of Judgment will uphold the limits set by the Lawgiver, understanding that it is far better for the sinner to be purified through a temporal punishment than to face an eternal one. The verse closes with “And let a number of believers witness their punishment.

” The Arabic ṭāʾifah means a group, which scholars have defined as at least two or three, or a gathering sufficient to make the event publicly known. Public witnessing serves multiple purposes: it ensures that the punishment is carried out justly, without excess, and it deters others from falling into the same sin. The shame and notoriety attached to the public flogging amplify the preventive effect.

Moreover, the presence of the faithful reminds the community that this harsh act is not personal vengeance but a religious duty, and they are encouraged to supplicate for the forgiveness of the punished person afterward. The wisdom behind the lashes being one hundred is a matter of divine decree, but it strikes a balance between severe chastisement and the preservation of the person’s ability to reform and reintegrate into society. The ruling originally applied to unmarried fornicators, while married adulterers are sentenced to stoning according to the Sunnah, which details that the Prophet (ﷺ) implemented rajm for a married man and a married woman.

Therefore, this verse lays down the basic law, which is then qualified by the prophetic practice that is equally binding. Thus, the enforcement of this command safeguards families, protects lineages, and maintains the moral fabric of the Islamic community.

=3=

With the punishment for unlawful intercourse set, Allah now reveals a moral and social law that governs the marriage choices of believers. The verse states: “A male fornicator would only marry a female fornicator or idolatress. And a female fornicator would only be married to a fornicator or idolater.” The immediate meaning is a declaration about the corrupt inclinations of unchaste people: those who do not restrain their desires and commit zinā tend to seek out partners who are similarly immersed in sexual sin or even shirk, because spiritual impurity attracts its like. Some early commentators understood this as a descriptive statement—a reflection of the sorry reality that an unrepentant fornicator, shunned by the pure, will inevitably end up with someone of the same character.

However, the majority of scholars also treat it as a prescriptive rule, especially in light of the final clause: “This is ˹all˺ forbidden to the believers.” The prohibition falls clearly on the believer: a chaste Muslim man or woman is forbidden to marry a person known to persist in fornication, unless that person sincerely repents. Marriage is a sacred bond meant to be a source of tranquillity, love, and the nurturing of a righteous next generation. A spouse who disregards Allah’s limits in sexual matters will bring immense spiritual and psychological harm into the household, corrupting the family environment. Connecting a pure heart to an impure one is an injustice to oneself.

The verse also links zinā with idolatry, underscoring the severity of sexual immorality: it can be a stepping stone away from faith. The Prophet (ﷺ) mentioned that an adulterer does not commit zinā while being a believer at the moment of the act; this is not to declare him an outright disbeliever, but to show that perfect faith leaves his heart during that disobedience.

Consequently, a habitual fornicator’s state resembles that of a mushrik in the loss of the protective barrier of taqwā. The practical application today remains: a believer who fears Allah must investigate the character and chastity of a potential spouse. If one discovers that the person is actively engaged in zinā and shows no repentance, then marrying that individual contravenes this clear divine warning. The prohibition extends to both men and women equally, closing any loophole for those who might think they can reform an unchaste partner after marriage. Reformation must precede the marriage, evidenced by genuine tawbah and changed conduct.

The verse also served a specific purpose after the incident of the Ifk; it reassured the believers that ʿĀʾishah, being pure and virtuous, was certainly not a fornicator, and that her rightful husband, the Prophet (ﷺ), was the purest of men. It turned the slander back on the slanderers, indicating that the false accusations came from hearts steeped in their own moral filth. In sum, this verse upholds the principle of kafāʾah (compatibility) in religion and chastity, a cornerstone of Islamic marriage.

=4=

Having ordained the punishment for the act of zinā itself, the surah now turns to protect the honour of innocent people from false accusations. The verse addresses “Those who accuse chaste women of adultery”—the Arabic word muḥṣanāt refers to free, chaste, Muslim women who have guarded their private parts, though the ruling extends to accusations against chaste men as well, as many jurists have clarified. The accusation meant here is qadhf, a clear, explicit charge of unlawful intercourse. If someone makes such a claim and then “fail to produce four witnesses”, the legal consequence is immediate: “give them eighty lashes each. ” This is the ḥadd for slander. The requirement of four witnesses is exceptionally strict; it is derived from Sūrah An-Nisāʾ (4:15) and reaffirmed later in this very surah (verse 13). The wisdom is that matters of sexual honour are so grave that they cannot be proven by a single testimony or circumstantial evidence, because the harm of a false accusation destroys families and lives. If the accuser cannot bring four upright men who actually witnessed the act of penetration, then the accuser himself becomes the criminal. Eighty lashes is a severe flogging, ten less than that for fornication, symbolising that although the slanderer has committed a major crime, he did not physically commit the illicit act. Beyond the physical punishment, the verse imposes a lasting social penalty: “And do not ever accept any testimony from them—for they are indeed the rebellious.

” The phrase “do not ever accept” in Arabic conveys a strong prohibition. The person who falsely accuses a chaste woman is branded a fāsiq, a flagrant sinner whose moral integrity is destroyed.

Consequently, his witness is no longer admissible in an Islamic court. The link between the rejection of testimony and their description as “the rebellious” (al-fāsiqūn) is critical: it is their open transgression that disqualifies them from bearing witness, because a witness must be of upright character (ʿadl). This ensures that the legal system is not polluted by liars.

However, the verse immediately opens a door in the next āyah for those who repent, leaving a scholarly discussion about whether the rejection of testimony is permanent or can be lifted upon sincere tawbah and rectification. The Ḥanafī school holds that the flogging for qadhf removes one’s ʿadālah forever and testimony is never accepted again, while the Shāfiʿī and Mālikī schools rule that if the slanderer repents and reforms, his testimony becomes acceptable because the reason for rejection—the sin—has been removed. The majority lean toward the latter, supported by the explicit exception in verse 5. This divine legislation upholds the dignity of the individual and deters careless talk about people’s honour. In an age where rumours spread instantly through social media, the principle is profoundly relevant: those who spread unverified scandals are, in the sight of Allah, rebellious liars who deserve severe punishment in this world and the next, unless they repent and mend their ways.

=5=

This verse is a ray of hope piercing the severity of the preceding judgment. After condemning the slanderer to lashes and a lifetime rejection of testimony, Allah makes an exception: “except those who repent afterwards and mend their ways.” The exception applies primarily to the legal disqualification—the rejection of testimony—and to the sin itself, but not to the physical penalty of eighty lashes, which remains a right that must be carried out once established.

The phrase “repent afterwards” means after committing the slander and before or after receiving the punishment, the person turns back to Allah with sincere remorse. Tawbah here is not a mere verbal “I repent”; it must be accompanied by “and mend their ways” (aṣlaḥū), meaning they correct their behaviour, publicly clear the name of the one they accused if possible, and demonstrate by their reformed conduct that they have abandoned their sinful ways. When this happens, “then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” The two divine names, al-Ghafūr (the Oft-Forgiving) and ar-Raḥīm (the Most Merciful), are paired to assure the wrongdoer that the sin, however enormous, can be erased by Allah’s infinite mercy. This directly targets a misconception that might arise from the harshness of the ḥadd—that the slanderer is permanently damned. Islam refuses to let despair overcome hope. The door of repentance stays open until death. In the historical context of the Ifk, the slander against ʿĀʾishah was spread by a group; some of them repented sincerely afterwards, such as Mistah ibn Uthātha and Hassān ibn Thābit. Mistah was a relative of Abū Bakr and a poor emigrant; despite his involvement, he later regretted his deed and was included in Abū Bakr’s resumed charity after the revelation of verse 22. Their repentance was accepted, and they were reintegrated into the community. The verse thus teaches that even the most egregious social sin—destroying a believer’s reputation—is forgivable if followed by genuine change. Legal consequences may remain in this world, but the spiritual stain is washed away.

For believers today, this means that no matter how far one has sunk into backbiting, gossip, or character assassination, one should never lose hope in Allah’s forgiveness. At the same time, the condition “mend their ways” indicates that a real transformation is required, not a cheap claim of regret while persisting in the same behaviour. It also guides the community: when a former slanderer demonstrates true reform, the community must not treat him as a permanent outcast, for Allah has forgiven him, and they should reflect that forgiveness. This balanced approach discourages both moral laxity and unforgiving self-righteousness.

=6=

From the general ruling on false accusation, the surah now moves to a specific, painful domestic scenario: a husband who sees his wife commit adultery. The verse says, “And those who accuse their wives of adultery but have no witness except themselves.” In the ordinary course, if a man accused his wife of zinā without four witnesses, he would face the punishment for qadhf—eighty lashes and the loss of his credibility.

However, a husband who actually witnesses his spouse’s infidelity is placed in an impossible situation: if he remains silent, he tolerates a heinous violation of his marriage and risks raising a child that is not his own; if he speaks out, he becomes a slanderer in the eyes of the law because he lacks the required four witnesses. Divine wisdom addresses this dilemma with the legislation of liʿān, the oath of imprecation. “the accuser must testify, swearing four times by Allah that he is telling the truth.” The husband’s single testimony is exceptionally accepted, not as a legal proof to punish the wife, but as a means to free himself from the hadd of qadhf and to dissolve the marriage. He must solemnly swear by Allah four separate times that he is truthful in his accusation of adultery. Each oath carries the weight of calling upon the Creator as his witness, and the repetition of four oaths substitutes for the four witnesses normally required. The procedure must be done before a judge or the ruler. The wisdom here is profound: marriage is built on trust and intimacy, and behind closed doors there are no witnesses except the spouses themselves and Allah. To demand external witnesses for a husband’s accusation would make it nearly impossible to address such a violation, while to accept his word unilaterally would expose innocent wives to vengeful false charges.

Thus the liʿān is a perfectly balanced solution that merges the solemnity of an oath with the fear of divine punishment. It is recorded in the Sunnah that the verses of liʿān were revealed concerning Hilāl ibn Umayyah, or according to another narration, ʿUwaymir al-ʿAjlānī, who came to the Prophet (ﷺ) and said, “O Messenger of Allah, what if a man finds another man with his wife? Should he kill him and be killed, or what should he do?” The Prophet (ﷺ) initially disliked such questions, but then the revelation came, and he summoned the couple and performed the liʿān. After the oaths, the Prophet (ﷺ) separated them forever and stated that the child would be attributed to the mother. The husband is thus shielded from the ḥadd for slander, but the wife, as we will see, is given an equal opportunity to clear herself. This legislation preserves the sanctity of the family unit while providing an exit from an irreparably broken marriage.

=7=

The testimony of the husband does not end with the four oaths; it climaxes with an even weightier invocation. “and a fifth oath that Allah may condemn him if he is lying.

” The Arabic phrase “anna laʿnata Allāhi ʿalayhi in kāna mina al-kādhibīn” means that the husband invokes the curse (laʿnah) of Allah upon himself should he be lying. Laʿnah means expulsion from divine mercy and a state of being accursed. By taking this fifth oath, the husband openly calls down upon his own head the worst spiritual catastrophe—the anger of Allah and distance from His mercy—if his accusation is false.

This is an immense deterrent against a husband who might consider fabricating such a charge out of jealousy, spite, or the desire to divorce without financial liability. The structure of four testimonial oaths plus a fifth oath of self-curse mirrors the earlier command to produce four witnesses, with the fifth element being the strong appeal to God’s punishment for falsehood. It is as if the husband is saying, “May I be deprived of Allah’s mercy and doomed to His wrath if I am fabricating this. ” In a believer’s heart, such an oath, made in the most solemn setting, cannot be taken lightly. The psychological and spiritual weight of this fifth oath is so heavy that even a sincere believer who knows he is lying would tremble at the prospect of inviting the curse of Allah. The ritual of liʿān, as recorded in the hadith, took place in the mosque in front of the Prophet (ﷺ) and the gathered believers. When the husband pronounced the fifth oath, the Prophet (ﷺ) warned him, “Be careful! The curse of Allah is upon the one among you who is a liar.” The public nature and the explicit warning amplify the seriousness.

Thus, the fifth oath serves as the ultimate test of truthfulness; a false accuser might risk worldly lashes, but only a reckless or disbelieving person would willingly invoke eternal damnation. At the same time, a truthful husband, devastated by betrayal, will not hesitate to utter it, knowing that Allah is his supporter. The verse thereby balances the scale: just as the wife’s honour is protected by the difficulty of proving adultery, the husband’s right to defend his honour and lineage is protected by this powerful mechanism that relies on the fear of God.

The passage continues with the wife’s reciprocal oaths, showing that she is not left defenseless against a potential liar.

=8=

The liʿān procedure now shifts to the wife, granting her an equal right to clear her name and avert the severe punishment for adultery. The verse states, “For her to be spared the punishment, she must swear four times by Allah that he is telling a lie.” The punishment referred to is the ḥadd for proven adultery, which would be stoning to death if she is a married woman (muḥṣanah), or lashes and possible imprisonment if unmarried. Without her counter-oaths, the husband’s sworn accusation would, in principle, expose her to that penalty, because his five oaths carry legal weight.

However, the Shariah offers the wife the shield of her own solemn testimony. She must testify by swearing by Allah four times that her husband is lying in his charge.

The word “lie” (kādhib) here is powerful: she is not merely pleading not guilty, she is directly rebutting his claim under oath, calling him a liar in the name of Allah. The four oaths match his four, creating a symmetrical legal exchange. This reflects the profound justice of Islam: she is given exactly the same number of oaths to defend herself as he used to accuse her. The oath is taken by Allah, the All-Knowing, so a guilty wife would be in the terrifying position of swearing repeatedly by the Creator while knowing she is false. The procedure, therefore, acts as a divinely designed truth-detector; only an innocent wife, horrified at the slander against her, or a brazenly irreligious one, would go through with it. The Shariah does not then cross-examine or investigate the matter physically—it leaves the truth to Allah on the Day of Judgment. The immediate worldly consequence is that she is spared the punishment and the marriage is dissolved. The hadith literature provides a moving example: after the husband and wife had performed liʿān, the Prophet (ﷺ) said, “If she gives birth to a child with such-and-such characteristics, then it belongs to the husband; if it has other features, then it belongs to the one she was accused of.” She gave birth to a child that matched the description of the man she was accused of, and the Prophet (ﷺ) remarked, “Had it not been for the oaths, I would have dealt with her severely. ” Yet the worldly punishment was averted because she took the oaths.

This verse reveals the high regard for a woman’s oath and her agency in a matter that could cost her life. It is a powerful check against a husband’s potential abuse of the liʿān law, because his accusation can be neutralized by her swearing. The legal scholars have detailed that the wife’s oaths must also be in the same judicial setting, preferably in a mosque or a court, and they are just as binding.

=9=

The wife’s defense reaches its climax with a fifth oath that mirrors the husband’s invocation, but with a significant and sobering difference. She must swear: “and a fifth oath that Allah may be displeased with her if he is telling the truth.” While the husband invoked the curse (laʿnah) of Allah upon himself if he lied, the wife invokes the ghaḍab—the wrath and displeasure—of Allah upon herself if her husband is truthful. Commentators note that the word ghaḍab conveys a more intense and active anger than laʿnah; it is Allah’s direct wrath, whereas laʿnah is the consequence of being expelled from mercy. The subtlety here is that if the wife is indeed guilty and the husband truthful, her sin is twofold: she committed adultery and she compounded it by swearing false oaths, accusing her innocent husband of lying, and destroying the family. Her false oath directly calls down upon herself the severest form of divine anger.

Thus, the bar for her to take this fifth oath is set extraordinarily high. An innocent woman, however, will have no hesitation, because she knows the truth and trusts Allah to protect her. The structure of the liʿān—four denials plus a self-invocation of wrath—completes the legal symmetry. Once both parties have completed their oaths, the judge separates them permanently; they can never remarry. The hadith confirms that the Prophet (ﷺ) separated the couple after liʿān and declared that the pregnancy would be attributed to the woman, not the husband. He also stated that if the wife was truthful, Allah would exonerate her, and if she was lying, Allah would punish her. The liʿān thus closes the marital chapter without declaring a definitive earthly verdict on guilt; the punishment in this world is averted, but the matter is deferred to the ultimate judgement of Allah. This legislation highlights the profound respect for the sanctity of oaths and the reliance on God-consciousness as the true deterrent against perjury.

For believers, the thought of inviting Allah’s wrath is far more terrifying than any worldly penalty. Some scholars point out that the use of “ghaḍab” for the wife and “laʿnah” for the husband reflects the typical roles; the husband’s false accusation would unjustly besmirch his wife’s honour, while the wife’s false denial would conceal a betrayal that corrupts lineage—an offence directly linked to the provocation of Allah’s anger. In both cases, the liʿān serves as a merciful exit from an impossible legal proof requirement, safeguarding individuals from public scandal while leaving the door of repentance open for whichever party was false.

=10=

This verse concludes the section on liʿān and transitions the discourse, reminding the believers of the boundless grace that permeates even the harshest of laws. The phrase “˹You would have suffered,˺ had it not been for Allah’s grace and mercy upon you” is a powerful interjection, implying that without divine intervention, the community would have been plunged into severe difficulty and punishment. The “grace and mercy” here encompasses the very legislation of liʿān itself, which resolved an impossible domestic crisis, saved spouses from despair, and protected the fabric of society. Without this merciful law, a husband witnessing his wife’s infidelity would either have to silently endure dishonour, kill her and face retaliation, or falsely accuse her and suffer the ḥadd for slander. Allah’s grace provided a way out.

The verse also reinforces the statement made earlier in verse 5 and looks forward to the similar refrain about the slander incident in verse 14. The repeated emphasis on divine favour teaches believers to always perceive Allah’s rulings as manifestations of mercy, not burdensome restrictions. The addition “and had Allah not been Accepting of Repentance, All-Wise” highlights two more attributes. “Tawwāb” (Accepting of Repentance) indicates that Allah continually turns in forgiveness to those who repent.

This is particularly poignant after the liʿān procedure, because one of the two spouses is definitely lying and has sinned grievously, yet the door of tawbah remains open. Even if a husband falsely invoked a curse, or a wife falsely invoked wrath, they can still repent later, and Allah may forgive them and spare them the ultimate consequences in the Hereafter. “All-Wise” (Ḥakīm) assures that every detail of this legislation, from the number of oaths to the wording of the invocation, is placed with perfect wisdom for the benefit of individuals and society.

The verse also serves as a bridge to the upcoming story of the slander (al-Ifk), where grace and mercy will once again be the themes. Without Allah’s grace, the rumour that shook Madinah could have led to an irreparable split. So the verse quietly prepares the listener: just as Allah’s mercy resolved the intimate dilemma of a single couple, so too will His mercy resolve the communal trial that threatened the reputation of the most beloved wife of the Prophet (ﷺ). In a broader sense, every believer should reflect on this verse and recognize that their own survival through sins and trials is only by the grace of Allah. The words “had it not been for Allah’s grace and mercy upon you, none of you would have ever been purified” in verse 21 echo the same sentiment. This appreciation of grace cultivates humility and gratitude, which are the essence of true worship.

=11=

The surah now turns to one of the most painful episodes in the early Muslim community—the slander against ʿĀʾishah, the Mother of the Believers. The verse begins, “Indeed, those who came up with that outrageous slander are a group of you.

” The word “ifk” means a great lie, a fabrication so heinous that it turns the truth completely upside down. The slander involved accusing ʿĀʾishah of adultery after she was inadvertently left behind on a journey and returned with a companion, Ṣafwān ibn al-Muʿaṭṭal. The “group of you” refers to a faction from within the Muslim community, not external enemies, which made the trial more bitter and confusing. The leader of this slander was ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy ibn Salūl, the chief hypocrite, but some sincere believers, out of naivety, also spread the rumour.

Thus, Allah calls them “a group of you,” emphasizing that they are not a separate nation; they are among the believers, which heightened the pain. Immediately, Allah addresses the Prophet (ﷺ) and the believers with words of consolation: “Do not think this is bad for you. Rather, it is good for you.” The shock and hurt caused by the slander seemed like an unmitigated disaster; the Prophet (ﷺ) was distressed for a month, and ʿĀʾishah fell ill.

Yet Allah declares it was ultimately good. The goodness lay in many things: the revelation of her innocence from the heavens, which elevated her status forever; the legal clarification of punishments for slander and the rules of liʿān and witness; the exposure of the hypocrites; and the purification and strengthening of the sincere believers’ faith as they navigated the trial.

This principle—that a believer’s trial contains hidden good—is a recurring Quranic theme. The verse then states, “They will be punished, each according to their share of the sin.” Those who participated in spreading the slander, each received a punishment commensurate with their role. The ringleaders and those who actively spread the lie were flogged with the ḥadd of qadhf. ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy, as the mastermind, received a special mention: “As for their mastermind, he will suffer a tremendous punishment. ” The “tremendous punishment” is generally understood to be in the Hereafter, though he might also have suffered humiliation in this world. Because he was a hypocrite who concealed his disbelief, the full torment will be in the Fire. This verse, therefore, establishes that evil plotted against the Prophet’s household and the believers will eventually recoil upon its perpetrators, and it teaches the community to trust that Allah extracts good from even the ugliest of trials.

=12=

Having identified the slanderers, Allah now gently reproaches the community for their failure to respond correctly. The verse sighs, “If only the believing men and women had thought well of one another…” The Arabic particle law lā expresses a longing, a wish that things had been different. It holds up a mirror to the believers, showing them the nobler path they missed. The foundation of a healthy Islamic community is ḥusn al-ẓann—thinking the best of one’s fellow believers. When the rumour reached them, instead of suspecting the worst about the purest woman among them and her companion, they should have immediately assumed innocence.

The phrase “thought well” means they should have defended ʿĀʾishah’s honour in their hearts and on their tongues, recognizing that a slander of this magnitude against the Prophet’s wife could not possibly be true. The verse continues, “and said, ‘This is clearly an outrageous slander!’” The proper verbal response was to brand the rumour as an obvious fabrication.

The word “ifk” appears again, and “mubīn” (clearly) indicates that its falsehood should have been apparent to anyone with a sound heart. This teaches that when a Muslim hears something evil about a fellow believer, especially someone known for piety, the immediate reaction should be to reject it and label it as a lie, not to repeat it or entertain it as potentially true. Several righteous companions, such as Abū Ayyūb al-Anṣārī and his wife, indeed reacted this way. When they heard the rumour, they said, “Glory be to You, this is a great slander,” demonstrating the proper attitude. The verse thus establishes a practical rule of engagement with rumours: a believer is not a passive receptacle for gossip; he has a duty to actively deny and shut down false speech. The failure to do so allowed the slander to spread from tongue to tongue, as the next verse will explain. The lesson remains profoundly relevant: in the age of social media, a rumour about someone’s honour can go viral in seconds. Believers must be the first to say, “This is clearly a slander,” refusing to share, like, or comment on unverified damaging information.

This is not being naive; it is obeying a divine command that protects the moral fabric of society. The verse also clarifies that the sin of spreading slander does not only belong to the originator; anyone who hears and passes it on without denouncing it shares in the culpability.

=13=

The reproach continues with a logical and legal challenge that the slanderers could never meet. “Why did they not produce four witnesses?

” The question is rhetorical, exposing the utter flimsiness of the accusation. The requirement of four witnesses for a charge of adultery had already been established in Sūrat An-Nisāʾ (4:15) and earlier in this surah. The slanderers claimed that ʿĀʾishah committed a crime, yet they brought not a single witness, let alone four. The obvious impossibility of producing witnesses who saw something that never happened proves that the entire scandal was built on lies and suspicion. By posing this question, Allah reminds the believers of the legal standard that should have immediately shut down the rumour. If no one came forward with four upright witnesses, the rumour was legally and morally dead on arrival.

The verse then delivers a conclusive judgment: “Now, since they have failed to produce witnesses, they are truly liars in the sight of Allah.” The phrase “in the sight of Allah” is a profound attestation of their falsehood in the divine knowledge. Even if some human beings might still have doubts, Allah Himself testifies that they are liars.

This is the ultimate vindication of ʿĀʾishah; the Creator of the universe declares her accusers to be liars. The wording also implies a permanent label: they are characterized as kādhibūn, liars, a damning moral indictment. This legal and theological logic applies far beyond that historical incident. It establishes that accusations of sexual immorality without the required proof are not just procedural errors; they are lies in the eyes of Allah, making the accuser a liar irrespective of his subjective belief.

This is because the Shariah sets a high evidentiary bar specifically to protect honour, and anyone who bypasses it by spreading accusations without satisfying that bar is willfully engaging in falsehood. The verse thus teaches a critical mindset: before believing or sharing a scandalous claim, a believer must ask, “Where is the evidence that meets the divine standard?” If it is absent, the claim must be dismissed and the speaker considered untruthful. This cultivates a society where honour is safeguarded by both law and conscience, and where mere suspicion is never allowed to snowball into character assassination. The foundation of this teaching is the absolute trust in Allah’s wisdom, who knows how destructive unverified words can be.

=14=

After rebuking the community for their mishandling of the rumour, Allah reminds them of the severe consequences they escaped only through His grace. “Had it not been for Allah’s grace and mercy upon you in this world and the Hereafter,”—the grace encompasses the sending of the Prophet (ﷺ), the revelation that cleared the matter, and the legislation that protects society.

The mention of “in this world and the Hereafter” indicates a continuous, all-encompassing mercy. The worldly grace manifested in the revelation of these verses before the community was irreparably torn apart, and in the legal punishment that served as expiation for some. The grace in the Hereafter refers to the forgiveness granted to those who repented and the fact that the community was not collectively destroyed.

The verse then states the terrifying alternative: “you would have certainly been touched with a tremendous punishment for what you plunged into.” The phrase “what you plunged into” (mā afaḍtum fīhi) vividly depicts the act of diving headlong into a dangerous flood of gossip. The believers who participated in spreading the talk, even without originating it, had plunged themselves into a grave sin. A “tremendous punishment” could have struck them—a punishment that could have been a worldly calamity, or the torment of the Hereafter, or both. The tone is one of warning and relief, prompting gratitude.

This verse also serves to bridge the gap between the specific incident and the general warning: the community must never forget how close they came to disaster due to careless tongues. It fosters a permanent awareness that gossip about chastity is not a minor sin; in Allah’s sight, it could warrant an immense punishment.

The repetition of this motif of “grace and mercy” as a shield against punishment appears multiple times in this surah (see verse 10, 20, 21), weaving a thread of divine compassion through the hard edges of the law. For the modern reader, the lesson is that when a society fails to check the spread of slander and indecency, it invites social decay and divine displeasure.

However, Allah’s mercy is always ready to intercept that destruction if people turn back. The verse also implicitly exonerates those who stayed silent or immediately denounced the slander; the punishment would have touched those who indulged in it.

Thus, it defines collective responsibility while leaving room for individual innocence.

=15=

This verse paints a detailed and damning picture of how the slander spread, functioning as a timeless anatomy of gossip. “when you passed it from one tongue to the other”—the Arabic expression talaqqawnahu bi-alsinatikum literally means “you received it with your tongues”, implying a chain of eager, thoughtless repetition. One person heard it, and without any verification, relayed it to another, who in turn passed it on. The tongue, the organ of speech, is identified as the primary vehicle of the sin. No one stopped to engage their intellect; the rumour simply rolled off the tongues in a frenzy.

The next phrase, “and said with your mouths what you had no knowledge of”, intensifies the condemnation. It is not merely that they spoke; they asserted something as if they knew it, while they had no real knowledge—no eyewitness, no proof, no certainty.

This is the essence of slander: speaking from conjecture and presenting it as fact. The Quran repeatedly forbids speaking without knowledge (qawl bilā ʿilm), equating it with following Satan’s footsteps. Then Allah highlights the psychological flaw: “taking it lightly while it is extremely serious in the sight of Allah.” The people considered it a trivial piece of conversation, idle talk that would be forgotten. They felt no weight on their conscience because they had not originated the slander. But in the divine scale, the matter was ʿaẓīm—enormous, grave, catastrophic.

This contrast between human perception and divine reality is a powerful corrective. What humans dismiss as “just gossip” may be enough to earn them severe punishment. The reputation of a chaste woman, especially the wife of the Prophet (ﷺ), is sacred, and its violation is no small matter.

This principle extends to all innocent people. A hadith warns that a person may say a word, not caring about it, by which he will plunge into the Hellfire for seventy years. The verse thus trains the believer’s heart to magnify the sin of backbiting and slander, seeing it through Allah’s lens rather than through the trivialising lens of social custom. The proper response, as the next verse shows, should have been immediate shock and refusal to utter such a thing. The anatomy of gossip is complete: receive, repeat, speak without knowledge, and belittle the sin. The cure is the opposite: reject reception, stop repetition, speak only with certain knowledge, and recognize the enormity of the sin. This verse remains one of the most powerful deterrents against gossip in any era.

=16=

After diagnosing the illness, the Quran prescribes the exact words that should have been the believers’ immediate response. “If only you had said upon hearing it…” The verse models the correct reaction, and the language is one of astonished disgust and immediate glorification of Allah. The recommended speech is a three-part shield. First, “How can we speak about such a thing! ” This is an expression of shock and moral rejection, questioning the very possibility of engaging with such a topic. It implies that even to repeat the accusation in order to deny it is something a pure heart shies away from, but when necessary to counter falsehood, the denial must be clear. The statement shuts down the conversation instantly, indicating that the matter is so foul that it is beneath discussion. Second, “Glory be to You O Lord!”—Subḥānaka. This phrase, known as tasbīḥ, declares Allah’s absolute perfection and transcendence above any flaw. When confronted with a hideous slander, a believer turns to glorify Allah, implying that only Allah is pure and that He is far removed from the wickedness being attributed to His servant. It is also an acknowledgment that the accusation is an injustice that Allah, in His perfection, will not leave unanswered. Third, “This is a heinous slander!”—buhtān ʿaẓīm. Buhtān is a false accusation of something abominable, a calumny that astounds due to its utter falsity. By labelling it explicitly, the believer fulfills the duty of declaring the truth and opposing falsehood, while maintaining the highest level of respect by not delving into the details. The companions who remained firm, like Abū Ayyūb al-Anṣārī and his wife, actually uttered similar words, showing the immediate practical application.

This verse teaches the powerful lesson of proactive verbal defence of a brother’s or sister’s honour. It is not enough to remain silent; silence can sometimes be misinterpreted as acceptance. The believer must speak up and declare the accusation a manifest lie, while simultaneously extolling Allah’s perfection to reinforce the gravity of the slander. This model speech is a fortress for community integrity: it denounces evil, affirms faith, and protects the reputation of the innocent. It also sets the tone for internal Muslim relationships—when one Muslim hears evil about another, the default is not curiosity but protective indignation. The verse further implies that believers should cultivate such a strong sense of brotherhood that any attack on the honour of a fellow believer feels like an attack on their own honour. This creates a society where slanderers find no receptive ears.

=17=

The preceding verses served as a lesson and a rebuke; now comes a direct and emphatic prohibition meant to prevent any recurrence. “Allah forbids you from ever doing something like this again…” The Arabic verb yaʿiẓukum literally means “He exhorts you” or “He admonishes you,” but in this context it carries the force of a strict prohibition.

The use of the divine name “Allah” underlines the authority of the command; this is not a human advice but the decree of the Lord of the worlds. The phrase “something like this” encompasses the entire chain of behavior that led to the slander: hearing a rumour about a chaste believer, spreading it without verification, taking it lightly, and failing to denounce it. The prohibition is categorical and timeless. Then the verse attaches a crucial condition: “if you are true believers.” This qualification makes faith itself the yardstick of compliance. Anyone who claims to believe in Allah and His Messenger must never participate in such slander again. It ties the avoidance of gossip to the core of one’s identity as a muʾmin. If a person engages in or even tolerates such behavior after this clear prohibition, their claim to faith is shaken. The condition is not meant to cast doubt on the faith of those who slipped before the prohibition, because they were subsequently forgiven and educated. Rather, it is a forward-looking deterrent: now that the ruling is clear, persisting in it would be a sign of weak or hypocritical faith.

The verse also indicates that learning from mistakes is an essential part of faith. The community had faltered badly, but Allah, in His mercy, did not cast them out; He gave them a second chance and a strict warning. The psychological impact of this verse is immense: the believers who lived through the Ifk would never forget the pain and the near-calamity, and this prohibition would be etched into their consciousness. For all generations, it stands as a divine red line. The “something like this” includes not just accusations of adultery, but any unverified damaging speech about the innocent. The prohibition thus extends to backbiting, slander, and rumour-mongering in all forms. A true believer, when reminded of this verse, should feel a shudder of caution before forwarding a message, liking a defamatory post, or engaging in casual character assassination. The verse is a shield and a test; it purifies the community by clearly marking slander as incompatible with sincere faith.

=18=

Following the prohibition, Allah provides a statement that reinforces the purpose and clarity of His revelation. “And Allah makes His commandments clear to you”—yubayyinu Allahu lakumu al-āyāt.

The word “commandments” translates āyāt, which can mean verses, signs, or proofs. Allah clarifies the divine signs, legal rulings, and moral lessons so that there is no room for ambiguity. This clarity is a manifestation of His mercy; He does not leave humanity to stumble in confusion about the limits of speech and honour. The detailed explanation of the slander incident, the punishments, and the proper conduct serves as a clear guidance for all similar situations.

The repetition of “Allah makes clear” throughout the surah (see also verse 59, 61) underscores that the believers have no excuse for ignorance. Then the verse concludes with two divine names: “for Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise. ” “All-Knowing” (ʿAlīm) signifies that Allah possesses perfect knowledge of all hidden intentions, the secret whispers that spread rumours, and the damage they cause. He knows who initiated the slander, who spread it innocently, who repented sincerely, and who harboured malice. Because He is All-Knowing, His legislation is perfectly calibrated to address the reality of human psychology and societal harm. No human lawmaker could account for the unseen nuances of intention and conscience, but Allah’s laws are based on absolute knowledge. “All-Wise” (Ḥakīm) indicates that every ruling, including the harsh hadd punishments and the stringent rules on witnesses, is placed with flawless wisdom. The prohibition of slander, the requirement of four witnesses, the liʿān procedure—all are wise, even if some might not immediately perceive the wisdom. This pairing of knowledge and wisdom assures the believers that they can trust the divine commandments completely, even when they go against personal desires or societal trends. It also serves as a comforting reminder that the agony of the Ifk was not random chaos; it unfolded under the watchful eye of the All-Knowing and All-Wise, who used it to deliver immortal guidance. The verse thus closes the specific lesson with a universal theological anchor, instilling confidence in the believer that every instruction in the surah is the product of infinite knowledge and wisdom.

=19=

After addressing the specific incident, the Quran broadens the warning to encompass a general character trait that lies at the root of such scandals: a love for the spread of immorality. “Indeed, those who love to see indecency spread among the believers…” The word fāḥishah (indecency) refers to any extraordinarily shameful act or speech, especially of a sexual nature.

The phrase “love to see it spread” goes beyond committing the act; it targets those who delight in the circulation of obscene scandals about believers. Such people relish the fall of the righteous, they feed on the humiliation of the chaste, and they actively disseminate or cheer on the dissemination of filthy talk. This verse identifies the corrupt heart that rejoices when a believing community is soiled by slander. The motive could be envy, hypocrisy, or a simple perverse enjoyment of dirt. For them, the verdict is severe: “will suffer a painful punishment in this life and the Hereafter.

” The pairing of punishment in both worlds is notable; in this life, it may come as the legal ḥadd, public disgrace, or other afflictions, and in the Hereafter, the torment of Hellfire. The hadith mentions that those who bring immorality to light and spread it, except by force (like in rape cases), are under this threat.

The verse then adds, “Allah knows and you do not know.” This is a chilling caution. Human beings may not be able to identify who exactly loves the spread of indecency; some may skillfully pretend to be concerned while secretly enjoying the chaos. But Allah knows the hidden lust of hearts. He knows the difference between someone who discusses a scandal out of genuine reformist concern or to seek justice, and someone who savours the scandal itself. The implication is that those who think they can hide their malicious joy are fooling themselves, because Allah’s knowledge encompasses all. Conversely, the believer who is pained by such talk and strives to suppress it is on the opposite side. This verse is a powerful deterrent for anyone who uses “news” or “awareness” as a pretext to circulate immoral content. It applies directly to modern forms of media where scandals about Muslims are sensationalized for clicks and views. The one who shares, retweets, or likes such content with a sense of relish falls under the scope of this warning. The prohibition is not against exposing genuine wrongdoing to the authorities for the sake of justice, but against the enjoyment of spreading shame. A true believer is grieved by the sins of the community and veils them while seeking reform, as opposed to the hypocrite who spreads them like a disease. This verse thus draws a sharp line between sincere reform and destructive gossip.

=20=

The passage on the slander draws to a close with a refrain that echoes verse 10 and verse 14, wrapping the entire section in a blanket of divine compassion. “˹You would have suffered,˺ had it not been for Allah’s grace and mercy upon you…” The same conditional phrase appears again, reminding the believers of the abyss they narrowly avoided. The “grace and mercy” here refers to the series of interventions that saved the community: the revelation of ʿĀʾishah’s innocence, the legislation of ḥadd for slander, the clear prohibition of repeating such behaviour, and the acceptance of repentance. Without these, the social fabric would have been torn to shreds; the Prophet’s household would have been thrown into a permanent crisis, and the hypocrites would have triumphed.

The repetition of this motif in the surah indicates that the entire legal and moral framework is an expression of raḥmah (mercy), not harshness. Then the verse piles on two more merciful attributes: “and had Allah not been Ever Gracious, Most Merciful.

” The word Raʾūf (Ever Gracious) denotes a gentle, tender kindness that anticipates needs and protects from harm before one even asks. Raḥīm (Most Merciful) is the continuous, overflowing mercy that responds to repentance and weakness. Allah’s Raʾfah is seen in the proactive legislation that shields honour; His Raḥmah is seen in forgiving those who slipped and repented. The combination reassures the believers that the Lord who legislated the lashing of slanderers and fornicators is the same Lord who is profoundly kind and merciful. The law is not a cold code; it flows from a heart of divine compassion that wants to protect human dignity. In the immediate context, this verse closes the Ifk narrative on a note of profound gratitude. The believers are meant to finish reading this section with their hearts swelling with love for Allah, recognizing that every painful trial contains hidden mercies and that the prohibition against slander is itself a gift of protection.

The verse also serves as a transition to the next section, which will warn against following Satan and command forgiveness, linking divine mercy to the believer’s duty to emulate that mercy by forgiving those who wronged them. Thus, verse 20 acts as both a conclusion and a hinge, ensuring that the severity of the preceding threats is never divorced from the loving mercy of the Lawgiver.
