=41=

After the powerful affirmation of divine creative power and the certainty of resurrection, the sūrah now turns to a practical example of true faith in action, the emigration of the oppressed believers. The verse begins, “As for those who emigrated in the cause of Allah after being persecuted,” pointing directly to the early Muslims in Mecca who suffered severe torture, social boycott, and economic strangulation at the hands of the Quraysh.

The phrase “in the cause of Allah” makes it clear that their emigration was not for worldly gain, trade, or personal comfort, but solely to preserve their faith and worship their Lord freely. This refers first and foremost to the group of Muslims who migrated to Abyssinia, seeking refuge under the just Christian king, the Negus. Later, this verse would also apply to the Hijrah to Medina. “After being persecuted” highlights that they did not leave out of weakness or cowardice; they endured immense hardship until it became unbearable and they were commanded or permitted to leave. The divine promise follows immediately: “We will surely bless them with a good home in this world.

” The Arabic word for a good home, mubawwa’an ḥasanan, indicates a safe, pleasant dwelling place where they can practice their religion without fear. For the emigrants to Abyssinia, they found exactly that: protection and peace under a benevolent ruler who respected their faith.

For those who migrated to Medina, the city became their blessed new home and the launching place of the Islamic state. This promise was a direct counter to the Quraysh’s attempts to uproot Islam by making life impossible for its adherents.

Yet the verse immediately elevates the believer’s perspective: “But the reward of the Hereafter is far better, if only they knew. ” The good home in this world is a foretaste and a blessing, but it pales in comparison to the eternal gardens awaiting them. The phrase “if only they knew” refers to those whose vision is limited to this world, including the Quraysh who thought they were inflicting a great defeat on the Muslims. In reality, the emigrants were trading temporary hardship for eternal bliss.

This verse establishes a core principle for all generations: when a believer sacrifices worldly comfort for the sake of Allah, He compensates them generously in this life and immeasurably in the next. The migration of oppressed Muslims today, whether from war or persecution, echoes this promise. The good home might be a place of physical safety, but the far better reward remains the ultimate hope, keeping the heart steady amid the trials of displacement.

=42=

This verse provides the spiritual profile of the emigrants mentioned in the previous verse, linking their reward directly to two foundational qualities of the believer. “It is they who have patiently endured” begins with the word “they,” emphasizing that the ones receiving the good home in this world and the far better reward in the Hereafter are precisely those characterised by patience and trust.

The Arabic word for “patiently endured” is ṣabarū, derived from ṣabr, a comprehensive term that includes perseverance in obedience, restraint from sin, and steadfastness during hardship. The emigrants demonstrated all three. They patiently endured the physical torture, the verbal abuse, the economic boycott, and the emotional pain of leaving their homeland, their families, and their property behind. They did not retaliate in kind when they were weak, they did not abandon their faith when pressured, and they remained obedient to Allah’s command to migrate despite the immense difficulty. This patience was not passive resignation but active, hopeful steadfastness.

The second quality is “and in their Lord they put their trust. ” This tawakkul, or complete reliance upon Allah, was the engine that powered their patience. They trusted that Allah’s command to emigrate, whether to an unknown land or to face an uncertain future, was for their ultimate good. They placed their trust in their Lord alone, not in their own strength, their tribal affiliations, or even in the protection of a foreign king, though they used these means. Their hearts were anchored in the certainty that Allah’s plan is perfect. The combination of ṣabr and tawakkul is the hallmark of the successful believer throughout the Qur’an. Patience without trust can become bitter and despairing; trust without patience can be a shallow claim that collapses under pressure. Together they form an unshakeable foundation. For the Prophet’s Companions, this verse was a badge of honour, a divine commendation that validated their suffering. For contemporary Muslims facing trials, whether personal, social, or political, this verse offers the blueprint for navigating hardship: couple patient endurance with absolute trust in Allah, and the promise of a good outcome in both worlds will be fulfilled.

The verse also serves as a quiet rebuke to those who claim faith but panic or abandon principles at the first sign of difficulty. True emigration of the soul from sin to obedience also requires this patient trust, making the verse applicable far beyond physical migration.

=43=

The sūrah now addresses a primary objection of the Meccan polytheists to the Prophet Muḥammad’s message: their insistence that a messenger should be an angel, not a mere human being who eats, walks in the markets, and has a family. The verse begins with the clear, universal principle: “We did not send messengers before you O Prophet except mere men inspired by Us.

” This statement refutes their objection by placing Muḥammad squarely in the line of all previous prophets. Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, peace be upon them all, were human beings, not angels. They ate food, married, and lived among their people. The distinguishing feature of a messenger is not a non-human nature but divine inspiration, the waḥy that descends upon them.

The phrase “inspired by Us” emphasizes that the source of their authority and knowledge is not their own human intellect but the revelation from Allah. The verse then turns the argument back on the polytheists: “If you polytheists do not know this already, then ask those who have knowledge of the Scriptures. ” This refers to the Jewish and Christian scholars who were present in Arabia and with whom the Quraysh had trade contact. These “People of the Book” could confirm that all prophets sent by God were human beings. The Quraysh, being an unlettered people without a revealed scripture of their own, could not claim expertise in matters of prophethood. By directing them to ask the knowledgeable, the Qur’an appeals to an external, historically verifiable source. It also implicitly acknowledges the continuity between the Abrahamic faiths on this fundamental point. The verse thus uses a rational and historical argument to dismantle the objection. It also carries a subtle lesson about the nature of prophethood: humanity is guided by one of their own, someone who understands their struggles, speaks their language, and serves as a relatable model. An angel could not be the model for human conduct. The verse reinforces the status of the Prophet Muḥammad while humbling the Quraysh, who prided themselves on their intelligence, by telling them to learn from others if they are ignorant. In modern times, the objection that religious leaders are “just men” is similarly answered by the fact that divine guidance has always been delivered through human channels, which is a mercy, not a deficiency.

=44=

This verse continues the description of the messengers, adding the substance of their mission. “We sent them with clear proofs and divine Books.” The “clear proofs” refer to the miracles and compelling signs that validated their prophethood, such as the staff of Moses or the healing of Jesus, and also the rational and moral arguments contained in their messages. The “divine Books” are the scriptures revealed to them, such as the Torah, the Gospel, and earlier scrolls.

Thus, every messenger came with both tangible signs and written guidance. The verse then transitions to the Prophet Muḥammad: “And We have sent down to you O Prophet the Reminder, so that you may explain to people what has been revealed for them.” The “Reminder” is a beautiful name for the Qur’an, implying that it awakens the primordial knowledge of God that is embedded in the human soul. The primary function of the Prophet is here defined as explaining the revelation.

This is the foundation of the Sunnah: the Prophet’s words, actions, and approvals are the divinely authorised clarification of the Qur’anic text. Without the Prophet’s explanation, many commands would remain ambiguous. For example, the Qur’an commands prayer and charity, but the details of how many prayers, at what times, and the exact amounts of zakah are provided by the Prophet’s teaching.

The verse also clarifies the purpose of this entire revelatory process: “and perhaps they will reflect. ” The revelation and its explanation are given so that people might engage in deep thought, contemplation, and internalization. The goal is not blind acceptance but reflective understanding that leads to faith and action.

The word “perhaps” in the Arabic context expresses hope and expectation, not doubt. Allah sends down the Reminder and the explainer with the confident hope that receptive hearts will reflect and be guided. This verse elevates the role of the Prophet as the divinely appointed exegete and establishes the inseparable link between the Qur’an and the Sunnah. It also calls contemporary believers to approach the Qur’an with the explanation of the Prophet, not relying solely on private interpretation detached from the prophetic context. The ultimate aim remains reflection, a deep engagement of the mind and heart that transforms the reader, making the Reminder a living source of guidance rather than a merely recited text.

=45=

The sūrah returns to warning the Meccan plotters with a series of rhetorical questions that instil fear and uncertainty. The verse begins, “Do those who devise evil plots feel secure…” This refers directly to the Quraysh leaders who were constantly scheming against the Prophet and the Muslims. Their plots included plans to imprison, exile, or kill the Prophet, as well as efforts to discourage pilgrims from listening to his message.

The word “evil plots” carries the connotation of secret, malicious planning done under the cover of darkness. The verse challenges their false sense of security: do they think that their cunning will protect them from divine retribution?

The first threatened punishment is “that Allah will not cause the earth to swallow them.” This is not a hypothetical fear but a reference to historical precedents, such as Korah (Qārūn) who was swallowed by the earth for his rebellion against Moses. The earth, which appears stable and solid under their feet, could at any moment become their grave by Allah’s command.

The second threat adds: “Or that the torment will not come upon them in ways they cannot comprehend?” This expands the scope of possible punishment beyond the specific image of an earthquake. The torment could come from the sky, from the sea, from a sudden plague, or from an unexpected military defeat, anything that their human minds have not anticipated and against which they have erected no defense. The key phrase is “in ways they cannot comprehend”; their plots are limited to what they can foresee, but Allah’s punishment can come from angles entirely outside their calculation.

This is a deeply unsettling warning. It strips the oppressors of the illusion of control. No amount of human planning can guarantee safety from the Creator’s grasp. For the believers, this verse is a source of comfort: the seemingly powerful schemers are utterly vulnerable before Allah. It also serves as a deterrent for anyone who would harm others through secret plots, reminding them that while they may escape human justice, they cannot escape the divine grasp, which may manifest in the most unexpected ways. The verse encourages the believer to live with a sense of awe and caution, never feeling secure from Allah’s plan, for such security is the hallmark of the spiritually heedless.

=46=

This verse adds a third terrifying scenario to the list of possible divine punishments. “Or that He will not seize them while they go about their day” shifts the focus to the suddenness and the mundane context of punishment. The people targeted are those who are going about their daily business, travelling for trade, moving from one place to another, fully absorbed in their worldly affairs, feeling completely safe and secure.

The Arabic word taqallubihim suggests their active movement, to and fro, their bustling about in their commercial journeys or their daily routines. It is precisely in the midst of this perceived normalcy and busyness that Allah’s seizure could occur. The verse says, “for then they will have no escape.” The suddenness of the punishment when they are dispersed and unaware makes escape impossible. Unlike a visible advancing army against which one might prepare defences, a divine seizure gives no warning and leaves no room for flight or repulsion. This would resonate deeply with the Quraysh, whose trade caravans were the lifeblood of their economy. The verse implies that on one of their seemingly routine journeys, perhaps through the very deserts they knew so well, Allah’s punishment could overtake them, and their wealth, knowledge of the terrain, and tribal alliances would offer no refuge.

This is linked to the preceding verse’s theme: the plotters feel secure, but that security is an illusion. Punishment does not require a dramatic build-up; it can arrive in the midst of the most ordinary moment. The verse is a powerful reminder of human fragility. We tend to feel safe in our routines, assuming that tomorrow will be like today. The Quraysh’s confidence in their trade and their plotting made them deaf to the warning.

This verse teaches that no place and no activity provides protection from Allah’s decree. For the believer, this awareness is not meant to induce crippling fear but to cultivate a state of constant humility and dependence. One should not feel immune from trials or punishment simply because life seems calm.

The phrase “no escape” underscores the finality of divine judgement when it arrives. The call is to repent and reform before that sudden seizure, from which there is no return, overtakes a person in the middle of their daily business, leaving them with nothing but regret.

=47=

This verse presents a fourth and psychologically distinct mode of punishment. “Or that He will not destroy them gradually?

” The Arabic word takhawwuf, translated as “destroy them gradually,” carries the nuance of causing them to diminish bit by bit, accompanied by fear. It refers to a slow, creeping punishment that erodes their power, wealth, and security over time, rather than a single sudden strike. This could manifest as successive losses, internal divisions, the gradual defection of allies, economic decline, or a series of small defeats that collectively lead to their ruin. The psychological aspect of fear is crucial: they live in a state of constant anxiety, witnessing their own decline without being able to stop it. This gradual punishment can be more agonizing than a swift end, as it gives them time to reflect and perhaps repent, yet their arrogance prevents them, so they suffer prolonged deterioration. After listing these terrifying possibilities, the verse immediately follows with a profound attribution of mercy: “But your Lord is truly Ever Gracious, Most Merciful. ” This juxtaposition is stunning. The statement could be read as explaining why the punishment is delayed or graduated; Allah’s ra’fah (ever-graciousness) and raḥmah (mercy) are the reasons the polytheists are not instantly annihilated. Every moment of respite is an act of mercy, an opportunity to turn back. Alternatively, it could mean that despite the severity of the warnings, the overarching attribute governing Allah’s relationship with creation is mercy, and believers should not despair. The combination of warning and mercy is typical of the Qur’an; it balances fear and hope. The Meccan plotters are warned that their continued existence is not a sign of divine approval but of divine forbearance, giving them time to abandon their evil.

For the believer, this verse instils a profound appreciation for Allah’s mercy in the very fact that we are given time to repent, that punishment is not immediate. It also warns against misinterpreting a period of ease for the wrongdoers as proof of their safety; they may be on a path of gradual destruction, all while being given the chance to change. The verse fosters a mindset of perpetual vigilance and gratitude, never taking Allah’s forbearance for granted and rushing to repent before the gradual destruction becomes complete.

=48=

The sūrah now shifts from warning to a profound reflection on a universal sign of creation that testifies to tawḥīd. The verse asks, “Have they not considered how the shadows of everything Allah has created incline to the right and the left as the sun moves?

” The question is an invitation to observe a daily, ordinary phenomenon with spiritual insight. Shadows are formed by objects blocking sunlight, and they shift their direction and length throughout the day, extending to the right and to the left. This movement is not random; it follows the precise laws set by Allah for the sun’s course. The verse states that these shadows are “totally submitting to Allah in all humility.” The submission of a shadow is metaphorical but profound. The shadow has no will of its own; it is completely subjected to the Creator’s command, following the sun’s path in perfect obedience. It prostrates, extending itself on the ground in a posture of humility, and then rises again.

This is a silent, visible act of worship performed by all physical objects. The polytheists worshipped created things like the sun and idols, yet here the very shadows of those created things, and even the shadows of the idols themselves, are submitting to Allah alone. The shadow of an idol bows to Allah, not to the idol.

This is a powerful refutation of their shirk. The act of noticing shadows and seeing them as signs of divine submission elevates the believer’s daily experience. Every time one sees a shadow, it can be a reminder that all creation is in a state of constant, involuntary worship of the Creator.

The phrase “in all humility” translates sujjadan, which is also the word for prostration. The shadows prostrate themselves, shifting from right to left, similar to the movements of a worshipper in prayer. This verse therefore connects the physical world directly to the act of Islamic prayer, showing that the believer, when prostrating, is joining the entire universe in its submission to Allah. The call to “consider” is a call to a conscious, reflective gaze upon nature, transforming the mundane into a source of faith. The verse encourages the scientific observation of nature not for its own sake but to see the Creator’s hand and to take lessons for one’s own voluntary submission.

=49=

This verse broadens the scope of submission from shadows to all living creatures and angels. “And to Allah alone bows down in submission whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth of living creatures.” The verb “bows down” (yasjudu) is the same used for ritual prostration. The verse declares that every living creature, from the birds in the sky to the fish in the sea and the beasts on land, is in a constant state of prostration and submission to Allah. This submission is not by rational choice for animals but by their very nature; they follow the instincts and laws programmed into them by their Creator, never deviating from their assigned role. The heavens and the earth encompass all realms, so the entire cosmos is engaged in this worship.

The verse then singles out the angels: “as do the angels—who are not too proud to do so.” The mention of angels serves a dual purpose. First, it highlights that even these pure, mighty beings, who are far more powerful and closer to Allah than humans, are not too proud to prostrate.

This is a direct contrast with the Meccan pagans, who were too proud to bow to Allah but were willing to bow to idols. Second, the verse refutes their claim that angels are daughters of Allah or independent deities; the angels themselves are humble worshippers of the One God.

The phrase “not too proud” echoes the earlier characterisation of the disbelievers as arrogant. The entire universe, animate and inanimate, including the greatest creatures like angels, submits without a trace of pride. Only some humans and jinn, those who choose disbelief, arrogantly refuse to join this universal chorus of submission. This verse profoundly humbles the human being. When a person prostrates in prayer, they are not doing something strange or demeaning; they are finally aligning themselves with the rest of creation. Their refusal to prostrate is the anomaly, the act of rebellion against the natural order.

The verse also implicitly argues against any worship directed to these creatures; if everything bows to Allah alone, then nothing else deserves worship. For the believer, observing animals or contemplating the heavens becomes a spiritual exercise, recognising the silent prostration happening all around and joining it willingly and lovingly, not out of compulsion but out of conscious choice.

=50=

This verse specifically refers to the angels mentioned in the previous verse, though some commentators extend the meaning to all creation that submits. The subject “They” points to the angels as the immediate context requires, but it also encompasses the broader reality of all obedient creation. “They fear their Lord above them” describes the angels’ constant state of awe and reverential fear of their Lord, who is above them in rank, power, and majesty.

The phrase “above them” signifies Allah’s transcendence and supreme authority over all creatures. The fear mentioned here is not a terror that paralyses but a profound awe and reverence that motivates perfect obedience. This fear is born of deep knowledge of Allah’s greatness and their own absolute dependence.

The second part of the verse, “and do whatever they are commanded,” emphasises the completeness and immediacy of their obedience. The angels do not hesitate, question, or alter the divine commands. Their submission is total and unwavering. This serves as a model for the believers, who are called to emulate the angels in their unquestioning obedience to Allah’s revealed commands. The contrast with the disbelievers is again stark: the angels, despite their immense power and purity, fear their Lord and obey completely, while the Meccan polytheists, weak and dependent, act arrogantly and disobey.

The verse also implicitly answers the pagan belief that angels have any independent power or will; they are mere servants, bound by fear and obedience to Allah. For the believers, this verse instills a sense of the proper attitude towards divine commands. The angels’ fear is not pathological but a natural consequence of recognizing the majesty of God. When a believer hears a command from the Qur’an or Sunnah, the ideal response is that of the angels: a fear of transgressing, and an immediate effort to comply.

The mention of “above them” also reinforces the Islamic concept of Allah’s elevation and transcendence over His creation, a doctrine that counters any notion of pantheism or incarnation. This verse, along with the preceding ones, builds a cosmic picture where all of existence is in a state of obedient worship, and the only discordant note is struck by the arrogant human who refuses to join this universal harmony. The call, therefore, is to humble oneself, fear the Lord, and do what is commanded, thus finding one’s place in the symphony of creation.

=51=

After the cosmic description of universal submission, the sūrah returns to the direct, verbal command of Allah. “And Allah has said” gives this statement the weight of direct divine speech.

The command begins with a prohibition: “Do not take two gods.” The minimum number of plurality is mentioned—two—to negate any form of multiplicity in divinity. If two gods existed, the universe would fall into chaos, as the Qur’an argues elsewhere. This simple prohibition covers all forms of polytheism, whether it involves two, three, or a pantheon of deities. The core of the message is that divinity is indivisible. Then comes the affirmation: “There is only One God. ” This is the positive declaration of tawḥīd.

The Arabic construction stresses exclusivity: there is no God but He. The logical conclusion of this exclusive oneness is the command: “So be in awe of Me alone.

” The word translated as “be in awe” is irhabūn, which carries meanings of fear, awe, and reverence, but also implies a sense of being vigilant and on guard. The object of this awe is exclusively Allah, not any idol, not poverty, not the enemy, not the schemes of the oppressors. The believer’s heart should be filled with such reverence for Allah that all other fears become insignificant. The verse thus connects correct belief with the correct emotional and spiritual response. Monotheism is not merely an intellectual position; it is a state of the heart where Allah alone becomes the focal point of all ultimate concern.

The sequence is beautiful: the prohibition of shirk, the affirmation of oneness, and the command to exclusive awe. This verse encapsulates the entire prophetic mission in a single, powerful sentence. For the early Muslims in Mecca, this was a revolutionary statement that directly challenged the entire religious, social, and economic structure of their society, which was built around the worship of multiple idols. To adopt this belief was to invite persecution, yet it was the liberating truth. In contemporary times, the “two gods” may not be physical idols but rather the dual worship of God and money, God and ego, God and ideology. The verse challenges every heart to examine its ultimate allegiances. The command “be in awe of Me alone” also addresses common anxieties; people often fear losing their livelihood, their status, or their lives. This verse redirects that fear to the only One who truly holds power, liberating the believer from all other fears.

=52=

This verse provides the logical foundation for the command to fear only Allah, which was stated in the previous verse. “To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth” establishes Allah’s absolute ownership and sovereignty. Every single thing in the entire universe, from the largest galaxy to the smallest particle, belongs to Him. Human beings may claim temporary ownership of property, but that ownership is a trust, not an absolute right. The true Owner is Allah alone. This has direct implications for worship: since He owns everything, He alone deserves thanks, praise, and obedience. The polytheists of Mecca acknowledged that Allah created the heavens and the earth, yet they worshipped other things that owned nothing.

The second part, “and to Him alone is the everlasting devotion,” uses the Arabic word dīn, which can be translated as religion, obedience, or recompense. Here it is interpreted as the perpetual, unwavering obedience and worship that is due to Allah at all times.

The word “everlasting” indicates that this devotion is constant, not intermittent or situational. He alone is the object of the eternal religion of truth that never changes. All other systems of worship are transient falsehoods.

The verse then closes with a rhetorical question that challenges the very basis of polytheism: “Will you then fear any other than Allah?” If Allah is the sole Owner and the only One deserving constant worship and devotion, what rational basis is there to fear idols, people, poverty, or the future? All those things are themselves owned and controlled by Allah. Fear of anything else is a misplacement of the heart’s natural awe, which belongs only to the Creator. This question is a powerful psychological tool; it forces the listener to confront the irrationality of their fears.

For the believer, this verse offers a comprehensive remedy for anxiety and worldly fear. When one truly internalises that everything belongs to Allah and that He is the only one owed ultimate devotion, the fear of creatures dissolves.

The verse also has a political dimension: the Quraysh feared that accepting Islam would lead to loss of tribal support and economic boycott. This verse asks them, why fear those things when the Owner of all is with you? The lesson is timeless: align your devotion entirely with Allah, and you will be freed from the tyranny of lesser fears.

=53=

This verse begins a new passage that exposes the psychological inconsistency of the polytheists. “Whatever blessings you have are from Allah” is a comprehensive and indisputable statement. Every blessing—health, wealth, children, safety, senses, intellect, and the very air one breathes—originates solely from Allah.

The word “whatever” leaves nothing out. Even the blessings a person thinks they earned through their own effort are ultimately from Allah, who created their body, their mind, and the means to achieve.

This is a truth that the Quraysh themselves would not deny, as they acknowledged Allah as the supreme Creator and Provider. The verse then describes a common human phenomenon: “Then whenever hardship touches you, to Him alone you cry for help.” When a crisis strikes—a storm at sea, a severe illness, a sudden danger—the human being’s innate nature surfaces. In that moment of extreme distress, the polytheist forgets his idols. He does not call upon Lāt, ʿUzzā, or Manāt. He cries out directly to Allah, the One true God.

This is because the fiṭrah, the primordial human disposition, knows that only the supreme power can remove genuine calamity. The idols are recognized, even if unconsciously, as powerless. The polytheist’s duʿāʾ in hardship is a sincere, desperate, and exclusive call to Allah. The verse exposes this as a contradiction: if Allah is the only one you turn to in hardship, why do you associate partners with Him in times of ease?

This is a powerful argument against all forms of conditional or temporary monotheism. The verse also serves as a profound comfort to believers: hardship itself can be a mercy because it forces the soul to turn back to its Lord with a sincerity that may be lacking in comfort. For many, spiritual awakening begins in the crucible of suffering. The verse encourages believers to maintain the same sincerity in ease that they naturally have in hardship. The hypocritical human tendency is to remember God intensely when in trouble and forget Him when the trouble passes, a theme the following verses will develop.

The phrase “to Him alone you cry” uses a grammatical structure that emphasizes exclusivity, capturing the essence of the natural, untainted prayer of a soul in distress.

=54=

This verse exposes the tragic and all-too-common human response to divine mercy. “Then as soon as He removes the hardship from you” describes the moment of relief. Allah, in His mercy, answers the desperate cry and lifts the calamity—the storm subsides, the illness is cured, the danger passes. The immediate response one would logically expect is overwhelming gratitude, a strengthening of exclusive worship, and a life dedicated to the One who saved them. But the verse describes a different reality: “a group of you associates others with their Lord in worship.

” The phrase “a group of you” indicates that not all fall back into this error, but a significant portion do. Once they feel safe and secure again, they return to their old habits of shirk, setting up partners alongside Allah, the very God who just delivered them.

This is the height of ingratitude and irrationality. The imagery is of a drowning man who calls upon God to save him, promising devotion, and then, upon reaching the shore, turns his back and bows to a statue. This pattern was visible among the Quraysh, who would invoke Allah sincerely when in distress at sea or in the desert, but upon returning safely to Mecca, would resume their idol worship.

Yet the verse is universal in its application. It describes a human tendency that persists across generations. Many people turn to God in foxhole prayers but become secular or idolatrous once the crisis is over. The verse serves as a mirror for the reader to examine their own life: do we remember Allah more intensely when we are ill or in trouble, and then become heedless when we are healthy and prosperous?

The word “associates” shows that they are not abandoning Allah entirely but mixing His worship with that of others, which is exactly what the Quraysh did. This is a severe warning that such behaviour is not a small slip but a fundamental betrayal of the natural covenant made in the moment of distress.

The verse also highlights the patience and forbearance of Allah, who, despite this repeated ingratitude, continues to rescue people when they call. However, the following verse will make clear that this pattern cannot continue forever without consequence.

=55=

This verse explains the motivation behind the behaviour described in the previous verse and delivers a stern warning. “Only returning Our favours with ingratitude” clarifies that the idolatry committed after the removal of hardship is a direct act of ingratitude for the very favour of deliverance.

The Arabic word for ingratitude is yakfurū, which is also the root for disbelief. This linguistic link shows that ingratitude and disbelief are deeply intertwined. The favour that saved their lives, restored their health, or secured their safety is repaid not with thanks but with the worship of others, which is the ultimate form of kufr.

This is not a passive forgetting; it is an active recompensing of good with evil. Allah describes it as “returning Our favours,” meaning that they take the blessing and use it as a foundation for further rebellion.

The verse then concludes with a chilling, sarcastic imperative: “So enjoy yourselves, for you will soon know. ” This is not a permission but a threat. The command “enjoy” is a form of istidrāj, a divine strategy where a sinner is given increasing blessings and time, which only increases their heedlessness, leading them step by step to their ultimate destruction.

The phrase “for you will soon know” points to the certainty of future punishment. Whether in this world, at the moment of death, or on the Day of Judgment, they will come to know the reality of what they denied.

The word “soon” indicates the brevity of worldly life; compared to eternity, even a long life is a fleeting moment of enjoyment before the inevitable reckoning. This verse is profoundly sobering. It warns believers never to become complacent or to misinterpret a comfortable life as a sign of Allah’s pleasure if it is accompanied by disobedience. The enjoyment of this world is short, and its consequences are eternal.

The verse also teaches that ingratitude is a serious sin that can invite divine punishment. The proper response to blessings and relief from hardship is not a temporary turning to God but a permanent transformation of life, marked by continuous gratitude and exclusive worship. Every time a believer experiences relief from a difficulty, they should remember this verse and ensure that their behaviour does not fall into the pattern of those who “enjoy” and forget.

=56=

This verse exposes a specific, egregious practice of the polytheists. “And they even assign to those idols—who know nothing—a share of what We have provided for them.” The Quraysh would dedicate a portion of their crops, livestock, and produce to their idols, setting it aside as an offering. At the same time, they would allocate another portion to Allah. This division of provision was a direct act of shirk in the economic sphere. They treated their idols as partners in the blessings that Allah alone had provided. The parenthetical phrase “who know nothing” emphasizes the absurdity: these idols are utterly ignorant, yet the polytheists grant them a share in the sustenance that the All-Knowing Creator bestowed. The idols cannot benefit from these offerings, nor do they know the intentions behind them. This practice also involved arbitrary prohibitions, where they declared certain animals or crops forbidden based on their own fabricated rules, attributing these prohibitions to the idols.

The verse then swears by Allah: “By Allah! You will certainly be questioned about whatever lies you used to fabricate against Allah.” This divine oath adds the highest degree of solemnity and certainty to the threat. The questioning will not be a casual inquiry but a severe interrogation on the Day of Judgment, where they will have to account for every lie they invented—claiming that Allah has partners, that certain foods are divinely forbidden, and that their idols deserve a share of their wealth.

The word “fabricate” indicates that they were not merely mistaken but actively constructed falsehoods. This verse is a powerful warning against all forms of religious innovation and false attribution to Allah. In a modern context, it condemns any act of dedicating one’s wealth or resources to causes or entities that are set up as rivals to God, as well as making unlawful what Allah has made lawful or vice versa based on human whims.

The verse also teaches that believers will be questioned about their financial choices: did they spend their provision in ways that please Allah, or did they assign shares to false gods, whether materialistic ideologies, ego, or corrupt institutions? The oath “By Allah!” brings a sense of urgency and fear, reminding believers to audit their allocation of resources and ensure that everything is directed solely towards the One who provided it all.

=57=

This verse shifts to another blasphemous claim of the Meccan pagans. Some Arab tribes, particularly the Quraysh and others, believed that the angels were female beings, and they claimed these angels were the daughters of Allah. “And they attribute angels as daughters to Allah” exposes this absurdity. They took the most honoured, pure, and obedient servants of Allah—the angels—and invented a familial relationship, calling them daughters of God. The immediate interjection “glory be to Him!” is a declaration of Allah’s absolute transcendence and freedom from such false attributions. He is far above having children, whether sons or daughters, as He is the Self-Sufficient, the Eternal, who neither begets nor is begotten.

The verse then demolishes their claim with a simple logical point: this attribution is “the opposite of what they desire for themselves.” The pagan Arabs despised having daughters. They considered daughters a source of shame and disgrace, as the following verses will graphically illustrate. They would bury their baby girls alive out of fear of dishonour.

Yet they had the audacity to assign to Allah what they themselves detested. This is a double injustice: first, claiming that Allah has offspring at all, and second, claiming that His offspring are of the gender they consider inferior and shameful. The argument is a rational and psychological slam against their own inconsistency. If daughters are so despised, why would they insult the Creator by attributing them to Him? The verse exposes the moral and intellectual bankruptcy of their theology.

For Muslims, this verse reinforces the core doctrine of tawḥīd in Allah’s essence: He is unique, without any partner, parent, or child. The practice of ascribing human qualities or family structures to God is a pagan residue that must be rejected.

The verse also critiques cultural misogyny by highlighting that their own disdain for daughters made their attribution to Allah even more heinous. It implicitly calls for a revaluation of the status of women: the very attitude that makes a man ashamed of a daughter is what makes his claim about God so monstrous. The “glory be to Him!” reminds the believer to constantly exalt Allah above all human projections and limitations.

=58=

Having exposed the contradiction of attributing daughters to Allah while hating them for themselves, the sūrah now paints a vivid, unflinching portrait of the pagan attitude towards female infants. “Whenever one of them is given the good news of a baby girl” uses the phrase “good news” with deep irony. The birth of a child is conventionally a cause for celebration, something for which one is congratulated. But for the pre-Islamic Arab, the news of a daughter was the opposite of good. The verse describes the immediate physical reaction: “his face grows gloomy.

” The Arabic muswaddan literally means “turned black,” reflecting the darkness of anger, shame, and depression that visibly overcomes his face. This is a physiological manifestation of his inner turmoil. He receives the news not with joy but with a feeling of disgrace.

The second phrase, “as he suppresses his rage,” is crucial. The word kaẓīm means someone who is choking with anger, filling with suppressed fury. He is not just sad; he is enraged by the birth of a daughter. He feels humiliated before his peers because a daughter is seen as a liability, a potential source of scandal, and an economic burden in a society that valued male warriors and sons to carry on the family name. He suppresses this rage only because of social constraints; the full expression of his anger might bring even more shame. This verse is a damning indictment of a culture that valued women so little that their very existence was considered a misfortune. It sets the stage for the next verse’s description of the horrific consequence of this mindset. By describing the scene with such emotional precision, the Qur’an calls its listeners to witness the ugliness of this custom and to feel revulsion towards it. It prepares the ground for the Islamic reform that would elevate the status of women, condemn female infanticide, and declare that the birth of a daughter is a divine blessing. For contemporary readers, this verse is a historical mirror that shows how far a society can deviate from natural human affection when it is governed by false concepts of honour and worth. It also reminds believers that every child, male or female, is a gift from Allah, and the only appropriate response is gratitude, not gloom or suppressed rage.

=59=

This verse continues the tragic narrative of the father who received news of a daughter’s birth. “He hides himself from the people because of the bad news he has received.” His shame is so profound that he withdraws from public view. He cannot face his community with what he perceives as a humiliating event. The “bad news” is the birth of the baby girl, which to him is a catastrophe. In his isolation, he wrestles with a terrible dilemma, captured in the question: “Should he keep her in disgrace, or bury her alive in the ground?

” The first option, to keep her, is equated with disgrace. He would have to endure the social stigma and the supposed burden of raising a daughter.

The second option, burying her alive, was the horrifying practice of wa’d al-banāt, female infanticide, which was prevalent among some Arab tribes. Fathers would take their newborn daughters and bury them in the sand until they died, a practice driven by fear of poverty and a twisted sense of honour. The fact that this is presented as a genuine, agonising choice reveals the depth of the societal depravity.

The verse then delivers the divine verdict: “Evil indeed is their judgment! ” The word “evil” (sā’a) is a categorical condemnation. Whatever choice they make within this framework is evil; even keeping her in disgrace is not a righteous act if it is done with resentment and a sense of humiliation. The ultimate evil is, of course, the murder. This Qur’anic passage is one of the most powerful denunciations of female infanticide and misogyny in any scripture. It does not merely prohibit the practice; it exposes the inner ugliness of the mindset behind it, the gloom, the rage, the shame, and the monstrous deliberation. By revealing this divine disgust, the verse planted the seeds for a radical transformation. Islam categorically forbade the killing of children and declared the birth of a daughter a mercy. The Prophet Muḥammad famously said that whoever raises daughters with kindness will be with him in Paradise like two fingers. This verse remains profoundly relevant in societies where daughters are still viewed as inferior or where gendercide occurs. It stands as an eternal witness against the devaluation of female life and a call to honor every child as a trust from Allah.

=60=

This verse concludes the section on the pagan misogyny and false attributions to Allah by drawing a sharp contrast between the character of disbelievers and the character of God. “To those who disbelieve in the Hereafter belong all evil qualities” translates the Arabic mathal as-saw’, meaning the worst possible description or attribute. Their entire worldview, built on the denial of accountability, produces evil outcomes: female infanticide, idol worship, ingratitude, arrogance, and the attribution of daughters to God while despising daughters for themselves. The root cause is their disbelief in the Hereafter. Because they do not believe they will be judged, they have no moral compass, and their values become inverted.

The phrase “all evil qualities” could also refer specifically to their own statement that daughters are a curse and a shame; this evil description belongs to them, not to Allah. In contrast, “whereas to Allah belong the finest attributes.

” The word mathal al-aʿlā, the highest description, refers to the attributes of absolute perfection, such as power, knowledge, wisdom, mercy, and transcendence. Allah is free from every imperfection they ascribe to Him. He is far above having children, feeling shame, or possessing any quality that is deficient.

The verse then seals this contrast with two of His names: “And He is the Almighty, All-Wise.” The Almighty (al-ʿAzīz) signifies that He is fully capable of punishing those who attribute such evil to Him, yet His punishment is delayed. The All-Wise (al-Ḥakīm) indicates that all His actions, including the respite given to the wrongdoers, are based on perfect wisdom. This concluding statement serves as both a warning and a comfort. The disbelievers’ evil descriptions and actions will not go unpunished, but the timing and method are determined by divine wisdom.

For the believer, this verse invites a deep reflection on the relationship between belief in the Hereafter and moral character. When the Hereafter is denied, moral standards plummet to accommodate human desires. When it is affirmed, the believer strives to emulate the divine attributes of mercy, justice, and generosity, as far as humanly possible.

The verse also teaches that one’s concept of God must be purified, attributing to Him only the highest perfection and rejecting any anthropomorphic or degrading descriptions, whether from ancient paganism or modern misconceptions.
