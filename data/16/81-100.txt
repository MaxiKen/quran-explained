=81=

This verse gathers several further blessings that demonstrate the completeness of Allah’s care, moving from homes and furnishings to shade, natural shelters, clothing, and armour. “And Allah has provided you shade out of what He created” points to the many things in creation that cast shade and protect from the harsh sun. In the Arabian desert, shade was not a luxury but a necessity for survival. Trees, clouds, mountains, and even the walls of houses are all among the things Allah created to provide relief from heat. The believer is invited to see a simple patch of shade not as a random physical phenomenon but as a deliberate gift from the Creator who knew His servants would need coolness and rest.

The verse then says, “and has given you shelter in the mountains.” This refers to the caves and natural recesses in the mountains that served as refuges for travellers, hermits, and even prophets. Mountains are not just majestic stabilisers of the earth but also natural fortresses offering protection.

The verse then mentions clothing: “He has also provided you with clothes protecting you from the heat and cold.” The Arabic mentions heat specifically, with cold understood as the opposite extreme. Clothing is a basic blessing that provides modesty and protection from the elements.

The mention of heat as the primary threat resonates with the desert context, but the principle applies universally. Then the verse adds “and armour shielding you in battle.” This shifts from protection against nature to protection against human harm. Coats of mail, shields, and helmets are part of Allah’s provision, inspired to humanity through the same creative wisdom that taught the bee to build its hive. The verse ties these together: “This is how He perfects His favour upon you, so perhaps you will fully submit to Him. ” The perfection of favour means that Allah has not left any essential need unaddressed. The purpose of this comprehensive care is to move human hearts to complete submission, Islam. When a person realises that their shade, their shelter, their clothing, and even their safety in war are all from Allah, the natural response is to surrender wholly to Him.

The verse teaches that gratitude should lead to submission, not just a fleeting “thank you. ” The believer who sees a tree’s shadow or puts on a coat should remember the Perfecter of favours and renew their commitment to live in obedience.

=82=

After listing the multitude of blessings that should logically lead to submission, the sūrah now addresses the reality of rejection. The verse begins with “But if they turn away,” referring to those who, despite all the signs and favours, still refuse to believe and submit. This turning away is not a passive neglect but an active aversion, a deliberate choice to reject the truth.

The verse then comforts the Prophet and defines the boundaries of his responsibility: “then your duty O Prophet is only to deliver the message clearly. ” The word “only” (innamā) restricts the Prophet’s obligation to clear conveyance. He is not responsible for forcing people to believe, for guiding their hearts, or for the ultimate outcome of their choices. The message has been delivered with clarity, supported by rational arguments, natural signs, and historical warnings. The eloquence and truth of the Qur’an are evident. If people still turn away, the Prophet is not to blame. This verse was crucial for the psychological well-being of the Prophet, who grieved deeply over the rejection of his people. It relieved him of a burden he was never meant to carry.

This principle is reiterated in several places in the Qur’an, emphasizing that guidance is solely in Allah’s hands. The duty of the messenger is to convey, and he fulfilled that duty perfectly. For all callers to Islam, this verse provides a model: one must strive to deliver the message with wisdom and clarity, but one must not become despondent or feel personally responsible when people reject it. The clarity of the delivery is the standard; the response is between the individual and Allah.

The verse also implies a warning to those who turn away: they cannot claim they were not warned or that the message was ambiguous. The clear delivery leaves them with no excuse. They turn away despite understanding, and thus their accountability is complete.

The verse also teaches that the value of our work is not measured solely by the results in terms of conversions, but by the faithfulness and clarity with which we convey the truth. The Prophet’s mission was a resounding success not because everyone believed, but because he delivered the message with perfect clarity, leaving a lasting proof for humanity. This liberates the modern believer from the tyranny of results, focusing instead on the quality of effort and sincerity.

=83=

This verse diagnoses the spiritual sickness of those who turn away after the clear delivery of the message. “They are aware of Allah’s favours, but still deny them” points to a knowing rejection. The disbelievers of Mecca, and by extension all who reject faith after clear evidence, do not lack information. They recognize the blessings in their lives—the rain, the cattle, the home, the shade—and they know deep within that these come from Allah.

The Arabic word yaʿrifūn indicates a deep, experiential awareness. They are not ignorant; they are in a state of wilful denial. This denial is not merely verbal but is expressed through their actions: they worship other beings, they fail to give thanks, and they use Allah’s gifts in disobedience.

The verse then adds, “And most of them are truly ungrateful. ” The word kāfirūn, translated as ungrateful, is the same word for disbelievers, revealing the intimate link between ingratitude and disbelief. To be ungrateful for Allah’s favours is a form of kufr, a covering up of the truth. The verse says “most of them,” indicating that there were some among the Quraysh who, despite the general atmosphere, were sincere seekers or who later embraced Islam. But the majority persisted in ingratitude. This verse is a profound reflection on human psychology. It is possible to live surrounded by blessings, to intellectually acknowledge their source, and yet to live as a practical atheist, giving devotion and ultimate thanks to other things—one’s own efforts, luck, nature, or false gods.

This is the tragedy of the human condition: knowledge that does not translate into gratitude and worship is a deeper form of ignorance. The verse invites believers to examine their own lives: do we “know” that our health, family, and provision are from Allah, yet fail to demonstrate that knowledge in our daily worship, charity, and obedience? The verse warns against the danger of familiarity; the Quraysh were so accustomed to the favours that they took them for granted and denied their significance. The cure is the constant, conscious practice of shukr, gratitude, which keeps the heart alive to the reality of the Giver.

The verse also serves as a warning that intellectual recognition without heartfelt gratitude and submission is a path to ruin.

=84=

The sūrah now turns to scenes from the Day of Judgment to underscore the consequences of the ingratitude and denial just described. The verse begins with “Consider, O Prophet,” an interjection to draw attention to the gravity of the coming day. “The Day We will call a prophet as a witness from every faith-community” describes a universal courtroom scene. Every nation that received a messenger will have that messenger brought forward as a witness. The prophet will testify about his people: who believed, who disbelieved, who obeyed, and who rejected. He will confirm that he delivered the message clearly and will bear witness to their responses.

This is both a vindication for the messengers and a devastating proof against the deniers. The prophets, who were mocked and persecuted in the world, will be honoured as the key witnesses, and their testimony will be decisive.

The verse then states the immediate consequence: “Then the disbelievers will neither be allowed to plead nor appease their Lord.” On that Day, the disbelievers will be silenced. They will not be given permission to make excuses or argue their case, because the evidence is overwhelming and their own prophet’s testimony is against them.

The word “appease” translates yuʿtabūn, which can mean to seek to please or make amends. They will not be given an opportunity to offer excuses or compensation to escape punishment. In the world, they might have talked their way out of trouble, but on that Day, mouths will be sealed and the facts will speak for themselves. This verse is a sobering reminder of the finality of divine judgment. The presence of the prophet as a witness underscores the mercy inherent in the sending of messengers; they are not just warners but also witnesses for or against their people. For the believers, this scene brings comfort: the truth for which they were ridiculed will be publicly vindicated, and the prophets they followed will stand as their advocates. For the deniers, it is a terrifying prospect: the very person they rejected will be the one whose testimony seals their fate. The verse encourages believers to live in a way that will make their prophet’s testimony a source of joy, not sorrow, on that Day.

=85=

This verse continues the description of the disbelievers’ state on the Day of Judgment. “When the wrongdoers face the punishment” uses the word ẓalamū, those who wronged themselves through disbelief and sin. The moment they are confronted with the reality of the torment they used to deny, their ordeal begins. The verse immediately eliminates any hope of relief: “it will not be lightened for them. ” The Arabic yukhaffaf indicates a reduction in intensity or duration. In this world, even the most severe suffering often has moments of respite, or one can hope for recovery or death to end the pain. In Hell, there is no such alleviation. The punishment is constant, unrelenting, and at full force.

This is because their crimes were not temporary lapses but a persistent state of denial and ingratitude that defined their lives. The second part of the verse, “nor will they be delayed from it,” addresses a different hope: perhaps the punishment could be postponed, giving them a break or a chance to negotiate. On the Day of Judgment, once the judgment is passed, there is no delay. The punishment begins and continues without reprieve. This verse underscores the severity and finality of divine justice. It also highlights the mercy of Allah in the world, where punishments are often delayed and opportunities for repentance are abundant. The contrast is stark: in this life, a sinner can wake up the next morning, breathe fresh air, and turn back to Allah. That window of delay is a gift. Once the Hereafter begins, that window closes forever.

For the believer, this verse is a powerful motivator to hasten to good deeds and repentance. It instills a healthy fear that prevents complacency. The wrongdoers in this context are those who rejected the clear message after being fully aware of Allah’s favours, as described in the previous verses.

The verse also provides a perspective on the sufferings of this world: even the worst worldly pain is temporary and can be lightened by hope or death; the punishment of the next world offers no such escape. This realization should reorient one’s priorities, making the avoidance of that eternal punishment the central concern of life. The verse is a pure, unadorned warning that leaves no ambiguity about the fate of those who die in a state of persistent, unrepented wrongdoing.

=86=

This verse zooms in on a specific, dramatic moment for the polytheists on the Day of Judgment. “When the polytheists see their associate-gods” indicates that the idols or beings they worshipped will be brought before them. Some commentators say that Allah will give life to the idols or that the false gods, whether devils, jinn, or even righteous beings who were worshipped, will be present. The polytheists, in a desperate attempt to shift blame or perhaps in a moment of confused hope, will cry out, “Our Lord! These are our associate-gods that we used to invoke besides You.” They point to those they worshipped, hoping perhaps that these beings will acknowledge them or intercede.

This is a pitiful scene of abandonment. The response is immediate and shattering: “Their gods will throw a rebuttal at them, saying, ‘You are definitely liars.’” The word “throw” suggests a forceful rejection. The very objects of their worship will disown them and brand them as liars.

This is the ultimate betrayal. The false gods, if they are devils or misguided leaders, will deny that they ever asked to be worshipped or that they had any divine authority. If they are idols, Allah will give them speech to refute their worshippers, declaring that the polytheists’ claims about them were lies. The polytheists used to claim that these idols were intermediaries or partners with Allah, but on that Day, the truth will be spoken by the very things they fashioned. This scene is deeply ironic and tragic. The beings they devoted their lives to, fought for, and sacrificed for, will turn against them. This verse exposes the utter bankruptcy of shirk. In the world, the polytheist invests so much hope and identity in false gods, but in the Hereafter, that investment yields only public humiliation and rejection.

For the believer, this verse is a powerful reinforcement of tawḥīd. It also serves as a warning about the attachments of the heart: anything we cling to besides Allah—whether a human idol, a career, a political ideology—will ultimately fail us and testify against our misplaced devotion. The only loyalty that endures is loyalty to the truth. The verse encourages the believer to sever any hidden attachment to false hopes and to anchor the heart solely in Allah, who will never deny His sincere servants.

=87=

This verse continues the scene of the polytheists’ despair. After the shocking rebuttal from their associate-gods, “They will offer full submission to Allah on that Day.

” The word “submission” translates aslamū, from the same root as Islam. On that Day, every being, willingly or unwillingly, will submit to the undeniable reality of Allah’s sovereignty. The polytheists who spent their lives in arrogant rebellion will finally bow in complete surrender. Their tongues, which used to mock the idea of submission, will now confess the truth. But this submission is not the voluntary, faith-based Islam that benefits a person in the world. It is the compelled submission of one who has no other choice, a recognition of reality when it is too late to change one’s destiny.

The second part of the verse delivers the outcome: “and whatever gods they fabricated will fail them.” The word “fail” translates ḍalla, meaning to be lost, to vanish, to be of no avail. The gods they invented—whether carved from stone, imagined in myths, or elevated from among the jinn and humans—will disappear from their sight and their grasp. They will be utterly alone, with no intercessor, no protector, no helper. The investment of a lifetime of worship, offerings, and devotion will prove to be a mirage. This verse encapsulates the entire message of the sūrah regarding shirk. The false gods are not merely powerless; they actively abandon their worshippers at the critical moment. The submission that the polytheists finally offer is a tragic counterpart to the voluntary submission of the believers described earlier. The believers submitted in the world, unseen, based on faith, and will be rewarded with peace and Paradise. The polytheists submit when the unseen becomes seen, and they receive only loss. For the contemporary reader, this verse prompts a deep reflection on what one is ultimately serving. Many modern “fabricated gods”—such as money, status, or the self—will similarly vanish when death arrives. The only reality that remains is Allah. The verse calls for a proactive, voluntary Islam now, while the choice is meaningful and the submission can be born of love and hope rather than forced despair. It is a reminder that the time for choosing one’s ultimate allegiance is limited to this world, and the fabricated gods that seem so real today will be exposed as nothing on the Day when all masks fall.

=88=

This verse pronounces a heightened punishment for a specific category of disbelievers: those who not only disbelieve themselves but actively prevent others from believing. “Those who disbelieve and hinder others from the Way of Allah” refers to the leaders of disbelief, the propagandists, the persecutors who blocked people from Islam. The Quraysh chieftains, like Abū Jahl and Abū Lahab, not only rejected the Prophet but stationed men to stop pilgrims from hearing the Qur’an, tortured converts to make them recant, and waged a relentless campaign of obstruction. The “Way of Allah” is the path of Islam, which leads to Him. Hindering from it is a grievous sin because it multiplies evil; the misguidance spreads, affecting generations. The verse declares the consequence: “We will add more punishment to their punishment.” This means that on top of the torment they deserve for their own disbelief, they will receive an extra dose of punishment for each person they misled or obstructed. The principle of double or multiple punishment for misleaders is also mentioned in other verses. The addition is described as being “for all the corruption they spread.

” The word fasād, corruption, encompasses the moral, social, and spiritual chaos they caused. By suppressing the truth and promoting falsehood, they corrupted the natural order of society and the souls of individuals. The “addition” of punishment is a terrifying concept; the punishment of Hell is already unimaginable, and to have it multiplied is a fate beyond human comprehension. This verse serves as a severe deterrent against any form of obstructing the path to God, whether through mockery, coercion, producing misleading media, or misrepresenting Islam to turn people away. It also applies to those who, within the Muslim community, create obstacles for people seeking religious knowledge or practicing their faith. Conversely, it implies a great reward for those who facilitate the Way of Allah and make it easy for others to come to faith. The verse is a powerful reminder of social responsibility: our actions regarding faith are not private; they ripple outwards. The Quraysh leaders thought they were defending their heritage; they were actually multiplying their own torment. The believer who reads this is motivated to be a means of guidance, not hindrance, and to use whatever influence they have to remove obstacles from the path of those sincerely seeking Allah.

=89=

This verse resumes the theme of witnesses from verse 84, with a direct and weighty address to the Prophet Muḥammad. “Consider, O Prophet, the Day We will call against every faith-community a witness of their own.” Every ummah will have its own prophet brought forward to testify. This witness will be “of their own,” meaning from among them, speaking their language, having lived among them, so his testimony is intimate and undeniable. Then comes the specific and awe-inspiring declaration: “And We will call you to be a witness against these people of yours.” The Prophet Muḥammad will be the witness over his own community, the Quraysh and all who came after him until the Day of Judgment. His testimony will encompass who believed, who rejected, who obeyed, and who innovated.

This is a tremendous honour for the Prophet and a heavy responsibility for his ummah. The verse then shifts to a description of the Qur’an that underpins this witness: “We have revealed to you the Book as an explanation of all things, a guide, a mercy, and good news for those who fully submit.” The Qur’an is described with four attributes. First, it is “an explanation of all things” (tibyānan li-kulli shay’), meaning it clarifies every fundamental matter of faith, ethics, and law, either in detail or through principles. Second, it is “a guide,” showing the straight path. Third, it is “a mercy,” because following it leads to eternal bliss and even worldly peace. Fourth, it is “good news” for the submitters, promising them Paradise and Allah’s pleasure.

The phrase “those who fully submit” (muslimūn) identifies the intended beneficiaries. The verse ties the Prophet’s role as witness to the clarity and completeness of the revelation he brought. He delivered the Book that explains everything; therefore, no one can claim ignorance. The verse is both a comfort to the Prophet, reminding him of his high station on that Day, and a warning to the community to live according to the Book, so that their prophet’s testimony is favourable.

For Muslims today, this verse instills a profound sense of accountability: we will be among those about whom the Prophet testifies. The Qur’an, which we have in our hands, is the very Book described as an explanation of all things, and our response to it will be witnessed.

=90=

This verse is one of the most comprehensive summaries of Islamic ethics in the Qur’an, often recited in the Friday sermon. “Indeed, Allah commands justice” begins with the foundational principle of ʿadl. Justice means giving everyone their due, whether in legal rulings, business dealings, or personal relationships. It requires fairness, impartiality, and upholding the rights of others, even against one’s own interests. Then the command elevates: “grace” (iḥsān), which is excellence and benevolence beyond what is strictly due. Iḥsān means to do good, to forgive, to be generous, and to worship Allah as if you see Him. It is the higher level of morality where one not only refrains from harm but actively seeks to benefit others.

The third command is “courtesy to close relatives” (ītā’i dhī al-qurbā), which emphasizes maintaining family ties, supporting kin, and showing special kindness to relatives. This triad of commands—justice, excellence, and family care—forms the positive moral framework.

The verse then moves to prohibitions: “He forbids indecency” (faḥshā’), which includes all shameful acts, particularly sexual immorality and obscenity. “Wickedness” (munkar) is any act universally condemned by sound reason and revelation, encompassing major sins. “Aggression” (baghy) is transgression against others, oppression, and the unjust use of power.

The verse then states the purpose of this moral instruction: “He instructs you so perhaps you will be mindful.” The goal is mindfulness, taẕakkur, which is a continuous awareness of Allah’s commands and prohibitions, leading to righteous conduct. This verse is profound in its breadth and balance. It combines the minimum standard (justice), the ideal standard (excellence), and the specific social bond (family). The prohibitions cover personal morality, public decency, and social justice. It is a complete ethical charter. According to some early commentators, this verse is so encompassing that if there were no other verse in the Qur’an, it would suffice as a guide to righteousness. For contemporary life, this verse offers a clear moral compass in a world of ethical confusion. Every decision, whether in policy, business, or personal life, can be measured against these commands. The believer is called to be a person of justice, to go beyond it with grace, to cherish family ties, and to avoid the triple evils of indecency, wickedness, and aggression.

The verse ends with the hope that this reminder will lead to a state of constant God-consciousness.

=91=

Following the comprehensive ethical commands, the sūrah now addresses the sanctity of oaths and covenants. “Honour Allah’s covenant when you make a pledge” uses the phrase “Allah’s covenant” (ʿahd Allāh) to elevate the seriousness of any solemn pledge, particularly those made in the name of Allah or with Allah as a witness. This includes the covenant of faith itself, the pledge of allegiance to the Prophet, treaties, and personal vows. The command to “honour” means to fulfill the pledge completely, without cheating or breaking it.

The verse then adds, “and do not break your oaths after confirming them.” The emphasis is on the moment after a solemn oath has been affirmed; to break it afterward is a sign of weak character and disregard for the divine witness.

The phrase “having made Allah your guarantor” heightens the responsibility. When a person swears by Allah or makes Allah a witness to their promise, they are invoking the highest authority. To then violate that oath is to show contempt for that authority.

The final clause, “Surely Allah knows all you do,” is both a reassurance for the faithful who keep their word and a warning for those who break it. Nothing is hidden from Him, and every broken oath is recorded. This verse was revealed in a context where the pagan Arabs had a custom of making alliances and treaties, but often broke them when it was convenient. The Muslims are commanded to be a people of their word, whose promises are ironclad.

The verse also has a deeper spiritual dimension: the covenant with Allah is the primordial testimony of tawḥīd that every soul has made, and breaking it through shirk is the ultimate betrayal of an oath. In daily life, the verse teaches that a Muslim’s word should be reliable. Business contracts, marriage vows, political treaties, and even casual promises are all encompassed. The modern world often treats promises as flexible, but this verse calls for a radical integrity.

The phrase “having made Allah your guarantor” is a powerful psychological barrier against breaking promises; it reminds the believer that the One who hears the oath will also hold them accountable. The verse also encourages carefulness before making an oath; one should not swear lightly, but when one does, fulfilment is a sacred duty.

=92=

This verse uses a vivid metaphor to illustrate the folly of breaking oaths. “Do not be like the woman who foolishly unravels her yarn after it is firmly spun.

” The image is of a woman who has painstakingly spun thread, twisting the fibres together until they form a strong, cohesive cord. Then, in a moment of folly, she unpicks her own work, reducing the strong yarn back to useless, tangled fibres. This metaphor is applied directly: “by taking your oaths as a means of deceiving one another in favour of a stronger group.” This refers to the pre-Islamic practice where a tribe would make a treaty with another, but if a stronger tribe appeared, they would break the first oath to ally with the more powerful group. The oath, which should have been a bond of trust, was treated as a tool for opportunistic deception. The unraveling of the yarn is the destruction of trust and the moral fabric of society.

The verse then says, “Surely Allah tests you through this.” The test is in the area of integrity. Will people keep their word when it is disadvantageous, or will they break it for worldly gain? The differing circumstances—a weak ally versus a strong one—expose the true content of character.

The verse concludes, “And on the Day of Judgment He will certainly make your differences clear to you.” The disputes and betrayals that occur over broken treaties and oaths will be fully adjudicated by Allah, who will reveal the truth and assign blame where it belongs. This verse is a powerful lesson in the ethics of commitment. The metaphor of the unraveled yarn is particularly striking because it shows that breaking an oath is not just a harm to the other party but a self-destructive act that undoes one’s own moral integrity. The effort of building trust is wasted. In contemporary contexts, this applies to all forms of contractual and personal dishonesty: breaking business agreements when a better deal comes along, betraying confidences for social gain, or reneging on promises when they become inconvenient.

The verse teaches that such behaviour is foolish, not clever, because it unravels one’s character and invites divine scrutiny. The test is a constant reality, and the Day of Judgment will expose the true winners and losers in the economy of trust.

=93=

This verse returns to the theme of divine will and human responsibility, connecting it to the preceding discussion of oaths and differences. “Had Allah willed, He could have easily made you one community of believers.” This asserts that Allah has the power to create all humanity as a single, uniformly believing community, without any differences in faith. He could have made humans like angels, programmed to obey without free choice. But He did not will that. Instead, He granted free will and revealed guidance.

The verse continues, “but He leaves to stray whoever He wills and guides whoever He wills.” This statement must be understood within the Qur’anic framework: Allah’s will to guide or leave astray is based on His wisdom and justice. Those who sincerely seek truth are guided; those who persistently reject it are left to stray. Allah does not randomly misguide; the straying is a consequence of the individual’s own choice, which Allah then seals.

The verse then makes it personal: “And you will certainly be questioned about what you used to do.” The address is plural, encompassing all humans. This shifts the focus from the divine decree to human accountability. Despite the overarching divine will, every person is responsible for their actions and choices, and will be questioned about them. The differences among people, including the breaking of oaths and the disputes mentioned previously, do not negate individual responsibility. This verse elegantly balances the doctrines of predestination and free will. It answers the fatalistic argument used by the polytheists who said, “Had Allah willed, we would not have worshipped other than Him.” The response is that Allah’s will to give free choice does not excuse wrong choices. You will be questioned about what you did.

For the believer, this verse inspires a balanced mindset: complete trust in Allah’s overarching will, combined with a keen sense of personal responsibility. One should not use divine decree as an excuse for sin or passivity. The fact that Allah could have made us all guided but did not, means that our current state of belief is a test, and our deeds are the measure of our success.

The verse also fosters humility: guidance is a gift from Allah, not a personal achievement, so one should constantly pray for steadfastness and not feel superior over those who are astray, since it is Allah who guides.

=94=

The sūrah returns to the specific warning about oaths, reinforcing the earlier metaphor with a more direct threat. “And do not take your oaths as a means of deceiving one another” repeats the prohibition of using solemn pledges for fraud.

The phrase “or your feet will slip after they have been firm” uses a powerful image. Feet that were firmly planted on the path of faith and integrity will slip and stumble. The breaking of oaths leads to a spiritual fall. A person who had been a believer, or at least appeared to be on the right path, can lose their footing entirely through consistent deceit. The “slip” is both in this world—loss of reputation, trust, and inner peace—and in the Hereafter—falling into Hell.

The verse then specifies the consequence: “Then you will taste the evil consequences of hindering others from the Way of Allah. ” The link between oath-breaking and hindering from Allah’s way is clarified. When people see a believer breaking oaths and acting treacherously, they are repelled from Islam. The bad example becomes an obstacle.

Thus, the oath-breaker sins not only personally but causes others to turn away from faith, incurring the multiplied punishment mentioned earlier. The verse climaxes, “and you will suffer a tremendous punishment.” The adjective ʿaẓīm magnifies the torment beyond ordinary description. This severe warning shows the gravity of deceit and its ripple effects. In the Meccan context, the Muslims were a small, vulnerable community. Their integrity was one of their greatest strengths. If they resorted to the same treacherous tactics as the pagans, they would lose the moral high ground and their dawah would be crippled. This verse therefore protects the community’s ethical fabric. For contemporary Muslims, the lesson remains vital: personal dishonesty is not a private matter. When a Muslim cheats in business, lies in personal relationships, or breaks promises, they not only harm themselves but also damage the reputation of Islam and become a barrier to others accepting the faith. The “tremendous punishment” is proportionate to the damage caused. The verse calls for a consistent integrity that keeps the feet firm on the path, recognising that a single slip through deceit can lead to a cascade of negative consequences both personal and communal.

=95=

This verse encapsulates the core motivation behind oath-breaking and offers the divine alternative. “And do not trade Allah’s covenant for a fleeting gain” uses commercial language. The covenant, which includes oaths, promises, and the broader commitment to faith, is a precious asset. The worldly gain—money, power, an alliance with a stronger tribe—is a “fleeting gain,” a small, temporary profit. The imagery is of a foolish trader who exchanges a priceless, eternal treasure for a pittance of perishable goods.

The phrase “Allah’s covenant” elevates every faithful promise to the level of a sacred bond with the Divine. The verse then presents the superior merchandise: “What is with Allah is certainly far better for you, if only you knew.” The reward that Allah has stored for those who keep their covenants—Paradise, His pleasure, and honour in this life—is incomparably greater than any worldly benefit gained through betrayal. The problem is a knowledge deficit: “if only you knew” (law kuntum taʿlamūn). If people truly grasped the value of what is with Allah, they would never trade it for temporary gains. This lack of knowledge is a lack of certainty (yaqīn). The believer who possesses yaqīn sees the Hereafter as more real and more valuable than the material world, and so they easily sacrifice the latter for the former. This verse is a powerful antidote to the temptations of dishonesty. When faced with a situation where breaking one’s word could bring financial profit or social advancement, the believer is reminded that the gain is fleeting, while the loss of Allah’s pleasure is eternal. The verse trains the mind to perform a cost-benefit analysis with an eternal timescale.

The phrase “what is with Allah” includes not just the rewards of Paradise but also the spiritual states of peace, dignity, and divine love that accompany integrity in this life. The verse is a call to elevate one’s ambitions beyond the immediate and the material. For the modern Muslim navigating a competitive, often unethical marketplace, this verse is a constant reference. The temptation to lie on a contract, to betray a partner, or to break a promise for a promotion is the same ancient trade.

The answer is the same: what is with Allah is better, and the fleeting gain will soon evaporate, leaving only regret.

=96=

This verse provides the foundational principle behind the argument of the previous verse. “Whatever you have will end” is a statement of universal truth. All possessions, all relationships, all worldly achievements, and even life itself are temporary and will inevitably perish or be left behind.

The word “end” (yanfadu) implies complete exhaustion. No matter how vast one’s wealth or power, it is finite and running out.

In contrast, “whatever Allah has is everlasting.” The treasures of the Hereafter, the reward, the pleasure of Allah, and the life of Paradise are eternal, never diminishing, never ending.

This contrast is absolute. The fleeting gain of the previous verse is part of “whatever you have,” which is doomed to end. The covenant’s reward is part of “whatever Allah has,” which is everlasting.

The verse then moves to a specific promise: “And We will certainly reward the steadfast according to the best of their deeds.” The steadfast (ṣābirūn) are those who patiently persevere in keeping their covenants, resisting the temptation of fleeting gains, and remaining firm in faith. They will be rewarded “according to the best of their deeds,” which means Allah will look at the finest actions they performed and reward them at that highest level, overlooking the lesser or mixed deeds.

This is an incredible act of divine grace. The verse provides a complete framework for decision-making. When a believer is torn between a worldly benefit and an ethical principle, this verse provides the clarity: the worldly benefit is part of the temporary, exhaustible category; the ethical principle connects to the everlasting. The patient person, who can delay gratification and endure the loss of the fleeting for the sake of the eternal, is promised a reward that is not just equivalent but based on the highest standard of their overall conduct. This verse encourages a lifestyle of renunciation (zuhd) not in the sense of rejecting the world entirely, but in the sense of not attaching the heart to what will inevitably end. The modern believer surrounded by consumer culture can use this verse as a daily meditation. Every time we are tempted to compromise integrity for a material gain, we can ask: is this gain part of “what will end” or “what is everlasting”?

The answer reframes the choice and makes patience not a burden but a wise investment with guaranteed, eternal returns.

=97=

This verse is a beautiful, inclusive promise that expands on the reward mentioned previously. “Whoever does good, whether male or female, and is a believer” establishes that the divine promise of reward transcends gender. Both men and women are equally capable of righteous action and equally eligible for its reward.

The phrase “does good” translates ʿamila ṣāliḥan, meaning righteous deeds performed sincerely for Allah. The essential condition is “and is a believer,” because without faith, deeds lack the foundation that makes them acceptable and eternally rewarded. The promise is twofold. First, “We will surely bless them with a good life.” This good life (ḥayātan ṭayyibah) is a pure, wholesome, and contented existence in this world, even if it involves outward hardship. It includes inner peace, satisfaction with lawful provision, the sweetness of faith, and the ability to endure trials with patience. The good life is not defined by material abundance but by the quality of the heart’s connection to Allah. The believer may have less wealth than a disbeliever, but their life is “good” because it is filled with purpose, tranquillity, and divine companionship. Second, “and We will certainly reward them according to the best of their deeds. ” This echoes the previous verse’s promise about the Hereafter, affirming that the reward will be based on the highest standard of their righteous actions. This verse is a cornerstone of hope and motivation. It demolishes any notion that piety leads to a miserable, deprived life. On the contrary, it promises that true goodness and faith produce the best form of life here and now, as well as the best eternal reward. The contemporary Muslim, facing the pressure to compromise faith for worldly success, is reminded that the “good life” is a direct promise from Allah to the righteous believer. It is a life that many wealthy and powerful people lack, despite their outward affluence.

The verse also encourages both men and women to actively compete in good deeds, knowing that the playing field is level and the reward is certain. The inclusion of gender explicitly counters any cultural bias that might undervalue female piety. The verse is a declaration that the path to the good life and the eternal reward is open to every human being who combines faith with righteous action.

=98=

After the extensive ethical teachings and promises, the sūrah now provides a vital spiritual tool for engaging with the Qur’an itself. “When you recite the Quran” addresses the believers directly, instructing them on the proper etiquette and spiritual preparation for approaching the divine word. The command is “seek refuge with Allah from Satan, the accursed.

” The Arabic phrase is istiʿādhah, a seeking of protection. This instruction acknowledges a spiritual reality: the Qur’an is the source of guidance, and Satan, the sworn enemy of humanity, will do everything in his power to prevent a person from benefiting from it. The accursed one (ar-rajīm) is the one who is expelled from divine mercy, and he attacks the human heart with distractions, doubts, and whispers, especially during the recitation of the Qur’an. He might cause the mind to wander, bring up worldly concerns, cast doubts about the meaning, or induce arrogance or self-admiration. By saying “Aʿūdhu billāhi min ash-shayṭān ir-rajīm” before reciting, the believer consciously disengages from the enemy’s influence and places themselves under Allah’s protection. This act is not merely a ritual formula but a genuine turning of the heart to Allah, acknowledging one’s vulnerability and seeking His shelter. The verse thus teaches that reciting the Qur’an is not just an intellectual activity but a spiritual encounter that requires divine aid. The command is general, applying to every occasion of recitation, whether in prayer, in personal study, or in teaching. This practice became a consistent Sunnah of the Prophet and all Muslims.

The placement of this verse in the sūrah is significant. The preceding verses dealt with oaths, covenants, and the temptations of worldly gain—all areas where Satan is particularly active in leading people astray. Seeking refuge before reciting the Qur’an is like clearing the channel of reception so that the divine message can enter the heart without static. For the modern believer, this verse is a practical reminder that the struggle against Satan’s whispers is ongoing, and the Qur’an is the primary target. One should never approach the Qur’an in a state of heedlessness or overconfidence. The simple, conscious act of seeking refuge is the key that unlocks the door of understanding and spiritual benefit.

=99=

This verse provides the reassurance behind the command to seek refuge. “He” refers to Satan. “He certainly has no authority over those who believe and put their trust in their Lord.

” The word “authority” (sulṭān) means power, control, or compelling influence. Satan cannot force a believer to sin or to disbelieve. He can only whisper, suggest, and beautify evil.

For those who have true faith (īmān) and who actively place their trust in Allah (tawakkul), Satan’s whisperings are ultimately ineffective. Faith serves as a shield, and trust in Allah serves as an active force that repels the enemy. The believer who is attacked by a satanic thought immediately turns to Allah, seeks refuge, and the whisper dissipates. The verse does not say that believers are never tempted; it says Satan has no authority over them, meaning he cannot overcome them or lead them to ultimate ruin. They may stumble at times, but they return to Allah, and their essential allegiance is not to Satan.

This is a profound comfort for the struggling soul. The feeling of being under attack by evil thoughts or desires does not mean Satan has control; it is the resistance and the turning to Allah that proves his lack of authority. The verse mentions two essential qualities: belief and trust. Belief is the intellectual and heart-felt acceptance of the truth. Trust is the practical reliance on Allah in all matters, including the battle against one’s own lower self and Satan. Together, they form an impenetrable fortress. For the contemporary believer facing the relentless onslaught of temptations in modern media and society, this verse is a source of immense strength. It assures that no matter how powerful the external or internal calls to sin may seem, they have no real power over the one who is fortified by faith and trust. The battle is won not by human willpower alone but by anchoring oneself in Allah.

The verse also clarifies the limits of Satan’s power; he is not a rival god but a creature with a limited role, and his machinations are ultimately under Allah’s control. The believer’s task is to maintain the qualities that render Satan powerless.

=100=

This verse completes the picture by identifying those over whom Satan does have authority. “His authority is only over those who take him as a patron” describes a voluntary, ongoing alliance.

The word “patron” (walī) implies a close, protective, and guiding relationship. These are people who choose Satan as their leader, following his suggestions and adopting his worldview. They reject divine guidance and instead embrace the satanic path, whether consciously or through consistent neglect of Allah’s commands.

The verse then specifies the ultimate manifestation of this alliance: “and who—under his influence—associate others with Allah in worship.” Shirk is the chief goal of Satan. He leads his patrons step by step until they commit the gravest sin of associating partners with Allah. While Satan’s authority extends to all forms of sin, the verse highlights shirk as the hallmark of his dominion because it is the unforgivable sin if died upon without repentance. The polytheists of Mecca are the immediate referent: they took Satan as their patron by following the traditions of their forefathers, and they ended up worshipping idols alongside Allah.

However, the principle applies universally. Anyone who consistently obeys Satan’s whisperings, making him their guide in life, falls under his authority. The verse does not mean that Satan physically controls them, but that they have placed themselves within his sphere of influence by their choices, and Allah, in His justice, allows Satan to lead them further astray. This verse serves as a stark warning. To take Satan as a patron does not require a formal ritual; it happens whenever a person consistently prefers his desires over Allah’s commands, when they mock the truth, or when they feel more loyalty to the satanic systems of the world than to the community of faith. The good news, however, is that this authority is not inevitable or irreversible. The door of repentance is open. As soon as a person renounces this patronage, seeks refuge in Allah, and embraces faith and trust, they move from the camp of Satan’s authority to the protected fortress of Allah, as described in the previous verse. Together, verses 99 and 100 draw a clear line between the people of faith and the people of Satan, leaving no middle ground. The believer is called to examine their allegiances and ensure they are not, in any area of life, taking the accursed one as a patron.
