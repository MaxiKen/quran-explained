=121=

This verse continues the description of Abraham begun in the previous verse, adding a quality that was the hallmark of his character and the direct cause of his elevated status. The phrase “utterly grateful for Allah’s favours” translates shākiran li-anʿumihi, indicating that Abraham was not merely occasionally thankful but lived in a constant state of profound gratitude.

The Arabic intensive form suggests that gratitude was woven into the very fabric of his being. Every blessing he received, from his creation to his prophethood to his deliverance from the fire, was met with immediate and complete acknowledgment of the Giver. This gratitude was not passive; it manifested in his actions, his worship, and his unwavering commitment to spread the message of tawḥīd. He recognized that every good he possessed—his family, his wealth, his knowledge—was a favour from Allah, and he used each one in obedient service.

The verse then states the divine response to this gratitude: “So He chose him and guided him to the Straight Path.” The word “chose” (ijtabāhu) implies a special divine selection, a pulling of the servant close, an election to intimate friendship. Abraham is known as Khalīl Allāh, the intimate friend of Allah, a title that reflects this divine choosing. The guidance to the Straight Path is both the initial guidance to monotheism in the midst of an idolatrous society, and the continuous, deepening guidance throughout his life that kept him firm and elevated his station. This causal link between gratitude and divine selection is profoundly instructive. It teaches that gratitude is not merely a virtue but a magnet for divine favour and increased guidance. The more a person recognizes and appreciates Allah’s favours, the more Allah draws them near and shows them the way. Abraham’s life exemplifies this dynamic: his rejection of idols, his debate with Nimrod, his willingness to sacrifice his son, his building of the Kaʿbah—all were expressions of his gratitude that led to his chosen status.

For the believer, this verse provides a practical model for spiritual growth. The cultivation of deep, active gratitude for every blessing, both material and spiritual, is the path to being chosen by Allah and remaining firmly on the Straight Path.

=122=

This verse details the comprehensive reward Abraham received as a result of his devotion and gratitude. “We blessed him with all goodness in this world” translates ātaynāhu fī al-dunyā ḥasanah.

The word “goodness” (ḥasanah) is wonderfully comprehensive. In Abraham’s case, this included a long life of faith and obedience, a righteous wife, illustrious children including Isaac and Ishmael who were prophets, immense wealth and hospitality, and a lasting legacy of praise and honour among all nations. He was blessed with the friendship of Allah, the station of prophethood, and the distinction of being the father of many prophets. His name is revered by Jews, Christians, and Muslims alike. The goodness also includes the physical blessing of the land, the rebuilding of the Kaʿbah, and the establishment of the pilgrimage that continues to this day. His supplication for a good name and a righteous progeny was answered in full. This worldly goodness was not without trials; Abraham faced exile, the fire of Nimrod, and the command to sacrifice his son. But the trials themselves were part of the goodness because they elevated his rank and were succeeded by immense blessings.

The verse then states, “and in the Hereafter he will certainly be among the righteous.” The word “righteous” (ṣāliḥīn) indicates that in the next life, Abraham’s station will be among the highest ranks of the pious, in the company of the prophets and the truthful. The certainty of this is emphasized by the “certainly.” This dual reward—goodness in this world and righteousness in the Hereafter—is the pattern for all who follow Abraham’s example.

The verse shows that true faith does not demand a rejection of all worldly good; rather, it invites a holistic blessing that encompasses both realms, provided the heart remains attached to the Hereafter as the ultimate goal. For the believer, this verse is a source of hope and aspiration. It teaches that following the Abrahamic path leads to a good life in this world, full of meaning, honour, and provision, and a secure place among the righteous in the eternal life. The comprehensive goodness promised to Abraham is a promise, in measure, to all who walk his path of devotion, gratitude, and uprightness.

=123=

After presenting Abraham as the model of excellence, the sūrah now directly addresses the Prophet Muḥammad with a command that defines the foundation of his mission. “Then We revealed to you O Prophet, saying: ‘Follow the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not one of the polytheists.’” This revelation establishes the essential continuity between the Abrahamic tradition and the final message of Islam. The Prophet Muhammad is not bringing a new or unfamiliar religion; he is commanded to follow the millah, the way and religion, of Abraham.

The word millah refers to the foundational principles of faith and practice that Abraham embodied: pure monotheism, submission to Allah, rejection of idols, and a life of righteousness. The command to follow Abraham’s faith is particularly significant because the Quraysh claimed descent from Abraham through Ishmael and prided themselves on being his heirs.

Yet they had corrupted his pure monotheism with idol worship. By commanding the Prophet to follow Abraham’s faith, the Qur’an reclaims Abraham for true Islam and exposes the Quraysh’s deviation. Abraham is described again as “the upright” (ḥanīf), the one who turned away from all falsehood and inclined solely to the truth. The explicit clarification that he was “not one of the polytheists” severs any link between Abraham and the pagan practices of Mecca. Abraham’s faith was the antithesis of shirk; he spent his life fighting against idolatry. For the Prophet and his followers, this command meant that their religion was not a break from the past but a restoration of the original, uncorrupted faith of their forefather Abraham. It gave them a powerful argument against the Quraysh and a deep sense of connection to a noble spiritual lineage.

For Muslims today, this verse emphasizes that their faith is rooted in the Abrahamic heritage. The call to follow Abraham’s way is a call to uncompromising tawḥīd, to standing firm against the idolatry of one’s time—whether in the form of literal idols, materialism, or ideologies—and to embodying the uprightness that characterized the father of prophets. It is a reminder that Islam is the path of Abraham, the original and universal way of submission to the One God.

=124=

This verse addresses a specific point of contention between the different Abrahamic communities, clarifying the status of the Sabbath. The phrase “Honouring the Sabbath was ordained only for those who disputed about Abraham” indicates that the strict observance of the Sabbath as a day of compulsory rest and ritual was a commandment given specifically to the Jews, not as an eternal, universal law, but as part of the legislation that followed the disputes and transgressions among the Children of Israel regarding Abraham’s legacy. The Jews and Christians argued about who truly followed Abraham; the Jews claimed him as exclusively theirs, while the Christians also claimed him. Their disputes led to divisions, and as part of the divine legislation for the Jewish people, the Sabbath was exalted as a sign.

However, this command was not part of Abraham’s original faith, which was pure monotheism without the heavy burdens of later legislation. The verse therefore implies that the Prophet Muhammad and his followers are not bound by the Sabbath ordinance; their connection to Abraham is through the essence of his faith, not through the specific laws that were later imposed on other communities due to their particular circumstances and disputes.

The verse then adds, “And surely your Lord will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their disputes.” All the disagreements about Abraham, about who correctly followed him, and about the laws, will be finally resolved by Allah on the Day of Judgment. He will make clear who was right and who was wrong.

This statement serves to caution against excessive engagement in such theological disputes; the ultimate Judge is Allah, and the focus should be on following the clear revelation rather than getting lost in historical arguments. For Muslims, this verse is a reminder that the essence of faith is not in rituals that were prescribed for specific times and peoples, but in the core tawḥīd and submission that Abraham exemplified. It also teaches patience in the face of religious disputes, knowing that the final judgment rests with Allah.

The emphasis on divine judgment encourages humility; no group should arrogantly claim exclusive possession of truth, as the final verdict belongs to the Lord of all.

=125=

This verse provides a comprehensive methodology for dawah, calling others to Islam. It begins with the command: “Invite all to the Way of your Lord.” The address is primarily to the Prophet, but it extends to all believers who carry the message. The “Way of your Lord” is the path of Islam, the straight path that leads to Allah. The invitation must be carried out with three distinct approaches, depending on the audience and situation.

The first is “with wisdom” (bil-ḥikmah). Wisdom involves sound knowledge, good judgement, choosing the right time and place, speaking to people according to their level of understanding, and prioritizing the most important matters. It also means using clear proofs and rational arguments that appeal to the intellect.

The second is “kind advice” (al-mawʿiẓah al-ḥasanah). This involves gentle, heartfelt exhortation that appeals to the emotions and the innate human desire for goodness and truth. It includes reminding people of Allah’s favours, warning them of the consequences of disobedience with compassion, and encouraging them with stories of the righteous. The advice must be “kind,” avoiding harshness, condemnation, and public shaming.

The third is “and only debate with them in the best manner.” When debate and intellectual discussion are necessary with those who oppose or question the faith, it must be conducted with the highest standards of etiquette: respect for the opponent, listening carefully, avoiding insults and personal attacks, using logical arguments, and being prepared to agree to disagree. The goal is not to win an argument but to convey the truth with dignity.

The verse then provides the ultimate reassurance: “Surely your Lord alone knows best who has strayed from His Way and who is rightly guided. ” The results of dawah are not the caller’s responsibility. After employing wisdom, kind advice, and best debate, the caller leaves the outcome to Allah, who alone knows the true state of hearts. This verse remains a timeless guide for Muslim outreach, education, and interfaith dialogue, emphasizing the combination of intellectual rigour, emotional warmth, and ethical conduct, all underpinned by trust in Allah’s knowledge and judgment.

=126=

This verse provides a balanced ethical teaching on dealing with harm and injustice, transitioning from the methodology of dawah to the personal conduct expected when facing opposition. “If you retaliate, then let it be equivalent to what you have suffered” establishes the principle of just retribution. If a believer is wronged and chooses to respond, the response must be proportional, never exceeding the original harm. There is no room for excessive vengeance, escalation, or transgression. The measure of retaliation is exact equivalence, a principle that ensures justice and prevents cycles of increasing violence. This aligns with the broader Qur’anic concept of qisas, legal retribution, where the punishment must fit the crime.

However, the verse immediately presents a superior moral option: “But if you patiently endure, it is certainly best for those who are patient.” Patience (ṣabr) here means forbearance, controlling the urge to retaliate even when one has the right to do so, and leaving the matter to Allah.

The phrase “it is certainly best” emphasizes that this higher path leads to greater spiritual reward, inner peace, and often better practical outcomes. Patience is not weakness; it is strength of character and trust in divine justice. This verse was particularly significant in the Meccan context, where the believers were subjected to severe persecution and had limited means to retaliate. It guided them to channel their energy into patience and perseverance rather than rash actions that could lead to greater harm.

For Muslims in every age, this verse presents a dual framework: the right to proportionate justice is affirmed, protecting against oppression, but the preference for patience and forgiveness is strongly encouraged as the path of true excellence. This teaching mirrors the divine attributes, as Allah is both the Just and the Forbearing. The verse calls the believer to rise above the natural impulse for vengeance and to aspire to the station of those who are patient, for whom the best reward is promised.

=127=

This verse directly addresses the Prophet Muḥammad with a series of comforting commands that also serve as lessons for all believers. “Be patient O Prophet” is a singular command, placing the burden of patience squarely on the leader. The Prophet faced immense pressure: mockery, rejection, persecution of his followers, and elaborate plots against his life. The command to patience is both a recognition of the difficulty and a divine empowerment.

The verse then clarifies the source of this patience: “for your patience is only with Allah’s help.” This is a crucial spiritual principle. Patience is not a purely human virtue generated by willpower; it is a gift from Allah bestowed upon those who turn to Him for assistance. The Prophet, with all his spiritual strength, needed Allah’s help to remain patient. This humility before the divine source of all virtues is a lesson for every believer who struggles to endure hardship.

The verse then addresses the emotional toll of rejection: “Do not grieve over those who disbelieve.” The Prophet’s deep concern for his people’s guidance caused him grief, as mentioned elsewhere.

This command does not negate his compassion but places a boundary on it. He is not to let their rejection consume him with sorrow, because his duty is to convey, and their choice is between them and Allah.

The final part, “nor be distressed by their schemes,” addresses the anxiety caused by the constant plotting of the Quraysh. Their conspiracies were real and dangerous, but the Prophet is told not to let his heart be constricted by worry. Allah, who is aware of all their schemes, is the ultimate protector. This verse is a profound source of comfort for all who call to truth and face hostility. It teaches that patience is sustained by divine help, that grief over others’ choices should be tempered by trust in Allah’s wisdom, and that anxiety over the plots of opponents should be dissolved by reliance on Allah’s overarching power. The Prophet’s heart is to remain tranquil, focused on his mission, and anchored in the certainty that Allah is in control.

=128=

The sūrah concludes with a concise, powerful assurance that encapsulates the entire message of the preceding verses. “Surely Allah is with those who shun evil and who do good deeds.

” The word “surely” (inna) emphasizes the certainty of this divine companionship. “Those who shun evil” translates alladhīna ittaqaw, the people of taqwā, those who are constantly mindful of Allah and protect themselves from sin and harmful actions. Taqwā is the comprehensive quality of God-consciousness that has been a central theme throughout the sūrah. “Those who do good deeds” translates alladhīna hum muḥsinūn, the people of iḥsān, those who perform righteous actions with excellence, going beyond the minimum and striving for perfection in their worship and conduct. The combination of taqwā (avoiding evil) and iḥsān (doing good) represents the complete spiritual program: the negative and the positive, the fear and the love, the restraint and the action. The promise that Allah is “with” such people is the ultimate reward. Maʿiyyah, divine companionship, means Allah’s special protection, support, guidance, and love. It is the source of strength, peace, and ultimate success. This verse serves as a summary of the entire sūrah, which detailed Allah’s signs and favours, condemned ingratitude and arrogance, established ethical commands, and called to the way of Abraham. The ending leaves the believer with the heartwarming assurance that their efforts are not in vain and that they are not alone. The Lord of all creation, the Almighty, the All-Wise, is with them. This companionship is conditional, however: it is for those who maintain taqwā and iḥsān. The verse is both a promise and a challenge, an invitation to live in such a way that one becomes worthy of this divine nearness. For the Prophet, who was commanded to be patient and not grieve, this final verse is the seal of comfort. For every Muslim who reads the sūrah, it is the ultimate goal: to be among those with whom Allah is, in this world and in the Hereafter.

Thus the sūrah ends on a note of profound hope, tying together all the lessons of gratitude, monotheism, and moral conduct into the single, beautiful promise of divine companionship.
