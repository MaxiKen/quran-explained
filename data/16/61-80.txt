=61=

Having exposed the evil qualities of those who disbelieve in the Hereafter and affirmed Allah's perfect attributes, the sūrah now addresses a question that naturally arises: why do wrongdoers seem to escape immediate punishment? The verse begins, “If Allah were to punish people immediately for their wrongdoing, He would not have left a single living being on earth.

” The word for wrongdoing is ẓulm, which encompasses every form of injustice, sin, and transgression, from the greatest crime of shirk to the smallest disobedience. If Allah were to seize every wrongdoer the moment they committed an offense, the earth would be swept clean of life.

This is because no human being is entirely free from wrongdoing. Even the believers commit sins, and the general human condition involves some degree of shortcoming. The immediate, automatic punishment of every wrong act would therefore result in the extinction of all people, and perhaps even animals and plants that suffer as a consequence of human injustice.

This statement is a profound testament to Allah's forbearance. The fact that we are alive, despite our countless sins, is proof of His mercy. He does not approve of the wrongdoing, but He withholds the punishment out of grace, giving time for repentance, correction, and growth.

The verse then provides the explanation: “But He delays them for an appointed term.” Every individual, every community, and indeed humanity as a whole has an appointed term (ajal musammā). This term is fixed in divine knowledge. The delay is not a sign of neglect or helplessness; it is a deliberate, merciful reprieve. Within this term, the wrongdoer has the opportunity to turn back, while the believer can increase in good deeds and purification.

However, the verse immediately balances this reassurance with a warning: “And when their time arrives, they cannot delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it. ” When the appointed term comes, whether for an individual at death or for a nation facing its destruction, there is absolutely no flexibility. No one can bargain for an extra hour, and the punishment cannot be brought forward before its designated time. The divine timetable is precise and unalterable. This verse provides a complete answer to the problem of evil and divine justice. It teaches that the apparent prosperity of wrongdoers is not a sign of divine approval but of divine respite. It instills in the believer a sense of urgency: the appointed term is approaching, and one must not waste the delay in continued wrongdoing. It also comforts those who suffer injustice, assuring them that the oppressor's time is limited and divinely measured. The perfect balance between mercy and justice in this verse reflects the two divine names mentioned at the end of the previous passage: the Almighty and the All-Wise.

=62=

This verse continues the critique of the pagan mindset, building on the earlier discussion of their attributing daughters to Allah and their evil judgments. “They attribute to Allah what they hate for themselves” refers back to their detestation of daughters while claiming that Allah has daughters. More broadly, it encompasses every false and deficient quality they ascribe to God while being unwilling to accept such qualities for themselves. They claim Allah has partners, yet they would hate to have partners in their own businesses or households who share equally in their authority. They set aside a portion of their provision for idols, yet they would resent anyone taking a share of their own wealth without permission. Their theology is a projection of their own flaws, riddled with inconsistencies.

The second part of the verse says, “and their tongues utter the lie that they will have the finest reward.” Despite their evil deeds and beliefs, they audaciously claim that they will receive a good outcome in the Hereafter, or that their idols will intercede for them and grant them paradise. Some commentators explain that when the Quraysh heard the descriptions of the believers' reward in Paradise, they boasted that if there is a resurrection, they would receive even better because of their wealth and status in this world.

This is a lie they fabricate and speak with their tongues, a self-deception that compounds their disbelief. The verse then delivers the definitive verdict: “Without a doubt, for them is the Fire, where they will be abandoned.

” The word “abandoned” translates mufraṭūn, which some scholars interpret as being left there eternally, forgotten by any helper, or as being pushed forward into the Fire with contempt. There is no hope of rescue, no intercessor, no second chance. Their fine words and false hopes will collapse into the reality of eternal abandonment. This verse dismantles the psychology of self-justification. Sinners often comfort themselves with the idea that everything will be fine in the end, despite their persistence in wrongdoing. The verse warns that such a claim is merely a lie spoken by the tongue, not a reality grounded in divine promise.

For the believer, it is a call to examine one’s own assumptions: am I relying on Allah’s true promise or on my own wishful thinking? The stark contrast between the “finest reward” they claim and the “Fire” that awaits them underscores the danger of spiritual arrogance. Only through sincere faith and righteous deeds, as defined by revelation, can one legitimately hope for a good reward.

=63=

The sūrah now takes a divine oath to comfort the Prophet and warn the Quraysh. “By Allah! We have surely sent messengers to communities before you O Prophet.” The oath emphasizes the certainty of the historical pattern. The Prophet Muḥammad is not the first to face rejection; every preceding community received its messengers. The verse explains the common cause of rejection: “but Satan made their misdeeds appealing to them.

” The word “misdeeds” refers to their idolatry, arrogance, and rejection of the prophets. Satan beautified these sins in their eyes, making them seem reasonable, noble, or even righteous. They followed their whims and cultural traditions, convinced that they were on the correct path while actually being deceived by the enemy of mankind.

This is the consistent method of Satan: to take evil and wrap it in attractive packaging so that the sinner feels no need to repent. The verse then says, “So he is their patron today.

” The word “patron” (walī) means a close ally, protector, and guide. Because they followed Satan in the world, he has become their guardian and companion on the Day of Judgment, leading them to their doom. On that Day, Satan will disown his followers, as the Qur'an describes elsewhere, saying he only called them and they responded; he has no power to save them from Allah's punishment. Their alliance with him in this world results in complete abandonment and enmity in the Hereafter.

The verse concludes, “and they will suffer a painful punishment. ” The consequence of being under Satan's patronage is the inevitable painful torment of Hell. This verse serves multiple purposes. For the Prophet, it is a consolation: the rejection he faces is not due to any deficiency in his message but follows a long history of satanic deception. For the Quraysh, it is a warning that their perceived correctness is a satanic illusion, and their alliance with their forefathers' religion is actually an alliance with Satan.

For believers of every era, it highlights the crucial necessity of self-examination. Our own inclinations and cultural norms can be tainted by satanic beautification. The only safeguard is to measure everything against the clear revelation.

The verse also exposes the deceptive nature of sin; it may appear pleasant and justified, but its end is a painful punishment. Seeking Allah's refuge from Satan is therefore not a mere ritual but a vital spiritual necessity to avoid having the devil as one's ultimate patron.

=64=

This verse presents the Qur'an as the divine antidote to the satanic deception and disputes mentioned in the previous verse. “We have revealed to you the Book” directly addresses the Prophet and emphasizes the divine origin of the revelation.

The phrase “only to clarify for them what they differed about” states the primary purpose of the Qur'an in its function as a decisive arbiter. Humanity, including the People of the Book and the polytheists, had fallen into deep disagreements over fundamental truths—the nature of God, the status of prophets, the reality of the Hereafter, moral laws, and ritual practices. The Qur'an comes as the final, clear criterion that separates truth from falsehood and resolves those disputes with absolute authority. It clarifies the true stories of the past, the correct beliefs about God, and the laws by which human life should be governed. For the Quraysh, who disputed about the resurrection, about the angels, and about the legitimacy of the Prophet's message, the Book provides unambiguous answers. Then the verse adds, “and as a guide and mercy for those who believe. ” The clarification is universal in its presentation, but the benefit of being a guide and a mercy is specifically for those who believe. The Qur'an guides them to the straight path in all aspects of life, leading them through the darkness of ignorance and confusion into the light of certainty. It is also a mercy because it saves them from eternal punishment, grants them peace of mind, and connects them to their Creator. The combination of “guide” and “mercy” indicates that following the Qur'an is not a burdensome duty but a compassionate gift that brings about the best of this life and the next. This verse elevates the status of the Qur'an as the ultimate reference point. In the face of endless human arguments and sectarian divisions, the believer returns to the Book to find clarity.

The verse also implies that those who do not believe, despite the Book's clarity, are rejecting not just a text but a divine mercy that could have saved them. For contemporary Muslims, this verse is a call to approach the Qur'an with the right attitude: not as a book of mere recitation, but as the clarifier of disputes, the guide for life, and the mercy that heals the heart. It instills confidence that in the Qur'an, one has access to the final word on the matters that divide humanity, and that adhering to it is the path to receiving Allah's special mercy.

=65=

Following the mention of the Book as a guide and mercy, the sūrah returns to the signs in the natural world, drawing a parallel between the life-giving rain and the life-giving revelation. “And Allah sends down rain from the sky” redirects the gaze to a familiar, yet miraculous, phenomenon. The rain descends from above, beyond human control, by the precise command and measure of the Creator.

The phrase “giving life to the earth after its death” paints a vivid picture. A dry, barren land, cracked and lifeless, is transformed by the rainwater into a vibrant, green landscape teeming with plants and life. This natural resurrection, repeated every season, is a tangible sign that the One who revives the dead earth can and will revive the dead bones.

The verse then concludes, “Surely in this is a sign for those who listen.” The specific mention of “those who listen” rather than “those who see” is significant. While the effect of rain is visible, the sound of rain, the thunder, and the message it carries require a different kind of attentiveness. Listening implies receptivity, understanding, and willingness to heed. A person can see rain and merely remark on the weather, but one who truly “listens” hears in the raindrops the message of divine mercy, power, and the promise of resurrection. The connection to the preceding verse is profound: just as rain revives the dead earth, the Qur'an revives dead hearts. The revelation is often compared to rain in the Qur'an; it descends from the sky of divine will and transforms barren souls into flourishing gardens of faith and good deeds. The polytheists who saw rain as a natural cycle missed the sign, while those who listen with the ears of faith perceive the Creator's hand. This verse is an invitation to a deeper engagement with the natural world. Every rainfall can be a moment of spiritual reflection, a reminder of our own resurrection, and an occasion to thank Allah for His mercy. It also teaches that the signs are abundant and clear, but their benefit is only for those who have the quality of sincere listening—an active, humble attentiveness to the truth. For the modern believer, surrounded by scientific explanations, the verse does not deny those explanations but points through them to the ultimate Cause, transforming a meteorological event into a spiritual experience.

=66=

The sūrah now returns to the signs in cattle, building on the earlier mentions of their benefits, but here focusing on a specific physiological miracle. “And there is certainly a lesson for you in cattle” uses the word ʿibrah, a lesson that bridges one reality to another, causing the observer to cross over from the physical to the spiritual. The specific lesson is about milk: “We give you to drink of what is in their bellies, from between digested food and blood: pure milk, pleasant to drink.

” This description is a remarkable piece of Qur'anic science and spiritual reflection. The cattle consume fodder, which becomes digested material in the stomach and intestines, and blood circulates in their veins. From between these two substances—the partially digested food and the blood—Allah extracts milk. Milk is neither the waste product of digestion nor blood, yet it emerges from a process that involves both. The verse describes it as “pure” (khāliṣan), free from any impurity, and “pleasant to drink” (sā’ighan), palatable and smooth, causing no revulsion despite its origin. The lesson is the divine power that can bring forth something so pure, nutritious, and delightful from within the complex, messy interior of an animal. If a human were to look at digested food and blood, the last thing they would expect to derive from them is a delicious, clean beverage.

Yet Allah's creative wisdom accomplishes this for the benefit of humanity. This sign points to the resurrection as well: the ability to extract the pure from the mixed, the living from the dead. It also serves as a metaphor for spiritual purification; Allah can bring forth a pure, righteous soul from a person previously immersed in the filth of sin, just as He brings forth pure milk from between blood and waste. The cattle, which the polytheists sometimes dedicated to idols, are in fact a standing testimony to the oneness and mercy of the Creator. The verse invites the believer to contemplate the daily glass of milk with profound gratitude and awe. In a contemporary context, where milk production is industrialised and taken for granted, this verse restores the sense of wonder. The complex biological processes that modern science details—the role of the mammary glands, the filtering of nutrients from the blood—are precisely the workings of the divine sign. The believer who understands this lesson will see in every sip of milk a reason to praise the One who designed this incredible system.

=67=

This verse continues with the theme of food and drink, but with a significant nuance. “And from the fruits of palm trees and grapevines you derive intoxicants as well as wholesome provision.” The verse mentions two of the most valued fruits in Arabia: dates from palm trees and grapes from grapevines. From these, people produce two very different types of products. “Intoxicants” (sakaran) refers to alcoholic beverages such as date wine and grape wine, which were widely consumed by the Arabs before Islam. “Wholesome provision” (rizqan ḥasanan) refers to the good, lawful foods and non-intoxicating drinks that can be derived from the same fruits, such as fresh dates, raisins, date syrup, and vinegar. The juxtaposition of intoxicants and wholesome provision, without an immediate explicit prohibition (the prohibition of alcohol came later), is a subtle moral lesson.

The phrase “wholesome provision” is a positive commendation, while “intoxicants” is mentioned without the qualifier “wholesome. ” The verse plants the seed of a moral distinction: from the same divine blessing, humans can choose to produce something that clouds the mind and leads to harm, or something that is pure and nourishing. The concluding statement, “Surely in this is a sign for those who understand,” appeals to the intellect. People of understanding will recognise that the existence of two such opposite outcomes from the same source is a test. The sign is not just in the fruit itself but in the human capacity to choose between the good and the harmful. This verse is often cited in discussions about the gradual prohibition of alcohol in Islam. It was revealed in Mecca, long before the clear prohibition came in Medina, laying the intellectual and spiritual groundwork. It encourages reflection on the nature of what we consume and the choices we make with Allah's gifts. The divine wisdom is that the same grape can be a source of benefit or a source of ruin, depending on human action.

This principle extends to all of Allah’s provisions: money can be used for charity or for oppression, intelligence for guidance or for manipulation. For the contemporary believer, the verse is a call to use the intellect to distinguish between the wholesome and the intoxicating, not just in beverages but in all aspects of life. The modern world is full of “intoxicants”—addictive, mind-numbing substances and entertainments—and also abundant “wholesome provision.” The sign is for those who understand and choose wisely.

=68=

The sūrah now reaches one of its most celebrated passages, the sign of the bee, from which the chapter takes its name. “And your Lord inspired the bees” uses the word awḥā, the same root used for divine revelation to prophets.

This is a powerful indication that the bee's intricate behaviour is not a product of random evolution or mere instinct; it is a form of divine inspiration, a programmed guidance from the Creator. The bee is a tiny creature, yet it operates with astonishing precision and complexity. The address “your Lord” personalises the relationship; the same Lord who guides the Prophet and the believers also guides the bee. The content of this inspiration is first about habitation: “Make your homes in the mountains, the trees, and in what people construct.” The bee is given flexibility to build its hives in natural crevices in the mountains, in the hollows of trees, or in man-made hives that people construct for beekeeping. This tripartite division covers all possible habitats—wild natural formations, living trees, and human-engineered structures—demonstrating that the bee's adaptability is itself a divine gift. The fact that the verse mentions “what people construct” shows that Allah anticipated the domestication of bees and the practice of apiculture, which would become a human craft. The bee instinctively knows how to build its hexagonal wax cells with mathematical precision, a feat of engineering that humans have long admired. The inspiration given to the bee is a clear sign that Allah is the Lord of all creatures, great and small. The bee does not possess intellect or free will like humans, yet it flawlessly executes its divinely assigned tasks. This mirrors the earlier theme of all creation submitting to Allah. The polytheists are invited to reflect on this tiny insect: could any of their idols guide a bee to build its home and produce honey?

The answer is obvious. For believers, this verse instills a sense of wonder at the natural world. The bee becomes a symbol of obedient service, working tirelessly, producing something sweet and beneficial. The modern study of bees, revealing their complex communication through dances and their highly organised social structure, only deepens the awe at the divine “inspiration” behind it all.

The verse teaches that Allah's guidance is not limited to scripture but is woven into the very fabric of creation.

=69=

This verse continues the divine instructions given to the bee. The inspiration continues: “and feed from the flower of any fruit you please.” The bee is given liberty to roam and gather nectar from a vast variety of blossoms, unrestricted except by the seasons and geography. The command “you please” indicates a divinely granted freedom within the bounds of its nature, and the bee does not overstep; it selects the best sources and returns to the hive. Then, “and follow the ways your Lord has made easy for you” describes the bee’s miraculous navigation.

The Arabic word subul (ways) refers to the paths the bee travels, sometimes miles away from the hive, yet it finds its way back unerringly. Modern science has uncovered the bee's sophisticated navigational techniques, including the famous waggle dance that communicates the direction and distance of food sources to hive-mates. All of this is “made easy” by Allah, meaning He has facilitated this complex behaviour as a matter of course for the bee, without it needing to learn in a human sense. The result of this guided labour is then described: “From their bellies comes forth liquid of varying colours, in which there is healing for people.” The honey produced is of different colours depending on the nectar sources—from pale gold to dark amber.

The phrase “in which there is healing for people” is a profound statement. Honey has well-documented medicinal properties, including antibacterial effects, wound-healing capabilities, and soothing for ailments of the throat and digestion.

The verse presents this as a sign, not a medical textbook, but its truth has been confirmed by centuries of human experience and modern research. The healing is placed in the context of a divine gift emerging from a complex process that began with inspiration.

The verse concludes, “Surely in this is a sign for those who reflect.” Reflection here goes beyond casual observation to deep contemplation of the entire chain: the Lord's inspiration, the bee's obedience, the varied nectar, the transformation in the belly, and the resulting healing liquid. It is a complete sign of the Creator's mercy, wisdom, and power.

For believers, honey becomes a reminder of Allah's subtle care. The verse also encourages the pursuit of natural remedies in accordance with divine guidance. The bee's work ethic and the sweetness of its output serve as a metaphor for the believer whose words and deeds, guided by divine inspiration, should bring healing and sweetness to the world.

=70=

Following the magnificent sign of the bee, the sūrah returns to the human condition, reflecting on the lifecycle that manifests Allah's power. “Allah has created you, and then causes you to die.” This simple statement summarizes the entirety of human existence from the perspective of divine decree. Creation out of nothing, the gift of life, and the inevitability of death are all in Allah's hands.

The verse then focuses on a particular stage within that lifecycle: “And some of you are left to reach the most feeble stage of life so that they may know nothing after having known much.” This refers to extreme old age, termed arḏal al-ʿumur, the most abject, weak period of life, where physical and mental faculties deteriorate. A person who was once sharp-minded, knowledgeable, and capable may regress to a state of forgetfulness and helplessness, becoming like a child again, not knowing what they once knew, and unable to care for themselves.

This is a stark reminder of human frailty. The abilities we pride ourselves on—intelligence, memory, strength—are not intrinsic to us; they are loans from Allah that He can recall at any time. The verse punctures human arrogance by showing the full arc of life: from weakness to strength and back to weakness.

The final part of the verse, “Indeed, Allah is All-Knowing, Most Capable,” connects this life stage to divine attributes. Allah's knowledge encompasses every phase of human life, and His power is such that He can easily reverse a person's condition. The decline of old age is not a defeat but a deliberate demonstration of the transient nature of worldly capabilities. Some commentators note that the one who reaches this feeble stage may have lost the worldly knowledge they accumulated, but their faith and the reward for their patience remain intact with Allah.

For believers, this verse inspires humility regarding youth and health. It encourages treating the elderly with compassion and respect, recognising that their state could one day be ours. It also motivates taking advantage of the period of strength and knowledge to do righteous deeds before the inevitable decline. The verse is a memento mori, a reminder of the entire human trajectory under divine control, urging preparation for the life to come when our earthly faculties fade into insignificance.

=71=

This verse shifts to the theme of economic provision and uses it as an argument against shirk. “And Allah has favoured some of you over others in provision” acknowledges a manifest reality: people differ in their wealth, sustenance, and resources. This disparity is part of Allah's divine wisdom and test.

The verse then presents a logical analogy drawn from human behaviour: “But those who have been much favoured would not share their wealth with those bondspeople in their possession, making them their equals. ” The wealthy masters do not consider their slaves or servants as equals in ownership of their wealth. A master would not give his slave an equal share in his property such that the slave becomes a partner with equal rights and decision-making power. If human beings are unwilling to make their own servants equal partners in their limited, temporary wealth, how can they dare to make created beings—who are ultimately Allah's servants—equal partners with Allah in His divine dominion and worship? The analogy is powerful and culturally immediate for the Meccan society where slavery was practiced. If a human master feels it absurd for a slave to be a co-owner, it is infinitely more absurd for a human to be set up as a partner to the Lord of all worlds.

The verse concludes with the rhetorical question: “Do they then deny Allah’s favours?” The favours refer to the provision and the reasoning capacity they have been given. By committing shirk, they deny the very gifts that should have led them to exclusive worship. This verse uses rational argument to dismantle polytheism, showing that the polytheists' own social norms contradict their theological claims. It also carries a subtle message about human dignity and the arbitrariness of social hierarchies; the favour of provision is from Allah, not a measure of intrinsic worth. The verse challenges the believer to reflect on how they use their God-given provisions: do they recognise them as favours and act gratefully, or do they hoard them in denial of the Giver? The analogy also encourages fairness and humility, reminding that no matter how wealthy one becomes, they remain servants of Allah, never reaching the status of a partner with Him in any sense.

=72=

Continuing the list of divine blessings, this verse highlights the blessing of family. “And Allah has made for you spouses of your own kind” refers to the creation of mates from the same human nature, so that they find comfort, love, and compatibility.

The phrase “of your own kind” (min anfusikum) literally means “from yourselves,” indicating the deep psychological and emotional bond between husband and wife, who share the same essence. The spouse is not an alien being but a companion crafted from the same human mould to provide tranquillity. Then, “and given you through your spouses children and grandchildren” expands the blessing.

The Arabic word ḥafadah, translated as grandchildren, can also include close relatives and descendants in general. Through marriage, Allah grants the joy of children and the further joy of seeing one's children have children, extending the family and providing support in old age.

This is a profound mercy that ensures continuity, love, and care across generations. The verse then adds, “And He has granted you good, lawful provisions.

” The term ṭayyibāt includes all pure, beneficial, and lawful things that sustain life and provide pleasure. The family is supported by these provisions, which are themselves a sign of Allah's care.

The verse ends with a cutting question: “Are they then faithful to falsehood and ungrateful for Allah’s favours? ” The falsehood (bāṭil) refers to the idols and all that they worship besides Allah. After receiving such immense and personal blessings—spouses, children, and sustenance—how can they have faith in false gods and be ungrateful to the true God?

The pairing of “faithful to falsehood” with “ungrateful for Allah’s favours” shows that shirk is the ultimate ingratitude. They redirect their devotion and thanks to entities that provided them none of these blessings. This verse is a powerful reminder for believers to see their family as a divine gift. Every moment with a spouse, every child and grandchild, every meal shared, is a favour that should increase gratitude and strengthen faith, not lead to heedlessness. In a modern context, where the family unit is often undervalued or redefined, the verse reaffirms the sacredness of the family structure as a deliberate, merciful design of the Creator. The appropriate response to these favours is exclusive faith in Allah and a life filled with gratitude.

=73=

This verse directly contrasts the true Provider described in the preceding verses with the false objects of worship. “Yet they worship besides Allah” emphasizes the contradiction. After all the detailed blessings—spouses, children, cattle, rain, and the intricate provision described throughout the sūrah—some people turn away from the Giver to worship others.

The phrase “those idols” is not in the original Arabic, which simply says “that which,” encompassing idols, false gods, and any being worshipped apart from Allah. The verse first states the empirical fact: these false gods “do not afford them any provision from the heavens and the earth.” Provision here includes rain from the sky, crops from the earth, and all the sustenance that has been meticulously listed. Not a single grain of wheat, not a drop of water, is provided by these idols. They have never given and can never give any form of sustenance.

The verse then delivers the final blow: “nor do they have the power to.” Not only do they not actually provide, but they lack even the potential capability to do so.

The Arabic word yastaṭīʿūn indicates complete, fundamental inability. They are not just passive non-providers; they are utterly incapable of providing, now or ever.

This is in stark contrast to Allah, whose ability to provide is absolute and demonstrated constantly. The verse is a succinct, logical refutation of idol worship. Worship is the ultimate expression of gratitude and dependence, and it logically belongs solely to the One who provisions and has the power to do so. To worship something that cannot provide even for itself—since the idols are made of wood or stone and need human makers to exist—is the height of irrationality. The verse calls the worshipper to a simple accounting: who gives you your food, your family, your life?

The answer is clear, and so the object of worship should be clear. In a modern context, the principle applies to anything people turn to as ultimate providers—the state, economic systems, science, or charismatic leaders. None of these have the power to create provision; they merely distribute or manipulate what Allah has already created. The verse reorients the heart to place its ultimate trust and worship only in the One who holds the keys to all provision.

=74=

This verse serves as the direct imperative conclusion derived from the preceding arguments. “So do not set up equals to Allah” is the command to abandon shirk entirely.

The word “equals” (andādan) refers to rivals, peers, or partners that are placed on par with Allah in worship, love, or obedience. The command is a prohibition born from the overwhelming evidence of Allah's exclusive power in creation, provision, and the intricate ordering of the universe. After demonstrating that idols provide nothing and possess no power, the conclusion is inescapable: do not make them equals.

The second part of the verse, “for Allah certainly knows and you do not know,” grounds this prohibition in the vast disparity in knowledge. Allah knows the true reality of all things. He knows that these so-called gods are false, that they have no power, and that worshipping them is a grave injustice. He knows the hidden consequences of shirk in this life and the Hereafter. Human beings, on the other hand, are limited in their knowledge. They may ignorantly assume that their idols intercede or that their false beliefs are harmless, but they do not truly know. The polytheists had no certain knowledge that their idols were divine; they were merely following conjecture and tradition. This verse highlights that faith must be based on knowledge and certainty, not on guesswork or blind imitation. Allah, the All-Knowing, has informed us of the truth, and we must submit to His knowledge.

The verse also implies that there may be hidden aspects of reality—the true nature of divinity, the consequences of actions, the details of the unseen—that are beyond human grasp, and therefore it is wise to follow the guidance of the One who knows everything. For contemporary believers, this verse is a call to evaluate all allegiances. Do we set up equals to Allah by obeying someone against His commands, by fearing someone as we should only fear Allah, or by loving someone with a devotion due only to Him? The command is not just about stone idols but about any rival that competes for the ultimate devotion of the heart. The humility to acknowledge “you do not know” and to trust Allah's infinite knowledge is the foundation of true submission.

=75=

To further illustrate the absurdity of shirk, the sūrah now presents a parable. “Allah sets forth a parable” introduces an analogy that makes the abstract concept of tawḥīd tangible. “A slave who lacks all means” describes a person in bondage, owned by another, possessing absolutely nothing—no wealth, no authority, no ability to act independently. He is completely under the control of his master, incapable of providing anything. “Compared to a free man to whom We granted a good provision, of which he donates freely, openly and secretly.” This free man is not only independent but wealthy and generous. He has been given abundant provision by Allah, and he spends it freely, both in public and in private, benefiting others without constraint. The contrast between the two is stark: one is helpless, the other is empowered and beneficent. Then the question: “Are they equal?

” The answer is obviously no. No rational person would equate a powerless slave with a generous, free provider. The parable then applies this obvious inequality to the comparison between Allah and idols. The idols are like the slave who lacks all means; they are owned, controlled, and utterly incapable of providing any good. Allah is like the free man, indeed infinitely above the analogy, possessing all wealth and power, and generously providing for His creation openly and secretly. “Praise be to Allah” is the natural response to recognising this truth; all gratitude and praise belong to the One who is the ultimate generous Provider.

The verse ends with “In fact, most of them do not know. ” This lament highlights that despite the clarity of the analogy, many people fail to grasp its implications due to heedlessness or stubbornness. The verse is a masterpiece of rhetorical persuasion, using a simple social reality to demolish the polytheists' complex theological error.

For the believer, this parable deepens appreciation for Allah's generosity and reinforces the logical necessity of exclusive worship. It also encourages the quality of generosity: the free man who donates secretly and openly is a model for believers, who should emulate Allah's attribute of provision by being charitable with what they have been given.

=76=

This is a second parable, continuing the theme of contrasting powerless false gods with the true God. “And Allah sets forth a parable of two men” introduces another dual comparison.

The first man is described with a series of severe deficiencies: “one of them is dumb, incapable of anything.” He is mute, unable to speak or communicate, and by extension unable to understand or respond. “Incapable of anything” translates lā yaqdiru ʿalā shay’, meaning he has absolutely no power to accomplish any task. He is completely impotent.

Furthermore, “He is a burden on his master.” Not only does he not contribute, but he requires constant care and supervision. He is a drain, a dependent who offers nothing in return. “Wherever he is sent, he brings no good” means that even when given a task, he fails utterly and produces no positive outcome. This man is a picture of total uselessness and dependency.

In contrast, the second man is described simply: “Can such a person be equal to the one who commands justice and is on the Straight Path? ” This second man is articulate, able to command and enjoin justice, indicating sound intellect, moral uprightness, and the ability to guide others. He is himself on the Straight Path, meaning his own life is aligned with righteousness, and he leads others to it. The parable, when applied, exposes the folly of comparing Allah—the One who commands justice, guides to the Straight Path, and possesses all perfection—with idols that are like the dumb, incapable man. The idols cannot speak, cannot answer prayers, cannot provide, and cannot guide. They are a burden on their worshippers, and wherever the worshippers turn to them, they find no good. The parable also has a secondary application: the contrast between a believer and a disbeliever, or between a leader who promotes justice and one who is morally and spiritually mute.

For the believer, the parable is a call to embody the qualities of the second man: to speak truth, command justice, and stay firm on the Straight Path. It also serves as a warning against being spiritually mute and incapable, a burden on society, contributing no good. The rhetorical question leaves the answer hanging in the air, forcing the listener to acknowledge the obvious inequality and, by extension, the irrationality of shirk.

=77=

This verse shifts back to affirming Allah's exclusive knowledge and power, particularly regarding the unseen and the Last Hour. “To Allah alone belongs the knowledge of the unseen in the heavens and the earth” establishes that all hidden realities, whether in the celestial realms or the terrestrial world, are known only to Him. This includes the future, the past that is unrecorded, the secrets of hearts, and the exact timing of the Day of Judgment. The verse implicitly refutes any claim that the idols, the soothsayers, or the jinn have access to the unseen. The Quraysh would often consult diviners and believe their idols had some hidden knowledge, but here the absolute exclusivity of Allah's knowledge is declared. Then the verse addresses the power to bring about the Hour: “Bringing about the Hour would only take the blink of an eye, or even less.” The disbelievers thought the resurrection was impossible or would be a massive, difficult undertaking. The verse corrects this notion by stating that for Allah, the establishment of the Hour is as easy and instantaneous as the blink of an eye, or even quicker. He does not require preparation, tools, or time. His command “Be” suffices, as stated earlier in the sūrah.

The phrase “or even less” emphasizes the absolute effortlessness; human language struggles to capture the instantaneous nature of divine action. The verse concludes, “Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything. ” This is the logical seal. The combination of absolute knowledge of the unseen and absolute power over all things, including the instantaneous creation of the Hour, leaves no room for doubt about the truth of the resurrection. For the believers, this verse reinforces the importance of trusting in Allah's plan, even when the unseen is obscure. Matters that are hidden, such as the timing of relief or the outcome of events, are in the hands of the One who knows all. It also instills a sense of the imminence of the Hour, not in a chronological sense, but in the sense that its coming is so easy for Allah that it could occur at any moment. This awareness encourages constant preparedness.

The verse also serves as a comfort: the same power that can bring the Hour in the blink of an eye can bring relief to the oppressed and justice to the wronged with similar swiftness.

=78=

The sūrah returns to a most intimate sign, the creation of each human being. “And Allah brought you out of the wombs of your mothers while you knew nothing” describes the state of complete ignorance at birth. An infant emerges from the womb possessing no knowledge of the world, utterly dependent and unaware. This starting point of absolute zero knowledge is a humbling reminder of human origins.

The phrase “brought you out” emphasizes the divine agency in the birth process, which is a miracle often overlooked due to its commonality. Then the verse mentions three specific faculties given by Allah: “and gave you hearing, sight, and intellect.” The order is significant and corresponds to developmental reality. Hearing is the first sense to develop and function, even in the womb, and is the primary channel for receiving revelation and guidance. Sight comes next, allowing the person to observe the signs in the world.

Finally, intellect (af’idah, often translated as hearts or minds) refers to the faculty of understanding, reasoning, and spiritual perception, which integrates the data from the senses and enables comprehension of higher truths. These three faculties are the means by which a human being acquires knowledge and navigates life. They are gifts, not achievements. The purpose of these gifts is stated at the end: “so perhaps you would be thankful.” The entire process—from the emergence out of darkness and ignorance into the world equipped with these powerful tools—is designed to elicit gratitude. The gratitude should be directed to the Giver, not merely expressed as a vague thankfulness but manifested in using those faculties for the purpose they were given: to know Allah, to worship Him, and to do good. The verse is a profound call to self-reflection. Every moment of hearing, seeing, and understanding is a blessing that demands appreciation. The disbelievers use these very faculties to deny their Creator, which is the ultimate ingratitude.

For the believer, this verse transforms the ordinary experience of sensory perception into a constant reminder of Allah's favours. It also places a moral responsibility on the use of these faculties: the ear should be kept from listening to falsehood, the eye from looking at what is forbidden, and the mind from entertaining corrupt thoughts. The verse connects the biological human development to the spiritual imperative of gratitude, forming a seamless link between the physical and the moral.

=79=

This verse draws attention to a readily observable sign of divine power: the flight of birds. “Have they not seen the birds glide in the open sky?

” The word “glide” (musakhkharāt) indicates that the birds are subjected and made subservient, flying effortlessly in the vast space of the sky. The phrase “in the open sky” (jaww as-samā’) refers to the expanse between heaven and earth, the atmosphere. A bird, heavier than air, suspended in mid-air without falling, is a daily miracle that people take for granted. The verse points out that this is not an autonomous natural phenomenon but a direct act of divine sustenance: “None holds them up except Allah.” The laws of aerodynamics that scientists later discovered are the mechanisms through which Allah's will operates. The bird does not hold itself up by its own power; it relies on the air, the design of its wings, its lightweight bones, and its instinctive flying skills, all of which are creations of Allah. The suspension of the bird is an ongoing act of divine power, moment by moment. This verse, like earlier ones, answers the materialist view that nature operates independently. It asserts that Allah is the immediate sustainer, holding the bird in the sky just as He holds the stars in their orbits.

The verse concludes, “Surely in this are signs for those who believe. ” The sign is specifically for believers because they have the faith that allows them to see the divine hand behind natural phenomena. The bird's flight becomes a symbol of the soul's dependence on Allah. Without His hold, it would plummet. Similarly, the believer's spiritual state is upheld only by Allah's mercy.

The verse also evokes a sense of the beauty and freedom of birds in flight, which is a source of aesthetic pleasure, yet another layer of divine favour. In a modern context, where air travel has made humans jaded, this verse invites us to look at birds not as mere biological machines but as living signs, performing their flight by the command and hold of Allah. It encourages a contemplative look at nature, where every bird becomes a preacher calling to faith for those who have eyes to see and hearts to believe.

=80=

This verse returns to the list of tangible blessings, focusing on dwellings and clothing derived from animals. “And Allah has made your homes a place to rest” begins with the most fundamental blessing after food and drink: shelter. The home is a place of sakīnah, tranquillity and rest, where a person retreats from the outside world, finds privacy, and enjoys comfort with family.

This is a divine gift often underappreciated until one is homeless. The verse then expands to mobile dwellings: “and has given you tents from the hide of animals, light to handle when you travel and when you camp.” This was directly relevant to the nomadic Arab lifestyle. Tents made from animal skins provided portable shelter that could be easily carried and set up during journeys and at campsites. They were light, practical, and perfectly suited to the desert environment. The verse acknowledges Allah's provision in both settled and nomadic contexts. Then, “And out of their wool, fur, and hair He has given you furnishings and goods for a while.” The “their” refers back to the cattle (anʿām) mentioned throughout the sūrah. Wool from sheep, fur from camels, and hair from goats are all raw materials that Allah has provided for making carpets, blankets, cushions, clothing, and other household furnishings.

The phrase “for a while” (ilā ḥīn) indicates that these goods are for a limited period, the duration of worldly life. They are temporary comforts, not eternal possessions. This verse ties together multiple threads: the blessing of animals, the blessing of shelter, and the temporary nature of worldly goods. It also implicitly addresses the nomadic Quraysh and other tribes, reminding them that their very shelters come from the animals created by Allah. The idols have no part in providing these necessities. For the modern believer, the verse encourages gratitude for the roof over one's head, the bed one sleeps in, and the clothing one wears, all of which trace back to the earth and its creatures that Allah has subjected. It fosters a minimalist spiritual attitude: these goods are for “a while,” so one should not become overly attached to them, but use them comfortably while focusing on the eternal home of the Hereafter. The verse beautifully combines the recognition of present blessings with the perspective of transience.
