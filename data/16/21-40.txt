=21=

This verse continues the demolition of idol worship by adding another layer to the description of the false gods. After declaring that the idols cannot create anything and are themselves created, the sūrah now describes them as “dead, not alive.” This simple contrast strips away any lingering illusion of power or personality that idolaters might attribute to their objects of worship. Life is the most fundamental quality we associate with agency, awareness, and the ability to respond. A dead thing possesses none of these. The idols, whether made of stone, wood, or metal, are utterly lifeless. They do not hear the prayers of their worshippers, they do not see their acts of devotion, and they cannot respond with help or harm.

The verse then adds a further dimension of their impotence: “not even knowing when their followers will be resurrected. ” This is a devastating point. The ultimate destiny of every human being is resurrection and judgement. Even if a person could find some worldly benefit from an idol, the idol is completely ignorant of the most critical event in that person’s existence. How can a worshipper rely on something that does not even know when its own devotees will be raised to face their Lord? The idol cannot intercede, cannot warn, and cannot prepare its follower for that Day. This echoes the Qur’anic theme that on the Day of Judgement, the false gods will disown their worshippers, and the worshippers will realise the utter futility of their devotion. The verse serves a powerful pedagogical purpose: it invites the polytheist to compare the living, all-knowing Creator, who is aware of every hidden thing and has power over resurrection, with these dead, ignorant objects. The logical conclusion is inevitable. The idol is worse than a dead animal; an animal at least once possessed life and senses. The idol never possessed life at all. In a modern context, the principle extends to any dead thing that people devote their lives to, whether it is the dead matter of money, lifeless technology, or expired ideologies. Only a living, eternal God is worthy of ultimate concern. The believer who understands this verse will feel a profound gratitude that they are connected to the Ever-Living, the Self-Subsisting, who knows all their states and holds their resurrection in His hands.

The verse also strengthens the heart against the fear of false powers, because it reminds us that all such powers are essentially dead before Allah.

=22=

After the sustained critique of idols, this verse returns to the positive proclamation of tawḥīd. “Your God is only One God.” The address “your” is singular, directed at the Prophet or at each individual listener, making the declaration deeply personal. The exclusivity of divinity is absolute; there is no room for a second, a partner, or an intermediary. The one God is the same God of Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and all the prophets.

The verse then immediately shifts to a description of the rejecters: “As for those who do not believe in the Hereafter, their hearts are in denial, and they are too proud.” This identifies the root cause of polytheism and disbelief. The rejection of the Hereafter is directly linked to the corruption of the heart.

The phrase “their hearts are in denial” indicates a deep-seated refusal to accept the truth, not because of a lack of evidence, but because of a spiritual disease. The word for denial, munkir, carries the sense of actively rejecting something they actually recognise inwardly. Their innate nature, the fitrah, testifies to the oneness of God, but their hearts have been overcome by denial.

The second characteristic, “and they are too proud,” reveals the deeper motive: arrogance. Believing in the Hereafter means accepting accountability, judgement, and the ultimate authority of a higher power. The proud person despises the idea of bowing to a Lord, of being held responsible for their actions, and of being told what is right and wrong. Idol worship, by contrast, allowed them to invent their own gods, which they could manipulate and which placed no moral demands upon them. They could live as they pleased, with their idols serving their desires rather than guiding them. The verse thus diagnoses the spiritual psychology of disbelief as a combination of intellectual dishonesty and moral arrogance. The heart knows the truth but denies it, and the ego refuses to submit. This diagnosis remains profoundly relevant today. Many reject faith not because they have examined the evidence and found it lacking, but because accepting it would require a radical reorientation of their lives, a humility they are unwilling to embrace. The verse is also a comfort to the believer who sees people rejecting clear truth; it explains that the issue lies not in the message but in the diseased state of the rejecter’s heart. The cure is a sincere turning to Allah to remove the pride and soften the heart towards His oneness and the reality of the Hereafter.

=23=

This verse follows directly from the diagnosis of the disbelievers’ state in the previous verse. Their hearts are in denial and they are arrogant, but their outward behaviour and their inner secrets are fully known to Allah. “Without a doubt, Allah knows what they conceal and what they reveal.

” The phrase “without a doubt” is an affirmation that dispels any illusion of hiddenness. The disbelievers might hide their plots, their mockery of the Prophet, their secret fears of the truth, or even a suppressed recognition of Allah’s oneness. They reveal their idol worship, their arguments against the Qur’an, and their open hostility. Both categories, the concealed and the revealed, are equally exposed to divine knowledge. This serves as a warning: there is no secret chamber of the heart or private meeting where they can escape Allah’s awareness.

The verse then adds a severe judgement: “He certainly does not like those who are too proud.” The Arabic word for pride here is mustakbirīn, those who actively seek to magnify themselves and refuse to submit to truth.

This statement is devastating because divine dislike is the precursor to divine punishment. Allah’s love is the source of all good, and His dislike portends utter ruin. The proud person is the opposite of the grateful, humble servant who recognises their total dependence on Allah. The pride of the disbelievers prevents them from accepting tawḥīd and the Hereafter. They consider themselves too great to follow a mere human messenger, too great to abandon the religion of their forefathers, too great to submit their desires to a revealed law.

The verse teaches that pride is not a minor character flaw; it is the fundamental barrier between a soul and its Lord. In the context of the sūrah’s flow, this verse links the knowledge of Allah to the rejection of the proud. Because Allah knows what they conceal, He knows their arrogance, their inner mockery, and their stubborn refusal, and He does not like them for it. This should terrify anyone harbouring pride and encourage them to cultivate humility.

For the believer, this verse prompts a constant examination of the heart: are we secretly proud of our piety, knowledge, or lineage? Such hidden pride is known to Allah and is disliked by Him. The remedy is to remember one’s humble origin from a sperm-drop and to attribute all good to Allah’s grace.

The verse also reassures the oppressed believers that Allah is fully aware of the hidden arrogance of their persecutors and will deal with them justly.

=24=

This verse provides a concrete example of the arrogance and denial described in the previous verses. The question “What has your Lord revealed?” was likely posed by the Quraysh to one another, or by newcomers to Mecca asking about the Prophet’s message, but it is framed here as a test. The disbelievers are asked to identify what their Lord—acknowledging that they did believe in a supreme Lord—had sent down. Their response is not a denial of revelation per se, but a dismissive characterisation of its content: “Ancient fables! ” The Arabic term asāṭīr al-awwalīn literally means the writings or tales of the ancient people. By calling the Qur’an ancient fables, they sought to reduce its authority in several ways. First, they implied that it was not a fresh divine revelation but a collection of old stories recycled by Muḥammad. Second, they suggested it was mythical and unworthy of serious attention, belonging to the realm of bedtime stories, not truth. Third, they attempted to strip it of its moral and legal weight; if it was just old tales, no one was obliged to obey its commands. This mockery was an expression of their pride and denial.

Historically, the Quraysh knew that the stories of previous prophets like Noah, Hūd, and Ṣāliḥ were not fabrications; they were part of the cultural memory of Arabia. But to acknowledge them as true would mean acknowledging the truth of Muḥammad’s prophethood and the coming judgement, which they were too proud to do. So they resorted to ridicule. The verse exposes the bankruptcy of their response; they had no rational argument, so they attacked with derision. This tactic is common among those who reject truth across ages. When they cannot refute the message, they mock it, calling it outdated, primitive, or mythical. This verse is a comfort to the Prophet and to all believers who face similar mockery. The response of the disbelievers is not a reflection on the message but on their own diseased hearts. It also provides a lesson for believers today: when we hear the truth being mocked, we should recognise the arrogance behind the mockery and not let it shake our faith, because the same was said to the Prophet. The verse sets up a contrast that will follow in the sūrah between the response of the disbelievers and that of the mindful believers when asked the same question, showing that the difference lies entirely in the state of the heart.

=25=

This verse announces the consequences of the mockery and the active misguidance of others. “Let them bear their burdens in full on the Day of Judgment” refers to the full weight of their own sins, particularly the sin of disbelieving and ridiculing the revelation. The imagery of bearing burdens evokes the crushing weight of sin, which will be an actual, felt reality on that Day. But their punishment is not limited to their own misdeeds.

The verse adds, “as well as some of the burdens of those they mislead without knowledge.” This is a principle of tremendous gravity: a person who leads others astray will carry, in addition to their own sins, a portion of the sins of those they misguided.

The phrase “without knowledge” indicates that these leaders of misguidance actively led others astray through false arguments, propaganda, or social pressure, preying on the ignorance of their followers. However, this does not absolve the followers of their own responsibility; they also bear the burden of their own misdeeds, but the misleaders share in it because they caused it. The burden of the misled person is not reduced; rather, the misleader receives an additional equivalent portion. A well-known hadith of the Prophet Muḥammad confirms this principle: “Whoever calls to guidance will have a reward similar to the reward of those who follow him, without their rewards being diminished in any respect. And whoever calls to misguidance will have upon him sin similar to the sins of those who follow him, without their sins being diminished in any respect.” This verse and this hadith together form a powerful deterrent for anyone in a position of influence, whether a political leader, a scholar, a media personality, or even a parent or friend. It also serves as a warning against spreading false ideologies, misleading content, or immoral lifestyles. In the Meccan context, the chieftains who actively worked to prevent people from embracing Islam would bear not only their own disbelief but also some of the burdens of those they successfully kept in idolatry.

The verse concludes with an exclamation: “Evil indeed is what they will bear!” This emphasises the horror of that load.

The word “evil” here is a judgement on the terrible nature of the burden itself. Reflecting on this verse should make a believer deeply cautious about what they call others to, and should inspire a sense of responsibility to only promote that which brings true guidance. Conversely, it offers hope to those who call to good, knowing that their reward multiplies without diminishing the rewards of others.

=26=

This verse provides a historical warning by referencing the fate of previous nations who plotted against their messengers. “Those before them” refers to earlier peoples like the people of Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, and Pharaoh’s folk, who conspired to undermine the prophets’ missions and maintain their systems of disbelief. The Quraysh, who were actively plotting against Muḥammad, are reminded that they are not the first to try such schemes. The nature of their plot is described with a powerful metaphor: “Allah struck at the very foundation of their structure. ” The schemers built their power, their arguments, and their society upon a foundation of falsehood. They thought their structure was solid, but Allah targeted the very base of it, which represents the core assumptions and pillars of their system. By destroying the foundation, the entire edifice collapsed. “So the roof collapsed on top of them” indicates that the destruction came from their own structure turning against them. The thing they had built for their protection and dominance became the instrument of their ruin.

This is a precise depiction of how divine punishment often operates: it turns the schemes of the wicked back upon themselves. The verse then adds, “and the torment came upon them from where they did not expect.” Their calculations never included the possibility of divine intervention. They had planned for every human threat but were completely blindsided by the judgement of Allah, which arrived from an angle they had not considered. This was literally true for many past nations that were destroyed by earthquakes, storms, or plagues, and metaphorically true for the Quraysh, who would eventually face the Muslim conquest, a turn of events they never imagined. The verse serves as both a warning and a comfort. It warns the plotters of Mecca that their intelligence and resources are useless if Allah opposes them. It comforts the Prophet and the believers that no matter how elaborate the schemes of the enemy appear, Allah can destroy them from their foundations in an instant and from a direction they never anticipated. In a modern context, it cautions those who place their trust in elaborate worldly systems built on injustice; these systems carry the seeds of their own destruction, and when Allah’s judgement comes, it will collapse upon its architects in ways they could never have foreseen.

=27=

This verse moves from the worldly punishment of past plotters to the eschatological punishment awaiting all who follow their path. “Then on the Day of Judgment He will humiliate them” uses a verb that implies open, public disgrace. The humiliation is not merely physical pain but psychological and social degradation before all of creation. The divine question, “Where are My so-called associate-gods for whose sake you used to oppose the believers?” is a devastating rebuke.

The phrase “My so-called associate-gods” is deeply ironic; they are called “associate-gods” with a sarcastic reference to the polytheists’ false claim, and the possessive “My” mocks the idea that Allah has any partners. The question reminds them that their entire worldly conflict with the believers was rooted in their insistence on defending gods that do not exist. They persecuted the believers, opposed the truth, and waged war against the Prophet for the sake of these fabricated deities. Now, in their hour of greatest need, those gods are nowhere to be found. The believers, who were once the objects of their opposition, will witness this humiliation, turning the tables completely.

The verse then introduces the statement of “those gifted with knowledge,” referring to the prophets, the angels, or the knowledgeable believers. They will declare, “Surely disgrace and misery today are upon the disbelievers.

” This is not a personal gloating but a factual recognition of divine justice. The knowledge they possessed in the world about the truth of tawḥīd and the Hereafter is now vindicated. Their statement serves as a divine seal upon the disbelievers’ state. The disgrace is the inner shame and external dishonour, and the misery is the suffering of the Fire. This verse offers profound comfort to believers who face mockery and opposition for their faith. It assures them that the present situation, where the arrogant appear powerful and the believers appear weak, is temporary. The Day will come when the roles are reversed in the most absolute sense, and the humiliation of the disbelievers will be a public, eternal reality. It also instills in the believer a sense of the worthlessness of the false gods of this world—whether idols, ideologies, or egos—for which so many conflicts are fought. All of them will vanish, leaving their devotees in disgrace.

=28=

This verse zooms in on the moment of death for the disbelievers described earlier. “Those whose souls the angels seize while they wrong themselves” paints a terrifying scene. The seizing of the soul is the moment of death, and it is carried out by the angels of death.

The phrase “while they wrong themselves” indicates that at the moment of death, they are still in a state of persistent wrongdoing. They spent their lives in disbelief, oppression, and sin, and they die without having repented.

The Arabic verb for seizing implies a forceful, harsh extraction, unlike the gentle taking of the believers’ souls that will be described later in the sūrah. As death arrives and the reality of the Hereafter becomes undeniable, the disbeliever makes a desperate attempt at denial and false repentance: “they will then offer full submission and say falsely, ‘We did not do any evil.’” Their sudden submission is not sincere faith; it is the compelled recognition of the truth when it is too late. Their claim that they did no evil is a blatant lie, an attempt to escape the consequences by denying the very actions that are now about to be judged.

However, the angels reply with a decisive rejection: “No! Surely Allah fully knows what you used to do.” The angels function as divine agents who speak the truth that cuts through the disbeliever’s falsehood.

The phrase “fully knows” emphasises the completeness and perfection of Allah’s knowledge; every deed, every word, every hidden intention is recorded. There is no room for argument or appeal. This verse exposes a tragic human tendency: to deny one’s sins until the last moment, and even then to try to wriggle out of responsibility. It is a stark warning that deathbed repentance, when the soul is being seized and the unseen becomes seen, is not accepted, because it is not voluntary faith but forced confession. True repentance must occur before the throes of death.

The verse also highlights the mercy inherent in Allah’s system; the fact that He fully knows means that justice will be perfect. No one will be wronged, and no cunning liar will escape.

For believers, the verse is a powerful motivator to live with constant awareness of death and to avoid the false comfort of thinking one can postpone reform until old age, because death might seize them while they are still wronging themselves.

=29=

This verse delivers the final sentence of the scene begun in the previous verse. After the angels refute the disbeliever’s false denial, they pronounce the judgement: “So enter the gates of Hell, to stay there forever.” The command “enter” is one of utter contempt and finality. There is no delay, no second chance, no court of appeal. The “gates of Hell” indicates that Hell has multiple entrances, perhaps corresponding to different categories of sinners or different levels of punishment.

The phrase “to stay there forever” seals their fate eternally. The punishment is not temporary reformatory discipline; it is permanent banishment and torment because their state at death was one of irreversible rejection and arrogance.

The verse then closes with a powerful exclamation: “Indeed, what an evil home for the arrogant! ” This explicitly links the eternal punishment to the sin of arrogance, which has been a recurring theme. The disbelievers did not merely make an intellectual error; they were mustakbirīn, those who thought themselves too great to bow to their Creator. Their arrogance made them reject the truth when it came, mock the messengers, and persecute the believers. Now, the home prepared for them is described as “evil,” a word that encompasses all the horror, suffering, and degradation of Hell. The rhetorical exclamation invites the listener to truly contemplate the awfulness of that destination. This verse serves as the ultimate warning against pride. In Islam, even a particle of arrogance is enough to bar one from Paradise, as stated in a hadith. The arrogant person’s insistence on their own greatness is the direct opposite of the attitude required to enter Paradise, which is humility, submission, and recognition of one’s utter dependence on Allah.

In the context of the sūrah, this verse concludes the section on the fate of the disbelievers who denied and mocked, showing that their worldly arrogance will result in eternal humiliation. For the believer, this should prompt a constant internal struggle against arrogance in all its forms, whether it is pride in knowledge, wealth, lineage, or piety. The realisation that Hell is the evil home for the arrogant should make the heart tremble and seek refuge in humility, which is the true station of the servant before the Master.

=30=

This verse presents the beautiful contrast to verse 24, where the disbelievers called the revelation “ancient fables.” The same question is posed: “What has your Lord revealed?” But this time it is asked to “those mindful of Allah,” the muttaqīn, the God-conscious believers. Their answer is immediate and exuberant: “All the best! ” The word khayr, translated as “all the best,” encompasses every type of goodness: truth, guidance, mercy, wisdom, justice, and blessing. The believers recognise the revelation for what it truly is because their hearts are pure and receptive. They do not parse it to find fault; they embrace it wholly as a gift from their Lord. The difference in responses is entirely a function of the state of the heart.

The verse then expands on the reward of such people: “For those who do good in this world, there is goodness.” The phrase “do good” translates aḥsanu, which implies excellence in action, doing things beautifully and with full dedication. For them, in this very world, there is ḥasanah, a good life. This includes inner peace, contentment, provision, and the sweetness of faith. The verse immediately directs attention upward: “But far better is the eternal Home of the Hereafter.” The goodness of this world, however wonderful, is nothing compared to the reward of the next life. The comparative “far better” dismisses any attachment that might make a believer satisfied only with worldly goodness. The Hereafter is the ultimate goal. The verse closes with an exclamation: “How excellent indeed is the home of the righteous!

” The word for “righteous” connects back to mindfulness. The home is the dwelling place of the righteous, which the next verses will describe. This exclamation is an invitation to marvel at and yearn for that home. The entire verse is a model of the believer’s psychology: immediate recognition of revealed truth, a life of excellence that brings worldly goodness, but a constant orientation towards the far greater reward of the Hereafter. The believer is not a pessimist who despises all worldly good, nor a hedonist who is satisfied with it. They receive the good of this life with gratitude, but their hearts are always beating for the eternal home.

=31=

This verse begins with a description of that excellent home mentioned at the end of the previous verse. “The Gardens of Eternity” translates Jannātu ʿAdn, the gardens of perpetual residence.

The word ʿAdn implies a settled, enduring abode from which one is never expelled. This contrasts starkly with the temporary homes of this world and with the eternal but horrific home of the arrogant in Hell.

The phrase “which they will enter” indicates full and unhindered access. In this world, even the most beautiful gardens have gates and restrictions; in Paradise, the righteous are welcomed as rightful inheritors. The imagery “under which rivers flow” is a recurring Qur’anic description of Paradise. It evokes coolness, abundance, and life. The rivers flow beneath their palaces and gardens, providing a continuous, effortless source of water, milk, honey, and wine of a pure nature. This was especially appealing to the desert-dwelling Arabs, for whom a garden with flowing rivers represented the pinnacle of comfort and luxury.

The verse then adds, “In it they will have whatever they desire.” This is a staggering promise. Every legitimate desire of the human soul, from the most basic to the most sublime, will be fulfilled. There is no deprivation, no unrequited longing, and no boredom. The human capacity to desire is vast, and Paradise will satisfy it completely. Importantly, the desires of the righteous in Paradise will themselves be purified, so they will only desire what is beautiful and good.

The verse concludes the description by stating, “This is how Allah rewards the righteous.” The word “righteous” again references the muttaqīn. The reward is not an arbitrary favour; it is the just and generous recompense for their mindfulness and good deeds in the world.

The phrase “this is how” emphasizes that the magnificent reward just described is the consistent pattern of Allah’s treatment of the righteous; it is His sunnah. The verse functions as a powerful motivator: when the believer hears that the righteous will have whatever they desire in eternal gardens, the temporary allure of sinful desires in this world loses its grip. The delayed gratification of Paradise is infinitely better than the immediate gratification of forbidden things. It also instills a profound sense of hope and longing, making the daily struggle to be mindful of Allah feel light, because the destination is so overwhelmingly beautiful.

=32=

This verse continues the description of the righteous’ reward by shifting back to the moment of death, creating a direct parallel with the earlier description of the disbelievers’ death in verse 28. “Those whose souls the angels take while they are virtuous” presents a scene of serenity and honour.

The Arabic word for “virtuous,” ṭayyibīn, means pure, good, and pleasant. The believers die in a state of spiritual purity, free from the major sins from which they have repented, and full of faith. The angels who take their souls come with gentleness and mercy.

This is a stark contrast to the harsh seizure of the disbelievers’ souls. The angels then greet the believing soul with “Peace be upon you!

” This is the salutation of Paradise, a word that conveys safety, peace, and the removal of all fear and grief. The angels are not accusers but welcomers, announcing the soul’s secure passage. Then comes the invitation: “Enter Paradise for what you used to do.” The command to enter Paradise is given even at the moment of death, as the soul begins its journey to the Barzakh, the intermediate realm, where it will experience a foretaste of the eternal bliss.

The phrase “for what you used to do” establishes the principle of reward based on deeds, all within the framework of Allah’s mercy. Their entry is not arbitrary; it is the direct consequence of their righteous actions, which were themselves enabled by Allah’s grace. This does not contradict the hadith that no one enters Paradise by deeds alone, but by Allah’s mercy; rather, deeds are the visible expression of faith and the means by which mercy is manifested in degrees of reward. This verse brings immense comfort to believers. The thought that the angels, those noble and pure beings, will greet a dying believer with peace and an invitation to Paradise eases the fear of death. It encourages the believer to strive to be among the ṭayyibīn, those whose inner and outer states are pure, so that when the angels come, they find a soul that is pleasant to receive. The stark contrast between this scene and the previous scene of the angels seizing the disbelievers highlights the dual outcome and reinforces the sūrah’s central theme: gratitude and mindfulness lead to peace and eternal bliss, while arrogance and denial lead to humiliation and eternal punishment.

=33=

After detailing the contrasting fates of the disbelievers and the righteous, the sūrah now directly challenges the disbelievers’ complacency. “Are they only awaiting the coming of the angels or the command of your Lord?

” The question is rhetorical and carries a tone of warning. The disbelievers are not expecting the truth to come to them through reflection on the signs; they are simply waiting, in their heedlessness, for one of two things: the arrival of the angel of death to seize their souls, or the final command of Allah, which could be the general judgement of the Hour or a specific punishment in this world. Both of these events will mark the end of their opportunity to believe.

The question implies that they are living on borrowed time, postponing faith until a moment when it will be too late. The verse then adds, “So were those before them.

” This is a brief but heavy statement. Previous nations also delayed, mocked, and waited, and then the angels or the divine command came upon them suddenly and destroyed them. The Quraysh were walking the same path. Then the verse establishes a crucial principle of divine justice: “And Allah never wronged them, but it was they who wronged themselves.” The punishment that overtakes them is not an act of injustice from Allah. He does not wrong anyone, not even by an atom’s weight. The wrong they suffer is entirely self-inflicted. They wronged themselves by rejecting the truth, by worshipping false gods, by burying their daughters alive, and by mocking the messengers. They were given clear signs, sound intellect, and free will. Their destruction is the natural consequence of their own choices.

This principle is vital because some might see the punishment described in the sūrah and mistakenly think of Allah as vengeful or cruel. The Qur’an repeatedly clarifies that Allah’s punishment is a manifestation of justice and is always preceded by ample warning and opportunity. For contemporary believers, this verse is a call to urgency. We do not know when the angels will come for us personally or when the major signs of the Hour will commence. The attitude of “awaiting” while remaining in sin is spiritually deadly. We must not postpone repentance and reform.

The verse also provides comfort: we can trust that Allah’s judgement, however severe, is perfectly just, and no one is ever punished unfairly. This frees the believer from resentment against divine decree and encourages a focus on rectifying one’s own self, which is the only source of potential ruin.

=34=

This verse summarises the fate of the past nations and, by extension, warns of what awaits the Quraysh. “Then the evil consequences of their deeds overtook them.

” The word “consequences” translates sayyi’āt, which can mean the bad results or the punishment for evil deeds. Their own actions generated a destructive force that eventually caught up with them. The verb “overtook” implies that they could not outrun it; it enveloped them completely. No amount of wealth, power, or plotting could shield them once the appointed time arrived.

The second part of the verse adds a layer of poetic justice: “and they were overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule.” The very thing they mocked—the warnings of the prophets about divine punishment—became their reality. They used to laugh at the idea that stones from the sky or an earthquake would destroy them. They ridiculed the notion of a Day of Judgement. Then that mockery turned into their actual, lived nightmare.

This is a recurring theme: the thing a people mockingly dismiss often becomes the instrument of their destruction, because their mockery reflects a profound lack of seriousness about truth, which leads them to ignore all warnings until it is too late. For the Quraysh, they ridiculed the Prophet’s warnings of punishment, calling them ancient fables. This verse foretells that the same will happen to them, and indeed, the Battle of Badr saw the Quraysh leaders who most viciously mocked Islam being killed and humiliated. In a broader sense, the verse describes a universal law of history. Civilisations that become arrogant and mock the moral laws of Allah eventually collapse under the weight of their own corruption. The believer takes this as a sobering reminder never to mock the signs of Allah, the warnings of scripture, or the idea of accountability. Even a casual joke about hellfire or judgement can be a symptom of a diseased heart.

The verse also teaches that sin is not just a private matter; it has accumulating consequences that can overtake a person or a society, often when they least expect it. The only protection is sincere faith and constant turning to Allah, so that one is not among those overwhelmed by what they once ridiculed.

=35=

This verse records one of the most famous and deceitful arguments used by the Quraysh to justify their polytheism. They argued using divine decree as an excuse: “Had Allah willed, neither we nor our forefathers would have worshipped anything other than Him, nor prohibited anything without His command.” The logic, on the surface, suggests that their idol worship must be acceptable to Allah because Allah, being all-powerful, could have prevented it if He disliked it.

This is a classic case of misusing the concept of divine will to evade moral responsibility. They attempted to shift the blame onto Allah, implying that He approved of their shirk. The verse immediately undercuts this argument with two responses. First, “So did those before them.” This argument is not new; previous nations used the exact same excuse to reject their messengers. This shows that it is a hollow, recycled justification rather than a sincere search for truth. Second, the verse asks rhetorically, “Is not the messengers’ duty only to deliver the message clearly?” This clarifies the true role of the prophets. The messengers were sent to convey the divine message with clarity, to warn and to give glad tidings. Allah’s will to permit something to occur is not the same as His approval of it. Allah wills, by His universal creative will, the existence of disbelief and idol worship as a test for humanity, but He does not love it or command it. The sending of messengers to prohibit these very practices is the proof that Allah disapproves of them. The presence of a clear prohibition through the messengers completely refutes their argument. If Allah were pleased with their idol worship, He would not have sent a messenger commanding them to stop it. The verse thus distinguishes between Allah’s permissive will and His legislative will. This argument is still used today by some who claim that everything that happens is God’s will, so they are not responsible for their actions. The Qur’anic response is that God’s giving us free will and allowing us to choose does not mean He endorses our choices; He has clearly communicated what is right and wrong through revelation, and we will be judged by that standard.

The verse also liberates the Prophet from feeling responsible for their rejection, reminding him that his task is only clear delivery.

=36=

This verse expands on the principle of clear delivery mentioned in the previous verse. “We surely sent a messenger to every community” establishes the universality of divine guidance. No nation in history was left without a warner, a messenger who spoke their language and conveyed the core message. The content of that universal message is succinctly stated: “Worship Allah and shun false gods. ” The Arabic for false gods is ṭāghūt, which encompasses everything worshipped or obeyed besides Allah, including idols, tyrants, and satanic forces. The command is a positive call to worship Allah and a negative call to avoid all false objects of worship.

This is the essence of every prophetic mission. The verse then describes the two-fold response: “But some of them were guided by Allah, while others were destined to stray. ” The guidance mentioned is the special guidance that results from a person’s sincere openness to the truth. Allah guided them because they were receptive. The straying of others is described as a just consequence of their rejection and arrogance; they became subject to misguidance because of their own choices. This does not imply that Allah randomly misguides people; rather, those who persistently reject the clear message are left to stray by Allah’s just decree.

The verse then invites the skeptical Quraysh to empirical investigation: “So travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers!” The ruins of ʿĀd, Thamūd, and the people of Lot were visible along the Arabian trade routes. The Quraysh passed by these ruins during their winter and summer journeys.

This is an appeal to tangible historical evidence. The fate of the deniers is written in the archaeological record of the desert. The command to travel and observe is a recurring Qur’anic method of proof. It turns history into a teacher. In a contemporary context, while we may not see the exact ruins described, the principle of studying history to see the consequences of denying truth remains valid. The rise and fall of civilizations that rejected moral and spiritual truth is evident.

The verse also instills a sense of humility: the same message of tawḥīd that we have received has been sent to all humanity across time. We are part of a long chain of communities tested with the same call, and we should learn from the fate of those before us rather than repeating their mistakes.

=37=

This verse is a direct address to the Prophet Muḥammad, peace be upon him, comforting him and explaining a fundamental reality of guidance. The Prophet was deeply grieved by the stubborn rejection of his people. He was ḥarīṣ, intensely eager and keen, for their guidance. His compassion for his community drove him to exhaust himself in calling them to the truth. The verse acknowledges this sincere concern but then draws a divine boundary: “Allah certainly does not guide those He leaves to stray.

” The Arabic construction implies that when Allah, as a result of their persistent, defiant rejection, has decreed misguidance for a person, that misguidance becomes a fixed state that the Prophet’s keenness cannot reverse. The verse is not saying that the Prophet should stop caring or preaching; it is saying that the outcome is ultimately in Allah’s hands, not his. This prevents the preacher from falling into despair or taking personal responsibility for the rejection of others.

The phrase “and they will have no helpers” underlines the finality of their situation. On the Day of Judgement, or even in this world when punishment descends, they will find no one to assist them against Allah. Their idols, their wealth, their allies—all will be powerless. This verse is crucial for understanding the Islamic doctrine of guidance. It balances the emphasis on human effort with the absolute sovereignty of Allah. The Prophet is commanded to strive and convey, but he cannot force faith into hearts. The verse is also a warning to the rejecters: there is a point of no return where the heart becomes so sealed that even the most sincere teacher cannot affect it. The hope for change lies in oneself, in softening the heart before it hardens completely. For modern duʿāt, preachers, and concerned believers, this verse is a source of solace. It teaches that one can care deeply for the guidance of others, but one must not transgress the limits of one’s role. The result is in Allah’s hands. This fosters a healthy detachment that prevents burnout and allows one to continue one’s work with a balanced spirit. It also warns against the arrogance of thinking that we can guide whom we love; even the Prophet could not guide his beloved uncle Abū Ṭālib, despite his deep keenness. Guidance is a treasure that Allah bestows upon whom He wills.

=38=

This verse exposes one of the core doctrines of the Meccan pagans: their denial of the resurrection. They would “swear by Allah their most solemn oaths,” meaning they invoked Allah’s name with the strongest possible emphasis, to assert that there is no life after death. The irony is that they swore by Allah to deny a power of Allah. They used the name of the Creator to claim that the Creator cannot recreate His creation. Their oath was not based on evidence but on a materialist assumption that the decay of the body made resurrection impossible. The verse responds with a single emphatic word: “Yes He will!

” The Arabic balā is a forceful affirmative that refutes their negation. The resurrection will most certainly occur. Then the verse describes it as “a true promise binding on Him.” The resurrection is a divine promise, and Allah’s promise is truth.

The phrase “binding on Him” does not mean that anyone forces Allah; it means that Allah, in His perfect wisdom and justice, has obligated Himself to fulfill this promise. This is an act of His mercy and justice, for without resurrection, the moral order of the universe would be incomplete. The verse closes with “but most people do not know.” The knowledge referred to is not mere intellectual recognition but a deep, transformative awareness that shapes behaviour. Most people, including the oath-swearers, do not truly grasp the reality of the resurrection. If they did, they would not swear against it, and they would live their lives in preparation for it. The ignorance is both of Allah’s power—that He who created from nothing can easily recreate—and of the purpose of life. This verse is a recurring theme in the Meccan sūrahs, which place great emphasis on establishing the reality of the Hereafter. The denial of resurrection was not just a theoretical error; it was the linchpin of their immoral lifestyle. If there is no accountability, one can live selfishly and oppress others without consequence. The affirmation of the resurrection is therefore the foundation of moral responsibility. For the modern believer, this verse strengthens conviction in the face of materialist worldviews that reduce existence to what is observable. The resurrection is a promise of the All-Powerful, and reflecting on the signs in creation, as the sūrah does, reinforces that the One who fashioned the universe from nothing can and will recreate humanity.

=39=

This verse provides the wisdom behind the resurrection. The pronoun “He will do that” refers back to the act of raising the dead. The primary purpose is “to make clear to them what they disagreed on.” Humanity has been in perpetual disagreement over the most fundamental truths: the nature of God, the purpose of life, the reality of the afterlife, and moral values. In this world, these disagreements persist, and the truth, though established by revelation, is often obscured by argument, propaganda, and stubbornness. The Day of Judgement is the day of ultimate clarity. Every hidden truth will become manifest. Those who worshiped false gods will see the falsehood of their beliefs. Those who denied the prophets will see their vindication. The disagreements that filled the earth with conflict and confusion will be resolved not by debate but by undeniable reality.

The second purpose is specifically directed at the disbelievers: “and for the disbelievers to know that they were liars.” During their worldly life, they called the messengers liars, they called the revelation lies, and they themselves fabricated lies about Allah, claiming He had partners or daughters. On that Day, their own inner state and the manifest reality will testify that they were the true liars all along.

This is the poetic justice mentioned earlier. Their false claims will be exposed before all of creation, adding public humiliation to their private regret. The verse highlights that divine punishment is not arbitrary; it is integrally connected to truth and justice. The resurrection is the great clarifying event that resolves the ambiguities of the world.

For believers, this verse provides immense comfort. In a world where falsehood often appears triumphant and truth is maligned, the promise that one day everything will be made clear and the liars will be exposed is a source of patience and hope. It also motivates the believer to be scrupulously truthful in all matters, knowing that every statement and belief will one day be subjected to that ultimate clarification. The fear of being exposed as a liar on that Day should be enough to make a person flee from falsehood in all its forms.

=40=

This powerful verse concludes the discussion on resurrection by addressing the core doubt of the deniers: how can decayed bodies be brought back to life? The answer is a profound statement about the nature of divine creative power. “If We ever will something to exist” indicates that whenever Allah intends a thing, no matter how impossible it may seem to human minds, the process of its creation is effortless. “All We say is: ‘Be! ’ And it is!” The command “Be” (kun) is a single word that brings a thing into existence instantaneously. There is no gradual process, no raw material requirement, no tool, no assistance.

This command is not a vocal utterance in the human sense; it is the expression of His will that directly translates into the existence of the intended thing. The verb “it is” (fayakūn) indicates immediate, inevitable realisation. The difficulty of recreating a decomposed body, which the polytheists considered impossible, is no obstacle to the One whose mode of creation is the simple command “Be.” This verse is one of the most concise and powerful expressions of Allah’s omnipotence in the Qur’an. It demolishes the materialist objection by establishing that the creation of the universe and the resurrection of humanity operate on the same principle: the divine will. The creation of the heavens and the earth from nothing, which was even more staggering than resurrection, was accomplished by the same “Be. ” The verse also has a deep theological implication: it shows that the relationship between cause and effect in the natural world is not autonomous; it is sustained moment by moment by Allah’s command. Natural processes are simply the habitual pattern of the “Be” command. When Allah wills a miracle or the breaking of the natural order, the same “Be” suffices.

For the believer, this verse is a source of immense hope and trust. When facing a seemingly impossible situation, one remembers that the One who merely says “Be” to bring things into existence can resolve any difficulty. It also fosters humility: all of creation is utterly contingent, existing only because Allah says “Be”. If He withheld that command for an instant, everything would cease to be. The verse thus invites the mind to transcend the limits of human causality and rest in the unlimited power of the Creator, a power that guarantees the resurrection of the dead with the same effortless command that first brought them to life.
