=101=

This verse addresses the phenomenon of abrogation, naskh, which the disbelievers used as a pretext to attack the Prophet. "When We replace a verse with another" refers to the divine wisdom whereby certain rulings or recitations were superseded by later revelations. This replacement was not a correction of an error but a deliberate, purposeful progression in legislation and guidance, suited to the evolving circumstances of the nascent Muslim community. The parenthetical clause, "and Allah knows best what He reveals," affirms that every revelation, whether abrogating or abrogated, is rooted in perfect divine knowledge. Allah knows what is most beneficial for His servants at each stage, and His command is always wise. The disbelievers, however, seized upon this concept maliciously. They said, "You Muḥammad are just a fabricator." Their accusation was that the Prophet invented verses and then changed them when it suited him, a claim of forgery and inconsistency. This attack ignored the reality that abrogation occurs within a single divine source and serves a clear educational and legal purpose. The verse responds by stating, "In fact, most of them do not know." Their ignorance is twofold: they do not understand the nature of prophethood and progressive revelation, and they do not recognize that the Qur’an’s internal coherence is actually strengthened by such divinely guided development. A fabricator would be inconsistent and self-contradictory, whereas the Qur’an’s abrogations are part of a wise legislative plan.

This verse also comforts the Prophet, assuring him that the accusation is born of ignorance, not of any genuine flaw in the revelation. For believers, it establishes that abrogation is a reality within the Qur’an, to be accepted with trust in Allah's wisdom, and that changes in rulings do not imply a change in divine knowledge but rather a change in the circumstances of those addressed. It calls for humility before divine legislation and warns against the arrogant dismissiveness of those who lack deep understanding.

=102=

This verse instructs the Prophet to respond to the accusations of fabrication with a clear declaration of the Qur’an’s divine origin. "Say, 'The holy spirit has brought it down from your Lord with the truth.'" The "holy spirit" refers to the Angel Gabriel, Jibrīl, who is described as al-rūḥ al-qudus, the spirit of sanctity and purity. He is the trusted emissary between Allah and His messengers.

The phrase "from your Lord" emphasizes that the ultimate source is Allah, not any human. The revelation is brought down "with the truth," meaning its content is entirely true, containing no falsehood, and it brings the truth that establishes justice and guidance.

The verse then outlines three purposes of this revelation. First, "to reassure the believers." The truth of the Qur’an provides certainty, stability, and peace to the hearts of those who accept it, especially in the face of accusations and doubts cast by the disbelievers. When the believers hear that the holy spirit brought the Qur’an, their faith is strengthened, and their hearts are settled. Second, it is "a guide," offering clear direction in all aspects of life, leading humanity from darkness to light. Third, it is "good news for those who submit to Allah," promising them eternal bliss and divine pleasure.

The word muslimūn, those who submit, is used here as the comprehensive term for the followers of the truth. This verse reframes the entire debate: the Qur’an is not a human fabrication but a heavenly descent through the holiest of angels, designed to bring certainty, guidance, and glad tidings.

For the believer, this verse enriches the appreciation of the Qur’an's spiritual provenance. The mention of the holy spirit highlights the immense sanctity of the revelation, carried by the purest of beings. The threefold function—reassurance, guidance, and good news—provides a complete spiritual program. In moments of doubt or when facing external attacks on the faith, the believer can recall that the Qur’an was sent specifically to reassure and guide, grounding them in a truth that transcends human criticism.

=103=

This verse addresses another specific accusation leveled by the Meccan polytheists to explain away the Qur’an’s miraculous nature. "And We surely know that they say, 'No one is teaching him except a human.'" The disbelievers, unable to accept that an unlettered man could produce such sublime scripture, claimed that the Prophet was being tutored by someone—a foreigner, perhaps a Christian or Jewish person, who fed him stories and information. The verse acknowledges that Allah is fully aware of their slander. It then refutes the accusation with a simple, devastating argument: "But the man they refer to speaks a foreign tongue, whereas this Quran is in eloquent Arabic." The alleged human source was a non-Arab, whose language was not the pure, highly stylized Arabic of the Qur’an. Some reports mention a Christian slave named Jabr or a blacksmith, both non-Arabs. The disbelievers’ claim that such a person could produce the Qur’an—a book of unmatched linguistic perfection, profound wisdom, and eloquence in the Arabic language—is absurd. The Qur’an’s Arabic is not merely grammatically correct; it is the pinnacle of eloquence, a miraculous standard that challenged the greatest Arab poets to produce even a single chapter like it, and they failed. A non-native speaker, however knowledgeable, could not possibly be the source of such linguistic genius. This refutation is based on rational observation and linguistic reality.

The verse also highlights the divine wisdom in revealing the Qur’an in clear Arabic, the language of the Prophet’s people, so that they could understand it directly and witness its inimitable quality. For believers, this verse is a reminder of the Qur’an's unique literary and spiritual status. It also teaches a methodology for responding to baseless accusations: address them with simple, logical facts. The critics often rely on the ignorance of the masses, but a clear-headed analysis exposes the hollowness of their claims. The eloquence of the Qur’an remains a living miracle, and the historical inability of its detractors to find a credible human source testifies to its divine origin.

=104=

This verse delivers a definitive verdict on those who persist in rejecting the clear signs and arguments presented throughout the sūrah. "Surely those who do not believe in Allah’s revelations" refers to the same people who called the Qur’an a fabrication and claimed it was taught by a human. They have been presented with overwhelming evidence: the perfect creation, the clear signs, the linguistic miracle, the historical lessons.

Yet they still refuse to believe. The verse states, "will never be guided by Allah. " This is a declaration of the divine law: when a person consistently and arrogantly rejects the truth after it has been made clear to them, Allah seals their heart, and they are deprived of the gift of guidance. It is not that Allah is unwilling to guide, but that their own persistent rejection makes them unworthy and unreceptive to guidance. The door of guidance is open to all who sincerely seek, but those who slam it shut with hostility and pride are left to their chosen path.

The verse then adds, "and they will suffer a painful punishment. " The consequence of their chosen misguidance is the torment of the Hereafter, described as "painful" to emphasize its severity. This verse serves as both a warning and a statement of justice. It warns those who toy with the truth, thinking they can always accept later, that a time may come when the heart becomes too hard to accept guidance at all. It also provides a perspective on the spiritual state of hardened rejectors, helping the believer understand why some people, despite seeing the same signs, remain unmoved.

For the believer, this verse instills a profound sense of reliance on Allah for steadfastness. One should never feel secure in their own faith, knowing that guidance is a continuous divine gift that can be withdrawn if one begins to reject Allah’s revelations. It motivates constant repentance and humility, and a deep gratitude for the faith that one has been given, recognizing it as a mercy, not a personal achievement.

=105=

This verse flips the disbelievers’ accusation against the Prophet back onto them. They called the Prophet a fabricator, but the verse declares, "No one fabricates lies except those who do not believe in Allah’s revelations." The act of fabricating lies against Allah—attributing false statements or revelations to Him—is the hallmark of the disbeliever, not the prophet. The Prophet Muhammad, who was known for a lifetime of truthfulness, was accused of something that only those who reject Allah’s signs would do. The disbelievers themselves were the fabricators of falsehood, inventing lies about Allah by claiming He had partners, daughters, or that certain foods were divinely forbidden without command. Their entire system of shirk was built on falsehood.

The verse then delivers a powerful moral verdict: "and it is they who are the true liars." The emphasis on "true" here indicates that while the Prophet was accused of lying, the reality is the opposite; the disbelievers are the ones who genuinely deserve the title of liars, both in their denial of the truth and in their invention of false beliefs. This verse is a profound lesson in the moral psychology of denial. Often, those who are most guilty of a sin are the first to accuse others of it. The Quraysh knew deep down that the Prophet was truthful; their accusation was a projection of their own inner state of dishonesty.

For the believer, this verse offers a comfort: when people of falsehood call the truthful liars, it is a testimony against themselves. It also serves as a warning against the grievous sin of fabricating religious falsehood, whether by directly inventing lies about Allah, or by spreading misinterpretations and innovations that have no basis in divine revelation. The true liars are those whose hearts are empty of faith, and their falsehood will be exposed in both this world and the next. The verse reinforces the integrity of the prophetic message and places the burden of proof squarely on the accusers, who are revealed to be the actual fabricators.

=106=

This verse addresses the grave issue of apostasy, but with a crucial exception that reveals the depth of Islamic mercy. "Whoever disbelieves in Allah after their belief" refers to those who abandon faith after having accepted it. This was a reality in Mecca, where some weak believers were tortured until they verbally renounced Islam, and others, like the hypocrites, willingly turned back to disbelief. The verse immediately clarifies an all-important exception: "not those who are forced while their hearts are firm in faith." This refers to individuals like ʿAmmār ibn Yāsir, who was tortured by the Quraysh until he uttered words of disbelief, but his heart remained steadfast in faith. Allah, the All-Knowing, distinguishes between the outward utterance under duress and the inner conviction. Such people are not guilty of apostasy; their faith is intact, and they are forgiven.

This is a foundational principle in Islamic law: compulsion negates culpability in matters of speech. The verse then describes the other category: "but those who embrace disbelief wholeheartedly. " These are the ones who willingly, without any external force, open their hearts to disbelief. They have made a conscious, internal decision to reject faith. For them, the verdict is severe: "they will be condemned by Allah and suffer a tremendous punishment." The condemnation is divine anger, and the punishment is the great torment of Hell. This verse balances justice and mercy perfectly. It protects the coerced believer from guilt and offers them a way to preserve their life while maintaining internal faith, a concept known as taqiyyah in its proper, limited sense. At the same time, it warns those who treat faith lightly, showing that willing apostasy is a matter of grave consequence.

For the believer, this verse inspires deep gratitude for Allah's mercy regarding human weakness under torture, and it also strengthens the resolve to protect one's faith internally even in the worst circumstances. It teaches that the heart's firmness is the true locus of faith, and that Allah's judgement is based on inner sincerity, not merely external words.

=107=

This verse explains the underlying motivation of those who willingly apostate, as described in the previous verse. "This is because they prefer the life of this world over the Hereafter." Their choice to abandon faith is not based on intellectual conviction but on a value judgment: they find the immediate pleasures, security, and status of this worldly life more attractive than the promise of the Hereafter. In Mecca, some apostates were lured by the prospect of regaining their social standing, avoiding persecution, or enjoying material benefits that Islam might restrict. The preference for dunyā over ākhirah is the root of many spiritual failures.

The verse then states the divine law: "Surely Allah never guides those who choose to disbelieve." The phrase "choose to" is crucial; it indicates a deliberate, conscious preference for disbelief. Because they have made this fundamental choice against the truth, Allah removes His guiding grace from them.

This is not an arbitrary deprivation but a just response to their own free decision. The verse highlights the principle that guidance is a light that Allah bestows, but it is withdrawn when a person repeatedly rejects it in favor of worldly gains. This verse serves as a powerful diagnostic tool for the believer. It prompts reflection on one’s own choices: when we sin, is it because we are preferring a fleeting worldly pleasure over the eternal reward with Allah? The cure for this disease is to strengthen yaqīn, certainty in the Hereafter, so that its reality outweighs the pull of the present world. It also serves as a warning to those who think they can return to disbelief for a worldly advantage and then easily come back to faith; the act of preferring this world can so darken the heart that the door to guidance closes.

The verse emphasizes the immense value of the Hereafter, portraying the apostate's trade as the most foolish of all bargains: selling the infinite for the finite.

=108=

This verse describes the spiritual state that results from the willing preference of this world over the Hereafter. "They are the ones whose hearts, ears, and eyes are sealed by Allah." The sealing of the heart means it becomes impervious to understanding spiritual truth and feeling the love of Allah. The sealing of the ears prevents them from hearing the message in a way that benefits them; they hear the words but not the meaning. The sealing of the eyes means they see the signs in creation and in revelation, but they fail to perceive them as evidence of the Creator. This triple sealing represents a complete spiritual shutdown, a self-inflicted sensory and cognitive blindness that Allah, in His justice, makes permanent. It is the consequence of their persistent rejection and preference for disbelief.

The verse then identifies them: "and it is they who are truly heedless." Heedlessness is the state of being unaware, distracted, and unconcerned with the ultimate reality. While many people may be temporarily heedless and then awaken, these individuals are described as "truly" heedless because their heedlessness is chronic and fatal, sealed by divine decree. This verse is a terrifying depiction of the end-stage of rebellion against God. It teaches that the faculties of perception are trusts from Allah, and their misuse leads to their loss. The believer should pray for a heart that is soft, ears that are open to truth, and eyes that see the signs of Allah. It also provides an explanation for the baffling inability of some people to respond to the clearest arguments; their internal receptors have been sealed by their own prior choices. The verse serves as a warning to guard one's heart against the love of this world and to constantly seek Allah's guidance to remain among those whose senses are alive to the truth.

=109=

This short, emphatic verse delivers the final verdict on the sealed, heedless ones described previously. "Without a doubt" translates the Arabic lā jarama, an expression of absolute certainty, leaving no room for speculation or hope of a different outcome. "They will be the losers in the Hereafter." The concept of loss, khusrān, in the Qur’an is the ultimate tragedy. It is not a financial loss that can be recovered but an eternal bankruptcy where one loses the opportunity for Paradise, the pleasure of Allah, and any possible good. Their entire life, with all its opportunities for faith and good deeds, will have been wasted. They traded eternal bliss for fleeting worldly gains and are left with nothing but punishment. The verse echoes the Qur’anic theme that true loss is the loss of one’s self and one’s potential for eternal happiness. This verse serves as a punchline to the preceding warnings, summarizing the end result of preferring this world, having one’s faculties sealed, and choosing disbelief. It instills a profound fear and urgency in the believer. In worldly terms, a person who loses wealth or health is considered unfortunate, but the Qur’an redefines loss as the state of a person who meets Allah with a heart sealed in disbelief. The verse encourages a constant evaluation of one’s spiritual ledger. Are we, in our daily choices, moving towards eternal profit or eternal loss? The emphatic "without a doubt" is a wake-up call, cutting through any complacency or false hope. It reassures the believers that their temporary worldly losses in the cause of Allah are not true losses; the real losers are those who seem to win in this world but lose everything in the next. This verse is a powerful tool for self-reflection and a motivator to ensure that one’s ultimate account is one of profit, not loss.

=110=

This verse returns to the theme of those who were coerced into renouncing Islam, providing a beautiful promise of redemption. It refers to the group of persecuted Muslims in Mecca who could not bear the torture and, under severe duress, uttered words of disbelief, and perhaps even remained among the disbelievers for a time. Afterward, they managed to emigrate, leaving Mecca for Medina or Abyssinia, and then "struggled in Allah’s cause," meaning they engaged in jihad both spiritual and physical to uphold the faith, and "persevered," meaning they remained steadfast in their subsequent faith and efforts. For such people, the verse delivers a powerful reassurance: "your Lord O Prophet is truly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful after all. " The phrase "after all" translates min baʿdi mā, emphasizing that even after the immense trial of being forced to renounce faith, followed by a period of weakness or even compliance, Allah’s forgiveness and mercy are vast and encompassing. Their emigration and subsequent struggle and patience are signs of the genuine faith that was in their hearts all along, and Allah, the All-Forgiving, wipes away the previous compulsion. This verse demonstrates that no matter how severe the fall or how desperate the situation, the door of repentance and divine mercy remains open. It is a message of hope for all who have faltered under pressure but whose hearts are still attached to Allah. The combination of emigration, struggle, and perseverance shows that true repentance is not just a word but a transformative action.

For the believer, this verse is a source of immense comfort, teaching that the past does not define the future if one turns back to Allah with sincere effort. It also highlights the merciful character of Allah, who looks at the heart’s true state and the subsequent efforts to rectify one’s path, forgiving even the gravest of outward lapses when they were not a true reflection of the inner self.

=111=

The sūrah now redirects attention to the Day of Judgment, providing a snapshot of its reality. "Consider the Day when every soul will come pleading for itself." The word "pleading" translates tujādilu, indicating that each soul will be intensely arguing on its own behalf, desperately trying to defend itself. In this world, people have advocates, lawyers, and intercessors to speak for them, but on that Day, the soul stands utterly alone, its attention consumed entirely by its own case. The relationships and alliances of the world are severed, and the only concern is personal salvation.

The verse continues, "and each will be paid in full for what it did." This means that every deed, good or bad, large or small, will be precisely recompensed. Nothing will be lost, forgotten, or undervalued. The scales of divine justice will be exact. Then the verse adds, "and none will be wronged." Even the smallest particle of injustice will be absent. No one will be punished for a sin they did not commit, nor will any good deed be unrewarded. This comprehensive statement of justice provides the ultimate context for all the moral commands and warnings in the sūrah. It tells both the oppressor and the oppressed that their final meeting with justice is inevitable.

The image of each soul pleading for itself is particularly striking, emphasizing the utter personal responsibility of every individual. No parent, child, or friend can help; no wealth can buy a defense. This verse encourages the believer to live with a strong sense of individual accountability, preparing the case one will plead before Allah by filling the record with righteous deeds. It also offers comfort to those who have been wronged, assuring them that a Day is coming when they will receive full justice and the scales will be perfectly balanced by the Most Just.

=112=

This verse presents a parable, the example of a community, often understood to be a reference to Mecca, though the principle is universal. "And Allah sets forth the example of a society which was safe and at ease, receiving its provision in abundance from all directions." This describes a town enjoying perfect security; no enemy threatens it, its inhabitants live in comfort and peace, and their sustenance flows in generously from every source. This was precisely the state of Mecca before the Prophet's mission; it was a sanctuary where even the powerful neighbors respected its sanctity, and its trade caravans brought prosperity from Yemen and Syria.

The verse then describes the tragic fall: "But its people met Allah’s favours with ingratitude, so Allah made them taste the clutches of hunger and fear for their misdeeds. " Instead of being grateful, they worshipped idols, oppressed the weak, mocked the messenger, and denied the one God who provided all their blessings. Their ingratitude was comprehensive, a practical denial of the Giver. The punishment was tailored to their crime: the security they took for granted was replaced with fear, and their abundant provision was replaced with the clutches of hunger. Commentators mention the severe famine that struck Mecca years after the Prophet’s migration, as well as the constant fear they lived in under the threat of the growing Muslim state, until the conquest of Mecca. This parable illustrates the sunnah of Allah: nations that respond to blessings with ingratitude and rebellion are eventually stripped of those blessings and made to suffer the opposite.

For the believer, this serves as a stark warning to never take peace and provision for granted. The blessings are a test, and gratitude is the means to preserve them.

The verse also provides a lens to understand history and current events, recognizing that societal collapse often follows widespread ingratitude and moral corruption.

=113=

This verse clarifies the specific form that the ingratitude of the society took: the rejection of the messenger sent to them. "A messenger of their own actually did come to them." This emphasizes the mercy and the justification of the subsequent punishment. The messenger was not a stranger; he was one of them, from their own lineage, speaking their language, known for his honesty before his mission. He came with a clear message, calling them to gratitude and monotheism. "But they denied him," meaning they rejected his claim, called him a liar, and opposed his mission. Their denial was not out of confusion but out of arrogance and attachment to their forefathers’ ways.

The verse then delivers the consequence: "So the torment overtook them while they persisted in wrongdoing." The punishment, which in Mecca’s case included the defeat at Badr, the prolonged fear, and the eventual conquest, caught them in the midst of their continuous wrongdoing.

The phrase "while they persisted in wrongdoing" indicates that they were given time, the message was repeated, and they had every opportunity to repent, but they stubbornly continued. The punishment did not come out of the blue; it was the climax of a prolonged period of rejection. This verse reinforces the Qur'anic principle that divine punishment is never sent until a messenger has clearly delivered the warning. It also serves as a warning to all who reject the messengers sent to them: the historical pattern is that the deniers are eventually overtaken.

For the believer, the verse highlights the value of accepting the messenger and his message while there is time, and it explains the afflictions of the world as potential consequences of collective denial. It calls individuals and communities to examine their response to the message of Islam, lest they fall into the same pattern as the destroyed towns.

=114=

After the parable of the ungrateful society, the sūrah turns to the believers with a direct command. "So eat from the good, lawful things which Allah has provided for you." The conjunction "so" links the command to the preceding warning: in contrast to the ungrateful who misused Allah's provisions, the believers are instructed to enjoy the good and lawful (ḥalāl ṭayyib) sustenance.

The word "good" includes both the quality of being wholesome and the purity of being lawful. The believers are not to abstain from the good things of life; rather, they are to partake of them with a clear conscience, within the boundaries set by Allah.

The second command is "and be grateful for Allah’s favours." The act of eating is to be coupled with shukr, a deep, active gratitude that acknowledges the Provider and uses the strength derived from the food in obedience to Him. Gratitude is the safeguard against the fate of the ungrateful society.

The verse ends with the conditional phrase, "if you truly worship Him alone. " This connects the act of eating and thanking directly to tawḥīd. True worship of Allah alone includes obeying His commands regarding food, recognizing Him as the sole Provider, and directing all thanks exclusively to Him. Eating from the lawful and being grateful are thus presented as essential components of monotheistic worship. This verse provides a holistic Islamic view of consumption. Eating is not a mundane, purely physical act; it is a spiritual practice when done with the right intention and awareness. In the modern context, where food is often consumed mindlessly, this verse calls believers to elevate their daily meals into acts of worship by ensuring the food is lawful, remembering Allah’s name over it, and cultivating a heart of gratitude. It also counters the extremist tendency to view all physical pleasures with suspicion; the Qur’an encourages enjoying the good things of life in a manner that increases one's connection to Allah. The verse thus defines the middle path of the grateful worshipper.

=115=

This verse specifies the prohibited foods, giving a concise list of major dietary restrictions in Islam. "He has only forbidden you to eat carrion, blood, swine, and what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah." The word "only" indicates that the list of absolutely forbidden foods is short. Carrion is the flesh of an animal that dies naturally or is not properly slaughtered. Blood refers to flowing blood, which is forbidden to consume. Swine is the pig, considered impure. The fourth category, "what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah," includes any animal dedicated to idols or upon which a name other than Allah’s is invoked at the time of slaughter. This prohibition is directly connected to tawḥīd, ensuring that even the act of taking a life for food is consecrated solely to the Creator.

The verse then provides the exception of necessity: "But if someone is compelled by necessity—neither driven by desire nor exceeding immediate need—then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. " If a person is in a life-threatening situation where no lawful food is available, they may eat just enough of the forbidden food to survive, without taking pleasure in it or transgressing the minimal amount needed. This exception reveals the flexibility and mercy within Islamic law; the preservation of life takes precedence over the dietary prohibition. The verse closes with the divine names All-Forgiving and Most Merciful, indicating that Allah overlooks such unavoidable consumption out of His compassion.

This verse establishes the foundational dietary laws and demonstrates a principle of Islamic jurisprudence: necessity makes the prohibited permissible, to a limited extent. For the believer, it provides clear guidance on what to avoid, and it reinforces the importance of intentionality in eating. The prohibition against what is slaughtered in other than Allah’s name ties dietary law directly to the core of faith, making every meal an affirmation of monotheism. The mercy shown in the exception encourages believers to think well of Allah and to know that His law is designed for ease, not hardship.

=116=

After specifying the lawful and prohibited foods, this verse warns against the sin of inventing religious rulings. "Do not falsely declare with your tongues, 'This is lawful, and that is unlawful,' only fabricating lies against Allah." The pre-Islamic Arabs had a habit of arbitrarily declaring certain foods, animals, or practices as forbidden based on their own whims or tribal traditions, and then attributing these prohibitions to Allah. This was a grievous sin because it involved speaking on behalf of God without any authority, effectively legislating in His name. The verse forbids the believers from falling into this trap. The authority to declare things lawful or unlawful belongs exclusively to Allah and His Messenger. Human beings, no matter how pious or learned, cannot invent religious rulings based on personal opinion.

The verse then states the outcome: "Indeed, those who fabricate lies against Allah will never succeed." Their fabricated rules may give them social power or a sense of piety, but in the ultimate sense, they will not achieve any real success; instead, they are destined for failure and punishment. This verse is foundational in Islamic legal theory: the default in matters of worldly benefit is permissibility unless proven otherwise by revelation, and the prohibition of something requires clear evidence. It also condemns the attitude of making religion overly restrictive or overly permissive based on human desires. For the contemporary Muslim, this verse is a call to strict adherence to the sources of Shariah—the Qur'an and Sunnah—and a warning against cultural innovations that are falsely given religious sanctity. It teaches the importance of saying "I do not know" and referring matters to the scholars, rather than pronouncing fatwas based on ignorance or bias. The fabrication of lies against Allah is one of the greatest sins, and this verse instills a healthy fear of ever misrepresenting the divine will.

=117=

This short verse comments on the temporary worldly benefit that the fabricators of lies against Allah might enjoy. "It is only a brief enjoyment" refers to whatever worldly gains they acquire through their false religious pronouncements—perhaps social status, followers, or economic advantage from rituals and prohibitions they invented. The word "brief" underscores the fleeting nature of such gains; measured against eternity, even a long worldly life is just a moment.

The verse then delivers the stark contrast: "then they will suffer a painful punishment." The enjoyment is fleeting, but the punishment is severe and lasting. This juxtaposition highlights the foolishness of trading eternal well-being for a momentary pleasure. The fabricators may think they have succeeded because they see the immediate results of their lies, but the verse exposes the reality that the enjoyment is a deceptive prelude to torment.

This principle applies universally: any sin that brings short-term pleasure but leads to long-term suffering is a terrible bargain. For the believer, this verse is a powerful deterrent against compromising religious integrity for worldly gain. Whether it is a preacher altering the message to please an audience, or an individual justifying a sin with a self-made ruling, the brief enjoyment is not worth the painful punishment.

The verse also comforts those who adhere to the truth despite lacking worldly benefits, reminding them that the fabricators' apparent prosperity is nothing but a brief illusion, soon to be replaced by unimaginable pain. It sharpens the believer’s perspective, training the heart to value the eternal over the temporal.

=118=

This verse draws a parallel between the dietary prohibitions given to the Jews and the preceding discussion. "To the Jews, We have forbidden what We related to you before." This refers to a previous revelation, specifically in Sūrat Al-Anʿām (6:146), where Allah mentioned that due to the transgressions of the Jews, certain foods that were originally lawful were made unlawful for them as a punishment, such as animals with undivided hoofs and the fat of cattle and sheep.

The verse clarifies that the strict dietary laws imposed on the Jews were not part of the original, universal law but were specific restrictions resulting from their rebellion. This context refutes the claim by some that the Islamic dietary laws were an innovation; rather, the laws given to the Prophet Muhammad were a return to the pure, original way, lifting the burdens that had been imposed as punishment on previous nations.

The verse then adds, "We did not wrong them, but it was they who wronged themselves." The restrictions were a consequence of their own wrongdoing, not an arbitrary act of divine oppression. This reinforces the Qur'anic principle that divine punishment, in whatever form, is always a result of human sin and injustice. For the Muslims, this verse serves as a reminder of the mercy in the Islamic Shariah, which has lifted undue burdens and provided a balanced, moderate law. It also warns against repeating the mistakes of previous nations, whose transgressions led to the tightening of restrictions. Gratitude for the ease of Islamic law should be expressed through obedience, not rebellion.

The verse also highlights that Allah's justice is perfect; He does not wrong anyone, and whatever hardship occurs is a reflection of human actions.

=119=

This verse opens a door of hope after the preceding discussions of divine punishment and the fate of fabricators. "As for those who commit evil ignorantly or recklessly" refers to people who sin due to ignorance of the law, or out of foolishness, impulse, and weakness, without the deep-seated arrogance and persistent rejection that characterize the hardened disbelievers. The verse does not excuse the sin—it is still evil—but it acknowledges a state of human frailty. The crucial condition is what follows: "then repent afterwards and mend their ways." Repentance (tawbah) involves sincere regret, immediate cessation of the sin, and firm resolve not to return to it. "Mend their ways" (aṣlaḥū) means they actively rectify the damage caused by their sin, restoring rights where possible and increasing in good deeds to compensate for their past.

The verse then gives the divine promise: "then your Lord is surely All-Forgiving, Most Merciful." The combination of the two names, Al-Ghafūr (the Oft-Forgiving) and Ar-Raḥīm (the Most Merciful), indicates that forgiveness covers the sin completely, and mercy encompasses the repentant sinner with subsequent blessings. This verse is a beacon of hope for every sinner. No matter how great the evil, if it was committed out of ignorance or human weakness rather than outright defiance, and it is followed by genuine repentance and amendment, Allah's forgiveness and mercy are assured. It negates despair and encourages immediate turning back to Allah.

The verse also establishes the complete formula for repentance: not merely a verbal apology, but a change of state and corrective action. For the modern believer struggling with recurring sins, this verse provides the motivation to keep repenting and reforming, trusting in the vastness of divine mercy. It shows that the goal is not a sinless life—which is humanly impossible—but a life of continuous turning back to Allah.

As the sūrah nears its conclusion, it presents the example of Abraham, the patriarch of monotheism, whose legacy the Quraysh claimed to follow. "Indeed, Abraham was a model of excellence" translates the word ummah, which here means an exemplary leader embodying all goodness, a complete community of virtue in one person. Abraham is presented as the ideal for believers to emulate.

The verse then describes his qualities: "devoted to Allah" (qānitan) means he was constantly obedient, humble, and devoted in his worship, never wavering. "Perfectly upright" (ḥanīfan) refers to his pure monotheism, his turning away from all falsehood and inclining solely towards the truth. He was not merely a believer but a paradigm of the original, uncorrupted religion of tawḥīd, the spiritual ancestor of all true faith.

The verse concludes with the critical affirmation: "not a polytheist." This negates any claim that Abraham was ever an idolater, which is important because the Quraysh, while venerating Abraham, practiced shirk and claimed his legacy. The verse reclaims Abraham for pure monotheism, asserting that his way was the complete opposite of the polytheism practiced at the Kaʿbah. This depiction of Abraham serves as a bridge between the earlier prophetic missions and the message of Muhammad, establishing that Islam is not a new religion but the restoration of Abraham's original faith.

For believers, Abraham is a role model of individual excellence, unwavering devotion, and the courage to stand for truth against a polytheistic society. The verse calls every Muslim to aspire to be an ummah of goodness in their own right, embodying devotion and uprightness, and to ensure their faith remains untainted by any form of shirk. It sets the stage for the subsequent verses that will elaborate on Abraham's qualities and his relationship with Allah.
