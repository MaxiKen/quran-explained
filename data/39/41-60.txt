=41=

After the stern warning of the previous verse, the surah reaffirms the purpose of revelation and human free will. The Book, the Quran, has been revealed “with the truth for humanity.

” The phrase “with the truth” assures that its content is not speculative or based on falsehood but is a reliable guide for all human beings, not just one tribe or era. Its message addresses the universal human condition. Then the verse presents a clear principle of moral autonomy: whoever chooses to be guided does so for the benefit of their own soul, and whoever chooses to stray does so to their own detriment. Guidance benefits the guided person; Allah does not need human obedience. Likewise, straying harms only the strayer; it does not diminish Allah’s majesty.

This is a liberating teaching, freeing the believer from the illusion that their righteousness adds to Allah or that someone else’s sin can harm Him. The verse then relieves the Prophet of an unbearable weight by telling him, “You are not a keeper over them.” He is not responsible for compelling people to believe. His duty is only to convey the message clearly. This echoes earlier verses that reminded him that he cannot save those against whom the decree of torment is justified. The Prophet is a warner and a bearer of glad tidings, not a controller of hearts.

This statement also serves as a guideline for every caller to Islam: after sincere effort and clear communication, the result is left to Allah and the individual’s own choice. Coercion in religion contradicts this very principle. The verse thus balances divine justice with human freedom, and it connects seamlessly to the next passage, which illustrates Allah’s power over life and death, further proving that only He is worthy of worship.

=42=

This verse moves to a profound reflection on life, death and sleep as signs of Allah’s absolute power. It states that Allah takes the souls at the time of death and also takes the souls of the living during their sleep.

The Arabic word tawaffā, translated as “calls back,” implies a complete taking of the self. During sleep, the soul is, in a partial sense, separated from the body’s conscious control, a temporary state that mimics death.

This is why sleep is called the “twin brother of death” in some traditions. Then Allah keeps the souls of those for whom He has decreed death; they do not return to their bodies. And He releases the others—the sleeping—back to their bodies to live until an appointed term, their eventual death. This daily cycle of sleep and waking is a reminder that our hold on life is not under our control. Every night we experience a miniature death, and only by Allah’s will do we wake up.

The verse ends with “Surely in this are signs for people who reflect.” The sign is clear: the One who takes and restores souls in sleep can easily take them permanently and then resurrect them for judgment. This addresses the disbelievers’ scepticism about the afterlife. If they would reflect, they would see that the God who manages their souls every night is fully capable of giving them life again after death.

The verse also cultivates gratitude: waking up in the morning is a gift that should prompt remembrance and devotion. The continuity with the previous verse lies in reinforcing Allah’s exclusive power, which makes reliance on false intercessors, mentioned next, utterly irrational.

=43=

The surah returns to the folly of idol worship, this time focusing on the claimed role of intercession. The Quraysh argued that their idols were intercessors who would bring them closer to Allah, a claim already refuted in verse three. Here the argument is different: what qualifies these idols to intercede? The verse points out that these idols lack two essential things: “authority” (mulk) and “intelligence” (ʿaql). Authority means they own nothing and have no power over anything in the heavens or the earth. Intelligence means they have no awareness, understanding or ability to even hear the prayers directed to them. They are either inanimate objects like stone and wood, or they are deceased righteous humans or angels who cannot act independently of Allah. A being with no authority and no intelligence cannot possibly intercede, because intercession requires the intercessor’s will, knowledge of the one they intercede for, and permission from the higher authority. The idols fail on all counts.

The question format “Or have they taken...” expresses astonishment: after all the clear signs of Allah’s power—creation of the heavens and earth, taking souls in sleep and death—how can they still turn to powerless things? The Prophet is told to pose this question, forcing the idolaters to confront the absurdity.

This verse also distinguishes the Islamic concept of intercession from the pagan one. In Islam, intercession on the Day of Judgment is real, but it happens only by Allah’s permission and for those He approves. The idols have no such permission. The verse thus undermines the entire foundation of shirk and leads naturally into the declaration of the next verse.

=44=

This verse delivers the positive truth that corrects the false concept of intercession. The Prophet is instructed to announce that “All intercession belongs to Allah alone.” This means that the power to grant intercession is entirely in Allah’s hands. No one can intercede without His permission.

This is a fundamental point of tawhid: sovereignty and authority are not shared. The verse then provides the reason: “To Him belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth.” The owner of the universe has the exclusive right to permit or deny any request within His domain. If someone owns a property, only they can allow someone else to speak on behalf of a guest. The whole universe is Allah’s property, so no being can assume the role of intercessor independently. Then the verse closes with “Then to Him you will all be returned.” This returning ties the issue of intercession to final accountability. On that Day, the false intercessors will vanish, and people will face their Lord alone, unless He mercifully permits a true intercessor like the Prophet to plead for the believers. The verse leaves no room for the pagan concept of autonomous divine intermediaries. It simultaneously reassures the believers that the door of intercession is not closed entirely; it is simply under Allah’s complete control. This balanced teaching is unique to Islam, avoiding both the denial of intercession and the abuse of it that leads to shirk.

The verse also serves as a powerful argument for calling people to rely on Allah directly rather than on imagined intermediaries, connecting beautifully to the later call for repentance.

=45=

This verse paints a psychological portrait of the disbelievers. When they hear the name of Allah alone, without their associate gods, their hearts contract with disgust. They feel a visceral aversion to pure monotheism because it strips them of their familiar traditions, their ancestral pride and the imaginary comfort of their idols. Their hearts are “filled with disgust” indicating a strong emotional reaction, not a rational objection. On the other hand, when their gods are mentioned—Lat, Uzza, Manat and others—they are filled with joy. This joy is not intellectual satisfaction but emotional attachment to the familiar, the cultural and the inherited. The verse exposes the true cause of their disbelief: it is not a lack of evidence but a disease of the heart that prefers the comfort of falsehood to the challenge of truth. This phenomenon is not limited to the Quraysh; it repeats in every society where monotheism threatens established systems of idolatry, be it materialism, nationalism, or personality cults. The heart that does not sincerely desire the Hereafter will find pure worship of Allah constricting, while it expands in the remembrance of worldly idols. The juxtaposition of the two reactions is a test for every believer: do you feel joy when Allah alone is exalted, or do you need to attach other names to feel spiritual comfort? The verse leads directly into the Prophet’s prayer to Allah as the ultimate judge, acknowledging that such people can only be corrected by divine guidance.

=46=

The Prophet is now taught to turn to Allah in a beautiful supplication that affirms the core truths. The address “O Allah—Originator of the heavens and the earth” acknowledges Allah as the Creator who brought the universe into existence without any prior model.

The name “Knower of the seen and unseen” confirms that Allah knows both the visible world and the hidden realities, including the secret thoughts and future events that humans cannot perceive. This comprehensive knowledge is essential for perfect judgment. The prayer then asks Allah to judge between His servants concerning their differences.

This is both a declaration of faith that ultimate judgment rests with Allah alone and an expression of the Prophet’s reliance on divine justice rather than human argument. The verse reminds the believers that when disputes over truth and falsehood drag on, they should entrust the matter to Allah, who will settle everything on the Day of Judgment. It also carries an implicit warning to the disbelievers: the One who originated the heavens and the earth and knows all hidden things will surely judge your denial. The supplication is a powerful model for believers facing opposition and confusion; they are taught to appeal to Allah’s attributes of creative power and perfect knowledge. This verse forms a bridge to the description of Judgment Day that follows, where the wrongdoers will desperately seek to ransom themselves.

=47=

The scene shifts to the Day of Judgment, revealing the depth of regret. The wrongdoers, those who associated partners with Allah and rejected the truth, would give all that the earth contains, and even double that amount, to ransom themselves from the punishment.

The phrase “twice over” emphasizes that no amount of wealth, no matter how multiplied, would be sufficient to buy their release. In this life they clung to wealth and power, thinking it made them secure. On that Day, they will be willing to give up everything they ever owned and more, but it will be utterly useless. The torment is described as “horrible” (sūʾ al-ʿadhāb), meaning the very essence of painful suffering.

The second part of the verse reveals why this desperation is so intense: “they will see from Allah what they had never expected.” This could mean that they will witness the magnitude of punishment they dismissed as a myth, or that they will see aspects of Allah’s judgment and anger that they never took seriously. Their worldly arrogance was based on a miscalculation; they thought either there would be no judgment or that their idols would save them. The verse shatters that false hope. It also serves as a powerful meditation for believers on the value of faith and good deeds, which will be the only currency on that Day.

The image of a person trying to pay with all the world’s riches and being rejected is a stark warning to prioritize the Hereafter over worldly accumulation.

=48=

Continuing the scene, this verse describes the complete exposure of their actions. The evil consequences of what they earned will appear right before their eyes, as if the deeds themselves take on visible forms. What they mocked—the warnings of punishment, the fire of Hell, the angels of torment—will descend upon them and encircle them completely. The verb “overwhelmed” (ḥāqa) conveys the idea of a punishment that surrounds and envelopes them, leaving no escape.

This is the reality of what they used to ridicule. The Quraysh would laugh at the Prophet’s descriptions of the Fire, the boiling water and the chains. They treated the unseen realities as fairy tales. On that Day, the joke will be on them, and the reality will be far more terrifying than the description. This verse illustrates a profound psychological principle: ridicule is often a defence mechanism against truths one is unwilling to accept. But when the truth manifests, the former mocker is the most exposed. The punishment matching the crime—being overwhelmed by what was ridiculed—is a form of perfect justice.

The verse also connects to the earlier description of the Book being revealed with truth. Those who ridiculed the truth will now face it directly. This scene serves as a solemn reminder to never make light of divine warnings, as the gap between mockery and regret can be terrifyingly small.

=49=

The surah returns to a recurring theme of human ingratitude, first introduced in verse eight. Here the focus is on the false attribution of blessings to one’s own merit. When hardship touches a person—illness, poverty, danger—they instinctively turn to Allah alone, making sincere supplications.

This is the natural fitrah surfacing. But when Allah responds and showers blessings upon them, giving them ease, wealth or knowledge, they arrogantly claim, “I have been granted all this only because of my knowledge.” They attribute the blessing to their own intelligence, skill, education or effort, forgetting the One who gave them those abilities in the first place. The Quran does not deny the role of human effort, but it condemns the arrogance that erases gratitude to the true Provider. The verse immediately corrects this delusion: “Not at all! It is no more than a test.

” The word “test” (fitnah) indicates that the blessing is a trial to see whether the person will be grateful or arrogant, obedient or rebellious. Wealth and success are tests, perhaps more dangerous than hardship, because hardship forces one to turn to Allah, while ease often leads to forgetfulness.

The verse ends with “But most of them do not know. ” They lack the deep spiritual knowledge that everything is from Allah and that worldly gifts are transient means of testing. This verse echoes the earlier story of Qarun, who said, “I have been given it only because of knowledge I possess,” and was destroyed. It is a warning to every successful person today to guard against the whisperings of the ego that turn blessings into a curse.

=50=

To emphasize that this arrogance is not new, the surah points to history. Previous nations uttered the same boastful claim when given blessings. They too attributed their success to their own knowledge and power, forgetting Allah. The statement “the same had already been said by those before them” shows the repetitive nature of human folly across generations.

The verse then swiftly reveals the outcome: “but their worldly gains were of no benefit to them.” Their wealth, knowledge and power could not save them from divine punishment when it came. The gains they boasted about vanished overnight, leaving them with nothing but regret. This verse serves as a sobering reminder that the material success of disbelievers is no proof of their correctness or safety. The ruins of mighty civilizations bear silent testimony to this truth. The Qur’anic reference to past peoples like the people of ‘Ad, Thamud and Pharaoh makes the warning immediate and tangible. It also connects to the earlier verses about the torment coming unexpectedly. The Meccans are being told that they are walking the same path as those destroyed nations, and if they do not change, their worldly gains will likewise prove useless. The verse prepares the ground for the following statement that the consequences of evil deeds will catch up with them, just as it did with their predecessors.

=51=

This verse applies the historical lesson directly to the Meccan audience. The evil consequences of the deeds of past nations overtook them, meaning the punishment they earned manifested and destroyed them.

The word “overtook” (aṣāba) suggests an inescapable encounter, like a pursuer catching up with a fugitive. Then the verse makes a direct prophecy: “And the wrongdoers among these pagans will be overtaken by the evil consequences of their deeds. ” The use of “these” points to the Quraysh contemporaries of the Prophet. It is a warning that their own record of oppression, shirk and mockery will bring punishment upon them, just as it did upon the previous wrongdoers.

The verse concludes with “And they will have no escape.” The Arabic term muʿjizīn conveys the idea of frustrating or eluding a pursuer. They will not be able to frustrate Allah’s plan or escape His grip. No worldly power, alliance, wealth or cleverness will save them. This verse gave the early Muslims immense hope and patience; the tyranny they suffered was not overlooked, and its end was decreed. Indeed, many of the Meccan leaders who persecuted the believers met their doom at the Battle of Badr, fulfilling this prophecy in their own lifetimes. For later readers, the verse remains a timeless principle: oppressive systems and individuals will eventually face the consequences of their wrongs, whether in this world or the next, and there will be no escape from divine justice. The connection with the previous verses on ingratitude is clear: the wrongs include the arrogance of attributing blessings to oneself.

=52=

The ingratitude and arrogance of the previous verses are addressed by this profound question. Do they not realise that Allah is the one who expands provision for some and restricts it for others? Wealth and poverty are not indicators of personal merit or divine displeasure in a simple, direct way. Allah gives abundantly to some as a test of gratitude and restricts for others as a test of patience. The one who boasts about his knowledge as the cause of his wealth fails to see that his knowledge itself is a gift from Allah, and that many with greater knowledge are poor, while many ignorant people are rich. The distribution of provision is based on divine wisdom, which encompasses factors like the overall good of society, the spiritual state of the individual and the nature of the test.

The verse then says, “Surely in this are signs for people who believe.” The signs are not just in the provision itself but in the alternating pattern of wealth and poverty and the constant reminder that Allah is the Provider. Believers, who have faith in Allah’s wisdom, can see these signs and respond with trust, gratitude and patience. Disbelievers, lacking that faith, misread the signs and fall into arrogance or despair. The verse corrects a fundamental error in human thinking, teaching that economic status is a test, not a rank. It connects back to the parable of the crops that wither, reminding that all provision is temporary and ultimately from Allah. This prepares the heart for the profound call to repentance and hope in Allah’s mercy that follows immediately in the next verse, the famous verse on forgiveness.

=53=

This is one of the most hope-giving verses in the entire Quran, a radiant call to every sinner. Allah addresses those who have “exceeded the limits against their souls,” meaning those who have committed all kinds of sins, major and minor, perhaps even shirk and murder, as long as they are still alive and able to repent. The call “O My servants” is filled with tenderness; despite their transgressions, Allah still honours them with the title of servitude. The command “Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy” attacks despair, which is a tool of Satan to keep sinners away from repentance.

The verse then makes a sweeping, absolute statement: “Allah certainly forgives all sins.” The word “all” encompasses every sin without exception for those who sincerely repent before death.

This is the consensus of the scholars regarding the forgiveness of even shirk if repented from in this life. The verse does not say He forgives all sins without repentance; rather, the context of returning to the Lord in the next verse makes repentance the implicit condition. But the statement is left absolute to magnify divine mercy and encourage the sinner to turn back, not to trivialize sin.

The verse ends with two beautiful names: the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. The repeated emphasis on forgiveness and mercy underscores that Allah’s mercy surpasses His wrath and that no sin is too great to be forgiven. This verse is a lifeline thrown to every human being who has ever felt crushed by guilt. It reflects the core Quranic message of hope and transformation. The historical context is that some Meccans, like Wahshi who killed Hamzah, or others with heavy pasts, hesitated to embrace Islam because they thought their sins were unforgivable. This verse removed that barrier and brought multitudes into the fold of repentance.

=54=

After the sweeping promise of forgiveness, the verse provides the required action: turn to your Lord in repentance and fully submit to Him. The Arabic word for “turn” is anībū, which implies a continuous, sincere returning with one’s whole being. Repentance is not just a verbal statement but an inner turning away from sin and towards Allah.

The phrase “fully submit to Him” translates aslimū, meaning to surrender one’s will entirely, embracing Islam. Together, repentance and submission constitute the complete spiritual transformation that qualifies one for the promised forgiveness. Then the verse adds a note of urgency: “before the punishment reaches you, for then you will not be helped.” There is a window of opportunity, which is this life. Once death arrives or the major signs of the Hour appear, the door of repentance closes. At that point, no one can help, no intercessor can step in, and the punishment will be inevitable. This urgency is a manifestation of mercy, as it spurs the procrastinating soul to act now. The verse does not merely command but appeals to human self-interest: the delay only increases the risk of missing the chance. It connects perfectly with the previous verse’s message of hope; the hope is genuine, but it is not a licence to delay. The balance between hope and fear is maintained.

The phrase “you will not be helped” echoes earlier warnings about the powerlessness of false gods, reminding that all help must be sought from Allah before it is too late.

=55=

This verse continues the call to action, specifying what submission entails: following the best of what has been revealed, namely the Quran. The Quran is “the best” because it contains the most beautiful teachings, the most just laws and the most truthful narratives. Following it means adhering to its commands, heeding its prohibitions and embodying its ethics.

The phrase “from your Lord” adds a personal dimension, reminding that the Book is not a human production but a mercy sent from the One who knows and cares. Again, the time limit is emphasized: “before the punishment takes you by surprise while you are unaware.” The punishment of the Hereafter will come suddenly, often while people are absorbed in their worldly pursuits, oblivious to their final hour.

The word “unaware” points to the common human condition of heedlessness. People think they have time, but death does not send a warning. By linking the following of the Quran to safety from sudden punishment, the verse connects practical obedience with eschatological salvation. It also calls the believers to continuously renew their commitment to the Quran and not take it for granted. The fact that the Quran is described as “the best” implies that one should prioritize its teachings over other ideologies and customs. This verse serves as a bridge between the call to repentance and the warning scenes of Judgment Day that follow, where souls will express regret for their neglect.

=56=

The surah now reveals the purpose behind the urgent warnings: to prevent the dreadful regrets of the Day of Judgment. The first regret voiced by the sinful soul is a cry of “Woe to me!” expressing extreme grief. The soul laments its negligence in fulfilling what was due to Allah.

The Arabic word janb Allah, translated here as “my duties towards Allah,” literally means “the side of Allah,” interpreted as the rights of Allah, obedience to Him, or closeness to Him that the person wasted. The sinner neglected prayer, charity, repentance and all forms of devotion, preferring worldly distractions. Added to this negligence was ridicule of the truth; they used to mock the Quran, the Prophet and the believers, treating the divine message as a joke. On that Day, the combination of wasted obligations and active mockery becomes a source of unbearable shame.

The verse teaches that sins of omission (neglect) and sins of commission (ridicule) both lead to regret, and often one feeds the other. The warning is personalized: each soul will be speaking only for itself, facing the consequences of its own choices.

This is the outcome that the previous verses were trying to prevent. The Prophet is being told to convey the message now, so that no one will have this regret later. It also serves as a mirror for the listener to examine whether any trace of negligence or ridicule exists in their own attitude towards the faith.

=57=

The second regret is a false excuse. The soul will say, “If only Allah had guided me, I would have been among the righteous.

” This statement tries to shift blame to Allah, implying that the lack of guidance was the reason for misguidance. The verse implicitly refutes this by presenting it as a vain wish. In truth, Allah had guided them by sending prophets, scriptures and reason. They rejected that guidance out of arrogance and desire. The verse does not state the refutation here explicitly because the next verse contains the divine response. But the placement of this wish alongside the other regrets shows that it is a self-deception, an attempt to avoid full responsibility. The soul wishes for a different past, but the past is sealed. The deeper lesson is that human beings are granted free will and the means of guidance; they cannot blame Allah for their own choice to stray.

This verse also connects to the earlier statement that whoever Allah leaves to stray will have no guide. That leaving to stray is a consequence of their own persistent rejection. So, their wish on that Day is meaningless. It serves as a warning to those who, in this life, already use the excuse of predestination to justify their sins. The Quranic response to such fatalism is that Allah guides those who sincerely seek guidance, and He leaves to stray those who turn away.

=58=

The third regret is a plea for a second chance. When the soul actually sees the torment with its own eyes, it will desperately wish to return to the world to become a good-doer (muḥsin). This wish is futile because the test is over. The verse echoes numerous Quranic passages where the damned beg to go back, only to be refused. The fact that they promise to be among the good-doers shows that at that moment they fully acknowledge the truth and the value of good deeds, but it is too late.

The verse teaches that the acceptance of faith and good deeds is only valid in the realm of the unseen, where belief requires trust in what one cannot see. Once the unseen becomes seen, the door of testing closes.

This is a powerful psychological motivator: act now while you have the chance of free will and hidden reality, for tomorrow’s certainty will not benefit you. The progression of the three regrets—neglect, blaming destiny, pleading for a return—covers the range of excuses people might offer. All of them are shown to be futile.

The surah then delivers the crushing response to all these excuses.

=59=

This is the divine rebuttal to all the excuses and wishes of the previous verses. “Not at all!” is a sharp dismissal. The soul is reminded that Allah’s revelations did come to them.

The word “My revelations” emphasizes that the signs and scriptures were clear and sufficient for guidance. They had the Quran, the teachings of the Prophet, and the universal signs in creation and conscience. But they rejected them. The verb “rejected” (kadhdhaba) implies they called them lies. They “acted arrogantly” (istakbara), meaning they looked down upon the truth with pride and refused to submit. And they were “one of the disbelievers,” making disbelief a defining identity, not a fleeting thought. This triple indictment—rejection, arrogance, active disbelief—covers intellectual denial, emotional pride and behavioural rebellion. The verse places the full responsibility squarely on the human soul. It answers the wish of verse 57: guidance was offered, but you rejected it. It answers the plea of verse 58: the chance was given, but you wasted it. And it justifies the cry of regret in verse 56: you neglected and ridiculed the truth despite having all the means to follow it. The tone is final, leaving no room for argument. This verse serves as a sobering reminder that hellfire is not an arbitrary punishment but the direct result of a conscious, repeated refusal of truth. It also reinforces the urgency of the call to repentance made earlier; the time to respond to revelations is now.

=60=

The surah paints a final image of the wrongdoers on that Day. The Prophet is addressed directly: “you will see,” making the scene vivid and personal. Those who lied about Allah—by inventing partners, claiming He has offspring, or denying His revelations—will have faces that are “gloomy,” dark and blackened with humiliation and despair.

This is the outward manifestation of their inner darkness. The blackening of faces on Judgment Day is a sign of disgrace, while the faces of the believers will be radiant. The rhetorical question “Is Hell not a fitting home for the arrogant?” echoes the earlier question about Hell being a fitting home for the disbelievers. Here, the focus is on “the arrogant,” those whose pride prevented them from accepting truth. This links back to the previous verse’s accusation of arrogance. Hell is exactly what they deserve; it is not an excessive punishment but a just recompense for a life spent in arrogant rebellion against the Creator. The verse brings closure to the series of warnings and regrets, confirming that the divine promise of punishment will be fulfilled. It also segues into the next section of the surah, which describes the reward of the righteous, maintaining the Quranic pattern of coupling warning with glad tidings.

The question is left for the listener to ponder: am I among the humble who will be saved, or the arrogant whose home is Hell?
