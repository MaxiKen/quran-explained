=21=

After describing the eternal mansions of the mindful, the surah turns to a vivid parable of this fleeting world. The verse opens with the rhetorical question “Do you not see,” drawing the listener’s attention to a familiar natural phenomenon that carries a profound lesson. Rain comes down from the sky, and Allah channels it through streams and rivers in the earth, a process that shows His precise management of water resources. From that water, the earth brings forth crops of various colours—green, yellow, red—displaying beauty and abundance. This stage represents the peak of worldly life: youth, health, wealth and splendour. But then the crops dry up, turning from fresh green to brittle yellow. “You see them wither” paints a picture of visible decline, which no human power can reverse.

Finally, Allah reduces them to chaff, dry broken pieces that the wind scatters as if they had never existed. This lifecycle of vegetation mirrors the lifecycle of human life, power, beauty and civilisations. The one who reflected on this would recognise that the world is a temporary spring that ends in decay.

Yet the sign is only a reminder for “people of reason,” those who use their intellect to move from the visible to the invisible, from the created to the Creator. The verse thus connects back to the earlier warnings about disbelievers enjoying their disbelief for a little while; the world is like the green crops that soon wither. It also contrasts with the permanent reward described in verse twenty. The reminder is an invitation to detach the heart from what will inevitably perish and attach it to what endures.

The mention of “various colours” also hints at the diversity of worldly attractions that can distract people, but all of them share the same end. This natural sign is accessible to every human being, regardless of learning, making the argument for the Hereafter deeply democratic and immediate. The careful selection of words—rain, streams, crops, wither, chaff—creates a mental film that lingers in the soul, urging it not to be deceived by the present bloom.

=22=

After the parable of the withering crops, the surah contrasts two types of hearts. The opening question “Can the misguided be like...” expects a clear no. On one side is the person whose heart Allah has expanded for Islam, a concept known as sharḥ al-ṣadr.

This is a divine gift where the chest feels wide, receptive and at peace with the truth, free from the tightness of doubt. Such a person walks in light from his Lord, meaning his insight, choices and path are illuminated by divine guidance. He sees reality clearly, and the fleeting world does not deceive him. On the other side is a dreadful state described with the word “woe,” a severe warning of destruction. This woe is directed at those “whose hearts are hardened at the remembrance of Allah. ” Hardening occurs when a person repeatedly ignores and resists truth until the heart becomes like stone, unreceptive, unmoved by Quranic verses or reminders of death and judgment. The remembrance of Allah that should soften the heart instead becomes a source of hardness because it is met with arrogance and aversion. Such people are declared to be “clearly astray,” wandering away from the path without even realising the depth of their loss.

The verse establishes that the decisive difference between the guided and the misguided is not intellect or knowledge in the worldly sense but the receptivity of the heart. The earlier verses praised people of reason; here we learn that the truly reasonable are those whose hearts are open to Islam. The hardened heart is a punishment that follows persistent wrongdoing.

This verse also provides a measure for self-evaluation: does the remembrance of Allah soften my heart, making me incline to obedience and fear, or does it leave me indifferent? The companions of the Prophet were known to weep upon hearing the Quran, while the disbelievers would plug their ears.

This contrast remains valid in every age, urging believers to guard their hearts against hardening.

=23=

The surah now highlights the miraculous nature of the Quran itself. It is Allah who has sent down the best message, the most beautiful and truthful discourse. It is described as a Book that is “perfectly consistent and repeated lessons.

” The Arabic term mutashābih means its verses resemble one another in beauty, precision and truth, with no contradictions. Mathānī means its stories, commands and admonitions are repeated in varied ways so that the message penetrates the heart and is easily remembered. Then the verse describes its immediate physical and spiritual effect on those who fear their Lord: their skin trembles at its warnings, a shiver of awe and fear that then gives way to a softening of both skin and heart when Allah’s mercy is mentioned. This alternating of fear and hope matches the description of the devout worshippers in verse nine. The trembling is not despair; it is a reverential shudder that makes the heart tender, ready to receive the glad tidings of mercy. This dynamic experience is a sign of a living heart.

The verse then states that this transformative effect is the guidance of Allah, which He grants to whomever He wills. This does not imply arbitrariness; Allah wills to guide those who sincerely seek truth and fear Him. And the verse ends with a severe warning: whoever Allah leaves to stray—because of their own persistent rejection—will find no guide, no one to lead them to the truth. The absence of a guide is the ultimate loss. This verse beautifully explains why some people are deeply affected by the Quran while others remain unmoved; the difference lies in the state of the heart and Allah’s response to it. It also connects to the previous verse: the opened heart trembles and softens, the hardened heart stays stone-like.

=24=

After the uplifting description of the Quran’s effect, the surah returns to a terrifying image of the Hereafter to shake the complacent. The verse asks, “Are those who will only have their bare faces to shield themselves from the awful torment... better than those in Paradise?” The obvious answer is no.

The image is of a person being dragged into the Fire, and the only thing they can use to protect themselves is their face—the most honoured part of the body, now exposed to the worst humiliation and pain. The face has no shield; it is bare, directly meeting the scorching heat. This contrasts powerfully with the elevated mansions described for the mindful. The wrongdoers, who used their faces to show arrogance and rejection, now find their faces defenceless. Then it will be said to them, “Reap what you sowed! ” This simple statement encapsulates the principle of divine justice: the punishment is the direct harvest of their own deeds. No one wronged them; they wronged themselves. The metaphor of sowing and reaping underscores that deeds have consequences that grow and manifest later.

For the disbelievers who mocked the warning, the reality will be a bitter harvest. The verse does not just describe punishment but also the deep regret and humiliation that accompany it. The rhetorical question at the beginning challenges the listener to compare the two ultimate destinies and choose which one to strive for.

This is a call to use reason in the most serious matter possible, connecting back to the people of reason who truly benefit from reminders. The torment is “awful,” and the mention of the bare face intensifies the horror, making the mind recoil and seek refuge in Allah.

=25=

To reinforce the warning, the surah points to historical precedent. The Meccan pagans are not the first to reject a prophet; many nations before them did the same. The verse emphasises that the torment came upon them “from where they least expected.” This means that the punishment did not come from a direction they had anticipated or prepared defences against. It could refer to the suddenness of destruction, like the flood that overtook the people of Noah while they were going about their daily lives, or the earthquake that seized the people of Lot in the morning. The unexpected nature of divine punishment strips away any false sense of security. The disbelievers of Mecca felt safe in their fortified city, prosperous trade and powerful alliances, but the verse warns them that Allah’s punishment can arrive from totally unforeseen quarters—perhaps through internal strife, natural disaster, or a military defeat they never imagined. This historical perspective serves to humble the arrogant. It also comforts the Prophet and the believers, letting them know that the apparent strength of disbelief is an illusion that can collapse suddenly.

The phrase “from where they least expected” also carries a psychological dimension: the very things they relied upon might become the source of their ruin. The connection to the next verse is seamless, as it explains that this pattern of unexpected punishment included humiliation in this world before the ultimate punishment in the Hereafter.

=26=

This verse expands on the fate of the previous rejectors. Allah made them taste humiliation—khizy, a word that implies disgrace, shame and abasement—in this worldly life. For some nations this was through defeat, captivity, or annihilation. The humiliation in this life is a foretaste and a warning of the greater punishment to come. The verse states that the punishment of the Hereafter is “far worse,” and adds the poignant phrase “if only they knew.” This lack of knowledge is not intellectual ignorance but a failure to truly grasp and internalise the reality, leading to heedlessness. If they really knew the weight of the Hereafter, they would not be so casual about disbelief. The contrast between the temporary worldly disgrace and the eternal punishment serves to put all earthly sufferings and successes in perspective. Even the worst humiliation in this life is nothing compared to the Fire, and the greatest worldly pleasure is nothing compared to Paradise. For the Meccans, this verse served as a prophecy; some of them did later taste humiliation at Badr and other battles, and ultimately at the conquest of Mecca. But the real warning remains the Hereafter.

The phrase “if only they knew” also hints at the tragedy of wasted human potential: knowledge is available through revelation and reason, but they do not benefit from it. This connects back to the people of reason who do know and thus act accordingly.

=27=

After recounting the fates of past nations and the parable of the withering crops, the surah states that the Quran contains every kind of lesson—amthāl, parables, similitudes, arguments, historical narratives, moral principles—that people need to reflect and take heed. The word “every” indicates the comprehensiveness of the Quran’s guidance; nothing essential for human salvation has been omitted. The purpose is “so perhaps they will be mindful,” meaning they will call to mind what benefits them, remember their duties, and guard against what harms them. The verse emphasises that the blockage is not in the Quran’s clarity or lack of evidence but in human failure to reflect. The expression “We have certainly set forth” underscores divine effort in making the truth accessible and appealing. The Quran uses diverse methods to reach different temperaments: logical arguments for the intellectual, emotional stories for the heart, vivid imagery for the imagination.

This verse also prepares for the next, which clarifies the linguistic perfection of the Quran as a means of that clarity. It reassures believers that they have a complete guide, and it challenges disbelievers to find any excuse for their heedlessness.

=28=

The verse further describes the Quran as an Arabic recitation. Its revelation in clear Arabic, the language of its first audience, is a mercy because they could understand it directly without the barrier of translation. The Arabs of Quraysh prided themselves on their linguistic skills; revealing the Quran in their own tongue in inimitable eloquence was a proof of its divine origin that they could not replicate. The Quran is described as being “without any crookedness. ” The word ‘iwaj means deviation, distortion, contradiction, or moral defect. The Quran contains no inconsistencies in its laws, no contradictions between its verses, no falsehood, no injustice. It is perfectly straight in its guidance, leading directly to Allah. This absence of crookedness contrasts with human writings that are riddled with errors and biases. The purpose of this clarity and perfection is “so perhaps they will be conscious of Allah” (yat-taqūn). Taqwa, God-consciousness, is the desired fruit of engaging with the Quran. The straight path of the Book produces straightness in the soul. The verse connects to the earlier description of the Quran as the best message, with perfect consistency and repeated lessons. Here the focus is on its linguistic purity and conceptual uprightness. For non-Arab believers, this verse highlights the importance of seeking to understand the Quran’s meanings, as its original form is flawless, and translations are only approximations. It also argues against those who claim that the Quran is poetry or magic; a crooked speech might come from a poet or soothsayer, but a straight, coherent, unwavering message can only come from the All-Wise.

=29=

This is a powerful rational argument against polytheism, cast in the form of a simple parable. The listener is asked to imagine a slave who belongs to multiple masters. These masters are constantly quarrelling and disagreeing; one commands him to go east, another to go west, one wants him to do one task, another a conflicting task. The slave is torn, confused, unable to please any of them fully, and lives in constant conflict. Now imagine a slave who is owned by only one master. His duties are clear, his path is straightforward, and he can serve with wholehearted devotion and find peace. The rhetorical question “Are they equal in condition?” demands that every rational person answer no. The polytheist, who worships multiple gods with competing demands and attributes, is like the slave of quarrelsome masters. His heart is divided, his life is confused, and he cannot find true peace or clear direction. The monotheist, who serves Allah alone, has a single clear purpose, a unified source of guidance, and a harmonious relationship with his Creator.

The verse then exclaims “Praise be to Allah!” affirming the beauty of pure monotheism. It ends with “In fact, most of them do not know,” pointing to the ignorance that underlies shirk. They do not realise the psychological and spiritual fragmentation they impose upon themselves. This parable resonates deeply with human experience and remains one of the clearest Quranic demonstrations of tawhid’s superiority. It also connects to the earlier argument of the slaves versus the free in other surahs, but here the focus is on the number of masters, making the point directly relevant to the Meccan idolaters who worshipped many deities alongside Allah.

=30=

After the parable, the surah addresses the Prophet and his opponents directly with a stark reminder of mortality. The verse states a simple fact: the Prophet, despite his lofty status as messenger, will die, and his opponents will die too. Death is the great equaliser.

This statement was partly a response to the Meccans who waited for the Prophet’s death to be rid of his message, or who thought that his movement would end with him. It reminds them that their own deaths are also certain, and that the afterlife will settle all differences. For the believers, it was a lesson that the Prophet is a human being who will pass away, so their ultimate reliance must be on Allah, not on any mortal. This verse is deeply moving; the one through whom the Quran was revealed is told that he will taste death. It humanises the Prophet and intensifies the urgency of the message.

The pairing of “you” and “they” creates a sense of shared destiny in this world, even though their fates in the next life will be utterly different. This verse directly sets up the next verse about the final dispute before Allah.

=31=

Death is not the end; it is a transition. After both the Prophet and the disbelievers die, they will rise again on the Day of Judgment, and all their disputes will be settled before Allah. The disputes between the Prophet and his opponents over tawhid, prophethood and the Hereafter will be decisively resolved, with truth made plain and falsehood condemned.

The phrase “before your Lord” indicates that Allah will be the judge. This verse brings comfort to the Prophet, who was constantly challenged and argued with. It promises that the final verdict rests with the Creator, and no false argument will stand. It also warns the disbelievers that their mockery and opposition are being recorded and will have consequences.

The sequence of verses 30 and 31 together dismantles any reliance on worldly outcomes; even if the Prophet dies before seeing victory, justice is inevitable in the next life. For the believers, it instils patience: they can endure false accusations knowing that the ultimate court is with Allah.

=32=

The surah now presents a powerful rhetorical question. The one who “lies about Allah” includes those who invent false gods, claim Allah has offspring, or make up laws in His name. “Rejects the truth after it has reached them” refers to those who recognise the truth of the Quran and prophethood but deny it out of pride or worldly interest.

The question “Who does more wrong” implies that such a person has reached the peak of wrongdoing, exceeding all others. This is because they are not merely ignorant; they have knowingly turned away from clear evidence, which magnifies their guilt.

The second rhetorical question—“Is Hell not a fitting home for the disbelievers?”—affirms that such ultimate wrongdoing deserves the ultimate punishment. The word “fitting” suggests that Hell is the only appropriate recompense for such a crime. The verse makes the listener pause and reflect on the severity of rejecting the truth, framing disbelief not as a trivial difference of opinion but as a grave injustice against one’s own soul and against truth itself. It also connects to the earlier warning about the bare faces and the Fire, building a coherent picture of the consequences of rejection.

=33=

In contrast to the liars and rejectors, this verse praises the Prophet and the believers. “The one who has brought the truth” is Muhammad, peace be upon him, who came with the Quran and pure monotheism. “Those who embrace it” are the believers who accepted his message and confirmed it. Together, they are declared to be “the righteous” (al-muttaqūn), the truly God-conscious.

This is a profound honour: the believers are placed alongside the Prophet in the rank of the righteous, due to their acceptance of the truth he brought. It shows that righteousness is not a matter of lineage or worldly status but of relationship with the truth.

The verse also provides an implicit definition of taqwa: bringing or embracing the truth and living by it. By using the word “embrace” (ṣaddaqa), it indicates active confirmation, not passive assent. This verse serves as a reply to the disbelievers who considered the early Muslims to be misguided fools; in Allah’s sight, they are the true elite. It also sets up the reward described in the next two verses, promising the believers a generous recompense that matches their righteousness.

=34=

The righteous will find in Paradise everything their hearts desire. The phrase “whatever they desire” encompasses all kinds of pleasures, honours and spiritual joys, beyond what any eye has seen or ear has heard. The desires of the people of Paradise will be purified, so they will only wish for what is beautiful and good. That these wishes are granted “with their Lord” implies nearness and honour; the source of the reward is Allah Himself.

The verse then says, “That is the reward of the good-doers” (muḥsinīn). Iḥsān is the highest level of faith: to worship Allah as if one sees Him. The good-doers are those who excelled in deeds and sincerity. This reward is not a wage calculated by strict equivalence but a generous gift from the Most Generous.

The mention of desires being fulfilled echoes the Quranic descriptions of Paradise where believers will have whatever they call for. This promise fuels hope and encourages believers to persevere in goodness despite present hardships. It directly contrasts with the torment that awaits the wrongdoers, making the choice between the two paths starkly clear.

=35=

This verse reveals the overflowing nature of divine mercy. Not only will the righteous receive what they desire, but Allah will also cover and forgive the worst of their deeds.

The phrase “absolve them of even the worst of what they did” means that the most shameful sins they committed will be wiped away, as if they never happened, so they will feel no disgrace. This is a tremendous source of hope for every believer who has fallen into major sins but then repented and done good.

Moreover, Allah will reward them according to the best of their deeds, measuring their reward by the finest actions they performed. If someone had a mixture of good, very good and excellent deeds, the reward will be based on the excellent ones, magnifying the recompense far beyond strict desert.

This principle reflects Allah’s generosity: He overlooks the bad and multiplies the good. The word “as such” connects this to the previous verse, indicating that this immense forgiveness and generous reward are the way Allah treats the righteous. This verse should inspire believers never to despair of Allah’s mercy, no matter what their past contains, provided they turn in repentance. It also shows the immense value of a single sincere act of goodness, which might outweigh many sins. This graceful treatment is a practical manifestation of the beautiful names “the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful” that will be explicitly mentioned later in the surah.

=36=

The surah returns to comforting the Prophet. The rhetorical question “Is Allah not sufficient for His servant?” is a powerful declaration of trust. The “servant” here is primarily the Prophet Muhammad, though some extend it to every sincere believer. The Quraysh would threaten the Prophet with the wrath of their idols, saying, “Do not insult our gods or they will harm you. ” This verse responds that Allah alone is enough; He protects and supports His servant, so no threat from false gods can cause harm.

The word “sufficient” encapsulates complete reliance, protection and provision. The verse then asks, “Yet they threaten you with other gods besides Him!” highlighting the absurdity of fearing lifeless idols that can neither harm nor benefit. Then a general principle is laid down: “Whoever Allah leaves to stray will be left with no guide. ” This serves as a warning to the persecutors; their misguidance is a result of Allah leaving them to stray due to their own choices, and they will find no one to lead them out of that darkness. For the Prophet, it is a reassurance that his enemies’ misguidance cannot touch his divinely guided path. This verse powerfully connects to the central theme of tawakkul, trust in Allah, and sets up the next verse that further affirms divine guidance.

=37=

This verse balances the previous one. While those left to stray have no guide, those whom Allah guides are safe; no power in the universe can mislead them.

This is an absolute guarantee for the Prophet and the believers: if they remain under Allah’s guidance, the plots of the enemies cannot deviate them from the truth. The verse then ends with a question that affirms two attributes: “Is Allah not Almighty” (ʿAzīz), possessing irresistible power and honour, and “capable of punishment” (Dhū Intiqām), the One who exacts retribution?

This is a dual comfort. The Almighty protects His servants, and the Lord of Retribution will deal with the oppressors.

The mention of punishment assures that the threats the Prophet received will not go unanswered. The verse encourages believers to seek guidance from Allah and fear straying, while also trusting that ultimate justice is with the All-Mighty. This pair of verses (36 and 37) beautifully summarises the doctrine of reliance on Allah and the reality of guidance and misguidance.

=38=

The surah now exposes the intellectual contradiction of the Meccan idolaters. They admitted that Allah alone created the heavens and the earth, yet they worshipped other deities. The verse instructs the Prophet to ask them about their idols: if Allah intended harm for someone, could those idols remove it? Or if Allah intended mercy, could the idols block it? The obvious answer to both questions is no. The idols are powerless, so worshipping them for protection or benefit is utterly illogical. The Prophet is then commanded to declare his own position: “Allah is sufficient for me. In Him alone the faithful put their trust. ” This is a beautiful statement of tawakkul. The true believers trust only in the One who holds all power, not in any created being. By saying “the faithful,” the verse extends this trust from the Prophet to all who believe, creating a community of mutual trust in Allah.

This verse also provides a powerful rational tool for dawah: one can ask anyone who believes in a higher power yet seeks help from intermediaries whether those intermediaries can override God’s will. The clarity of this argument led some of the Quraysh to deep reflection. It reinforces the message of verse thirty-six and practically demonstrates the sufficiency of Allah.

=39=

After presenting the clear rational arguments, the surah moves to a tone of final warning. The Prophet is told to address his people directly with the words “O my people,” a phrase filled with concern and a last attempt to reach their hearts. He tells them to persist in their ways, a statement that is not approval but a declaration that the argument has reached its conclusion. They have been shown the truth, and if they insist on their path of disbelief, they may continue, but the Prophet will equally persist in his path of pure monotheism and obedience.

The phrase “You will soon come to know” is a stern warning of impending consequence. It leaves the matter suspended, creating a sense of dread. This verse marks a transition from debate to a near ultimatum. It echoes the earlier command to say “Enjoy your disbelief for a little while,” but here it is addressed to “my people,” which carries an emotional weight. The Prophet’s persistence is not stubbornness but unwavering commitment to the truth.

This verse also teaches that after clear communication, one should not waste time in futile argument but should entrust the outcome to Allah.

=40=

This verse completes the warning of the previous one. “You will soon come to know” is now explained: they will know who will receive a humiliating torment in this life and an everlasting punishment in the next. The humiliating torment in this life could be defeat, captivity, or a visible disgrace that shatters their pride. The everlasting punishment is the Fire of the Hereafter.

The word “overwhelmed” indicates that the punishment will descend upon them and settle permanently, with no escape. The two punishments cover both worlds, leaving the disbelievers with no respite. The verse does not specify the exact nature of the worldly torment, keeping it open so that every generation of disbelievers may see it fulfilled in different ways. For the Quraysh, the humiliating torment was witnessed in the Battle of Badr and later the conquest of Mecca, where their pride was crushed.

The verse also gives the believers confidence that the arrogant rejectors will not escape justice, whether in this life or the next. It brings closure to this section by re-emphasising the contrast between the fates of the believers and the disbelievers, a theme that will be picked up again in the following verses.
