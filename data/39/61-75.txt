=61=

In stark contrast to the gloomy faces and fitting punishment of the arrogant described just before, this verse presents the radiant outcome of the God-conscious. The word “deliver” translates yunajjī, which conveys a sense of rescue and safe arrival. It paints a picture of Allah personally saving the mindful from every peril of the Day of Judgment and escorting them to their place of ultimate triumph. This “place of triumph” is mafāzah, a word that indicates a great success and a place where one finds everything desirable without any fear of failure. It is Paradise, the final home of safety and honour. The promise that “no evil will touch them” is comprehensive. It covers physical pain, emotional distress, the torment of Hell, the terror of the Standing, and even the memory of past hardship. The righteous will be completely shielded.

Moreover, “nor will they grieve” assures them of eternal peace of mind. Grief arises from loss, and in Paradise there is no loss; there is only permanence, increase, and the pleasure of Allah.

This is a powerful source of hope for believers who suffer in this world, reminding them that their struggles and tears will be replaced by an unassailable joy. The verse connects back to the earlier mention of the elevated mansions and the promise that Allah never fails in His promise. Here the fulfilment is described as a personal act of deliverance by Allah.

The mention of the mindful (ittaqaw) again highlights that the safety is the direct fruit of taqwa. The believer is encouraged to live a life of consciousness, knowing that the final outcome is not just escape from Hell, but an active, triumphant deliverance into bliss, where no sorrow from the past or present can ever find them.

=62=

This verse establishes the foundation upon which the promise of deliverance and the warning of punishment rests. It declares two all-encompassing attributes of Allah. First, He is the Creator of all things. Everything that exists, from the smallest particle to the largest galaxy, owes its origin to His creative will. Nothing comes into being without His decree. This includes human actions, though He gave humans free will, He created them and the power by which they act. Second, He is the Maintainer of everything.

The Arabic word wakīl implies a trustee, a guardian, an ultimate disposer of affairs. He is not a creator who then abandoned His creation; He actively sustains, manages, and disposes of all matters. This comprehensive authority explains why He alone can deliver and judge. The verse serves as a powerful monotheistic statement. Because He is the Creator, no idol, angel, or prophet shares in His creative power. Because He is the Maintainer, reliance should be placed on Him alone. The early believers in Mecca, living among polytheists who acknowledged Allah as Creator but turned to idols for maintenance and intercession, were reminded that the Creator is also the only Caretaker. The duality they imagined was false.

This verse also logically underpins the next: if He is the Creator and Maintainer, then the treasuries of the heavens and the earth belong to Him, and rejection of His signs is the ultimate loss. In a believer's life, this verse inspires complete tawakkul, entrusting every affair to the One who created and manages the entire cosmos. It is an antidote to anxiety and a foundation for gratitude.

=63=

Having stated that Allah is the Creator and Maintainer, the surah now reveals that to Him belong the keys of the treasuries of the heavens and the earth. The “keys” are a metaphor for exclusive authority and control. The treasuries include the stores of rain, provision, mercy, guidance, life, and death. No one can unlock these stores except Allah. This imagery demolishes the belief that idols or other beings can grant provision or avert harm. The keys are with Allah; He opens and closes according to His wisdom. This phrase also carries a profound spiritual meaning: the keys to hearts are in Allah’s hand. He can open a hardened heart to Islam, as described earlier. After affirming this absolute ownership, the verse pronounces judgment on those who rejected the signs of Allah. They are the true losers.

The word “true” indicates that their loss is real and ultimate, as opposed to the apparent loss that believers might face in this world, like poverty or persecution. The rejectors may have worldly gains, but they have lost their souls, their chance at Paradise, and their connection to the ultimate reality. This loss is final and irredeemable. The verse connects back to the earlier declaration that the true losers are those who lose themselves and their families on Judgment Day. By tying the keys of the treasuries to the verdict of loss, the surah emphasizes that disbelief is not just a theological error but a catastrophic personal failure to access the very mercy and provision that Allah holds. The believer, in contrast, holds the key of faith and supplication, which unlocks the infinite treasuries of divine grace.

=64=

The polytheists of Mecca, in their arrogance, would sometimes try to compromise with the Prophet. They suggested that he worship their idols for a year and they worship his God for a year, or that he at least show some respect to their deities. This verse is a sharp, uncompromising response. The Prophet is instructed to address them with a rhetorical question filled with astonishment: “Are you urging me to worship anyone other than Allah, O ignorant ones? ” The word “urging” suggests that they were persistently trying to persuade him to dilute his pure monotheism.

The term “ignorant ones” is a direct assessment of their spiritual state. This is not a petty insult but a statement of fact: ignorance of Allah’s majesty and exclusive right to worship is the root of their request. How could they, after all the signs and proofs, suggest that the Prophet worship false gods?

The question exposes the absurdity of their proposal. The Prophet’s mission is to call to tawhid; for him to associate partners would be the ultimate betrayal of his calling.

The verse also serves as a timeless reminder that there can be no negotiation on the core principle of monotheism. In every age, believers face pressures—social, political, or economic—to compromise their faith by acknowledging false ideologies, bowing to ungodly systems, or blending truth with falsehood. The response taught here is to firmly reject such overtures, recognizing them as the product of ignorance. This uncompromising stance is immediately grounded in the next verse, which warns of the dire consequences of shirk not just for the Prophet but for all prophets before him.

=65=

To justify the firm rejection in the previous verse, the surah reveals a universal divine law. The message that shirk nullifies all good deeds and leads to complete loss has been revealed to the Prophet Muhammad and to every prophet before him. This shows that the invalidity of associating partners with Allah is not a new teaching specific to Islam but the consistent core of all divine revelation. The threat that “your deeds will certainly be void” is terrifying. It means that a lifetime of prayer, charity, and good works can be utterly wiped out by shirk, because the foundational condition of all deeds—sincerity to Allah—has been shattered. The deeds lose their spiritual value and weight. The person then becomes “truly one of the losers,” echoing the description of the true losers in the previous verse.

This warning, while addressed to the Prophet in its wording, is a powerful message for the entire community. If even a prophet would suffer such a fate hypothetically, then no believer can feel complacent.

The verse also provides a deep theological insight: the gravity of shirk lies not merely in its wrongness but in its comprehensive destruction of a person’s spiritual capital. At the same time, it assures the believers that the message of pure monotheism is the eternal thread running through all prophethood, from Adam to Moses to Jesus to Muhammad. This historical continuity emboldens the Prophet against the Meccans who claimed to follow the religion of Abraham while falling into idolatry. The verse leads directly to the positive command that follows, which balances the warning with an exhortation to worship and gratitude.

=66=

After the warning of deeds being voided by shirk, the verse gives the positive and only alternative. The word “Rather” serves as a strong corrective, turning away from the prohibited association of partners to the exclusive worship of Allah. The command “worship Allah alone” reiterates the central call of the surah. This worship must be free from any taint of shirk. But the verse adds something profound: “and be one of the grateful.” Gratitude here is not a superficial thankfulness but a comprehensive state of heart and action that recognizes every blessing as from Allah and directs all devotion back to Him. True worship is the highest expression of gratitude. The person who knows that Allah is the Creator, the Maintainer, and the Holder of the keys of all treasuries cannot help but overflow with gratitude. Ingratitude, as shown in earlier verses, is the root of shirk. When people fail to be grateful for blessings, they attribute them to their own knowledge or to false intermediaries. So gratitude is the protective shield of tawhid. By linking worship with gratitude, the verse teaches that acts of devotion are not a burden but a joyful response to unending divine favours. The Prophet is told to be among the grateful, and by extension, the believers are called to cultivate this quality. This verse connects beautifully to the earlier mention that if you are grateful through faith, He will appreciate it. The chain is complete: faith leads to gratitude, gratitude leads to sincere worship, and sincere worship saves deeds from nullification and leads to triumph.

The emphasis on gratitude also prepares the heart for the awe-inspiring description of Allah’s majesty in the following verse, reminding the believer that the One they worship is far above all comparisons.

=67=

This verse delivers a shattering image of divine majesty. It accuses the disbelievers of failing to estimate Allah with the estimation He deserves. Their shirk is a direct result of their belittling of Allah’s greatness. To correct this, the surah unveils a scene from the Day of Judgment: the whole earth will be in His Grip, and the heavens will be rolled up in His Right Hand. This imagery, whose exact modality is known only to Allah, conveys absolute ownership, control, and power. The earth, which seems so vast and solid to us, is nothing but a small object in Allah’s Grip. The heavens, with all their galaxies and incomprehensible expanse, are rolled up like a scroll in His Right Hand.

This is how the Prophet explained the verse in a hadith: on that Day, Allah will take the earth and fold the heavens with His Right Hand, declaring His absolute sovereignty. The verse then exclaims, “Glorified and Exalted is He above what they associate with Him!” This glorification follows the description to show that the One who holds such unparalleled power cannot possibly have partners. Idols, angels, and prophets are all part of the creation that will be in His Grip; they cannot be His equals. The verse is a profound wake-up call for anyone who trivializes the Creator. It also gives immense comfort to the believers: the One who will judge them has power over everything, so no oppressor can escape and no good deed is lost. The verse connects to the earlier theme of Allah’s supreme authority, bringing the surah to a climactic moment before describing the blowing of the Trumpet and the final judgment in the following verses.

=68=

After the image of Allah’s grip over the cosmos, the surah depicts the end of the current order. The Trumpet, a tremendous instrument associated with the angel Israfil, will be blown.

The first blast will cause universal terror and death. Every living being in the heavens and the earth will collapse, dying instantly from the sheer horror and the divine command. The exception “except those Allah wills to spare” indicates that some beings, perhaps certain angels like Gabriel and Michael, or the martyrs according to some interpretations, will be spared this death by Allah’s will. This universal death shatters any illusion of immortality or self-sufficiency. Then the Trumpet will be blown a second time. This blast will bring all the dead back to life, rising from their graves and assembling for judgment.

The phrase “looking on in anticipation” paints a picture of the resurrected standing erect, looking around in stunned silence, waiting to see what happens next. Their eyes are wide with the terror and awe of the occasion.

This sequence echoes many Quranic passages and forms a cornerstone of Islamic eschatology. It serves as the ultimate proof of Allah’s power over life and death, a theme introduced earlier with sleep and death. The disbelievers who mocked the idea of bodily resurrection will witness it firsthand.

For believers, this verse instills a sense of urgency and readiness. The world as we know it will end, and we will all stand before our Lord. The verse bridges the descriptions of Allah’s power with the fair judgment that follows, where the earth will shine with divine light.

=69=

This verse describes the scene of the great tribunal. The earth, which will be changed to a different earth, will shine with the light of its Lord. This light is a manifestation of divine justice and presence, illuminating every corner so that nothing remains hidden. Then the record of deeds will be laid open.

This is the book that each person will receive, containing every action, word, and thought, meticulously preserved by the recording angels. Nothing will be omitted.

In addition to the personal records, the prophets will be brought forward as witnesses over their nations, testifying whether they delivered the message clearly. The “witnesses” include the angels who recorded deeds, and the Muslim community that will testify against other nations on behalf of the Prophet.

The mention of these multiple testimonies emphasizes the absolute fairness of the proceeding. Judgment will be passed on all with fairness.

The word “fairness” translates qisṭ, indicating a perfect, balanced justice. The verse then makes a categorical statement: “None will be wronged. ” No soul will receive a punishment greater than it deserves, and no good deed will be diminished.

This is the ultimate realization of divine justice, which the oppressed believers eagerly awaited and the arrogant disbelievers arrogantly denied. The verse connects back to the supplication that Allah will judge between His servants regarding their differences. Here, that promise is fulfilled. The light of the Lord, the open record, and the gathering of witnesses together form a court of absolute transparency, leaving no room for denial or evasion.

=70=

This verse summarizes the principle of the judgment. Each soul will be recompensed exactly for what it earned. The expression “paid in full” indicates that the reward or punishment will be complete, without any shortfall or excess beyond justice. The deeds are what the soul “has done,” emphasizing personal responsibility, a recurring theme in the surah.

The second part, “for Allah knows best what they have done,” provides the basis for this complete justice. Allah’s knowledge is perfect, encompassing the outward actions, the hidden intentions, and even the potential deeds that were never performed. Unlike human judges who rely on limited evidence and can be deceived, the Divine Judge sees directly into the heart. This knowledge ensures that no one is wronged and that the recompense matches the true quality of the deed.

This verse also serves as a warning and a comfort. The warning is for those who perform deeds for show or who harbor evil intentions: Allah knows, and the outer show will not avail them. The comfort is for those whose good deeds are unseen by others, or who are accused falsely: Allah knows their reality, and their reward is secure. This deep connection between divine knowledge and perfect justice is a hallmark of Quranic teaching. The believer is trained to focus on the inner quality of actions, knowing that the all-knowing Judge will reward sincerity and forgive faults.

The verse then seamlessly leads into the graphic descriptions of the fates of the disbelievers and the believers, showing the justice of Allah in action as groups are driven to their final destinations.

=71=

The surah now depicts the fate of the disbelievers in a scene that gives the surah its name, Az-Zumar, the successive groups. The disbelievers will be herded towards Hell in group after group, each community with its leaders and followers, a humiliating parade of the damned. This arrival in groups signifies that their unity in disbelief in the world becomes their collective march to punishment. When they reach Hell, its gates, which were previously shut, will be opened for them in a terrifying welcome. The keepers of Hell, stern angels, will pose a crucial question that confirms the justice of their punishment. They will ask whether messengers from among themselves—sharing their language and understanding their condition—did not come reciting Allah’s revelations and warning them of this Day. This question establishes the hujjah, the clear proof against them. The disbelievers will have no defense; they will confess, “Yes indeed! ” Acknowledging that the message reached them but they rejected it. Their addition, “But the decree of torment has come to pass against the disbelievers,” is a statement of grim realization.

The word of punishment, the divine decree based on His knowledge that they would persist in disbelief, has been rightfully fulfilled. This is not an excuse; it is an admission of their own role in earning that decree. The scene dismantles any claim of injustice, as the keepers’ question and the disbelievers’ answer establish that the torment is the direct result of their rejection of clear guidance. The believers hearing this are reminded of the value of the warning they accepted, and the disbelievers are left with no room to protest.

=72=

Following the acknowledgment of their guilt, the final command is given. They are told to enter the gates of Hell, and this entry is not for a limited period but “to stay there forever.

” The word khālidīn denotes an eternal, unending stay, which is the fate of those who died upon disbelief and rejected the truth completely. This permanence is the ultimate loss, the very opposite of the everlasting bliss promised to the mindful. Then the verse adds a powerful comment: “What an evil home for the arrogant!” This echoes the earlier rhetorical questions about Hell being a fitting home for the arrogant and the disbelievers.

The emphasis on arrogance throughout the surah is deliberate. It was arrogance—pride in false gods, pride in lineage, pride in worldly knowledge—that prevented them from bowing to the truth. So their punishment is degradation and humiliation in an evil home.

The word “home” suggests that this is their final residence, a permanent dwelling of horror. The verse serves as a chilling warning to anyone in any age who treats divine guidance with contempt. The connection between arrogance and Hell is a central Quranic theme: the arrogant cannot enter Paradise because Paradise is the home of the humble. By linking the entry into the Fire directly with the trait of arrogance, the surah invites self-reflection: does my heart harbor that same arrogance that rejects truth when it comes from an unexpected source? The believers are urged to cultivate humility, knowing that it is the very quality that saves.

=73=

The scene shifts to the opposite destiny, completing the picture of the surah’s name, the successive groups. The God-conscious are led to Paradise, not driven like the disbelievers, but “led” with dignity and honour, in groups. This grouping likely reflects the various ranks of believers and the different communities of the righteous across time. The journey to Paradise is one of joy and anticipation. When they arrive, they find its gates “already open.” Unlike the gates of Hell which are opened upon arrival as a terrifying surprise, the gates of Paradise stand permanently open for them, a gesture of eager welcome. The keepers of Paradise, angels of mercy, greet them with the salutation of peace: “Peace be upon you!

” This is the believers’ own greeting in the world, returned to them in the most beautiful way. The angels then declare, “You have done well,” acknowledging the purity of their lives, the goodness of their deeds, and their successful passing of the worldly test. Then comes the invitation: “So come in, to stay forever.” The everlasting nature of the stay is highlighted, confirming that the bliss will never end, no fear of eviction, no death, no decay. The contrast with the previous verse is stark: the arrogant enter Hell for their arrogance, while the mindful enter Paradise with peace and praise. This verse is a source of immense hope and motivation, reminding believers that every act of mindfulness will be greeted by this divine hospitality. The Prophet is comforted that his followers will be honoured guests of Paradise.

=74=

Once inside Paradise, the believers express their gratitude. Their first words are praise for Allah.

This is not a forced speech but the natural overflowing of hearts finally seeing the fulfilment of every promise they held onto by faith in the world. They specifically praise Allah for fulfilling His promise. They recall the verses of the Quran, the words of the Prophet, all the descriptions of Paradise that seemed invisible to worldly eyes. Now they see it is true.

The phrase “made us inherit the everlasting land” is profound. The earth of Paradise is called “the land,” and they inherit it. This inheritance implies that it was prepared for them and they take possession of it as their rightful due, by Allah’s mercy and their deeds. The ability to “settle in Paradise wherever we please” indicates the vastness of Paradise and the complete freedom and satisfaction of its inhabitants. They are not confined to a single plot; the whole expanse is theirs to enjoy and dwell in as they wish. Then the verse adds a commentator’s exclamation: “How excellent is the reward of those who work righteousness! ” This is either a continuation of their speech or a divine interjection. It emphasizes that this reward is not arbitrary but the fitting recompense for the doers of good.

The word “work” implies effort and consistent action. This inspires the believer to strive in this world, knowing that the labour will be repaid with an inheritance of unimaginable splendour. The verse connects back to the earlier mention of the reward of the good-doers, bringing the theme of divine fulfilment full circle.

=75=

The surah concludes with a magnificent cosmic scene. The Prophet is told, “You will see,” making him a witness to this majestic spectacle. The angels, those pure and mighty beings, are positioned all around the Throne of Allah, encircling it in reverent rows. They are glorifying their Lord with His praise, continuously declaring His perfection and beauty. This scene conveys the absolute order and worship in the highest realms, contrasting with the chaos and rebellion of earthly disbelief. The angels’ glorification is then linked to the reason: “for judgment will have been passed on all with fairness.” The conclusion of the Day of Judgment, with the righteous in Paradise and the wicked in Hell, is the manifestation of perfect justice that gives the angels cause for glorification. They praise Allah for His wisdom and justice in decreeing the final separation. Then the final announcement is made: “Praise be to Allah—Lord of all worlds!

” This is the last word of the surah and, in a sense, the last word of the entire cosmic drama. It begins with alhamdulillah and ends with alhamdulillah. The praising voice could be the angels, the believers, or a divine proclamation. The ascription “Lord of all worlds” is a reminder that Allah’s lordship extends over every realm—the world of angels, of humans, of jinn, of the heavens and the earth. The surah that opened with the revelation of the Book ends with the universal praise of its Revealer. This ending gives the believer a taste of the harmony and peace that will finally prevail when all false claims are silenced and only the truth remains. It provides a serene closure to the intense warnings and promises, leaving the listener with the echo of Allah’s praise.
