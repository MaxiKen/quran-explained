=1=

The sūrah opens with a series of solemn oaths, and the first of these is “By the dawn.” The Arabic word fajr refers to the break of day, the first light that cracks the darkness of night and spreads across the horizon. It is a time of quiet transition, when the world moves from stillness and sleep to activity and life. The dawn is a daily miracle, a sign of the precise order of the cosmos, and a symbol of new beginnings. By swearing by the dawn, Allah draws the human heart to reflect on this familiar yet profound event. The darkness of night represents heedlessness, ignorance, and the cover of sin. The dawn represents the piercing light of truth, the arrival of divine revelation that awakens the soul. It is the time of the Fajr prayer, when the believer rises from sleep to stand before the Lord, a moment of spiritual awakening that mirrors the physical dawn. The oath also hints at the ultimate dawn, the Day of Resurrection, which will break upon humanity after the long night of the grave. Just as the dawn inevitably follows the night, the Day of Judgment inevitably follows the life of this world. The Meccans who heard this oath witnessed the dawn every morning, yet they failed to see it as a sign. The oath challenges them: if the dawn is so reliable and so beautiful, pointing to a Wise Creator, how can they deny the coming dawn of the resurrection? The believer, reciting this, is reminded to cherish the dawn prayer and to live with the awareness that every sunrise is a promise from Allah that His light will always overcome the darkness. The dawn is also a witness; it testifies to the deeds done in the night and the deeds begun in the day. The angels of the night and the day gather at Fajr, as mentioned in authentic ḥadīth, making it a time of special divine observation. The oath thus sets a tone of solemnity and beauty, preparing the listener for the oaths that follow, each one a piece of a mosaic that forms a powerful argument for the truth of the coming judgment.

=2=

The second oath is “and the ten nights.” The Arabic layālin ‘ashr refers to ten specific nights, and the most authoritative interpretation, based on the sayings of the Prophet (ﷺ) and the early companions like Ibn ‘Abbās, is that these are the first ten nights of the month of Dhul-Ḥijjah, the month of the great pilgrimage. These nights are among the most sacred and blessed times in the Islamic calendar. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “There are no days in which righteous deeds are more beloved to Allah than these ten days. ” Within these nights fall the Day of ‘Arafah, the day of standing on the plain of ‘Arafāt, and the Day of Sacrifice, the ‘Īd al-Aḍḥā. These nights are filled with the worship of the pilgrims and the believers worldwide, with takbīr, talbiyah, prayer, and charity. By swearing by these ten nights, Allah elevates their status and draws attention to the immense mercy and spiritual opportunity they contain. The oath also links to the theme of time as a sacred witness. The ten nights are a limited period, a brief window of immense opportunity. They teach that life itself is a limited opportunity, a set of days and nights that will soon pass. The disbelievers waste these precious moments in heedlessness, while the believers seize them for acts of devotion. The number ten also holds significance; it represents completion and perfection. The ten nights are a complete package of blessing. Some early exegetes also mentioned that the ten nights refer to the last ten nights of Ramaḍān, which contain Laylat al-Qadr, or the first ten nights of Muḥarram.

However, the context of the following oath by the even and the odd, which perfectly matches the days of Dhul-Ḥijjah (the 9th, odd, and the 10th, even), strongly supports the interpretation of the first ten of Dhul-Ḥijjah. The oath by these nights reminds the listener that time is not neutral; certain moments are divinely charged with grace. To ignore them is a profound loss. The believer’s heart is stirred to honour these nights, to seek forgiveness and do good deeds in them, and to recognise that the Lord who created the ten nights is the Lord who will hold humanity accountable for how they spent all their nights. The oath builds the anticipation for the next pair, the even and the odd, adding another layer of sacred specificity.

=3=

The third oath is “and the even and the odd.” The Arabic wa al-shaf‘i wa al-watr uses two words that have been the subject of rich scholarly reflection. Al-shaf‘ means the even, the pair, the double. Al-watr means the odd, the single, the solitary. Together, they encompass all of mathematics, all of creation, and all of worship. Many interpretations have been given, and they all converge on profound truths. The most compelling interpretation, especially given the proximity to the ten nights, is that the even refers to the Day of Sacrifice, the 10th of Dhul-Ḥijjah, which is an even number, and the odd refers to the Day of ‘Arafah, the 9th, which is an odd number. Both are the climax of the great pilgrimage. Others said the even refers to all of creation, which was created in pairs, as the Qur’ān says, “And of everything We have created pairs,” and the odd refers to Allah Himself, who is One, unique, and without partner. Others said the even refers to the prayers that have an even number of rak‘ahs, and the odd refers to the Witr prayer, the odd-numbered prayer that seals the night’s worship.

Yet others said it refers to the very rhythm of existence: night and day, heaven and earth, this world and the Hereafter, all are even pairs, while the ultimate reality of the Creator is the unique, indivisible One. The oath by the even and the odd is an oath by the entire structure of reality, which is built on the harmony of pairs and the singularity of the Divine. It is a sign for those who reflect that the order of the universe points to a single, all-powerful Originator. The disbelievers who worshipped multiple gods are being told that the very mathematical fabric of existence screams of the One and the pair. The oath also echoes the balance of justice: the even and the odd will be accounted for. Every deed, whether done in pairs or alone, is recorded. The believer is reminded to perform the Witr prayer, to seek the singularity of devotion to the One, and to see in the alternation of even and odd the pulse of a universe that glorifies its Maker. The oath is a masterpiece of concision, a few words that open a universe of meaning, all pointing to the certainty of the judgment that the surah will soon describe.

The following oath by the night when it passes completes the time-based quartet.

=4=

The fourth and final oath in this opening series is “and the night when it passes!” The Arabic wa al-layli idhā yasr uses the verb yasr, which means to travel, to depart, to pass by in its course. The night is not a stationary block of darkness; it is a traveller, constantly moving, sliding over the earth as the planet rotates.

The phrase “when it passes” captures the moment of transition, the last portion of the night as it fades away, making room for the dawn. Some early commentators, including Mujāhid and Qatādah, understood this specifically as the night of Muzdalifah, the night the pilgrims spend under the open sky after ‘Arafah, which then passes as they move towards Minā for the sacrifice. This links it directly to the ten nights and the even and odd, forming a complete picture of the Ḥajj season. Others interpreted it more generally as every night that passes, a symbol of the relentless passage of time. The night is a covering that Allah spreads over the world for rest, and its passing is a daily reminder of mortality. Every night that departs takes a piece of our lives with it. The oath by the passing night is an oath by the ephemeral nature of the world. The night, no matter how long or dark it seems, inevitably passes. The darkness of the disbelievers’ oppression, the night of their denial, will also pass, and the dawn of judgment will break. The verb yasr also implies a smooth, silent, yet unstoppable movement. No power can halt the night or prevent its passing. Similarly, no power can halt the coming of the Day of Judgment. The believer is reminded to utilise the passing hours of the night for prayer, for the night prayer is the habit of the righteous, and to reflect that every passing night is a step closer to the final dawn. The four oaths together—dawn, ten nights, even and odd, passing night—paint a vivid portrait of sacred time, of moments imbued with divine significance. They are witnesses that will testify for or against humanity. The stage is now set for the question that follows, challenging the disbeliever to recognise the weight of these oaths. The swearing by these temporal signs is a powerful method to ground the abstract reality of judgment in the concrete experience of time, which every human being intimately knows.

=5=

The four solemn oaths are followed by a rhetorical question that serves as the jawāb al-qasam, the conclusion for which the oaths were taken: “Is all this not a sufficient oath for those who have sense?” The Arabic hal fī dhālika qasamun li-dhī ḥijr is a challenge to the intellect of the listener. After swearing by the dawn, the ten nights, the even and the odd, and the passing night, the Qur’ān asks: is there not, in these oaths, a convincing, sufficient proof and affirmation for the one who possesses ḥijr?

The word ḥijr literally means a stone, a barrier, a restriction. It is used here to mean intellect, reason, and understanding—the faculty that restrains a person from folly and directs them to the truth. A person of ḥijr is someone whose mind acts as a barrier against falsehood and impulsiveness.

The question is directed at the Meccan disbelievers and, by extension, at every human being. The oaths are weighty; they invoke profound, observable realities of time and sacred seasons. If a person truly possesses intelligence and the ability to reflect, these oaths should be enough to convince them of the truth of the message and the certainty of the coming judgment. The verse implies that the rejection of the disbelievers is not due to a lack of evidence, but due to a lack of ḥijr—a deficiency in their reasoning, a failure to use the intellect as a barrier against their desires.

The question is also a powerful rhetorical tool to make the listener pause and examine their own mind. Am I a person of ḥijr? Do I reflect on the signs? The oaths are not empty figures of speech; they are divine arguments. The dawn and the night, the even and the odd, the ten blessed nights—all of these are observable, experienced realities. They bear witness that the world operates on a precise, meaningful order. The One who created that order will certainly bring about the final Day of Reckoning.

The verse concludes the opening section with a note of solemn warning and a call to intellectual honesty. The following verses will now move from the oaths to the historical evidence, asking if the disbelievers have not seen how the Lord dealt with the tyrannical nations of the past.

The question “Is this not a sufficient oath?” creates a seamless bridge, implying that if the signs in time are not enough, then let them consider the signs in history. The believer, reciting this, feels his faith strengthened, knowing that the Qur’ān addresses the intellect with respect, providing oaths that are fully sufficient for any thinking person.

=6=

The sūrah now transitions from the oaths by time to the evidence of history, asking a direct and probing question: “Did you not see how your Lord dealt with ‘Âd?” The Arabic a-lam tara kayfa fa‘ala Rabbuka bi-‘Âd addresses the Prophet (ﷺ) and, through him, all listeners. The verb tara means “did you see,” but it implies “have you not learned, have you not considered.” The “seeing” here is the seeing of the heart and the mind, informed by the reports and the ruins that remain. The subject is “your Lord” (Rabbuka), a deeply personal and relational title. The same Lord who is the sustainer and cherisher of the Prophet and the believers is the One who executed judgment upon the arrogant transgressors of the past.

This is a comforting reminder for the believers and a terrifying warning for the deniers. The object of the question is the tribe of ‘Âd, an ancient Arabian people who lived in the region of al-Aḥqāf, the sand dunes, in the southern part of the Arabian Peninsula. They were the successors of the people of Nūḥ and were renowned for their physical strength, their tall stature, and their impressive civilisation. The Prophet Hūd was sent to them, calling them to abandon their idols and worship Allah alone. They rejected him with arrogance, boasting, “Who is mightier than us in strength?

” The question “Did you not see” implies that the story is well known, its ruins perhaps still visible along the trade routes. It challenges the Meccan pagans to connect their own denial with the denial of ‘Âd, and to realise that the same Lord who destroyed ‘Âd is their Lord too.

The use of the past tense “dealt” indicates a completed action; the destruction of ‘Âd is a historical fact, a divine sentence already executed. It is a sign for all who come after. The verse introduces a section that will list three mighty civilisations, all of which were destroyed because of their transgression. The argument is simple yet devastating: if the Meccans, who are far weaker than ‘Âd, Thamūd, and Pharaoh, persist in their denial, why should they think they are immune? The Lord who dealt with those superpowers can and will deal with them.

The question is left hanging, allowing the listener to reflect on the fate of ‘Âd before the next verse describes their specific identity and their unique greatness.

=7=

The people of ‘Âd are further identified and described in verse seven: “the people of Iram—with their great stature.” The Arabic Irama dhāti al-‘imād provides two specific details that deepen the portrait of this ancient civilisation. Iram is understood by many early commentators to be the name of the tribe, the city, or the eponymous ancestor of ‘Âd. Some said Iram was the name of their great city, a magnificent metropolis of pillars and wealth. Others said it refers to ‘Âd al-Ūlā, the first ‘Âd, distinguishing them from a later tribe.

The phrase dhāti al-‘imād has been interpreted in several complementary ways. The word ‘imād means pillars, columns, or lofty structures. The most famous interpretation, associated with Ibn ‘Abbās and others, is that it refers to the great physical stature of the people of ‘Âd. They were giants, possessing tall, pillar-like bodies of immense strength. This interpretation is supported by the Qur’ānic verse where Hūd reminds them, “Remember when He made you successors after the people of Nūḥ and increased you in stature extensively.” The ‘imād thus describes their tall, columnar frames. Another interpretation is that dhāti al-‘imād refers to their city, which was adorned with tall pillars and magnificent buildings, a symbol of their architectural power.

Yet another is that it refers to their tent poles, as they were a nomadic people who used massive tents with high pillars. All these meanings converge on the image of a people of extraordinary power, height, and construction. They were a superpower of the ancient world, unmatched in their physical capabilities. The verse highlights their greatness to make their subsequent destruction all the more striking and to serve as a warning. If a people of such towering stature could not withstand the punishment of Allah, what chance do the ordinary Meccans have?

The description also carries a moral lesson: their physical greatness became a source of arrogance. They trusted in their bodies and their buildings, but these pillars could not support them when the divine command came. The believer is reminded that true strength lies not in physical stature or material structures, but in faith and humility before Allah. The verse creates a vivid image of a lost civilisation of giants and columns, a memory preserved only in the Qur’ān and scattered ruins, a testament to the fleeting nature of worldly power.

=8=

The unique greatness of ‘Âd is emphasized with the final clause in verse eight: “unmatched in any other land.” The Arabic allatī lam yukhlaq mithluhā fī al-bilād uses the passive verb yukhlaq, meaning “had not been created.” It literally says that the like of this people, or this city, had never been created in the lands.

This is a divine testimony to the unparalleled stature and power of ‘Âd. They were a singular phenomenon in human history, a peak of physical and possibly architectural achievement that no other civilisation has replicated.

The phrase “in any other land” (fī al-bilād) indicates that this uniqueness was global; no land, no country, no region had produced their like. This intensifies the argument. If the most exceptional, mightiest people ever created were annihilated because of their disbelief and transgression, then the Meccan pagans, who are ordinary in comparison, should be in no doubt about their own vulnerability. The verse is also a subtle refutation of the idea that worldly greatness is a sign of divine favour. The people of ‘Âd were unmatched, but they were also the most hated and punished. Greatness in the dunyā is not an indicator of success in the ākhirah. The believer learns from this that one should never envy the worldly might of the disbelievers, for it may be nothing more than a prelude to destruction.

The verse also invites the listener to explore history and archaeology with an eye to the divine signs. The ruins of great civilisations are scattered across the globe, each one whispering the same warning: we were unmatched, and yet we fell. The Meccan pagans, who passed by the dwellings of ‘Âd on their trade journeys to Yemen, could see the physical evidence. The Qur’ān is asking them to open their eyes to the historical facts that surround them. The description of ‘Âd as unmatched also serves to glorify the power of Allah, who destroyed them with a wind that made them like uprooted trunks. Their uniqueness did not protect them; it only made their fall more dramatic and more instructive.

The surah now moves to the second example, Thamūd, continuing the roll call of the mighty who were humbled.

=9=

The second historical example is introduced in verse nine: “and Thamûd who carved their homes into the rocks in the Stone Valley.” The Arabic wa Thamūda alladhīna jābū al-ṣakhra bi-al-wād refers to the people of the Prophet Ṣāliḥ, who were the successors to ‘Âd. They inhabited the region of al-Ḥijr, the Stone Valley, which lies in the northwestern part of Arabia, along the trade route to Syria. The Meccan caravans would pass by the remains of their dwellings, the magnificent structures carved directly into the rocky mountainsides. The verb jābū means they cut, hollowed out, and carved.

The word ṣakhr is the solid, massive rock. The phrase bi-al-wād, “in the valley,” localises their civilisation to the specific valley that was well known to the Arabs. Thamūd were given a unique skill: the ability to carve secure, elaborate homes out of the solid rock mountains. The Qur’ān elsewhere mentions that they “carved out the mountains to make dwellings, feeling secure.” This architectural feat was a sign of their advanced civilisation, their engineering prowess, and the blessings Allah had given them.

Yet, like ‘Âd, they responded with arrogance. They worshipped idols, oppressed the weak, and when the Prophet Ṣāliḥ brought them a miraculous she-camel as a sign, they hamstrung it in defiance. Their punishment was a devastating earthquake or thunderous blast that left them lying prostrate in their homes, their rock-carved dwellings becoming their tombs. The verse is a direct warning to the Meccans who admired these ruins. The same stone that they thought provided eternal security became a witness to their annihilation.

The mention of the Stone Valley makes the warning geographically specific and tangible. It is not a myth from a distant land; it is a reality carved into the rocks they see. The believer reflects on the transience of even the most solid human constructions. Homes carved into rock seem permanent, but they could not protect their inhabitants from the divine command. True security is only with Allah.

The verse also teaches the danger of feeling secure in one’s material achievements. Thamūd trusted in their rock-hewn homes; the Meccans trusted in their trade and their fortified city; modern humans trust in their technology and concrete. The lesson remains the same: the only reliable fortress is the remembrance and obedience of Allah.

=10=

The third and final example of the destroyed tyrants is presented in verse ten: “and the Pharaoh of mighty structures?” The Arabic wa Fir‘awna dhī al-awtād names Fir‘awn, the Pharaoh of Egypt, the archetypal oppressor in the Qur’ān. He is described as dhī al-awtād, literally “the possessor of pegs” or “stakes.” This phrase has been interpreted in several profound ways. The most common interpretation is that awtād refers to the vast, firmly established structures of Pharaoh’s kingdom—the pyramids, the obelisks, the great buildings that were like pegs driven into the ground, symbols of his immense power and stability. Just as pegs anchor a tent, these structures seemed to anchor his dominion. Another interpretation is that awtād refers to the huge armies and hosts that Pharaoh commanded, the multitudes that pegged his authority firmly in the land. A third, more gruesome interpretation is that it refers to the stakes and instruments of torture that Pharaoh used to punish his enemies, especially the believers among the Children of Israel. He would drive pegs into their hands and feet, inflicting horrific suffering. All these meanings paint a picture of a ruler of absolute power, immense wealth, and brutal oppression. Pharaoh did not just have structures; he was the lord of structures, the embodiment of a civilisation that seemed invincible. He even claimed divinity, saying, “I am your lord, most high. ” Yet, when the Prophet Mūsā came with clear signs, he rejected and pursued the Israelites with his great army. His end was drowning in the Red Sea, his body preserved as a sign for future generations. The verse links Pharaoh with ‘Âd and Thamūd as a trilogy of transgression. Each excelled in a different form of worldly power: ‘Âd in physical stature, Thamūd in architectural carving, Pharaoh in military and structural might. All three were utterly destroyed. The Meccans, who knew these stories well and passed by the ruins and the body of Pharaoh (as some narrations indicate), are being asked: can you not see the pattern? The mighty structures of Pharaoh did not save him. The believer is reminded that no human power, no matter how formidable, can withstand the decree of Allah. The structures we build, the empires we admire, will all crumble. The only structure that endures is the edifice of faith and good deeds built for the Hereafter. The verse completes the historical argument, preparing the ground for the summary of their common crime and their common punishment.

=11=

After listing the three mighty nations, the sūrah identifies their common crime in verse eleven: “They all transgressed throughout the land.” The Arabic alladhīna ṭaghaw fī al-bilād uses the plural relative pronoun to refer collectively to ‘Âd, Thamūd, and Pharaoh. The verb ṭaghaw comes from the root ṭaghā, which means to overflow, to exceed all bounds, to become arrogant and tyrannical. Ṭughyān is not just a minor sin; it is a state of drowning in rebellion, where a person or a people defies every limit set by Allah and by basic morality.

The phrase “throughout the land” indicates that their transgression was not confined to a small area; they filled the entire land, the whole region of their dominion, with their corruption and injustice. Their tyranny was widespread, systemic, and all-encompassing.

This verse teaches that the destruction of those nations was not an arbitrary act of divine anger. It was the direct consequence of their own actions. They filled the earth with ṭughyān, and so the earth was cleansed of them.

The word ṭaghā is particularly resonant with the image of a flood; their evil overflowed like a raging river. The land itself, which they were supposed to cultivate and care for as vicegerents, became a witness against them. The Meccans, who practised their own forms of ṭughyān—cheating in trade, oppressing the weak, burying infant daughters, and persecuting the believers—are being told that they are walking the same path. The verse is a universal warning: when a people collectively transgress beyond all bounds, when injustice becomes the norm, when the powerful trample the rights of the weak without fear, the cup of divine wrath fills, and the punishment becomes inevitable. The believer learns from this to oppose ṭughyān in all its forms, starting with the tyranny of the ego over the soul. True faith restrains the human being from overflowing into sin.

The verse also provides a clear criterion for analysing the fall of civilisations: they collapse not primarily because of external enemies but because of internal moral decay and transgression. The structural might of Pharaoh, the carved homes of Thamūd, the giant bodies of ‘Âd—none could protect them from the consequences of ṭughyān.

The following verse will specify the particular nature of their corruption, linking the general to the specific.

=12=

The general charge of transgression is given a specific, concrete expression in verse twelve: “spreading much corruption there.” The Arabic fa-aktharū fīhā al-fasād uses the verb aktharū, meaning they made abundant, they multiplied.

The word fasād refers to corruption, decay, mischief, and all that disrupts the natural order of justice and righteousness. They did not just commit some isolated acts of corruption; they caused an abundance of fasād, spreading it far and wide. The particle fa- (so, and thus) indicates that the corruption was the direct result and manifestation of their ṭughyān. Transgression overflows into corruption. The fasād of ‘Âd included their idolatry, their arrogance, their oppression of the weak, and their mockery of the Prophet Hūd. The fasād of Thamūd included their economic exploitation, their hamstringing of the she-camel, and their defiance of the Prophet Ṣāliḥ. The fasād of Pharaoh included his claim to divinity, his enslavement of the Israelites, his murder of their sons, and his persecution of Mūsā and his followers. In each case, the corruption was not only a sin against Allah but also a sin against humanity. The land was filled with bloodshed, injustice, and falsehood.

The phrase “there” (fīhā) refers to the lands they inhabited. The earth itself was corrupted by their presence.

The verse establishes a fundamental Qur’ānic principle: fasād is the direct opposite of iṣlāḥ (rectification). The believers are sent to the earth to reform it, to establish justice and worship. The disbelieving tyrants spread corruption. This corruption is the justification for their destruction. The Meccan pagans, by oppressing the believers, by cheating in their trade, by their moral degradation, were also spreading fasād in their land. The verse is a stark warning that such behaviour does not go unpunished.

For the believer, the verse instills a deep sense of responsibility to be an agent of iṣlāḥ, not fasād. Every act of goodness, charity, justice, and da‘wah is a counter to the corruption that tyrants spread. The believer is also taught to see the connection between spiritual corruption and societal decay. When a people abandon the worship of the One God, they inevitably fall into all forms of moral and social fasād. The verse serves as a lens to understand the state of the world and a motivation to work for its reform, while also trusting that the ultimate, perfect iṣlāḥ will be accomplished by Allah on the Day of Judgment.

The next verse reveals the divine response to this rampant corruption.

=13=

The response to the rampant transgression and corruption is revealed in verse thirteen: “So your Lord unleashed on them a scourge of punishment.” The Arabic fa-ṣabba ‘alayhim Rabbuka sawṭa ‘adhāb uses the powerful image of ṣabba, meaning He poured out, He unleashed in a sudden, overwhelming torrent. The punishment is described as sawṭa ‘adhāb.

The word sawṭ literally means a whip, a lash. The punishment is likened to a whip being cracked down upon them, a scourge that delivers a sharp, painful, and decisive blow. The imagery combines the suddenness of a poured flood with the stinging pain of a whip. It is a punishment that is both extensive and intensely painful.

The phrase “your Lord” is again used, reinforcing the personal connection to the Prophet and the believers, and reminding the disbelievers that the Lord they deny is the very One who can unleash such a scourge. The punishment of ‘Âd was a howling, violent wind that raged for seven nights and eight days, destroying everything in its path. The punishment of Thamūd was a deafening blast or earthquake that seized them. The punishment of Pharaoh was drowning in the sea, along with his army. Each was a specific, fitting scourge that matched their particular arrogance. The verb “unleashed” implies that the punishment was held back, then released at the appointed time with full force. The worldly punishment of these nations was a prelude to their punishment in the Hereafter, which is even greater. The verse is a terrifying warning to the Meccans, who were mocking the Prophet and demanding to see the punishment. It tells them that the Lord has a whip, and He will unleash it when the time is right.

The image of the whip was particularly vivid for the Arabs, who used whips on their camels and understood its capacity to inflict sharp pain. The verse also comforts the believers: the same Lord who unleashed the scourge on the oppressors is the Lord who will defend them. Justice may be delayed, but the whip is in the hand of the Merciful, and it will fall on the necks of the unrepentant. The believer learns to fear the punishment of Allah and to seek His mercy, knowing that the same power that destroyed the tyrants can also protect the righteous.

The following verse will summarise the reason for this divine action, attributing it to the constant vigilance of the Lord.

=14=

The sūrah concludes the section on the destroyed nations with a powerful statement of divine attribute in verse fourteen: “For your Lord is truly vigilant.” The Arabic inna Rabbaka la-bi-al-mirṣād uses the emphatic particles inna and la-.

The word mirṣād is a noun of place from the root raṣada, which means to watch, to observe, to lie in wait, to be on the lookout. Al-mirṣād is the watchtower, the observation post, the place from which everything is observed and no one can escape.

The phrase “bi-al-mirṣād” literally means “is indeed in the observation post” or “is indeed ever watchful.” It paints a picture of the Lord as being constantly, actively, and perfectly vigilant, watching the deeds of His creation, so that nothing escapes His notice and the oppressors cannot flee His judgment. The verse explains the reason behind the destruction of ‘Âd, Thamūd, and Pharaoh. They were not destroyed by random chance or by a distant, indifferent deity. Their Lord was watching them. He saw their transgression, He recorded their corruption, and when the time was right, He struck. The vigilance of Allah is comprehensive; it includes His knowledge of the secret and the open, His patience in giving respite, and His eventual execution of justice.

The word mirṣād, with its connotation of a watchtower, implies that the Lord is not passively observing but is actively positioned to intercept and hold accountable. This is both a terrifying warning and an immense comfort. For the oppressor, it means there is no hiding place. For the oppressed, it means that their cries are heard at the highest watchtower, and the response will come. The Meccan pagans, who thought their nightly plots and secret mockery were hidden, are told that Allah is at the mirṣād, observing them with unblinking precision. The believer is reminded to live with the constant awareness of the divine vigilance, to never commit a sin in secret thinking no one sees. The Prophet (ﷺ) defined iḥsān as “to worship Allah as if you see Him, and if you do not see Him, He certainly sees you.” This verse is the foundation of that consciousness. The vigilance of the Lord also extends to the believers; their struggles, their patience, their hidden acts of worship are all seen and will be rewarded. The verse elegantly bridges the historical examples with the contemporary situation in Mecca, and it sets the stage for the next section, which diagnoses the human psychological disease that leads to such transgression: the misunderstanding of the tests of wealth and poverty, and the failure to care for the vulnerable. The vigilant Lord is watching how humanity responds to both His gifts and His withholdings.

=15=

With the historical examples complete, the sūrah turns to a profound analysis of human psychology and the root cause of moral failure. Verse fifteen describes the first half of a pair of contrasting tests: “Now, whenever a human being is tested by their Lord through His generosity and blessings, they boast, ‘My Lord has deservedly honoured me!’” The Arabic fa-ammā al-insānu idhā mā ibtalāhu Rabbuhu fa-akramahu wa na‘‘amahu fa-yaqūlu Rabbī akraman uses the word ibtalāhu, meaning “He tested him.” The crucial point is that the generosity and blessings are a test, not necessarily a reward or a sign of divine pleasure. The human being, in his ignorance and arrogance, misinterprets the test. When Allah expands his provision, gives him wealth, health, status, and comfort, the person boasts, “My Lord has honoured me.” He sees the worldly gifts as proof of his own merit and his special standing with Allah.

The word akraman (has honoured me) implies that he thinks the honour is a reflection of his inherent worthiness, a deserved elevation. This is the sin of pride and self-deception. The verse exposes the materialistic worldview that equates divine favour with worldly success. The Meccan pagans were steeped in this mindset; their wealthy leaders thought their riches proved that their gods were pleased with them and that their religion was correct. The verse shatters this illusion by redefining prosperity as a test. Will the person be grateful, humble, and generous, or will he become arrogant and self-congratulatory? The believer is warned against this trap. Wealth is a fitnah, a trial, and the appropriate response is not to boast but to thank Allah, to acknowledge that it is an unearned gift, and to use it in obedience.

The phrase “My Lord has honoured me” also contains a subtle irony; the one who truly honours the human being is the Lord who provides, but the human twists that honour into a claim of self-worth. The verse is a mirror for every soul that has experienced ease. Do I see my blessings as a test, or do I see them as a divine endorsement of my lifestyle? The correct prophetic understanding is that Allah gives worldly kingdom to those He loves and those He does not, but He gives faith only to those He loves.

The following verse will present the opposite test, completing the picture of human misunderstanding and setting up the critique of their actual behaviour towards the vulnerable.

=16=

The mirror image of the previous verse is presented in verse sixteen, completing the analysis of human misunderstanding of divine tests: “But when He tests them by limiting their provision, they protest, ‘My Lord has undeservedly humiliated me!’” The Arabic wa ammā idhā mā ibtalāhu fa-qadara ‘alayhi rizqahu fa-yaqūlu Rabbī ahānan uses the same verb ibtalāhu, establishing both prosperity and adversity as tests. The phrase qadara ‘alayhi rizqahu means “He restricted, measured out, straitened his provision for him.” It is the act of divine wisdom that withholds wealth, health, or comfort in varying degrees. The human being, faced with this limitation, jumps to a false conclusion: “My Lord has humiliated me. ” The word ahānan (has humiliated me) indicates that he sees the lack of worldly provision as a personal insult, an unfair degradation, a sign that he is worth nothing in the eyes of Allah.

This is the sin of despair, resentment, and questioning divine wisdom. The person in adversity fails to see that the restriction might be a form of protection, a means of spiritual growth, an expiation of sins, or a test of patience. Instead, he accuses his Lord of humiliation. This exposes a profound theological error. Allah does not humiliate a person by giving them less wealth; the true humiliation is in sin and distance from Allah. The believer is taught to respond to poverty or hardship with patience, contentment with Allah’s decree, and the certainty that the restriction contains hidden wisdom. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “How wonderful is the affair of the believer! All of his affairs are good for him. If prosperity comes to him, he is grateful, and that is good for him. If adversity befalls him, he is patient, and that is good for him.” The disbeliever lacks this framework, so he swings between boasting and protesting. The verse is a powerful diagnosis of the human heart. It reveals that many people worship their desires rather than Allah, using His gifts or withholdings as a measure of His love, which reduces the Lord to a mere dispenser of worldly goods. The Meccan pagans mocked the poor believers, thinking their poverty was a sign of divine disfavour. The Qur’ān refutes this by showing that both abundance and restriction are tests, and the true measure of honour is taqwā. The verse sets the stage for the “Absolutely not!” that follows, which will reveal the real reason for divine displeasure: their failure to care for the weak and their obsession with wealth.

=17=

The false assumptions of the boasting and protesting human are met with a sharp, emphatic rebuttal in verse seventeen: “Absolutely not! In fact, you are not even gracious to the orphan.

” The Arabic kallā is a powerful negation that sweeps away the previous delusions. It says: it is not as you claim. Neither prosperity nor poverty is a simple sign of divine honour or humiliation. The real issue is something else entirely.

The verse then shifts to the second person plural, “you,” directly addressing the Meccan elite and all who share their mindset. The charge is specific and damning: “you are not even gracious to the orphan. ” The phrase lā tukrimūna al-yatīm uses the verb tukrimūna from ikrām, which means to honour, to treat generously, to bestow kindness.

The word yatīm is the orphan, the child who has lost their father and is among the most vulnerable members of society. The verse accuses them of failing to show even the basic decency of honouring the orphan. In pre-Islamic Meccan society, orphans were often deprived of their inheritance, exploited, and mistreated. The wealthy, instead of using their prosperity to protect the orphan, used their power to prey upon the weak.

The word “even” (in the translation) captures the sense of a minimal duty that they fail to perform. The accusation is not that they merely lack supererogatory charity, but that they lack the fundamental human compassion that any decent person should possess.

The use of the word ikrām is significant. They claimed that their wealth was an ikrām from Allah (verse 15), but they themselves do not perform ikrām towards the orphan. They want to be honoured by God but refuse to honour God’s creation.

This is the height of hypocrisy. The verse exposes the direct link between their false theology and their moral corruption. Because they misunderstood the test of wealth, they hoarded it and refused to use it for the vulnerable. The believer is taught that true faith in Allah’s tests manifests as generosity and care for the orphan. The Prophet (ﷺ) was himself an orphan, and he said, “I and the caretaker of the orphan will be like this in Paradise,” holding his fingers together. The verse is a call to examine one’s treatment of the weakest in society as a litmus test of genuine faith.

The next verse will add the second major social failing, the neglect of the poor, building the case that their worldly success was not a sign of honour, but a prelude to punishment because of their hard-heartedness.

=18=

The indictment of the Meccan elite’s social failures continues and deepens in verse eighteen: “nor do you urge one another to feed the poor.” The Arabic wa lā taḥāḍḍūna ‘alā ṭa‘āmi al-miskīn uses the verb taḥāḍḍūna, from the root ḥaḍḍa, which means to urge, to incite, to encourage strongly. The accusation is not merely that they themselves fail to feed the poor, but that they do not even encourage each other to do so. There is no societal culture of caring for the impoverished.

The word miskīn refers to the destitute person, one who is utterly in need, unable to provide for their basic sustenance. In the tribal society of Mecca, feeding the guest and the needy was traditionally a source of pride and honour. But the commercial greed of the wealthy had eroded this virtue. They competed in building grand houses and accumulating capital, but they neglected the hungry. The verse highlights that a society that does not collectively urge the feeding of the poor is a society that has lost its moral compass. The individual failure is compounded by a communal failure. There is no public advocacy for the rights of the poor, no social pressure to do good.

The phrase “urge one another” indicates that the believers are meant to build a community where charity is encouraged, where feeding the hungry is a shared value actively promoted. The absence of such urging is a sign of a hardened heart and a decaying social fabric. The verse connects back to the false theology of verses fifteen and sixteen. The one who believes that his wealth is a sign of divine honour will see no obligation to feed the poor, viewing them as humiliated by God. The Qur’ān demolishes this by declaring that the true humiliation is not in poverty, but in the failure to show mercy. The Prophet (ﷺ) was the greatest advocate for the poor. He said, “He is not a believer who goes to bed full while his neighbour goes hungry.” The verse is a direct challenge to the believer to not only feed the poor personally but to be a voice that urges others, that creates systems of social support, that makes charity a defining characteristic of the community.

The next verse will add the sin of greed for inheritance, completing the portrait of a society obsessed with wealth and devoid of compassion.

=19=

The third major accusation is levelled in verse nineteen: “And you devour others’ inheritance greedily.” The Arabic wa ta’kulūna al-turātha aklan lamman uses the powerful imagery of eating to describe the consumption of inheritance. The verb ta’kulūna means “you eat,” implying a voracious, indiscriminate, and habitual consumption. The object is al-turāth, the inheritance, the wealth left behind by the deceased. The description “greedily” translates aklan lamman, with lamman meaning to gather up completely, to sweep up everything, leaving nothing behind. It is an eating that gathers and devours everything without restraint or selectivity. The practice of the pre-Islamic Arabs was to deny women, orphans, and even minor male children their rightful share of inheritance. The powerful male relatives would seize the entire estate, consuming it unjustly. The Qur’ān later established the precise laws of inheritance to protect the rights of all eligible heirs. This verse is a powerful condemnation of that predatory behaviour.

The word “devour” turns the act of taking wealth into a beast-like consumption, stripping it of any civility or justice. The greed is so overwhelming that the rightful heirs are left with nothing. The verse links to the earlier failure to care for orphans; often, the orphan’s inheritance was the very thing being devoured by unscrupulous uncles and cousins. The root of this sin is the same as the previous ones: a corrupt understanding of wealth, a love of the world, and a lack of faith in the divine accounting. The one who devours inheritance greedily does so because he thinks this wealth will benefit him absolutely and that there is no Day of Reckoning. The verse serves as a timeless warning against all forms of financial injustice, especially the exploitation of the vulnerable in matters of inheritance. It calls on believers to meticulously respect the shares decreed by Allah, to fear the fire of Hell that awaits those who unjustly consume the wealth of orphans, and to see wealth as a trust, not a spoil to be devoured. The vivid language of eating and gathering imprints the ugliness of the sin on the heart.

The next and final verse of this batch will complete the four-fold indictment with the root cause of it all: the intense, fervent love of wealth.

=20=

The sequence of accusations reaches its root cause in verse twenty: “and love wealth fervently.” The Arabic wa tuḥibbūna al-māla ḥubban jamman uses the verb tuḥibbūna, “you love,” and the object is al-māl, wealth, property, all material possessions. The love is described with the word jamman, meaning abundant, intense, overflowing, fervent. It is a love that is excessive, passionate, and all-consuming. The verse diagnoses the underlying spiritual disease that produces the preceding sins: the failure to honour the orphan, the failure to urge the feeding of the poor, and the devouring of inheritance. All of these are symptoms of a heart that has fallen in love with wealth with an intensity that overrides compassion, justice, and the fear of Allah. The love of wealth is not condemned in itself as a natural inclination, but the fervent, overwhelming love that becomes the primary driver of action and the central concern of the heart is severely condemned. It is this love that causes the human to boast when given and to protest when deprived. It is this love that blinds him to the tests of Allah and the rights of the vulnerable.

The word jamman paints a picture of a love that gushes forth like an unstoppable spring, filling every corner of the heart and leaving no room for the love of Allah or the love of one’s fellow human beings. The Meccan elite, with their trade caravans and their hoarded treasures, were exemplary of this disease. The verse is a profound warning for every believer to examine his heart. How much of my love is directed towards wealth? Does the love of money dictate my decisions, my emotions, my relationships? The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “Two hungry wolves sent against a flock of sheep are not more destructive to it than a man’s greed for wealth and status is to his religion.” The cure for this fervent love is to redirect the heart’s love towards the Eternal, to see wealth as a means and not an end, and to break the grip of materialism through regular charity, gratitude, and the remembrance of death and the Hereafter. The verse brings the entire section to a powerful climax, revealing that the social injustices of the world are not merely political or economic problems; they are spiritual problems rooted in the loves of the heart. Only by purifying the heart from the fervent love of wealth can a society of justice and compassion be built. The surah will now move to the consequences of these failures on the Day of Judgment, and the contrasting fate of the tranquil soul. The batch of the first 20 verses is thus complete, having moved from the majestic oaths to the historical warnings, through the psychological diagnosis of human error, to the root sin of loving wealth.
