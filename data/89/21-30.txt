=21=

The sūrah now shifts dramatically from diagnosing the human love of wealth to depicting the cosmic events that will shatter that love forever. Verse twenty-one opens with the emphatic particle “Enough!” translating the Arabic kallā, a forceful negation and a sharp turn. It is as if the discourse says: let there be an end to this delusion, this boasting and protesting, this devouring of wealth. The reality is far different from what you imagine.

The verse then begins a conditional clause with “When the earth is entirely crushed over and over.” The Arabic uses the verb dukkat, from dakka, meaning to pound, to crush, to level, to pulverize with repetitive blows. The earth, which seemed so solid and permanent, the very ground upon which they built their grand houses and hoarded their treasures, will be pounded into dust.

The phrase “crushed over and over” translates dakkā’ dakka, a repetition that intensifies the meaning. The earth will not be crushed just once, but repeatedly, until it is completely flattened and all its structures are ground to powder. Mountains will crumble, valleys will be levelled, and the entire surface of the planet will become a smooth, empty plain, just as earlier surahs described.

This is the great leveling that will erase all the distinctions of wealth and power that the disbelievers cherished. The palaces of the rich and the hovels of the poor will be equally pulverized. The treasures they hoarded and the inheritances they devoured will be buried and destroyed, utterly worthless. The verb dukka also echoes the fate of the ancient nations; their mighty structures, like those of Pharaoh and Thamūd, were crushed by divine punishment, and now the entire earth will undergo the same treatment. This verse is a profound shock to the heart that is attached to worldly property. It declares that the very real estate they fought over, the land they owned, the buildings they prided themselves on, will all be pounded into oblivion. The repeated crushing indicates absolute and irreversible destruction. The believer, hearing this, is reminded to keep a light grip on the dunyā. The earth that we walk on is not eternal; it is a temporary platform that will be dismantled. The only thing that will survive is the deeds done on it. The verse sets the stage for the grand arrival of the Lord and His angels, preparing the scene for the ultimate judgment. The crushing of the earth is the removal of the stage upon which the drama of human arrogance was played out, leaving the actors exposed before the only true Sovereign.

The next verse will describe that majestic and terrifying arrival, completing the opening of the Day's events.

=22=

The scene of absolute divine sovereignty unfolds in verse twenty-two: “and your Lord comes to judge with angels, rank upon rank.” The Arabic wa jā’a Rabbuka wa al-malaku ṣaffan ṣaffan describes the arrival of the Lord, an event that transcends human comprehension and must be understood in a manner befitting His majesty without anthropomorphism. The verb jā’a, “comes,” indicates a manifestation of His judgment and authority that will be undeniable. The early scholars said that we believe in this coming without asking how, knowing that nothing is like Him.

The phrase “your Lord” (Rabbuka) is again intensely personal. The same Lord who dealt with ‘Ād and Thamūd, who tested with wealth and poverty, who was ever-vigilant at the mirṣād, is now coming personally to execute judgment. The angels accompany Him in vast, ordered ranks.

The phrase ṣaffan ṣaffan repeats “rank upon rank” to indicate the countless, organized formations of angelic hosts descending. This is the supreme court of the universe. There will be no jury, no lawyers, no appeals; the Lord Himself presides, and His angelic army stands in readiness to carry out His decrees.

This image dwarfs every earthly display of power. The kings and pharaohs who marched with their armies are utterly insignificant compared to the Lord of all worlds coming with His angels in rank after rank.

For the disbelievers, this is the source of ultimate terror. The Lord they denied is now undeniably present, and His arrival means judgment. For the believers, the arrival of their Lord is the moment of longed-for justice and mercy.

The phrase “your Lord” carries a special comfort for the Prophet and the faithful: the One who is coming is their protector and guardian. The angels in ranks are a sign of order, majesty, and the complete preparedness of the divine realm for this day. The verse connects to the description of the Overwhelming Event in the previous surah and the Day of Judgment throughout the Qur'ān. It teaches the believer to live with the anticipation of this arrival. Every act of worship, every decision, is shaped by the certainty that one day the Lord will come with His angels to settle all accounts. The scene is so grand that it makes the worldly love of wealth seem pathetically trivial. How can anyone cling to coins and properties when the Lord of the Throne is coming with His celestial hosts? The verse is a call to prioritise the eternal meeting over the temporary possessions.

The next verse will add the final element to the courtroom: the bringing forth of Hell itself.

=23=

The courtroom of the Hereafter is completed with a terrifying visual in verse twenty-three: “and Hell is brought forth on that Day.” The Arabic wa jī’a yawma’idhin bi-jahannam uses the passive verb jī’a, “is brought,” indicating that the Hellfire, which Allah created for the rebellious, will be physically dragged or presented to the scene of judgment. Narrations describe that Hell will be brought forth with seventy thousand reins, each held by seventy thousand angels, roaring and seething. It will be a visible, overwhelming presence, not a distant concept.

The phrase “on that Day” anchors this event to the specific moment of judgment. Then the verse declares: “this is when every disbelieving person will remember their own sins.

” The word yatadhakkaru means to remember, to become acutely aware. The sight of Hell, the arrival of the Lord, the crushing of the earth—all combine to strip away the veil of heedlessness. The disbeliever, who spent his life ignoring the reminders and denying the resurrection, will suddenly remember every sin in vivid detail. The record of his deeds, the vigilant angels, the scales of justice—everything will flash before his consciousness. But the verse immediately follows with the devastating rhetorical question: “But what is the use of remembering then?

” The Arabic wa annā lahu al-dhikrā translates as “How can the remembrance be of any benefit to him at that point? ” The word dhikrā means a reminder that leads to action and repentance. On that Day, the time for action is over. The door of repentance is closed. The memory serves only to increase the torment, to make the person fully aware of why he is being punished. It is the remembrance of regret, not the remembrance of reform.

This is a profound warning to the living. The remembrance that benefits is the remembrance done now, in the life of opportunity. The Qur’ān is the dhikrā that should awaken us today. If we postpone our awakening until we see Hell with our own eyes, it will be too late. The verse urges the believer to engage in constant muḥāsabah, self-accounting, before the Day when the only accounting will be from the Lord.

The question “what is the use” echoes through the soul, a chilling reminder that the present moment is the only moment for change. The disbeliever’s cry in the next verse will be the fruit of this useless remembering, a wish that can never be fulfilled. The verse thus masterfully combines a description of the future with an urgent call to the present, using the psychology of regret to motivate immediate action.

=24=

The torment of useless remembrance culminates in the verbal cry of regret in verse twenty-four: “They will cry, ‘I wish I had sent forth something good for my true life.’” The Arabic yaqūlu yā laytanī qaddamtu li-ḥayātī is the voice of the damned soul. The phrase yā laytanī is an expression of intense, painful wishing for something that is now impossible. It is the sigh of eternal loss. The person will express the desperate desire to have “sent forth” something good. The verb qaddamtu means to advance, to send ahead. It is the same concept encountered in earlier surahs: the deeds that one sends ahead to the Hereafter are the true investment. The disbeliever, who spent his life amassing wealth that he had to leave behind, realises that he sent nothing forward. He consumed his inheritance, loved wealth fervently, neglected the orphan and the poor, and now stands empty-handed before his Lord.

The phrase “for my true life” translates li-ḥayātī. This is a profound recognition. He now understands that the life of the dunyā was a fleeting illusion, and the true, everlasting life is the life of the Hereafter. He calls it “my life,” finally acknowledging that his real existence was what he lost. In the world, he considered the dunyā his life and the ākhirah a myth. Now the truth is inverted, and he sees that the dunyā was the dream and the ākhirah is the wakefulness. But this recognition comes too late. The cry is a testimony to the justice of Allah; the damned are their own witnesses. They admit that they did not send forth good, that they neglected their true life. The wish “I wish” indicates that they would give anything for a second chance, but no second chance will be given. This verse is one of the most poignant in the Qur’ān. It is a direct appeal to the listener: do not let this be your cry. Send forth good now, while the currency of time is still in your hand. The “true life” is not the life of the body, but the life of the soul in the presence of its Lord. The believer is taught to constantly ask himself: what have I sent forth today? Every prayer, every charity, every act of kindness, every restraint from sin is a provision sent ahead for the true life. The verse transforms the abstract concept of the Hereafter into a personal, urgent reality. The cry of the disbeliever is the echo that should resonate in the believer’s heart, motivating him to act before the cry becomes his own.

The following verses will describe the severity of the punishment that prompts this cry, reinforcing the warning.

=25=

The nature of the divine punishment is specified in verse twenty-five: “On that Day He will punish them severely, like no other.” The Arabic fa-yawma’idhin lā yu‘adhdhibu ‘adhābahū aḥad uses a unique grammatical construction. It literally says, “None will punish as He will punish.” The verb yu‘adhdhibu is negated for anyone except Allah, meaning His punishment on that Day is utterly unique in its severity and manner. No human tyrant, no worldly torture, no natural disaster can compare to the punishment that the Lord will personally inflict.

The earlier verses described the destruction of ‘Ād, Thamūd, and Pharaoh, which were severe worldly punishments, but even those are nothing compared to the punishment of the Hereafter. The phrase “like no other” emphasises the absolute singularity of the divine wrath. It is not just that no one else will punish; it is that the quality and intensity of the punishment are beyond anything anyone else could conceive or execute.

This is because the crime—persistent, arrogant disbelief and injustice—is an offense against the infinite majesty of Allah, and only an infinite punishment can match the crime. The verse is a stark warning to the oppressors who thought they could escape justice. The One who destroyed the mighty nations of old will personally administer a punishment that makes their earthly agonies seem like a gentle touch.

The verse also serves to magnify the justice of Allah: He does not delegate the punishment of His most arrogant enemies to angels alone; He Himself will punish them. For the believers, this is a source of awe and a reinforcement of their trust. The same Lord who punishes the wicked with unmatched severity will reward the righteous with unmatched mercy.

The verse also calls the believer to reflect on the seriousness of sin. We often think of divine punishment in abstract terms, but this verse makes it personal and terrifying. It instills a healthy fear that motivates repentance and obedience. The love of wealth and the neglect of the orphan, described earlier, are not minor infractions; they are acts of rebellion against the Lord who will punish with a severity that has no parallel. The verse leaves the soul trembling and seeking refuge in the forgiveness of the All-Merciful.

The next verse will complete the description of the punishment by adding the dimension of binding, showing that the torment is both physical and utterly inescapable.

=26=

The uniqueness of the divine punishment is further detailed in verse twenty-six: “and bind them tightly, like no other.” The Arabic wa lā yūthiqu wathāqahū aḥad mirrors the previous verse’s structure. The verb yūthiqu means to bind, to fetter, to tie up securely.

The word wathāq is the binding itself, the chains and shackles. The verse states that no one binds as Allah will bind. The disbelievers will be fettered with chains that no power can break. In this world, tyrants bound and imprisoned the innocent, thinking their ropes and chains were strong. On that Day, the tyrants will be bound by the Lord of all worlds with chains of fire and iron, and there will be no escape. The binding is both physical and psychological. The physical chains will wrap around their bodies, and the psychological chain of regret and despair will wrap around their souls.

The word “tightly” translates the intensity of the wathāq; it is a binding that leaves no room for movement, no possibility of release. The repetition of “like no other” in both verses emphasizes that both the active punishment and the restraining of the criminal are divinely unique.

This is the perfect justice: the ones who freely roamed the earth committing sins are now bound forever. The binding also symbolises their state of being trapped by their own choices. In the world, they were free to repent but chose to chain themselves to their desires.

In the Hereafter, that spiritual chaining becomes literal. The verse is a terrifying conclusion to the warning section, before the surah’s beautiful turn to the fate of the righteous.

For the believer, this verse inspires a profound gratitude for the freedom to worship Allah, and a determination to use that freedom to do good before the Day when all movement will cease. The binding is the final act of the courtroom; after it, the damned are cast into the Fire, while the saved are summoned to the blissful welcome. The contrast is about to be painted in the most gentle and beautiful terms, as the surah shifts from the cries of the damned to the divine invitation to the tranquil soul. The believer is meant to feel the chill of the binding and then the warmth of the coming call, moving from fear to hope, from the darkness of the punishment to the light of the divine embrace.

The next verse opens that radiant section with the most tender address in the Qur'ān.

=27=

The sūrah now takes a breathtaking turn from the horrors of punishment to the sublime peace of the righteous. Verse twenty-seven is a direct divine address: “Allah will say to the righteous, ‘O tranquil soul!’” The Arabic yā ayyatuhā al-nafsu al-muṭma’innah is one of the most beautiful and comforting calls in the entire Qur’ān. It is the words that the believers long to hear from their Lord. The nafs is the soul, the self, the essence of the human being. It is described as muṭma’innah, from the root iṭma’anna, which means to be tranquil, to be at peace, to be calm and satisfied.

This is the soul that has found its rest in the remembrance of Allah, that is no longer agitated by the storms of doubt, desire, or fear. It is the soul that was tested with wealth and poverty and passed the test, remaining grateful in prosperity and patient in adversity. It is the soul that honoured the orphan, urged the feeding of the poor, respected inheritance, and restrained its love of wealth. This soul did not boast when given or protest when deprived. It lived in the world with a heart attached to the Creator, not the creation. The address “O tranquil soul” is a recognition of its state, a divine acknowledgment of its peace.

The word muṭma’innah implies a profound, settled confidence. The believer, throughout life’s trials, maintained an inner calm because of faith in Allah’s wisdom and promise. Now, at the moment of death and resurrection, that inner calm becomes an outward greeting from the Lord Himself. The call is direct and personal, a loving invitation that makes the soul forget every hardship it ever endured. The early believers in Mecca, who faced persecution and poverty, were particularly in need of this reassurance. They were told that their suffering was not in vain, and that a direct, loving call from their Lord awaited them.

The verse teaches that the highest goal of the spiritual life is to attain this state of the muṭma’innah soul. It is achieved through consistent dhikr, obedience, and the purification of the heart from the love of the world. The address marks the beginning of the soul’s eternal journey into divine pleasure.

The next verse will issue the three-fold command that follows this greeting, a command filled with honour, welcome, and the ultimate reward. The verse stands as a beacon of hope, a window into the intimate, loving relationship between the Lord and His faithful servants. It is the very opposite of the severe punishment and tight binding of the damned, the radiant face that contrasts with the downcast, exhausted face of the previous surah.

=28=

The beautiful address to the tranquil soul continues with a command of ineffable honour in verse twenty-eight: “Return to your Lord, well pleased with Him and well pleasing to Him.” The Arabic irji‘ī ilā Rabbiki rāḍiyatan marḍiyyah is the invitation to come home. The verb irji‘ī, “return,” is the exact opposite of the hopeless cry of the damned. The soul is not being dragged to punishment; it is being invited to return to its true origin, its Lord. The journey of life was a temporary separation, and now the return is at hand.

The phrase “to your Lord” (ilā Rabbiki) is profoundly intimate. The Lord is not a distant judge but the welcoming host. The soul returns to the One who created it, nurtured it, and guided it. The two adjectives rāḍiyah and marḍiyyah are a perfect pair. Rāḍiyah means “well pleased,” “satisfied,” “content.” The soul is completely pleased with Allah—with His decree in the world, with the trials He gave, with the reward He has prepared. There is no complaint, no regret, only pure, radiant satisfaction. The soul looks at the bliss of Paradise and says, “My Lord, I am pleased.” Marḍiyyah means “well pleasing,” “accepted,” “with whom He is pleased.

” This is the greater half of the relationship. Not only is the servant pleased with the Lord, but the Lord is pleased with the servant. This mutual pleasure is the ultimate success. The Prophet (ﷺ) said that Allah will say to the people of Paradise, “I bestow My pleasure upon you, and I will never be displeased with you after that.” The verse brings to mind the earlier description of the faces that are “fully pleased with their striving.” Here, that pleasure is consummated. The soul that left the world in a state of loving awe returns in a state of loving contentment.

The word rāḍiyah also implies that the soul has no lingering attachment to the dunyā; it is completely satisfied with what is in front of it. The command “return” would be given at the moment of death, as the soul departs the body, and again at the resurrection when it enters Paradise. The believer is taught to live in such a way that this call can be heard with joy. The trials of life are the polishing that makes the soul rāḍiyah and marḍiyyah. The verse is a soothing balm for every believer who struggles, reminding them that the ultimate goal is not just a place, but a relationship—a return to the Lord in a state of mutual, eternal pleasure.

The next verse will call the soul to join the company of the Lord’s honoured servants, adding the social joy to the personal bliss.

=29=

The invitation expands from the personal to the communal in verse twenty-nine: “So join My servants.” The Arabic fa-adkhulī fī ‘ibādī is a command that opens the doors to the blessed community of Paradise. The particle fa- (so, therefore) links the command to the soul’s state of being pleased and pleasing. Because you have attained this state, now enter among My chosen servants.

The phrase “‘ibādī” (My servants) is a title of immense honour. In the Qur’ān, the true servants of the Most Merciful are those who walk humbly on the earth, who pray through the night, who spend in charity, and who are the heirs of Paradise. To be called “My servant” is the highest accolade. The soul is not entering as a stranger or a guest; it is entering as a member of the household of the righteous. The command to “join” indicates that there is already a gathering of these honoured servants within Paradise, a community of prophets, truthful ones, martyrs, and the righteous. The believer who often felt isolated in the world because of his faith will now find himself in the best of company. The believers are not alone; they are part of a caravan that stretches across history.

The phrase “My servants” also carries a possessive intimacy. Allah is claiming them as His own, in a bond of love and guardianship. The soul that was called “O tranquil soul” is now called to take its rightful place among the ibād. The verse provides a powerful motivation for brotherhood and sisterhood in this world. The community of believers is the training ground for the community of Paradise. Those who love one another for the sake of Allah, who sit together in circles of remembrance, who support each other in times of hardship, are practicing for that ultimate joining. The command “join” also implies an end to separation. Death and the trials of the world had separated the believers, but now they are reunited in a gathering that will never break up. The verse is a warm, welcoming embrace. For the soul that was rejected and mocked by the world, here is the Lord of all worlds saying, “Come, join My chosen ones.

” The final verse will complete the invitation with the mention of the destination itself, the place where these servants dwell, the ultimate home of the tranquil soul.

=30=

The sūrah concludes with the climax of the divine invitation, the final, breathtaking command in verse thirty: “and enter My Paradise.” The Arabic wa adkhulī jannatī is the ultimate goal, the eternal home. After the call of the tranquil soul, after the command to return in mutual pleasure, after the invitation to join the honoured servants, the Lord says: “Enter My Paradise.” The possessive pronoun “My” (yā’ of jannatī) adds a dimension of intimacy and grandeur that is unparalleled.

This is not just any garden; it is the garden personally attributed to Allah, the garden of His special presence and His special preparation. The phrase “My Paradise” indicates that this Paradise is the place of the greatest honour, the highest level of the gardens, where the vision of the Lord’s countenance and the pleasure of His company are the supreme rewards. The early Muslims, who were promised gardens under which rivers flow, now hear the ultimate description of that garden: it is Allah’s own. The command “enter” is the final permission, the opening of the gates that were prepared for them. The soul that was tested, that struggled, that purified itself, that remembered its Lord and prayed, now hears the words it has longed for. There is nothing after this; the journey is complete. The home is reached. The verse brings the entire sūrah full circle. The surah began with oaths by the dawn, the ten nights, the even and odd, and the passing night. It spoke of the destruction of mighty nations. It diagnosed the human being’s false understanding of tests and his love of wealth. It warned of the crushing earth, the coming of the Lord, and the useless remorse of the damned. And now it ends with this gentle, majestic, personal welcome. The dawn that was sworn by at the beginning has now fully broken, and the tranquil soul enters the eternal day of My Paradise.

For every believer, this final verse is the goal that gives meaning to every prostration, every charity, every patient endurance of hardship. The Qur’ān, the reminder, the glorious word preserved in the Preserved Tablet, leads to this moment. The soul that clung to it, that lived by it, that loved it, will hear this call.
