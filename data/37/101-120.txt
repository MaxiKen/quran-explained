=101=

Abraham’s heartfelt supplication for a righteous offspring was answered in the most beautiful way. “So We gave him good news of a forbearing son.” The good news was delivered by angels, as detailed in other surahs, who visited Abraham in human form and announced the birth of a boy. This was a moment of immense joy for the aged prophet who had left his homeland and had no child to inherit his prophetic mission. The son is described as “forbearing” (ḥalīm), a quality that combines patience, gentleness, intelligence, and the ability to control anger. Forbearance is one of the most beloved traits to Allah, and it is particularly fitting for a prophet and a son who would be tested.

This description of the son, who is Ishmael according to the predominant view, foreshadows the great test of sacrifice that would soon come. A forbearing son would accept the divine command with patience and submission, just as his father would.

The word “forbearing” also indicates that the boy would grow into a man of remarkable emotional maturity, a leader among his people. The good news was given to Abraham and, through the mention in the Quran, to all believers, showing that Allah’s mercy responds to sincere prayer even when all material circumstances suggest impossibility. Sarah, Abraham’s wife, was barren and old, yet the promise was fulfilled. This teaches that no situation is beyond Allah’s power. For the Meccan believers, many of whom were separated from their families or had lost children, this verse was a comfort that Allah can bestow the gift of righteous offspring in the most unexpected ways. It also set the stage for the story of the great sacrifice, demonstrating that the birth of Ishmael was not just a personal joy but a crucial part of divine planning for the future of monotheism. The forbearing son would be the forefather of the Prophet Muhammad, linking the Meccan pagans’ lineage to this tested and faithful servant of Allah.

=102=

The narrative now moves to one of the most profound moments in the Quran: the test of sacrifice. “Then when the boy reached the age to work with him,” meaning Ishmael had grown old enough to walk alongside his father, assist in daily tasks, and engage in meaningful conversation. The affection between them had deepened through years of shared life and waiting. At this pinnacle of fatherly love, the test arrived. Abraham said, “O my dear son! I have seen in a dream that I must sacrifice you.

” The phrase “my dear son” (yā bunayya) is an expression of the utmost tenderness in Arabic. Abraham does not hide the command but shares it gently, showing the transparency and trust between them. The dream of a prophet is a form of revelation; it is a command from Allah, not a random thought. Abraham knew that the dream was true and must be acted upon, yet he asked, “So tell me what you think.” This was not a request for permission, as the command was divine and binding, but an inquiry to prepare his son’s heart and to test his willingness. It also demonstrates the Islamic principle of shura (consultation) even in the most difficult matters. The son’s reply is nothing short of heroic: “O my dear father! Do as you are commanded. Allah willing, you will find me steadfast.” The son mirrored his father’s tenderness and added absolute submission.

The phrase “do as you are commanded” shows that he recognized the dream as a divine order. Then he added, “Allah willing, you will find me steadfast,” invoking Allah’s name and promising patience.

This is the hallmark of the forbearing son: immediate acceptance, trust in Allah, and a firm resolve not to waver. The entire exchange is a masterclass in faith, parenting, and communication. There is no argument, no panic, only serene submission. The believer learns that true righteousness in offspring means raising them to love Allah above all else, so that even the most daunting command is met with “do as you are commanded.” This test prefigured the tests the Meccan Muslims would face, and it remains a timeless model of sacrifice and family unity in obedience to the Creator.

=103=

The verse describes the actual execution of the command, capturing the intense emotional and physical moment. “Then when they submitted to Allah’s Will,” meaning both father and son had fully surrendered their wills to the divine decree.

The word “submitted” (aslamā) is from the same root as Islam, indicating that this act was the very essence of Islam: complete and peaceful submission. There was no internal resistance left. “And Abraham laid him on the side of his forehead for sacrifice.” Abraham placed his beloved son down, positioning him so that the forehead—the most noble part of the face—was on the ground, perhaps to avoid seeing his son’s eyes during the act or as a gesture of complete prostration. The forehead touching the earth symbolized the son’s humility before Allah even at the moment of sacrifice. This scene is incredibly poignant. Abraham, the affectionate father, had sharpened the knife, and the son lay still. The submission was not a theoretical belief but a physical act carried to its final step. Everything was in place for the slaughter. This demonstrates that faith is not merely sentiment but action. Both of them passed the test internally and externally. The early Muslims, some of whom were tortured or killed for their faith, could find in this image the ultimate example of holding nothing back for Allah.

The verse also refutes any notion that the command was symbolic only; the physical action was required to prove their sincerity. It teaches that when Allah commands, the believer does not hesitate even when the command seems to contradict human affection or logic. The son’s willingness to be laid down and Abraham’s willingness to perform the act highlight the unique relationship they had with their Lord. The forehead placed on the ground was a silent testimony that the greatest love is for the One who gave life.

=104=

At the very precipice of the sacrifice, divine intervention arrived. “We called out to him, ‘O Abraham!’” The call came directly from Allah, either through a voice or through the angel Gabriel, halting the act at its final moment. The fact that Allah called out by name—“O Abraham!”—is a mark of intimacy and honour. It is a personal address that conveyed immediate comfort and recognition. Abraham, knife in hand, fully intent on completing the command, heard his Lord’s voice calling him. The call came after the submission was proven, not before. The test was not to actually shed the son’s blood but to demonstrate the willingness to do so for Allah’s sake. Once that willingness was fully actualized, the objective was achieved. This teaches that Allah does not burden a soul beyond its capacity, and that the culmination of a test is often immediate relief and divine nearness. The call “O Abraham!” resonated through the valley, transforming a scene of potential tragedy into one of triumph and divine friendship.

The use of the intimate address also echoes the earlier relationship between Allah and Abraham, the khalīl (friend) of Allah. The believers in Mecca, who were being called to sacrifice their tribal ties and worldly attachments for Islam, would take immense solace from this moment. The same Lord who stopped the knife for Abraham sees their struggles and will provide relief in due time. The call reminds every believer that Allah is fully aware of the difficulty of the test and that He is closer than one’s jugular vein. The moment of greatest trial is often the moment just before the greatest relief.

=105=

The divine call continued with words of confirmation and release. “You have already fulfilled the vision.

” This statement is profoundly merciful. Allah declared that the purpose of the dream had been accomplished without the actual death of the son. Abraham had translated the vision into action to the fullest extent of his human ability. He did not hesitate or hold back; thus, in the sight of Allah, the vision was fulfilled.

The word “vision” (ruʾyā) reaffirms that it was a prophetic dream, a true form of revelation. The fulfillment was in the sincere effort and complete submission, not in the spilling of blood. This established a principle in Islamic theology: Allah’s commands are achieved by the sincerity of the heart and the effort of the limbs, and He may, out of His mercy, substitute the original requirement once the test is passed.

The phrase also removes any doubt that Abraham might have failed or that the command was somehow invalid. The reassurance “you have already fulfilled” soothed Abraham’s heart, which was undoubtedly aching at the thought of harming his beloved son. The test was graded and passed with distinction.

For the believer, this is a source of immense hope. The struggles we go through for the sake of Allah, the sacrifices we make, are seen and accepted even if the outcome is different from what we expected. The vision was fulfilled because the essence of worship—unconditional obedience—was perfected. This verse is a capstone on the lesson of the sacrifice, teaching that the ultimate aim of all divine commands is to purify the soul and deepen its connection to the Commander, and that aim was brilliantly achieved on that hillside.

=106=

After affirming the fulfillment, the Quran comments on the nature of the event. “Indeed, that was truly a revealing test.

” The word “test” (balāʾ) indicates a trial that distinguishes the true from the false, the sincere from the pretender. The test was “revealing” (mubīn) because it made manifest the extraordinary degree of faith possessed by Abraham and Ishmael. It laid bare their hearts for all of history to witness. No one could doubt their total devotion after this. The test was not a secret; it became a public legacy through the Quran, revealing the highest level of human submission.

The word “truly” emphasizes the magnitude of the trial. There is no test more severe for a father than to sacrifice his own beloved son with his own hands. By passing it, Abraham demonstrated a love for Allah that superseded all natural loves. This test also revealed Allah’s mercy, as He did not desire the child’s death but the father’s heart. The Meccan pagans, who claimed to honour Abraham, are being reminded that the real Abraham was not an idolater but a man whose faith was proven through the ultimate trial. They are challenged to measure their own devotion against his.

For the believer, this verse frames trials in a new light: they are opportunities for the true nature of one’s faith to be revealed to oneself and to others. The test was not a punishment but a gift that elevated Abraham to the rank of khalīlullah. Every difficulty a believer faces can be a revealing test that, when passed with patience, strengthens the soul and brings one closer to Allah. The verse serves as a conclusion to the sacrifice narrative, setting up the reward and ransom that immediately follow.

=107=

With the test successfully completed, Allah’s mercy manifested as a tangible substitute. “And We ransomed his son with a great sacrifice.” The knife was removed, and in the place of Ishmael, a ram was provided to be slaughtered instead.

The word “ransomed” (fadaynāhu) implies a payment or substitution that secures release. The son’s life was spared, and the blood that was to be shed was now shed through the ram. The “great sacrifice” refers to a magnificent ram, which according to Islamic tradition was a fat, horned ram that had been prepared in Paradise or was one of the best of its kind, sent by Allah to be offered. The greatness of the sacrifice lies not only in the quality of the animal but in the divine origin of the substitute. This event became the foundation of the ritual of udhiyah (sacrifice) on Eid al-Adha, where Muslims worldwide slaughter an animal in commemoration of Abraham’s obedience and Allah’s mercy. The ransom demonstrated that Allah does not desire human suffering but rather the submission of the heart. The son was a gift, and the ram was a gift; both were from Allah. The Meccan pagans who offered sacrifices to idols are shown that the true legacy of sacrifice is in the worship of the One God, following the footsteps of their forefather Abraham.

For the believer, the ritual sacrifice is a yearly reminder of this event and an opportunity to revive the spirit of submission. The verse also carries a deeper spiritual meaning: when we sacrifice something beloved for Allah’s sake, He replaces it with something better. The ram was a “great sacrifice” because it was a symbol of divine acceptance and a means of drawing closer to Allah. The lesson is that nothing we give up for Allah is ever lost; a greater compensation awaits.

=108=

As with Noah before him, Abraham was granted an eternal legacy of praise. “And blessed Abraham with honourable mention among later generations.

” The word “blessed” (taraknā) implies that Allah left upon him a lasting, beautiful remembrance. Abraham is revered by Jews, Christians, and Muslims as the father of prophets and the friend of Allah. His name is mentioned with honour in scripture, in prayer, and in the daily speech of billions.

The phrase “honourable mention” (lisāna ṣidq) is the same used for Noah, indicating a truthful and praiseworthy reputation. This blessing is a direct answer to Abraham’s earlier prayer for a righteous legacy. Not only was he given righteous offspring, but his name itself became synonymous with faith, hospitality, and submission. The Meccan pagans who boasted of their descent from Abraham are being told that true connection to Abraham is not through bloodline alone but through following his pure monotheistic faith. The honourable mention was for his tawheed and his sacrifice, not for idol worship. For the believers, this verse shows that if one sincerely serves Allah, He will take care of their reputation in ways they never imagined. Abraham was a lone stranger in a land of idolaters; now he is beloved by the whole world.

This is a consolation for those who feel isolated or marginalized for their faith. The “later generations” include all the prophets who came after him, all the believers, and even we who recite the Quran today are part of that honourable mention. The verse builds a bridge between the stories of the prophets, showing that each of them shares in this eternal blessing, and the phrase will be repeated for others in the surah.

=109=

The honourable mention is concretized in the divine salutation: “Peace be upon Abraham.” This greeting is the highest honour. “Peace” (salām) comes directly from Allah and is also pronounced by the angels and the believers. It is a prayer for safety, mercy, and blessings. When Allah sends peace upon a servant, that servant is secured from all harm, his heart is at rest, and his station is elevated. This salutation is part of the enduring legacy: every time a believer recites the Quran or sends blessings upon the prophets, they say “peace be upon Abraham.” It is a timeless connection of love and respect.

The verse also echoes the greeting the people of Paradise will receive, as mentioned earlier in the surah. Abraham, the friend of Allah, is already in that state of divine peace. The Meccan pagans, who might have thought that their idols could bring them peace, are shown that true peace is only with the prophets and their way.

For the believer, sending peace upon Abraham is an act of worship and a means of drawing closer to Allah. It reminds us of the brotherhood of prophets and the single chain of tawheed. This verse is a beautiful pause in the narrative, allowing the listener to internalize the greatness of Abraham and to join in the salutation. The peace that Abraham earned through his immense trials is a peace that no worldly disturbance can shake. It is the peace of a heart that has fully submitted and been accepted. Believers long for a share of that peace in this life and the next.

=110=

The refrain appears again, linking Abraham’s reward to a universal principle. “This is how We reward the good-doers.” The deliverance from the fire, the gift of righteous offspring, the ransom from the sacrifice, the honourable mention, and the eternal peace—all of this is the standard reward for those who achieve the rank of iḥsān, spiritual excellence. Abraham was a muḥsin, one who worshipped Allah as if seeing Him, and his reward is a template for all who follow.

The repetition of this refrain after each prophet in the surah drives home the consistency of Allah’s justice and mercy. It assures the believers that their good deeds are never lost, and the pattern of divine generosity is unchanging. The Meccan Muslims, who were being persecuted and had little worldly reward, are reminded that the real reward comes in forms that far surpass material wealth. The refrain also connects Abraham back to Noah, and forward to the prophets yet to be mentioned, weaving a golden thread through the tapestry of prophethood. For the contemporary believer, this verse is a powerful motivational tool: strive to be a good-doer, and the same Lord who rewarded Abraham will reward you in ways appropriate to your own test and context. The “good-doers” are not necessarily those who achieve great fame, but those who perfect their faith and action, wherever they are.

=111=

As with Noah, the final commendation of Abraham is that he was a faithful servant. “He was truly one of Our faithful servants.

” The phrase “faithful servants” (ʿibādinā al-muʾminīn) combines complete servitude with deep, unwavering belief. Abraham was not only a believer in heart but a servant in every action. His entire life was a testament to this dual quality.

The word “truly” emphasizes the certainty and depth of this description. This is the pinnacle of human achievement: to be recognized by Allah as a faithful servant. It is not a title one can claim for oneself; it is a divine attestation. Abraham’s faithfulness was proven through the fire, the exile, the sacrifice, and the endless hospitality. He never wavered. For the believers, this verse is an invitation to examine their own faith and servitude. Are we truly faithful servants, or do we serve our desires and the pressures of society more than we serve Allah? The legacy of Abraham calls us to a life of complete dedication.

The verse also serves as a bridge to the mention of Isaac and the continuation of Abraham’s lineage, showing that his faithfulness bore fruit in a line of prophets and righteous people. The highest honour is not in wealth or power but in being inscribed in the register of Allah’s faithful servants.

=112=

After the narrative of the sacrifice, the Quran mentions the second major son of Abraham. “We later gave him good news of Isaac—a prophet, and one of the righteous.

” The word “later” indicates that the glad tidings of Isaac came after the events of the sacrifice and after Ishmael’s birth. This was a further blessing. Abraham, now very old, and his wife Sarah, who was also advanced in age, were given the good news of another son, Isaac. The good news was delivered by the same angels who were on their way to destroy the people of Lot. The verse describes Isaac with two magnificent qualities: “a prophet” and “one of the righteous.” This shows that Isaac was not only a prophet himself but was also deeply rooted in personal piety. His prophethood was a fulfillment of Allah’s promise to Abraham that his progeny would include prophets and righteous leaders.

The mention of Isaac here also sets the record straight for the Jewish and Christian communities who trace their spiritual lineage through Isaac. The Quran honours Isaac as a noble prophet and does not diminish his status, while also affirming Ishmael’s honoured place. The “good news” of a prophet son is a tremendous gift, showing that Abraham’s legacy would be carried on through multiple channels.

For the believer, this verse demonstrates that Allah often grants more than what we initially ask or imagine. Abraham had prayed for righteous offspring; he was blessed with Ishmael and then Isaac, and through them, countless prophets. It also teaches that the righteous are an interconnected family, and the greatest joy for a believing parent is to see their children walking the path of prophethood and righteousness, even if they themselves are not prophets.

=113=

The verse summarizes the legacy of Abraham and Isaac. “We blessed him and Isaac as well.” The blessing (baraknā) encompassed prophethood, righteous progeny, scripture, and lasting honour. The “him” refers to Abraham, and Isaac is specifically named again, emphasizing that both father and son were jointly blessed. Then the verse states a profound principle of justice and individual responsibility: “Some of their descendants did good, while others clearly wronged themselves.” This indicates that being from the lineage of prophets does not automatically guarantee righteousness. Among the descendants of Abraham through Isaac and Ishmael, there were those who did good—the prophets and the sincere believers—and those who clearly wronged themselves through disbelief, idolatry, and sins. This was a direct message to the Meccan pagans who claimed Abraham as their ancestor and assumed that this lineage would save them. The Quran tells them that Abraham’s true legacy is followed only by those who do good, while those who deviate and do wrong are accountable for their own actions, regardless of their ancestry.

The phrase “clearly wronged themselves” indicates that the evidence was manifest, and they had no excuse. It also applied to the Jews and Christians who deviated from the original teachings of their prophets.

For the believer, this verse severs any reliance on family background or saintly lineage as a means of salvation. Each soul is responsible for its own deeds. The blessing of being born into a Muslim family or a righteous community is a favour, but it must be matched with personal faith and good actions.

The verse also highlights the justice of Allah: He does not punish the descendants for the sins of their forefathers, nor does He reward them merely for ancestral piety. Everyone stands on their own record.

=114=

The surah now moves to the next set of chosen servants. “And We certainly showed favour to Moses and Aaron.

” The word “certainly” emphasizes the abundance and certainty of the favours bestowed upon them. Moses (Mūsā) and his brother Aaron (Hārūn) were jointly granted prophethood, divine support, and a tremendous mission to confront Pharaoh and liberate the Children of Israel. The “favour” includes the revelation of the Torah, the miracles of the staff and the shining hand, the parting of the sea, the destruction of their enemy, and the guidance provided to their people. Mentioning them together highlights their partnership in prophethood, as Moses asked Allah to appoint Aaron as his helper and Allah granted that request. This demonstrates the beauty of brotherly cooperation in the path of Allah. The favour shown to them was not because of their personal merits alone but was purely from Allah’s grace. The Meccan pagans were aware of the story of Moses and Pharaoh from the Jews and Christians; the Quran presents it concisely, focusing on the pattern of deliverance. For the believers, this verse is a reminder that Allah’s favour upon His prophets is immense and that those who follow their guidance share in that favour.

The mention of Moses and Aaron leads into a series of brief vignettes that highlight different aspects of divine assistance and honour, consistent with the refrain that follows. The believers who were a small, oppressed minority in Mecca could see in Moses and Aaron’s struggle against the mighty Pharaoh a parallel to their own struggle against the Quraysh, and find hope that Allah’s favour would eventually grant them victory and deliverance.

=115=

The specific favour is now detailed. “And delivered them and their people from the great distress.” The “great distress” refers to the long period of enslavement, humiliation, and slaughter that the Children of Israel suffered under Pharaoh. The baby boys were being killed, and the people were forced into hard labour. This distress was both physical and psychological. Allah’s deliverance came through the mission of Moses and Aaron, culminating in the Exodus: the parting of the Red Sea, the crossing, and the drowning of Pharaoh’s army. The delivery was not just from an abstract difficulty but from a life of utter degradation to a life of dignity and freedom to worship Allah.

The phrase “their people” shows that the deliverance was collective; Moses and Aaron were saved along with the believers among their nation. This mirrors the deliverance of Noah and his family from the flood and connects to the overall theme of the surah: the chosen servants are saved, while the wrongdoers are destroyed. The “great distress” can also be seen as a type of all the worldly and spiritual trials that afflict believers, with Allah’s deliverance always near. For the Meccan Muslims, suffering under the persecution of the Quraysh, this verse whispered the promise of a coming deliverance.

The word “We delivered them” underscores that the source of salvation is Allah alone; the prophets and their strategies were only means. This gives believers confidence that no matter how powerful the oppressor seems, the One who drowned Pharaoh can deliver His servants from any distress.

=116=

The deliverance was followed by a decisive victory and superiority. “We helped them so it was they who prevailed.” The help from Allah came in multiple forms: the miracles that confounded Pharaoh’s magicians, the plagues that struck Egypt, the safe passage through the sea, and the destruction of the pursuing army. The result was that Moses, Aaron, and the Children of Israel became the victors, while Pharaoh and his hosts became a lesson for the worlds.

The word “prevailed” (gālibīn) indicates they were the dominant, triumphant party. They were no longer slaves but a free nation under divine guidance. This victory was a manifestation of the earlier promise in the surah that the forces of Allah will certainly prevail.

The verse teaches that the believers’ path, though fraught with trials, ends in ultimate success, whether in this world or the Hereafter. The Meccan Muslims, who were at that moment a weak and persecuted group, received a clear promise that they too would be helped and would prevail.

This promise was later fulfilled in the conquest of Mecca and the spread of Islam. For the individual believer, this verse is a reminder that any struggle for the sake of Allah comes with divine support. The conditions for this help are faith, patience, and taqwa. Moses and Aaron did not prevail by their own numbers or weapons; they prevailed because Allah helped them.

Thus, the believer should always seek Allah’s help and trust in His plan, rather than despairing at the apparent power of falsehood.

=117=

After physical deliverance and victory, Allah granted Moses and Aaron a spiritual treasure. “We gave them the clear Scripture.” The “clear Scripture” is the Torah, the book of guidance, law, and light that was revealed to Moses.

The word “clear” (mustabīn) means that the Scripture was self-evident in its truth, detailed in its instructions, and unambiguous in its core message. It clearly distinguished between right and wrong, lawful and unlawful. This gift was the culmination of their mission; liberation from Pharaoh was not just for political freedom but for the establishment of a community guided by divine law. The Scripture was the constitution of the new nation, providing them with a comprehensive way of life. The verse connects to the earlier mention of angels reciting the Reminder; the Torah was part of that chain of divine reminders. For the Prophet Muhammad in Mecca, this was a reminder that his own gift of the Quran was following the same pattern: after trials and persecution, a clear Scripture is given to guide humanity. The believers were longing for the complete revelation and the formation of a community under its guidance; this verse showed that their situation had a precedent. For us, the clear Scripture is the Quran, and we must treat it with the same reverence and obedience that Moses’ people were expected to show the Torah. The clarity of the Scripture is a mercy that leaves no excuse for ignorance. The believer should immerse themselves in it to find light in every distress.

=118=

The gift of the Scripture was accompanied by active divine guidance. “And guided them to the Straight Path.” The “Straight Path” (al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm) is the path of pure monotheism, righteous deeds, and the way of life that leads to Allah’s pleasure. The guidance was both in the form of the revealed text and in the prophetic example of Moses and Aaron. Allah did not just hand them a book and leave them; He guided their understanding and their steps. The Straight Path is the one that avoids the extremes of excess and deficiency, the path of moderation and truth. This verse is directly connected to Surah Al-Fatiha, where believers daily ask to be guided to the Straight Path. Moses and Aaron and their followers were shown that path clearly. The “them” includes all who believed with them, not just the prophets. The guidance was a continuous process throughout their desert journey. For the Meccan believers, this verse was a reassurance that the Prophet Muhammad was also guiding them to the Straight Path, which would lead them out of the darkness of idolatry into the light of tawheed. The lesson for us is that possessing a Scripture is not enough; we need Allah’s ongoing guidance to understand and implement it correctly. We must constantly seek that guidance through prayer, reflection, and companionship with the righteous. The Straight Path is the path of all the prophets, and the sequence in the surah shows that Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Aaron all walked and guided to this same path.

=119=

The refrain that appeared for Noah and Abraham now appears for Moses and Aaron. “And We blessed them with honourable mention among later generations.” The pair of prophets are granted a lasting, truthful, and praiseworthy reputation that extends throughout history. Jews, Christians, and Muslims all honour Moses and Aaron as great prophets. Muslims in particular are required to believe in them and speak of them with respect.

The phrase “honourable mention” (lisāna ṣidq) is identical to what was granted to their predecessors. This consistency reinforces the unity of the prophetic mission and the equality of their core message. It also serves as a motivation for believers: a life spent in sincere service to Allah results in a legacy that outlives earthly existence. The “later generations” include us today. When we recite this verse or speak of Moses and Aaron with reverence, we are part of the fulfillment of this divine blessing. The Meccan pagans, who mocked the Prophet and attempted to erase his message, are being warned that they cannot erase the honourable mention Allah bestows. The believers who are unknown and oppressed can take heart that Allah can make their names honoured in ways they cannot foresee.

The verse also subtly refutes the negative propaganda that Pharaoh’s regime must have spread about Moses; Pharaoh’s name is cursed, while Moses’ name is blessed. Truth always prevails in the long term.

=120=

The honourable mention crystallizes into the divine salutation: “Peace be upon Moses and Aaron.” As with Noah and Abraham, the peace of Allah descends upon these two faithful servants. The peace is eternal and unshakeable. It encompasses their souls, their memory, and their station. When a believer hears this, they are encouraged to echo the salutation in their hearts and upon their tongues.

The mention of peace is particularly poignant for Moses and Aaron, who faced a life of struggle, defiance from their own people, and the immense burden of leadership. Now, after their passing, they are in a state of perfect peace, far removed from the turmoil of the world. This salutation is also a refutation of any claims that might belittle them; Allah Himself sends peace upon them, so no believer can harbour anything but love and respect for them. The early Muslims, living in a hostile environment, would find in this verse a reminder that the peace of Allah is the ultimate goal, far more valuable than the fleeting peace offered by compromise with falsehood. The salutation also links the prophets together in a chain of peace, which will be extended at the end of the surah to all messengers. This consistent structure in the surah creates a rhythm that embeds the lesson deeply: the chosen servants are those who receive divine peace, and we must strive to be among those upon whom the angels descend with peace.
