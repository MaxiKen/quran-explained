=121=

The refrain appears yet again, this time concluding the account of Moses and Aaron. The verse states, “Indeed, this is how We reward the good-doers.

” The word “Indeed” imparts absolute certainty, leaving no room for doubt that the magnificent treatment granted to Moses and Aaron—the deliverance from great distress, the help that made them prevail, the gift of the clear Scripture, the guidance to the Straight Path, the honourable mention among later generations, and the divine salutation of peace—is not an isolated occurrence. It is the established, unchanging pattern of divine recompense.

The phrase “this is how” draws attention to the specific way Allah deals with those who achieve the rank of iḥsān. The good-doers are those who perfect their worship and their character, worshipping Allah as though they see Him, and who combine deep faith with meticulous action. Moses and Aaron exemplified this through their tireless struggle against Pharaoh, their patient leadership of the Israelites, and their profound reliance upon Allah in the face of immense hardship. When Moses stood before the Red Sea with Pharaoh’s army closing in, his iḥsān was manifested in his words, “No, my Lord is with me, He will guide me. ” When Aaron managed the rebellious Israelites, he demonstrated forbearance and commitment to unity. The reward was comprehensive: they were saved, empowered, given divine law, and eternally honoured. For the Meccan believers, this refrain was a powerful reassurance that their own struggles, sacrifices, and good deeds, however small they seemed, would be rewarded in the same magnificent pattern. It taught them to look beyond immediate circumstances and trust in the consistent, generous justice of Allah. For the contemporary believer, this verse serves as both motivation and comfort. Every act of kindness, every prayer performed with concentration, every temptation resisted, and every patience shown in adversity is seen by the same Lord who rewarded Moses and Aaron. The reward may come in this world as victory, honour, or peace of heart, and it will certainly come in the Hereafter in the Gardens of Bliss.

The verse also warns that the opposite pattern holds true for the wicked, making it a call to strive for iḥsān in all aspects of life, so that one may be counted among those whose reward is divine friendship and eternal peace.

=122=

The final seal on the story of Moses and Aaron is this divine testimony: “They were truly two of Our faithful servants.” The emphasis on “truly” (innahumā) affirms the depth and authenticity of their servitude.

This is the highest praise a human can receive—to be described by the Creator as a faithful servant. The phrase combines two essential dimensions: servitude (ʿibādah) and faith (īmān). They were not only believers in their hearts but also servants in every action and moment of their lives. Their will was fully aligned with Allah’s will.

The word “two” acknowledges their unique partnership in prophethood. Moses had asked Allah to make his brother Aaron a helper and a sharer in his mission, and Allah granted that, elevating both together. This shows the beauty of brotherly cooperation in the path of truth, and how Allah honours those who support one another in righteousness. Being a faithful servant meant that Moses did not rely on his own strength when confronting Pharaoh; he turned entirely to Allah. Aaron, likewise, was a steadfast supporter who did not seek personal glory. Their faithfulness was tested in the crucible of oppression, in the wilderness, and in dealing with a stubborn people, yet they remained unwavering. For the believers in Mecca, this testimony provided a powerful lens through which to view their own situation. The Quraysh mocked them as abandoning the ways of their ancestors, but they were walking the path of Moses and Aaron, the faithful servants. True honour is not in tribal lineage but in this divine attestation.

The verse also connects to the series of prophets mentioned in the surah: Noah, Abraham, and later Elias, Lot, and Jonah all receive similar commendations. This creates a golden thread of faithfulness across history. The believer aspires to this title—to be counted among the faithful servants of Allah, those whose inner faith and outer actions are perfectly harmonized, and whose ultimate reward is the divine peace and honourable mention that never fade.

=123=

The surah now shifts to another honoured prophet, introducing him with the same formula. “And Elias was indeed one of the messengers.” The “indeed” (wa-inna) serves to confirm the prophethood of Elias, known in Arabic as Ilyās, who is identified with the biblical Elijah. The Meccan pagans might have heard of Elias from the Jews and Christians, but they did not necessarily honour him as they should. The Quran here places him firmly within the chain of divine messengers, emphasizing that his message was the same core truth delivered by all prophets: the call to tawheed. Elias was sent to a people who had deviated into idol worship, specifically to the worship of an idol called Baʿl. The historical context, as understood from various traditions, places him in the region of Baalbek or among the Israelites after the time of Solomon. The people had become prosperous but had abandoned sincere worship of Allah, turning to a fertility god and indulging in moral corruption. Elias stood as a lone voice calling them back.

The phrase “one of the messengers” connects him directly to Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Aaron, showing that he was not a reformer with a new philosophy but a link in the unbroken chain of divine guidance. For the Prophet Muhammad in Mecca, this mention was a source of comfort; he was following in the footsteps of these noble messengers who all faced similar rejection.

The verse also subtly refutes those who might deny Elias’s prophethood or reduce him to a mere storyteller figure. Allah Himself testifies to his station.

For the believer, every prophet mentioned in the Quran is a role model, and Elias’s story, though briefly told, holds profound lessons about the courage required to confront a society deeply entrenched in false worship and the patience needed when the truth is rejected.

=124=

The narrative begins with the core of Elias’s message, placed in a rhetorical question full of reproach and love. “Remember when he said to his people, ‘Will you not fear Allah?’” The command “Remember” invites the Prophet and the believers to reflect on Elias’s approach. He began with the call to taqwā, the fear and consciousness of Allah.

This is the foundation of all prophetic missions: if a person truly fears Allah, they will refrain from shirk and sin. The question “Will you not” carries a tone of gentle yet firm admonishment, implying that their lack of fear is astonishing given the clear signs of Allah’s power and mercy around them. Elias was not asking them to fear a distant, indifferent deity; he was asking them to fear the One who created them, sustained them, and would hold them accountable. The fear of Allah is not a cowering terror but a reverent awe that inspires obedience and love.

In the context of his people who had turned to Baʿl, this fear had been replaced by a careless attachment to a false god that could neither benefit nor harm. Elias’s question also highlights that the root problem of his people was spiritual heedlessness. They had lost the sense of accountability, so they worshipped a lifeless idol. The Meccan pagans were in exactly the same state, and the Prophet Muhammad similarly asked them, “Will you not fear Allah? ” For the believer, this verse is a timeless reminder that the first step to reform is to awaken the consciousness of Allah in the heart. We must constantly ask ourselves: do we truly fear Allah in our private and public lives? That fear is what guards against sin and motivates towards good. Elias’s simple yet profound question remains as relevant as ever.

=125=

Elias now makes the contrast explicit, exposing the absurdity of their worship. “Do you call upon the idol of Ba’l and abandon the Best of Creators?

” The phrase “call upon” (tadʿūna) means to invoke, to worship, and to seek help from. Baʿl was a well-known idol of the ancient Near East, often associated with storms, fertility, and the sun. The people of Elias had dedicated temples and rituals to this lifeless object, praying to it for rain, children, and protection. Elias’s question is designed to shock them into reflection. They were leaving the worship of Allah, the Best of Creators—the One who perfected everything He made, who created the heavens and the earth with infinite wisdom, and who fashioned human beings in the finest form—for a piece of stone or wood that their own hands had carved. The title “Best of Creators” (Aḥsan al-Khāliqīn) is a powerful emphasis on Allah’s unmatched creative perfection. All other so-called creators—whether human artisans who carve idols or the false deities themselves—are utterly inferior.

The word “abandon” (tadharūna) implies a wilful forsaking of something vastly superior for something worthless. The verse cuts deep into the psychology of idolatry: it is a trade of the real for the illusory, of the Benefactor for the beneficiary. For the Meccan pagans who called upon Hubal, Lāt, and ʿUzzā while abandoning the Lord of the Kaaba, this was a direct and humbling challenge.

The verse teaches the believer to evaluate all attachments and priorities. Do we, in any way, abandon the Best of Creators—His commands, His pleasure—for the sake of modern idols like wealth, status, or base desires? The rhetorical question frames this choice as utterly irrational, inviting the listener to immediately recoil from such a foolish exchange and return to the worship of the One who alone deserves it.

=126=

Elias immediately answers the implied question, identifying the Best of Creators. “Allah, your Lord and the Lord of your forefathers.” The verse uses the name “Allah,” the personal name of the one true God, combining it with the concept of Lordship.

The word “Lord” (Rabb) signifies the Creator, Sustainer, Owner, and Ruler. By saying “your Lord,” Elias reminds them that Allah is not a distant, foreign deity; He is their own Lord who has been caring for them from birth. Then he adds “and the Lord of your forefathers.

” This is a strategic and profound addition because the pagans’ primary justification for idol worship was that they were following the religion of their ancestors. Elias dismantles that argument by stating that Allah, not Baʿl, was the Lord of their forefathers. True ancestors, the ones who were rightly guided, worshipped Allah alone. If the forefathers had later fallen into idolatry, that was a deviation, not a tradition to be proud of. The chain of true tradition leads back to monotheism. The Meccan pagans made the same claim, saying they found their fathers upon a religion and were following their footsteps. The Quran, through Elias’s words, refutes this: Allah is the Lord of the forefathers, and they should have worshipped Him. This verse thus establishes that ancestral custom is no excuse for shirk when it contradicts divine Lordship. The believer learns to honour the true heritage of tawheed passed down through the prophets and to evaluate cultural traditions in light of this ultimate truth. The Lord of the forefathers is the same Lord of the later generations, and His worship is the only constant that unites the righteous across time. This unbroken continuity is the foundation of the Muslim ummah’s identity.

=127=

Despite the clear and compassionate call of Elias, the response of his people was rejection. “But they rejected him, so they will certainly be brought for punishment.

” The Arabic word for “rejected” (kadhdhabū) implies that they called him a liar and denied the truth of his message. They not only refused to abandon Baʿl but actively opposed Elias, likely mocking him and threatening him. The consequence is stated with certainty: “they will certainly be brought.” The verb “brought” (muḥḍarūn) means they will be forcibly presented, herded like prisoners to the place of reckoning and punishment. They will not be able to escape or hide. This applies to the punishment in the Hereafter, and according to many scholars, it also refers to a worldly punishment that befell them, such as a severe drought and then destruction. The verse reflects the unchanging divine law: when a people persistently reject the messenger sent to them after clear warning, punishment descends. This law was seen with Noah’s people, Pharaoh, and the people of Lot, and the Meccan pagans are being warned that the same law applies to them.

The phrase “will certainly be brought” also links to the earlier scenes in the surah where the angels are commanded to gather and lead the wrongdoers to the path of Hell. There is a terrifying continuity between the rejection in this world and the humiliation in the next.

For the believer, this verse is a stark reminder of the seriousness of denying the truth once it has been made clear. It also provides solace: the prophets are not defeated when their people reject them; the victory belongs ultimately to the truth, and the rejecters will face a sure and inevitable reckoning. The faithful servants, however, are exempted, as the next verse reminds.

=128=

As has become the beautiful rhythm of this surah, the announcement of punishment is immediately followed by the exception. “But not the chosen servants of Allah.” Among the people of Elias, not everyone rejected him. There was a group of sincere believers who responded to his call, purified their worship for Allah alone, and held fast to the truth despite the hostile environment. These were the chosen servants (al-mukhlaṣīn), those whom Allah had purified, guided, and selected for His mercy. They were saved from the punishment that overtook their people.

The word “chosen” implies both divine election and personal sincerity. They were sincere in their devotion, so Allah chose them. This exception provides a crucial balance to the narrative: it shows that Allah’s justice does not sweep away the innocent with the guilty. Even in the most corrupt society, the righteous remnant is preserved. The believers in Mecca, small in number and surrounded by idolaters, were exactly such a chosen remnant. This verse was a direct reassurance that they would be saved while their persecutors would be brought to punishment. It also teaches that being a chosen servant is not a passive state but is achieved through sincere worship and steadfastness in the face of rejection. The refrain also connects Elias’s story to the overarching theme of the surah: the ultimate triumph of the chosen servants of Allah, whether through worldly deliverance or through honourable mention and peace after their passing. The exception serves as a call to every listener to strive to be among these chosen ones, by purifying their faith and clinging to the rope of Allah, so that when the day of distinction arrives, they stand among the saved.

=129=

The reward for Elias’s steadfastness, like that of the prophets before him, was not limited to his own lifetime but extended across history. “We blessed him with honourable mention among later generations.

” The phrase “blessed him” (taraknā ʿalayhi) means that Allah left upon him a lasting, beautiful legacy. The “honourable mention” (lisāna ṣidq) is a tongue of truth and praise, meaning that future generations speak of Elias with reverence, respect, and love. His name is recorded in the Quran, recited in prayers, and honoured by millions of believers.

This is a direct compensation for the rejection and slander he faced from his own people. They may have called him a liar or a troublemaker, but Allah ensured that his true reputation would be one of honour until the Day of Judgment. The “later generations” include the Israelites, the Christians who revere Elijah, and the Muslim ummah, which is required to believe in all prophets and speak of them with respect. This verse underscores a profound principle: a believer’s true reputation is not in the fleeting opinions of contemporaries but in the enduring judgment of Allah. Many prophets were dismissed as mad or dangerous in their time, yet their names are now blessed, while the names of their arrogant opponents are cursed or forgotten. The Meccan pagans were trying to tarnish the Prophet Muhammad’s name, calling him a mad poet, but this verse reassured him that Allah would similarly bless him with honourable mention. Today, the adhan echoes around the world with Muhammad’s name, fulfilling that promise.

For the believer, this inspires a detachment from seeking human approval and a focus on earning Allah’s lasting praise. The righteous legacy is not material wealth or power but a name that is remembered with good by the community of faith.

=130=

The honourable mention is crystallized into the divine salutation: “Peace be upon Elias.” This is the same greeting of peace that was sent upon Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Aaron.

The word “Peace” (Salām) is a comprehensive prayer for safety, security, mercy, and blessings. When Allah sends peace upon a servant, it means that servant is in the abode of peace, free from all harm, fear, and grief. Elias, who faced the enmity of his people and the hardships of daʿwah, now dwells in a state of perfect, eternal peace. This salutation is not merely a historical note; it is a living reality. The angels greet him with peace, and the believers are taught to repeat this salutation when they read or hear his name. The verse invites the heart of the listener to join in this divine greeting, fostering a personal connection of love and respect for the prophet Elias.

In the context of the surah, the repeated salutation upon each prophet builds a powerful sense of the brotherhood of messengers and the single, harmonious path they all walked. The peace upon Elias is a testament to his success; the mission that seemed so difficult in the world has resulted in eternal tranquility. For the Meccan believers, this was a reminder that the peace they were denied in their city by the persecutors would be granted to them by the Lord of the worlds, just as it was granted to Elias.

The verse also teaches the believer to value the peace that comes from Allah above all worldly peace. The peace of a clear conscience, the peace of faith, and the ultimate peace in Paradise are the true goals. By saying “Peace be upon Elias,” we affirm our place in the long line of believers who honour and love all the prophets.

=131=

The refrain repeats, anchoring Elias’s story in the universal divine principle. “Indeed, this is how We reward the good-doers.” All the honours bestowed upon Elias—the prophetic mission, the deliverance of the chosen servants, the honourable mention among later generations, and the eternal peace—are presented as the standard reward for those who attain the rank of iḥsān, spiritual excellence. Elias was a good-doer because he combined deep, sincere faith with courageous action. He stood alone against a society that had normalized idol worship, calling them to fear Allah with wisdom and compassion. His good-doing was not a passive inner state but an active struggle to establish tawheed. The verse reaffirms that Allah’s reward system is consistent and just; no act of goodness, no matter how small, is overlooked, and no good-doer is ever left without a magnificent recompense. For the believers who heard this in Mecca, the refrain was a constant injection of hope. Their own acts of patience, their secret prayers, their charity, and their endurance of abuse were all part of the same pattern of iḥsān, and the same Lord who rewarded Elias would reward them.

The emphasis on “this is how” draws attention to the specific manner of divine reward: it is comprehensive, covering both this world and the next, and it often includes the transformation of hardship into lasting honour. The verse challenges the listener to elevate their own deeds to the level of iḥsān—to worship Allah as if seeing Him, to perfect their character, and to call others to goodness—so that they may deserve this magnificent reward. It is a call to excellence in all facets of life, rooted in the certainty that the rewarder is the Best of Creators.

=132=

The story of Elias concludes with the highest commendation, identical to that given to the prophets before him. “He was truly one of Our faithful servants.

” The phrase “faithful servants” (ʿibādinā al-muʾminīn) joins complete servitude with unwavering belief. Elias’s entire being was oriented towards Allah; he was a servant in thought, word, and deed, and a believer whose heart was filled with the light of certainty.

The word “truly” (inna) emphasizes the reality and depth of this quality. This is not a title that Elias claimed for himself; it is a testimony from the Lord of the worlds, which makes it the most profound validation possible. Elias’s faithfulness was proven in his willingness to stand against the current, to deliver the difficult message, and to endure the loneliness of rejection without compromising the truth. His servitude meant that his will was entirely merged with Allah’s will; he desired what Allah desired and opposed what Allah opposed.

This verse also subtly refutes any suggestion that Elias was a fiery, impatient figure; the Quranic picture is of a man of deep faith and complete submission, despite his people’s stubbornness. For the believers in Mecca, this description of Elias provided a mirror for their own aspirations. They too were being called to be faithful servants amidst a hostile environment.

The verse teaches that the ultimate identity of a believer is not based on tribe, nation, or social status, but on being a faithful servant of Allah. It is an identity that transcends time and connects the believer to the most noble company: the prophets and the righteous. The subsequent stories of Lot and Jonah will continue this pattern, each adding unique dimensions to the portrait of the faithful servant, whether through steadfastness in a wicked society or through the journey of repentance and return.

=133=

The surah now moves to the next prophet in the series, Lot (Lūṭ). “And Lot was indeed one of the messengers.” The “indeed” again confirms his station emphatically. Lot was the nephew of Abraham, and he accompanied his uncle on the journey to the blessed land. He was later sent as a messenger to the people of Sodom and Gomorrah, cities known for their moral depravity, especially the abominable act of homosexuality that they were the first among humankind to practice openly. The verse places Lot squarely within the brotherhood of messengers, showing that his mission was divinely ordained and his message was the same call to tawheed and moral purity. The Meccan pagans were aware of the story of Lot’s people from the passing caravans and from Jewish and Christian traditions. By mentioning Lot, the Quran highlights a different type of test: one where the corruption was not just idolatry but extreme moral perversion. The faithful servant must confront all forms of deviation. For the Prophet Muhammad in Mecca, this mention provided another model of patience and trust. Lot endured the wickedness of his people for years, offering them guidance and warning them of the consequences. The verse sets the stage for a concise yet powerful summary of his deliverance and the destruction of his people. It reminds the believer that the call to Allah includes the call to moral and social righteousness, and that the faithful servant must remain pure in the most corrupt of environments. Lot’s story, like the others, is a testament to the fact that the chosen servants are saved, while the defiantly wicked are utterly destroyed, leaving behind ruins as a sign for those who travel and reflect.

=134=

The core of Lot’s narrative in this surah focuses on deliverance. “Remember when We delivered him and all of his family.” The command “Remember” invites reflection on this specific act of divine salvation. The deliverance of Lot occurred when the angels, who had first visited Abraham in the form of handsome guests, came to Sodom to execute the punishment. They instructed Lot to leave the city with his family during the night and not to look back.

The phrase “all of his family” indicates that the door of salvation was open to his entire household. Lot’s family, primarily his two believing daughters, was included in this deliverance. This mirrors the earlier pattern of Noah, where the prophet and his family were saved from the great distress. The deliverance was an act of pure mercy, snatching the righteous out of a society that had become utterly corrupt and was about to face a catastrophic punishment.

The word “We delivered” emphasizes that the rescue was entirely by Allah’s power and planning, not by any worldly means. The believers were a tiny minority in a deeply perverse city, yet they were not forgotten. For the Meccan Muslims, this was a direct parallel: they were a small, persecuted community in a city steeped in idolatry and immorality. The verse promised that just as Lot and his family were delivered from the heart of corruption, they too would be delivered. The lesson for every believer is that no matter how dark the environment, Allah’s hand of rescue is always near for those who maintain their faith. Deliverance may come in the form of migration, a change of circumstances, or the ultimate salvation in the Hereafter.

The emphasis on family also highlights the importance of striving to keep one’s household upon faith, so that all may be saved together.

=135=

The comprehensive deliverance had one tragic exception, stated with brevity. “Except an old woman, who was one of the doomed.” The old woman was Lot’s wife, who did not share his faith. Despite living in the household of a prophet and witnessing his righteousness, her heart was with the sinners. She conspired with the people of Sodom, informing them of the visiting guests, and she had no intention of leaving her wicked ways. When the command to depart came, she lingered or looked back, and she was thus caught in the punishment. The description “one of the doomed” (al-ghābirīn) means she was among those who remained behind and were destroyed. This exception is deeply sobering: marriage to a prophet or proximity to righteousness does not guarantee salvation if the individual’s own faith is absent. It illustrates the principle that no soul bears the burden of another, and that familial ties are severed when based on disbelief. The verse serves as a stark warning to the Meccans who claimed that their lineage to Abraham or their association with the Prophet’s tribe would save them. Lot’s wife was of the same household as the messenger, yet she perished. For the believers, this exception reinforces the urgency of personal faith and the danger of being attached to a righteous community outwardly while harbouring disbelief or hypocrisy inwardly.

The mention of her age as “an old woman” perhaps highlights that years of living with a prophet had not softened her heart, making her fate a cautionary tale about the hardening of the heart over time. The verse also magnifies the perfection of Allah’s justice: the deliverance was only for the true believers, and the disobedient were excluded without any favouritism, emphasizing that the title “faithful servant” is earned, not inherited.

=136=

After the deliverance of Lot and his believing daughters, the divine punishment was unleashed. “Then We utterly destroyed the rest.” The “rest” refers to the entire population of Sodom and the surrounding cities that had rejected Lot’s message and persisted in their heinous sins.

The word “utterly” (dammarnā) indicates a thorough, catastrophic annihilation that left nothing standing. The destruction involved the cities being turned upside down and a rain of stones of baked clay pelting them. This was a direct, miraculous punishment that matched the enormity of their crime, which included not only sexual perversion but also highway robbery and open defiance of the prophetic warning. The verse is concise because the surah’s focus is on the pattern and the lesson, not the graphic details, which are elaborated in other surahs. The destruction of the rest is the flip side of deliverance: the chosen servants are saved, and the rejecters are wiped out. This consistent outcome reinforces the gravity of rejecting a messenger. The people of Lot had been warned repeatedly, and when they even tried to assault the angelic visitors, their measure of sin was full. The verse thus shows that divine patience has a limit. For the Meccan pagans, who were also rejecting their messenger and plotting against him, this was a direct threat. They are the “rest” of their time if they do not change. The believer learns from this that the power of Allah to destroy is as real as His power to save, and that one must never feel secure from the divine plan. The ruins of the destroyed cities, as the next verse points out, were left as a visible sign for all who pass by, a permanent reminder etched into the geography of the region.

=137=

The surah now turns the historical narrative into a direct, present-tense address to the Meccan disbelievers. “You Meccans certainly pass by their ruins day.” The Arabs of Quraysh conducted regular trade caravans to Syria, a journey that took them along the route near the Dead Sea, where the ruins of Sodom and Gomorrah were located. The verse tells them that they “certainly pass by” these ruins, making the destruction of Lot’s people not a distant legend but a visible, tangible reality integrated into their travel experience.

The mention of “day” indicates their daytime journeys, when the ruins were plainly visible. The destroyed cities were a silent testimony to the truth of what the Quran was narrating. This direct address is a powerful rhetorical strategy: it brings the warning into the immediate lived reality of the audience. They could not deny the existence of these ruins; their own eyes had seen them. The verse implicitly asks: you see the punishment that befell those who rejected Lot, yet you reject Muhammad and repeat the same sins? The ruins serve as an open-air classroom, but the lesson requires a heart willing to reflect.

For the believer, this teaches that the signs of Allah’s power and justice are all around us in history, archaeology, and the natural world. We should not be like those who travel through life seeing the remnants of destroyed nations without internalizing the message.

The verse also emphasizes the credibility of the Quranic narrative; it appeals to empirical observation that the Meccans could verify. The ruins were a standing proof of the pattern the surah relentlessly highlights: rejection of the warners leads to utter destruction.

=138=

The verse completes the temporal dimension of their passing by the ruins, adding “and night,” meaning they pass by both during the day and during the night—perhaps on their return journeys, or it simply emphasizes that the ruins are always there, impossible to miss. The constant presence of this evidence makes their heedlessness even more blameworthy. Then the verse ends with a piercing question: “Will you not then understand?

” This is an appeal to the intellect (ʿaql). The ruins are not just stones; they are a message. The faculty of understanding should connect the visible effect (the destroyed cities) to its cause (the rejection of the messenger and the commission of vile sins), and then apply that causal chain to one’s own situation.

The question is rhetorical and laden with rebuke. They had minds, but they were not using them to grasp the most crucial truth. The Meccans prided themselves on their intelligence and their poetic wit, yet they failed to make the simplest logical deduction from the archaeological evidence before their eyes.

This verse teaches that true understanding is not merely intellectual accumulation but the ability to derive moral and spiritual lessons from the signs that Allah has placed in the world. For the believer, it is a call to be a person of understanding, who reflects on the stories of the past, the patterns of history, and the consequences of actions, and then adjusts their life accordingly.

The question “Will you not then understand?” echoes across the centuries, challenging every reader and listener to avoid the fate of those who see the signs but remain blind to their meaning. It transitions seamlessly into the next story, that of Jonah, which will present a different type of trial and a unique lesson.

=139=

The surah now introduces the final prophet in this sequence, Jonah (Yūnus), with the same formula of confirmation. “And Jonah was indeed one of the messengers.” The “indeed” (wa-inna) affirms his prophethood with certainty, even though his story contains an episode of apparent flight from duty. Jonah is also called “Dhun-Nun” (the Man of the Whale) and “Ṣāḥib al-Ḥūt” (the Companion of the Fish) in the Quran. He was sent to the people of Nineveh, a great city in Mesopotamia. His mention here places him in the same honoured line as Noah, Abraham, Moses, Aaron, Elias, and Lot, emphasizing that his status as a messenger is not diminished by the trial he underwent. The Meccans, like the people of Jonah, were being warned. The story that follows will illustrate a different aspect of the prophetic experience: not only patient endurance, but also the moment of human weakness, followed by repentance, and then Allah’s immense mercy. This provides a more complete picture of the faithful servants; they are not infallible robots but human beings who, when they err, turn back to Allah with intense devotion. The Prophet Muhammad was told in another surah to be patient like the “Companion of the Fish,” indicating that this story was a source of comfort and instruction.

For the believer, Jonah’s story is a powerful reminder that even the chosen ones can face overwhelming pressure, but the door of repentance and Allah’s mercy is always open. The verse sets the stage for the concise yet deeply moving account of his flight, the storm, the whale, and the eventual deliverance and success, all of which encapsulate the surah’s theme of reward for those who ultimately turn to Allah.

=140=

The narrative of Jonah’s trial begins with his departure. “Remember when he fled to the overloaded ship.” The command “Remember” draws attention to this critical moment. Jonah, after calling his people to Allah for a long time and seeing no response but rejection, became angry and frustrated. He left his city without Allah’s explicit permission, essentially abandoning his assigned post.

The word “fled” (abaqa) is particularly striking; it is the same verb used for a slave who runs away from his master. This indicates that Jonah’s departure was a lapse from the complete servitude expected of a prophet, a momentary escape from the burden of his mission. He fled “to the overloaded ship,” meaning he went to the sea and boarded a ship that was already full of passengers and cargo. The detail of the ship being “overloaded” (mashḥūn) sets up the tension and the subsequent events. The ship, carrying too much weight, would soon face a storm that threatened to sink it. Jonah’s flight was from duty to a place of seeming escape, but it led him directly into a more constricted trial. This teaches that attempting to flee from Allah’s decrees or responsibilities only leads to tighter corners, as the divine plan cannot be outrun. For the Meccans, this was a subtle warning: they could not flee from the truth brought by the Prophet. For the believers, Jonah’s story is relatable; sometimes the pressures of life and daʿwah become so intense that one wants to withdraw.

Yet, the lesson is that true relief comes not from fleeing the situation, but from turning back to Allah in sincere repentance, as Jonah would ultimately do in the belly of the whale. The verse captures the beginning of a journey that would bring the prophet to the depths of the sea and then to the heights of divine mercy, illustrating the profound journey of the soul from error to redemption.
