=161=

The surah now turns to address the Meccan idolaters directly, drawing a decisive conclusion from the preceding arguments about their false beliefs. The verse states, “Surely you pagans and whatever idols you worship.

” The word “surely” (fa-innakum) conveys absolute certainty, leaving no room for doubt. Allah speaks directly to the polytheists, grouping them together with the objects of their worship.

The phrase “you pagans” addresses the human worshippers, while “whatever idols you worship” encompasses all their false deities—the stone statues, the angels whom they claimed were daughters of Allah, the jinn they associated with Him, and any other beings or objects they called upon besides Allah. This grouping is deeply significant because it places the worshipper and the worshipped on the same level of powerlessness. The idols are not presented as mighty gods who might defend themselves; they are merely “whatever,” a dismissive term that strips them of any pretense to divinity. In the earlier verses, the surah demolished the intellectual basis of idolatry, showing that these false gods can neither speak, nor eat, nor create, nor benefit, nor harm. Now, having established their utter impotence, Allah lumps the pagans together with their idols as equally helpless before His power. The Meccan pagans who took pride in their noble lineage and their mighty gods are being told that they and their gods are, in reality, nothing before the Lord of the worlds. This sets the stage for the next two verses, which will describe the absolute limitation of their power to mislead.

The verse also functions as a transition from the refutation of their creed to the affirmation of Allah’s sovereign control over guidance and misguidance. By addressing them directly, the Quran shakes them out of the role of distant observers and forces them to see themselves as the subjects of this divine judgment.

For the believer, this verse reinforces the understanding that all false gods—whether ancient idols or modern-day attachments—are ultimately powerless, and those who cling to them share in their weakness.

=162=

The verse continues the thought, revealing the crucial limitation of the pagans and their idols. “Can never lure anyone away from Him.

” The word “lure” translates a verb that carries the meaning of enticing, seducing, and leading astray through temptation. The pagans actively tried to lure believers away from the worship of Allah. They used social pressure, mockery, economic threats, and even promises of reward to try to make the early Muslims revert to idolatry.

However, Allah declares here that they “can never” succeed in luring away anyone whom Allah protects. The negation is absolute and eternal. It is not that they might have limited success; rather, their entire effort is futile against those whom Allah has willed to guide.

This is a powerful reassurance for the oppressed believers in Mecca who were facing immense pressure to abandon their faith. The verse tells them that no matter how persuasive or threatening the pagans become, the faithful heart that Allah has filled with the light of tawheed cannot be stolen by idolaters and their lifeless gods. The idols themselves have no power to lure; they are inert. The human pagans have tongues and schemes, but they too are powerless to change the hearts that Allah has secured. This reflects the Quranic principle that guidance and misguidance are ultimately in Allah’s hands. The Prophet could not guide those he loved; likewise, the enemies of faith cannot misguide those Allah loves.

The verse also implies that the pagans and their idols are themselves lured away from the truth; how then can they lure others to it? They are the blinded leading the blind.

For the believer today, this verse is a fortress of comfort. When we face pressures to compromise our faith—whether from society, media, or personal relationships—we must remember that no created power can lure us away from Allah unless we willingly follow. The protection is in holding firmly to the rope of Allah.

This verse also serves as a warning to those who actively try to lead believers astray: their efforts are doomed, and they will only increase their own burden. The power to lure is an illusion; true hearts are in the hand of the Merciful.

=163=

The absolute statement of the previous verse is now qualified with a crucial exception. “Except those destined to burn in Hell.” This does not mean that the pagans and their idols have the power to lure even these people; rather, it explains that the only ones who will fall into the trap of idolatry and misguidance are those who are already written by Allah’s foreknowledge to be among the inhabitants of Hell. Their being lured away is the manifestation of their own choice and their predetermined fate based on that choice.

The phrase “destined to burn” (ṣālū al-jaḥīm) refers to those who have sealed their own doom through persistent rejection of truth, arrogance, and love of falsehood. They are not innocent victims of powerful seduction; they are willing participants whose hearts are already inclined to Hell. The pagans might think they are successfully luring people, but in reality, they are only attracting those who were already on the path to Hell. The true believers are immune. This exception is a profound theological point: Allah’s knowledge encompasses all things, and He knows who will choose disbelief and who will choose faith. The “luring” is a test that reveals the inner state of a person. The one who is destined to burn will find the lure attractive and will follow it; the one who is destined for Paradise will see through its falsehood. This does not negate human free will; rather, it asserts that Allah’s knowledge is perfect, and He has already written the outcomes based on the choices people will freely make. For the Meccan believers, this was a clarification that those who apostatized under torture or persuasion were not truly guided in the first place; their faith was not rooted. The believers should not grieve over them. For the contemporary believer, this verse inspires a healthy fear and self-reflection. We should never be complacent, but we trust that if we sincerely seek Allah, He will protect us from the lures that lead to Hell.

The verse also underscores the justice of Allah: those who burn in Hell are not there because the pagans overpowered them, but because they themselves chose the path of destruction. The lures are only effective on the hearts that have already turned away from the truth.

=164=

After exposing the pagan claims about the angels, the surah now allows the angels themselves to speak, through divine revelation, to clarify their true nature. The verse says, “The angels respond, ‘There is not one of us without an assigned station of worship.’” The pagans claimed the angels were daughters of Allah and worshipped them as divine intercessors. The angels, who are the chosen servants of Allah mentioned in the exception earlier, now refute this blasphemy in the most beautiful way. They declare their complete servitude.

The phrase “There is not one of us” (wa-mā minnā illā) is an absolute negation, meaning every single angel, without exception, has a specific, assigned station. The word “assigned station” (maqām maʿlūm) refers to a fixed position of worship and service. The angels are not aimless beings; they are organized in a perfect hierarchy, each one knowing its place and its duty, never overstepping it. This echoes the very opening of the surah, where the angels are described as being “lined up in ranks. ” They do not descend to earth without permission, they do not intercede without leave, and they certainly do not claim divinity. Their “station” is one of complete obedience and humility before the Majestic Lord. This response is both a rebuke to the pagans and a lesson for humanity. The angels, despite their immense power and proximity to Allah, are utterly submissive. They do not consider themselves worthy of worship; they are worshippers. For the Meccans who elevated these angels to divine status, the angels themselves declare, “We are only servants with assigned duties.” The believer learns that true honour lies in knowing one’s station before Allah and fulfilling it with devotion. Every human being also has an assigned station in the sense of their role, responsibilities, and the path laid out for them by Allah. The key is to embrace servitude as the angels do.

This verse also provides a powerful model of monotheism: even the highest creatures in the universe are but slaves. So how can a human worship another human, a stone, or a celestial body? The angels’ testimony dismantles the foundation of all shirk by revealing that the entire unseen realm is structured upon the principle of servitude to the One God.

=165=

The angels continue their testimony, now specifying the nature of their station. “We are indeed the ones lined up in ranks for Allah.

” This is a direct reference to the first verse of the surah, “By those lined up in ranks,” creating a beautiful literary and theological loop. The angels confirm that the oath taken by Allah at the beginning of the surah was about them. They stand in disciplined rows, perfectly aligned, waiting for the divine command to issue forth.

The phrase “lined up in ranks” (ṣāffūn) paints a picture of orderly, humble servitude. There is no jostling, no competition, no rebellion. Each angel knows its place in the row, and the rows extend as far as the divine will determines. This formation indicates their readiness for any command—whether it be worship, delivering revelation, executing punishment, or managing the natural world. The addition of “for Allah” emphasizes that their lining up is exclusively for His sake, in His service, and by His command. They are not a chaotic assembly of autonomous spirits; they are a perfectly disciplined army of worshippers. For the pagans who claimed the angels were independent gods or daughters, this statement is a devastating refutation. Gods do not stand in rows waiting for orders; servants do. The angels themselves reject any divinity and affirm their total subordination. The believer, reading this, should reflect on the beauty of order and discipline in worship. The angels’ ranks are a model for the rows of worshippers in the mosque, where believers stand shoulder to shoulder, equal and united, before their Lord.

This verse also instills a sense of awe at the unseen world: above us, there are beings of light who are perpetually arrayed in ranks, glorifying Allah without fatigue. The cosmic order is not random but highly structured. It further reinforces that the ultimate purpose of existence is worship, and the angels are the perfect exemplars of that purpose. We, as humans, are called to join them in spirit, aligning our lives in obedience to the One they serve.

=166=

The angels conclude their testimony with the second part of their station, which is the very activity that defines them. “And we are indeed the ones constantly glorifying His praise.

” The word translated as “glorifying” is “musabbiḥūn,” derived from tasbīḥ, which means to declare Allah free from all imperfections. The addition of “constantly” indicates that this is their perpetual state. They never cease from glorifying Allah and praising Him. This echoes the earlier verse of the surah, “and those who recite the Reminder,” linking the three opening oaths to this angelic confession. The angels glorify and praise Allah in unison, and their glorification is itself an act of worship that they perform without boredom or lapse. Their praise of Allah is inseparable from their glorification; they praise His attributes of beauty and perfection while declaring Him above any defect. This stands in stark opposition to the pagan claims, which attributed to Allah the imperfection of having daughters and consorts. The angels, who were the very subjects of those claims, testify that they spend their existence negating such imperfections. They are the first to pronounce “Subḥāna Allāh,” clearing His name from all the slander the polytheists utter.

For the believer, this verse invites participation in the cosmic chorus of tasbīḥ. When we say “Subḥāna Allāh” after our prayers, when we reflect on His creation and feel our hearts moved to glorify Him, we are harmonizing with the angels. The Prophet taught that the angels surround the gatherings of remembrance, and that the tasbīḥ is a light and a treasure. The constant glorification of the angels also reminds us of our own deficiency; we are distracted and forgetful, while they are in perpetual, perfect remembrance. This should motivate us to increase our own dhikr.

The verse also reassures the believers in Mecca that the universe is not silent about the pagan blasphemies; the highest assemblies of angels are continuously countering those falsehoods with glorification and praise. Truth, at the cosmic level, is overwhelmingly upheld. The angels’ testimony is thus a powerful conclusion to the refutation of shirk, as it comes from the very beings the pagans slandered, and it affirms the absolute transcendence and oneness of Allah.

=167=

After the angelic testimony, the surah returns to expose yet another contradiction in the Meccan pagans’ position. The verse says, “They certainly used to say.

” The phrase “certainly” (wa-in kānū) indicates that this was a habitual, well-known saying of the Quraysh before the Quran was revealed to them. They would repeatedly make a specific statement, which will be quoted in the next verse. This serves to remind the Prophet and the believers of a claim the Arabs used to make before Islam, and then to contrast it with their actual response when that claim was fulfilled. The fact that they “used to say” this indicates that deep in their hearts, they recognized a deficiency in their own tradition. They looked at the Jews and Christians, the “Peoples of the Book,” who possessed scriptures like the Torah and the Gospel, and they felt a sense of inferiority. They wished they too had a revealed book, a prophet, a divine law, so that they could be guided and attain a status like those nations. This desire was not necessarily a sincere longing for truth but a sort of competitive jealousy. They thought that having a scripture would make them a great nation with a clear path. The verse sets the stage for the irony that follows: when Allah actually answered that unspoken wish and sent them a messenger from among themselves with a clear Arabic Quran, they rejected him. Their previous words now stand as a witness against them.

For the believer, this is a lesson about the danger of empty wishes. Many people say, “If only I had guidance, I would be devout,” yet when guidance comes to them, they turn away because it challenges their desires. The verb “used to say” emphasizes that this was not an isolated statement but a recurring sentiment. It also demonstrates Allah’s knowledge of what is in the hearts; He knew what they were saying among themselves. The verse serves as a prelude to the dramatic revelation of their hypocrisy, which will be laid bare in the subsequent verses.

=168=

The specific statement of the pagan Arabs is now quoted. “If only we had a Reminder like those of earlier peoples.

” The word “Reminder” (dhikr) refers to a divine scripture, a revealed book that reminds people of their Creator, their purpose, and the Day of Judgment. The Meccans used to look at the Jews who had the Torah and the Christians who had the Gospel, and they would say, “If only we had a book like theirs, we would be guided. ” They knew these earlier peoples had received divine revelation containing laws, wisdom, and stories of the prophets. The Arabs, as an unlettered nation that had not received a major prophet or book since Ishmael’s time, felt this absence keenly. They envied the scriptural communities and imagined that possessing such a book would elevate them.

However, this wish was not entirely innocent; it was often accompanied by a sense of self-justification. They used it as an excuse: “We have not received a warner, so we are not to blame for our idolatry.” But deep down, they had the legacy of Abraham and Ishmael, the original monotheism of the Kaaba, which they had corrupted. The “earlier peoples” refers to the nations who received prophets, like the people of Moses and Jesus. The verse captures a moment of spiritual longing that could have been a stepping stone to faith. There was a flicker of recognition that their own traditions were lacking and that divine guidance was valuable.

For the believer, this verse reminds us that Allah answers the sincere desires of hearts. The Arabs, even with mixed motives, expressed a wish for guidance, and Allah, in His mercy, sent them the greatest Reminder, the Quran.

This is a manifestation of the divine name “The Responsive. ” It should make us reflect on our own wishes: if we truly desire closeness to Allah, He will provide the means, but we must be ready to accept them. The tragedy of the Quraysh is that the very thing they claimed they wanted was given to them, and they spurned it.

=169=

The Arabs completed their wish with a bold claim of what they would do if they received a Reminder. “We would have truly been Allah’s devoted servants.

” The phrase “devoted servants” (ʿibāda Allāh al-mukhlaṣīn) means they claimed they would be sincere, pure worshippers of Allah, dedicating their religion entirely to Him, unlike the Jews and Christians who, in their view, had gone astray or fallen into internal conflicts. They boasted that if they had a scripture, they would be the best of nations, more guided, more obedient, and more united.

The word “truly” (la-kunnā) adds emphasis to their self-confident assertion. They presented themselves as people who only lacked the opportunity; given the right tool, they would excel. This boast reflects a common human trait: blaming external circumstances for one’s spiritual failures while overestimating one’s own virtue. The pagan Arabs thought their idolatry and moral corruption were due to the absence of a divine book, as if the innate fitrah, the signs in creation, and the legacy of their forefather Abraham were insufficient to guide them. The verse thus exposes a profound self-deception. They imagined that if a heavy, demanding scripture with laws, responsibilities, and prohibitions came to them, they would embrace it wholeheartedly. But when it actually came, their reaction proved otherwise.

For the believer, this is a cautionary tale: it is easy to imagine how devout we would be if circumstances were different, but the test is how we respond to the guidance that is already in our hands. Many people today say, “If I had more knowledge, I would be more pious,” while neglecting to act upon what they already know. The sincerity of one’s devotion is proven by obedience, not by hypothetical scenarios.

The verse also sets up the crushing irony that these people who claimed they would be “devoted servants” are now calling the actual devoted servant, the Prophet Muhammad, a mad poet and a liar. Their own words condemn them, showing that their problem was never the lack of guidance but the arrogance and attachment to their ancestral ways that prevented them from accepting the guidance when it arrived.

=170=

The irony reaches its climax with this devastating revelation. “But now they reject it, so they will soon know.” The “it” refers to the Reminder, the Quran, which Allah sent to them in answer to their supposed longing. When the very thing they said they wished for was granted to them—a clear Arabic scripture, a messenger from their own lineage, a book more eloquent and comprehensive than any previous revelation—they turned their backs on it. Instead of becoming “devoted servants,” they denied it, mocked it, and called it magic or poetry.

The word “now” emphasizes the present, shocking contrast. The very people who had expressed envy of the earlier peoples for having scriptures are now the rejecters of the final and greatest scripture.

The phrase “they will soon know” is a severe warning. It is not an empty threat; it points to the immediate future in this world—the Battle of Badr, the conquest of Mecca, the eventual triumph of Islam—and more importantly, to the punishment of the Hereafter. They will know the truth of what they rejected when the Blast of the Trumpet sounds, and they cry out, “Woe to us!

” This is a classic Quranic technique: after exposing a contradiction or a false claim, a warning is issued that the deniers will soon see the consequences of their actions. For the Meccan Muslims who had heard these very pagans say they wished for guidance, this verse was a vindication and a source of strength. It showed that the pagans were not sincere seekers but hypocritical boasters. Their rejection was not based on intellectual difficulty but on stubbornness.

For the believer, this verse teaches that we should never envy the spiritual status of others while neglecting the guidance we have received. We have the Quran and the Sunnah; the question is whether we are living as devoted servants or whether we, too, are rejecting the Reminder through heedlessness and lack of action. The “soon will know” should resonate in our hearts as a call to take our present guidance seriously before the time comes when knowledge will no longer benefit.

=171=

The surah now shifts to a message of reassurance for the Prophet Muhammad, grounding it in the unchanging divine decree concerning all messengers. “Our Word has already gone forth to Our servants, the messengers.” The “Word” (kalima) refers to the divine decree, the unalterable promise and judgment that Allah pronounced in His eternal knowledge. This Word “has already gone forth,” meaning it was established and settled in the Preserved Tablet long before the creation of the heavens and the earth. It is not a new decision; it is the sunnah of Allah that has been in operation since the first prophet was sent. The recipients of this Word are “Our servants, the messengers,” a title of honour that combines their servitude with their mission. The messengers are the closest to Allah, and this Word is a covenant of support and victory for them. The content of this Word is not fully detailed here but is summarized in the next two verses: they will be helped, and Allah’s forces will prevail. This verse comes as a soothing balm to the Prophet, who was facing intense opposition and the apparent success of the deniers. It reminds him that he is not alone in this struggle; he is part of an ancient and honoured company of messengers, and the same decree that applied to them applies to him.

The phrase “Our Word” emphasizes the authority and certainty of the promise. What Allah says is the truth, and His Word cannot be changed or thwarted. For the believers in Mecca, who might have wondered if the small community of Muslims would ever overcome the mighty Quraysh, this verse assured them that victory was not a matter of numbers or weapons but of divine decree.

The Word had already gone forth, and history was unfolding according to that Word. The believer learns to trust in the promises of Allah, even when circumstances seem hopeless. The messengers were all tried, but the outcome was always in their favour.

This verse also connects beautifully to the earlier stories of Noah, Abraham, Moses, and the others; they all experienced the fulfillment of this Word. The Meccans who rejected the Prophet are thus warned that they are not fighting a lone man but opposing a divine decree that has been set in stone.

=172=

The first part of the divine Word is now explicitly stated. “That they would surely be helped.

” This is the promise of divine aid and victory for the messengers. The word “helped” (mansūrūn) is in the emphatic plural form, indicating a definite, guaranteed assistance. This help encompasses many forms: sometimes it is visible, like the physical salvation of Noah from the flood, the deliverance of Abraham from the fire, the defeat of Pharaoh’s army, and the destruction of the people of Lot. Sometimes it is less spectacular but equally real, like the firmness of heart granted to a prophet in the face of persecution, the conversion of a tyrant, or the establishment of the messenger’s community after his passing. For the Prophet Muhammad, this help materialized in various ways: the miraculous victory at Badr, the gradual spread of Islam despite the Quraysh’s opposition, the eventual conquest of Mecca, and the preservation and triumph of his message after his death.

The word “surely” leaves no room for doubt. It is an iron-clad guarantee from the Lord of the worlds. The help does not necessarily come on the timeline humans expect, nor in the form they imagine, but it comes. The believers might suffer, some might even be martyred, but the ultimate victory of the truth is assured. This verse was a direct challenge to the Meccan pagans who thought they could extinguish the message by killing or mocking the Prophet. It told them that the forces of history were against them, and the divine plan would be executed regardless of their efforts.

For the believer in any era, this promise is a source of immense courage and patience. When one strives for the sake of Allah, facing obstacles and setbacks, they should recall this verse and know that Allah’s help is on the way, conditioned upon their continued faith and righteousness. The help is not for the arrogant or the hasty, but for those who follow the path of the messengers with sincerity.

The verse also ties back to the surah’s opening depiction of the angels as disciplined forces; these forces are part of the divine help, always at the ready to assist the messengers when the command is given.

=173=

The second part of the divine Word complements the first. “And that Our forces will certainly prevail.” The “forces” (junūd) of Allah refer to all the means of support He employs to establish the truth and defeat falsehood. This includes the angels who fought alongside the believers at Badr, the natural elements like the wind that destroyed ʿĀd, the water that drowned Pharaoh, the birds that pelted the army of Abraha, and even intangible forces like the fear cast into the hearts of enemies or the arguments that render falsehood intellectually bankrupt. The forces of Allah are not limited to physical armies; they encompass every instrument of divine will in the cosmos.

The word “certainly prevail” (la-hum al-ghālibūn) is an emphatic declaration of their undefeatable nature. Whatever Allah sends to support His messengers, whether it be a storm, an angel, a plague, or a shift in political fortunes, that force will overcome all opposition.

This statement is an ultimate reassurance for the Prophet and the believers. It tells them that they are not a small, isolated band of people but part of a grand cosmic movement backed by the Lord of the worlds. The battle between truth and falsehood is not a fair fight; it is weighted decisively in favour of the truth because the Creator of the universe has decreed its victory. The Meccan pagans, with their wealth and numbers, thought they were the powerful ones, but the verse declares that the real power belongs to Allah’s forces, and they will be the ones who prevail.

For the believer, this cultivates a deep trust in Allah. It does not mean passivity; the believers are themselves part of Allah’s forces when they strive in His path. But it does mean that the ultimate outcome is not in doubt. The forces of falsehood may have temporary ascendancy, but the overall trajectory of prophetic history is their defeat and the triumph of tawheed.

This verse also connects to the eschatological promise: the Day of Judgment will be the final, absolute victory of Allah’s forces, when the chosen servants enter Paradise and the wrongdoers are cast into Hell. It is a verse that fills the heart with hope and steadfastness, enabling the believer to endure the darkest hours with the certainty of the coming dawn.

=174=

Having established the certainty of divine support and eventual victory, Allah now gives the Prophet a strategic and psychological instruction. “So turn away from the deniers for a while O Prophet.

” This command to “turn away” (tawalla) does not mean to abandon the mission of daʿwah completely, but to temporarily withdraw from the exhausting and fruitless arguments with those who are obstinate. The phrase “for a while” (ḥattā ḥīn) indicates a limited time, a divinely appointed respite. The Prophet was deeply grieved by his people’s rejection, and he exhausted himself trying to convince them. Allah, in His mercy, commands him to step back, to give his heart a break, and to focus his energy on those who are receptive. This “turning away” is not out of fear or despair but out of wisdom and obedience. It is a strategic withdrawal to preserve spiritual strength for the long struggle ahead. The deniers are to be left in their arrogance until the appointed time when the divine punishment or victory arrives. This mirrors the earlier command to Abraham, who physically left his people, and to other messengers who separated themselves from the stubborn rejecters. The “while” refers to the period before the hijrah to Medina, or before the major battles, or before the conquest of Mecca. In a broader sense, it teaches that there are times when engaging with hostile people is counterproductive; one should focus on building the community of believers and conveying the message to those who might listen. For the contemporary believer, this verse provides guidance on dealing with relentless argumentation and toxicity. Sometimes the most spiritually healthy course is to disengage, to protect one’s faith and energy, and to leave the outcome to Allah. It does not mean giving up on people forever, but recognizing when the heart of the other is sealed and further debate only hardens it. The command is accompanied by a promise in the next verse that both the Prophet and the deniers will see the outcome, so the turning away is not a retreat but a repositioning while waiting for the unfolding of the divine decree.

=175=

The command to turn away is immediately followed by a promise of mutual witnessing. “You will see what will happen to them, and they too will see!

” The repetition of “see” emphasizes the certainty and the imminence of a visible outcome. The Prophet is told that he will witness the consequences of their denial—whether in this world through their defeat at Badr, their humiliation at the conquest of Mecca, or in the Hereafter through their punishment. Similarly, the deniers will see the truth of what the Prophet brought: they will see his victory, the spread of Islam, and on the Day of Judgment, they will see the reality of resurrection and the recompense.

The phrase is a powerful declaration that the current state of affairs is temporary. The Prophet is not to be discouraged by their mockery, because a day is coming when both sides will see the truth with their own eyes. The deniers’ sarcastic questions about the punishment will be answered when it descends upon them.

This verse also contains a subtle warning and a note of hope. The warning is for the deniers: “you will see” means your eyes will be opened forcibly to what you now deny. The hope is for the Prophet: “you will see” means your patience will be rewarded by witnessing the triumph of your mission. The mutual seeing creates a dramatic tension that the surah resolves in the final verses. It also connects to the earlier scene of the believers in Paradise seeing the disbeliever in Hell; that seeing is the ultimate fulfillment of this promise.

For the believer today, this verse instills patience. When we are in the midst of a struggle and the forces of falsehood seem dominant, we must remember that a time of clear seeing is coming. We will see the victory of Allah’s religion in ways perhaps we cannot imagine, and the deniers will see their error. The verse frees the heart from the need to immediately vindicate itself; Allah will handle the vindication. All we need to do is remain steadfast and turn away from fruitless conflict, focusing on our own salvation and the daʿwah to those who are open.

=176=

The surah now turns back to the deniers with a rhetorical question brimming with rebuke. “Do they really wish to hasten Our punishment?” The Meccan pagans, in their arrogance and mockery, would often challenge the Prophet by saying, “If you are truthful, then bring upon us the punishment you threaten us with.” They thought that the delay in punishment was proof that it would never come, and they sarcastically asked for it to be sped up. This verse exposes the foolishness of such a request. Wishing to hasten punishment is like a criminal demanding the executioner to hurry up, not realizing the horror that awaits.

The phrase “Our punishment” reminds them that it is not a light matter; it is the punishment of the Lord of the worlds, which is utterly devastating. The question “Do they really wish” (a-fa-bi-ʿadhābinā yastaʿjilūn) carries a tone of incredulity. How can any sane person wish to hasten something that will destroy them? The verse is a warning that they should be praying for mercy and more time to repent, not foolishly challenging the divine power.

This is a common theme in the Quran: when people mock the warning of punishment, Allah reminds them that once the punishment arrives, there is no escape, and their mockery will turn into desperate regret. The verse also serves to reassure the believers that the delay is not out of weakness but out of mercy, giving the deniers a chance to turn back. The haste of the disbelievers is contrasted with the patience of the believers, who await Allah’s decree with hope and fear.

For the believer today, this verse is a reminder never to be flippant about divine warnings. When we hear about the punishment of the grave or the fire of Hell, our response should be to seek refuge in Allah, not to question why it has not come. The wish to hasten punishment is born out of a heart that is hardened; it is a sign of being far from Allah. The verse thus calls us to take the warnings of the Quran with utmost seriousness, to repent before the day when the punishment does arrive, and it is no longer possible to return.

=177=

The surah now paints a chilling picture of the moment the punishment actually arrives, answering their foolish wish. “Yet when it descends upon them: how evil will that morning be for those who had been warned!

” The word “when” (fa-idhā) shifts the scene from their current arrogance to the inevitable future. The punishment is described as “descending” (nazala), a verb that implies it comes from above, sudden and inescapable, like a torrent or a divine strike. The specific mention of “morning” (ṣabāḥ) is significant. In Arabic culture, armies would often attack at dawn, and the morning was associated with the unveiling of events. Many divine punishments in history, like the destruction of Lot’s people, occurred at or just before the break of day. The morning, which usually brings light and hope, becomes a morning of horror and despair for the warned deniers. The verse exclaims, “how evil will that morning be” (fa-sāʾa ṣabāḥu al-mundharīn), a rhetorical expression of the utmost dreadfulness.

The phrase “those who had been warned” (al-mundharīn) is a painful reminder that they were not taken by surprise; they were amply warned by the messengers, and they chose to mock and challenge the warning. The morning of punishment is thus the bitter harvest of their own rejection. For the Meccan pagans, this was a direct prophecy of a coming disaster in this world—perhaps the famine, the defeat at Badr, or the conquest—and certainly the Day of Judgment. The verse is designed to strike fear into the hearts of the listeners. It asks them to imagine waking up to the punishment they used to scoff at. There would be no escape, no chance to go back and believe.

For the believer, this verse is a powerful deterrent from complacency. It reminds us that the warnings we hear in the Quran are not distant theories; they are a reality that can descend suddenly. We must live in a state of preparedness, grateful for the time we have been given to repent and do good.

The verse also comforts the oppressed believers that the mockers will not have the last laugh; their morning of reckoning will be so evil that their previous laughter will seem like a distant, cruel joke. The contrast between the “morning” of punishment and the “Gardens of Bliss” awaiting the believers could not be starker.

=178=

The command given earlier in verse 174 is repeated almost verbatim, demonstrating its importance and providing a rhythmic refrain in this concluding section. “And turn away from them for a while.

” The repetition serves to comfort the Prophet and reinforce the strategy. It is as if Allah is telling him, “I have told you to turn away, and I tell you again: turn away. Do not let your heart be weighed down by them. Entrust their affair to Me for this appointed period.” The “while” is a divinely measured interval before the punishment or the victory.

This repetition also indicates that the Prophet’s natural compassion and desire to save his people might keep pulling him back into fruitless, draining arguments. So Allah reiterates the command to protect his mental and spiritual well-being. The command is not abandonment of the mission; the Prophet continued to invite people to Islam, but he was to withdraw from engaging with the hardcore mockers who only wanted to argue and insult.

This is a lesson in time management and spiritual economy: one should invest energy where there is hope of receptivity, not exhaust it on those who have made a conscious decision to reject. The repetition also creates a literary effect, building anticipation for the final outcome described in the next verses.

For the believer, this verse is a reminder that sometimes the most obedient thing to do is to step back, to be silent, and to let Allah handle the matter. In our personal lives, when we face persistent hostility or mockery for our faith, we are not always obliged to engage. We can “turn away for a while,” preserving our peace and waiting for Allah’s decree to unfold. This turning away is not cowardice; it is an act of trust and a following of the prophetic example. The command is bracketed by the mutual seeing of the outcome, which underscores that the final judgment is not our responsibility but Allah’s.

=179=

The promise of mutual witnessing is repeated, almost as an echo of verse 175, but now it stands as a firm conclusion to the command to turn away. “You will see, and they too will see!” The brevity and symmetry of this verse add to its powerful impact. The Prophet is addressed directly, and the deniers are referred to in the plural. Both sides will be spectators of the unfolding truth.

The repetition reinforces certainty. The Prophet will see the punishment that befalls the deniers and the victory granted to the believers. The deniers will see the fulfillment of the Prophet’s warning and the truth of the resurrection they denied. This dual seeing encompasses both the worldly events—like the Battle of Badr where the leading mockers were killed, and the conquest of Mecca where the idols were smashed—and the scenes of the Hereafter, where the Prophet will witness his followers entering Paradise and the deniers will witness the fire. The verb “see” (fa-sa-yubṣirūn) carries a connotation of insight and realization. It will not be a superficial glance; it will be a profound understanding of the reality that they had rejected.

For the believer, this verse is a source of consolation. There are many situations in life where we feel wronged or where the truth seems to be losing. This verse assures us that the ultimate clarification is coming. We do not need to force people to see the truth; Allah will make them see it, either in this world through signs and events, or definitively in the Hereafter.

The repetition also serves as a rhetorical closure to the section, leaving the deniers with a final, ominous warning, and the Prophet with a final, uplifting promise. It is a verse that strengthens the heart to remain patient and steadfast, knowing that the blindness of the rejecters is temporary and will be removed in a moment when it is too late for them. The believer’s seeing, however, will be a source of joy and gratitude, as they witness the fulfillment of Allah’s Word.

=180=

The surah now reaches its majestic conclusion, beginning with a glorification that echoes the themes of the entire passage. “Glorified is your Lord—the Lord of Honour and Power—above what they claim!

” This is a sublime declaration of tanzīh, declaring Allah’s transcendence and perfection, far removed from the false descriptions and fabrications the pagans have uttered throughout the surah. The phrase “your Lord” (rabbika) is personal and intimate, addressed to the Prophet, reminding him of the close, nurturing relationship he has with his Master. The Lord is then described with two magnificent attributes: “the Lord of Honour” (rabbi al-ʿizza) and “the Lord of Power.” Honour (ʿizza) refers to might, dignity, invincibility, and exaltedness. Allah possesses all honour; none can humiliate Him or overpower Him. Power (in the Arabic phrasing included in the verse, though the translation here renders “Honour and Power” as the combined meaning, it encompasses jabarūt or strength that compels submission). He is the Compeller, the One whose will is irresistible. These attributes directly counter the pagan claims of weakness and neediness implied by attributing daughters and partners to Him. A Lord of such absolute Honour and Power is far above having children, consorts, or rivals.

The phrase “above what they claim” is the recurring refrain that dismisses all the slander of the polytheists—their claims about angels, jinn, idols, and the denial of resurrection. The glorification at the end of the surah mirrors the glorification of the angels at the beginning and their testimony in the middle. The entire surah is framed by tasbīḥ. This structure teaches the believer that the ultimate response to falsehood is to glorify Allah and affirm His transcendence. We may refute arguments, but our hearts should rest in the glorification of the One who is far above all imperfections. For the Meccans, this was a final, thunderous declaration that their beliefs were not just wrong but a vile affront to the Majestic Lord.

For the believer, it is an invitation to join in this glorification, to fill our hearts with the awe of the Lord of Honour and Power, and to cleanse our faith of any concept that does not befit His majesty. This verse sets up the final two verses of the surah, which extend peace to the messengers and praise to Allah, bringing the entire magnificent discourse to a serene and powerful close.
