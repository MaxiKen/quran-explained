=141=

After Jonah fled his people and boarded the overloaded ship, the journey took a perilous turn. The verse says, “Then to save it from sinking, he drew straws with other passengers.” A violent storm struck, and the heavily laden ship began to founder. In the ancient world, when a ship was in danger and all efforts failed, the crew would sometimes resort to drawing lots to determine if someone on board was the cause of the misfortune, perhaps an escaped slave or a person under divine wrath. By casting lots, they sought to identify and throw that person overboard to lighten the ship and appease whatever force they believed was angry. Jonah participated in this drawing of lots, and “he lost and was thrown overboard.” The lots singled him out. This was not a matter of random chance; it was the operation of Allah’s decree. Jonah, who had fled his prophetic responsibility, was now being drawn out and exposed by a seemingly neutral process. The casting of lots is mentioned in the Quran as a lawful means of decision-making in some contexts, and here it was the means by which the divine will manifested. The fact that Jonah was the one identified indicates that his situation was no secret to the Creator who controls the storm and the lots. The passengers and crew, not knowing Jonah’s true identity, saw the result and acted upon it, throwing him into the raging sea. This moment marks the lowest point of Jonah’s physical trajectory: from the security of the ship to the engulfing darkness of the water.

Yet, it was also the beginning of his rescue, for the depths would become the place of his seclusion with Allah. The verse teaches that one cannot run from Allah’s decree; Jonah fled to the sea but found himself face to face with the very trial that would bring him back.

For the believer, it is a lesson that when we make mistakes, Allah may orchestrate circumstances that corner us, not to destroy us, but to bring us to a state of humility and reliance. The apparent defeat of being thrown overboard was, in reality, the first step towards a magnificent deliverance. The surah’s theme of the chosen servant being saved is about to unfold in the most literal way, within the belly of a great fish.

=142=

As Jonah hit the water, divine intervention took a startling form. “Then the whale engulfed him while he was blameworthy.

” The Arabic word used is “ḥūt,” which can mean a large fish or a whale. By Allah’s command, this great creature swallowed Jonah whole, without tearing his flesh or breaking his bones. The whale became a living prison, a dark, constricted space beneath the waves.

The verse adds a crucial moral detail: “while he was blameworthy.” Jonah was not swallowed as an innocent victim of circumstances; he was in a state of being blameworthy (mulīm) for leaving his people without divine permission, for his impatience and anger. This word carries the sense of doing something that calls for blame, though not necessarily a grave sin that invalidates prophethood. Prophets are protected from major sins but may experience minor lapses or leave what is more preferable, and Allah, in His mercy, educates them through trials. Jonah’s blameworthiness was that he fled before receiving the command to do so, giving up on his people prematurely. The whale’s belly became the crucible in which that blame would be purified.

This image is the ultimate symbol of constriction after flight. Jonah had sought to escape the confines of his difficult mission, only to be confined within a much tighter space, under the sea, in the dark, alone. The physical darkness of the whale’s belly, the darkness of the ocean, and the darkness of the night combined to create a threefold darkness that he would later describe in his supplication: “There is no god except You; exalted are You. Indeed, I have been of the wrongdoers.” The lesson is profound: when a believer, especially one in a position of responsibility, makes a mistake, the way to redemption is not further flight but immediate repentance and glorification. The whale was not a punishment in the sense of damnation, but a divine detention designed to bring Jonah to his knees in humble admission. The Meccan believers, some of whom might have wavered under persecution, are reminded that even a prophet experienced a moment of frailty, yet the door of return was wide open. The whale, a creature that normally evokes fear, became an instrument of divine tutelage, preparing Jonah for a greater mission.

=143=

The surah now reveals the key that unlocked Jonah’s rescue. “Had he not constantly glorified Allah.” The verse introduces a conditional statement that will continue in the next verse, but this clause alone is packed with meaning. It points to the spiritual habit that Jonah had cultivated throughout his life before this trial: he was among those who constantly perform tasbīḥ, glorifying Allah by saying “Subḥān Allāh” (Glory be to Allah) and engaging in prayer and remembrance.

The word “constantly” (musabbiḥīn) indicates a frequent, sustained practice, not just a one-time utterance. This glorification is what eventually saved him. In his normal life, Jonah had filled his days and nights with the remembrance of Allah, and when the darkness of the whale’s belly closed in, that deep-rooted habit surfaced. He turned not to despair but to glorification and the acknowledgment of his own fault. The famous supplication he made inside the whale is taught by the Prophet Muhammad as a powerful prayer for distress: “Lā ilāha illā anta subḥānaka innī kuntu min aẓ-ẓālimīn” (There is no god but You, glory be to You; I have been among the wrongdoers). This was his glorification: declaring Allah’s transcendence and his own shortcoming.

The verse teaches that the daily, consistent worship and remembrance we practice are not just for good times; they build a spiritual reservoir that sustains us in the darkest moments. Jonah’s glorification was his lifeline. If he had not been among those who habitually glorify, he might have reacted with panic or anger, but his tongue and heart were trained to turn immediately to Allah. For the believers in Mecca, enduring persecution and the temptation to despair, this was a powerful encouragement to maintain their prayers, their recitation of the Quran, and their remembrance, for these are the deposits that will save them in times of crisis.

The verse also highlights the mercy of Allah, who looks at the heartfelt glorification of a blameworthy servant and grants him a way out. It is not the magnitude of the mistake that determines the outcome, but the sincerity and constancy of the servant’s connection to his Lord.

=144=

The conditional statement is completed with a terrifying hypothetical. “He would have certainly remained in its belly until the Day of Resurrection.” If not for his prior habit of glorification and the sincere repentance it produced, Jonah’s prison would have become his grave, and the whale’s belly would have been his holding place until the end of time. This does not necessarily mean the whale would have lived until the Resurrection; rather, it means the belly would have been his tomb, and he would have remained dead and entombed there, his fate sealed without deliverance, only to be raised on that Day for judgment. Some scholars interpret that the whale’s belly would have been his abode of punishment, a prison until the Resurrection, as a recompense for his blameworthy state. The severity of this image underscores the absolute seriousness of abandoning a prophetic mission without divine leave, and it magnifies the mercy that actually occurred. The verse serves a dual purpose. On one hand, it highlights the virtue and saving power of glorification. Jonah’s tasbīḥ was so valuable that it transformed a potential eternal prison into a temporary trial and a place of spiritual rebirth. On the other hand, it is a stark warning that no one, not even a prophet, can escape the consequences of their actions without sincere return to Allah. The “until the Day of Resurrection” phrase also connects this narrative to the overall theme of the surah: the resurrection is a reality, and the deeds of this life have enduring consequences. The Meccan pagans, who denied resurrection, are indirectly told that even a prophet inside a whale would have faced that Day, so their denial does not change the reality.

For the believer, the verse inspires immense hope and immense fear—hope that glorification can rescue from the darkest pit, and fear that without it, even a prophet would be lost. It is a call to never abandon the rope of remembrance, especially in moments of weakness, because the alternative is unthinkable. The verse thus sets up the actual deliverance, which follows immediately, as an act of pure divine grace responding to sincere repentance.

=145=

After the whale’s belly and the glorification, Allah’s mercy physically manifests. “But We cast him onto the open shore, totally worn out.

” The word “But” signals the turning point from the hypothetical horror to the actual salvation. Allah commanded the whale to eject Jonah and deposit him safely on a beach.

The phrase “cast him” (fa-nabadhnāhu) indicates a forceful expulsion; the whale was an obedient instrument casting him out onto the land. The location was an “open shore” (ʿarāʾ), meaning a barren, empty stretch of land without shelter, trees, or shade. Jonah was “totally worn out” (saqīm). His body had been affected by the digestive fluids of the whale, leaving him weak, hairless, and with extremely sensitive skin, like a newborn baby. He was physically broken, yet he was alive and free. This moment of deliverance is a visual mercy: the vast open space contrasts with the constricting darkness of the whale, and the fresh air contrasts with the stifling interior.

However, being cast onto an open shore, exposed to the sun, and in a state of total physical exhaustion created a new form of vulnerability. He needed shelter and nourishment. The divine care that had delivered him from the sea would continue to provide for him.

The sequence teaches that deliverance from one trial often places us in a situation of need that drives us to rely even more on Allah. Jonah could not pat himself on the back; he was utterly helpless, a naked, sick man on an empty beach. This was a complete reset of his strength, preparing him for the second phase of his mission. For the Meccan believers, who might have been physically weakened by torture, hunger, and exile, this verse was a picture of hope: the same Lord who cast Jonah onto the shore will restore you and provide for you. The humbling state of “totally worn out” is not a sign of Allah’s displeasure but often a prelude to His nurturing care, as the next verse shows with the plant. The lesson is that after a period of severe trial and repentance, restoration comes, but the servant remains acutely aware of his total dependence on Allah.

=146=

The divine care after deliverance is beautifully illustrated with a specific detail of nature. “And caused a squash plant to grow over him.

” The Arabic word “yaqṭīn” refers to a squash, gourd, or pumpkin plant with large, broad leaves. This plant was miraculously caused to grow rapidly over Jonah to provide him with essential protection and healing. The broad leaves acted as a canopy, shielding his sensitive, weakened skin from the scorching sun. The plant also likely attracted insects away, provided a cooling microclimate through its broad leaves, and perhaps yielded fruit that he could eat. The fact that it was specifically a squash plant is significant; the Prophet Muhammad mentioned that it was a gourd, and he had a fondness for it. Squash plants are known for their leaves’ ability to provide shade and their natural resistance to flies and insects, making them an ideal covering for a person in a vulnerable state. This small but profound detail shows the meticulous and tender mercy of Allah. He did not just deliver Jonah to dry land and leave him; He provided immediate, customized care through a plant that addressed his exact needs at that moment. The lesson is that when we are at our weakest, having been delivered from a major crisis, Allah’s mercy extends to the smallest details of our recovery. The believer learns to see in the natural world the hand of the Provider. A simple gourd plant becomes a sign of divine love and attention. The plant growing “over him” suggests that Jonah lay under its shade, recovering his strength, both physically and spiritually, and reflecting on the immense grace that had been shown to him. For the Meccan Muslims, who were in a harsh desert environment often lacking shade and food, this verse was a promise that Allah, who shaded a prophet on an empty shore, would provide for them in their hardship. It also serves as a reminder that the signs of Allah are everywhere in creation; even the shade of a leaf can be a reminder of the One who sent it.

The story now moves from Jonah’s personal recovery to the renewal of his public mission.

=147=

After Jonah recovered, his mission was reinstated. “We later sent him back to his city of at least one hundred thousand people.

” The word “later” indicates a passage of time for healing and divine pardon. Once Jonah was fully restored, Allah commanded him to return to the very city he had fled. This shows that his repentance was accepted and his prophetic commission was renewed. The city is described as having “at least one hundred thousand people” or even more (the Arabic “miʾati alfin aw yazīdūn” implies a large number, possibly more than a hundred thousand).

This detail emphasizes that Jonah had been sent to a major urban centre, a massive population, and his abandoning them was a serious matter because of the great number of souls at stake. His return was not to a small, easy task but to a huge city, yet this time he went not on his own impulse but by Allah’s clear instruction. The surah previously mentioned that the chosen servants are saved and honoured; here we see that Jonah’s honour included being sent back as a prophet to a vast nation that would now believe in him. This was a greater triumph than any he could have imagined.

The verse also hints at the magnitude of the mercy that was to descend: over a hundred thousand people would be guided. For the Prophet Muhammad, who was facing a city of stubborn idolaters, this was a reminder that the outcome is in Allah’s hands; a nation that seems hopeless today may tomorrow become a nation of believers.

For the believer, the reinstatement of Jonah teaches that failure and mistakes do not define our end if we repent. Allah can restore us to even greater service than before. The mission is not over until Allah says it is, and His mercy can transform a deserter into a successful guide of multitudes. The sheer number of people also underscores the value of every soul; Allah cared about those hundred thousand and sent Jonah back to save them.

=148=

The fruit of Jonah’s returned mission is now stated. “Who then believed in him, so We allowed them enjoyment for a while.” When Jonah returned and called his people again, this time, they responded with faith. The verse says they believed “in him,” meaning they believed in his message and in Allah. The entire city, over a hundred thousand people, embraced monotheism and turned from their idols. This was an extraordinary mass conversion, a unique event in prophetic history where a whole nation accepted the message without experiencing destruction. In other stories, the rejection of a messenger led to annihilation, but Jonah’s people, upon seeing the signs of impending punishment, repented and were saved. The Quran elsewhere mentions that the people of Jonah were the only ones who believed at the last moment and were spared.

The phrase “so We allowed them enjoyment for a while” indicates that after their belief, Allah extended their lives in comfort and security, letting them enjoy the good things of the world until their appointed term. Their enjoyment was not an eternal reward—that is in the Hereafter—but a temporary respite in this life because of their faith. This demonstrates that collective faith can bring worldly blessings and the lifting of divine punishment. For the Meccan pagans, this was both a warning and an invitation: unlike Jonah’s people, who believed before it was too late, they were still persisting in denial. If they would believe, they too could be granted enjoyment for a while instead of facing the imminent punishment that the Prophet was warning them about.

For the believer, this verse shows the power of sincere repentance on a societal level. It also highlights that the ultimate goal is not just worldly enjoyment, but the enjoyment is a natural consequence of aligning with divine will. The “while” is the period of their lifespan, after which they would face the final judgment as believers, with their eternal reward secure. The story of Jonah ends here in this surah on a note of mercy and triumph, perfectly illustrating the refrain: the chosen servants are rewarded, and even those who falter are welcomed back when they glorify Allah and repent. It is a fitting conclusion to the series of prophets, showing the full spectrum of the faithful servant’s journey, from trial to redemption to massive success.

=149=

The surah transitions from the stories of the prophets to a direct confrontation with the Meccan pagans’ absurd beliefs about Allah. The verse commands, “Ask them O Prophet if your Lord has daughters, while the pagans prefer to have sons.

” This is a rhetorical question designed to expose the deep contradiction and insult in their theology. The pagans of Mecca believed that the angels were female beings and that they were the daughters of Allah. They worshipped these angels as intercessors. At the same time, these same pagans considered daughters to be a source of shame and disgrace for themselves. When one of them received news of the birth of a daughter, his face would darken, and he would hide from people in embarrassment, as the Quran describes elsewhere. They even practiced female infanticide, burying their baby girls alive. So the Prophet is instructed to ask them: how can you attribute to Allah what you yourselves despise and reject? If daughters are so lowly in your eyes that you bury them, how dare you claim that Allah has daughters?

The question is a devastating logical and moral blow. It shows that their belief is not only false but also hypocritical and insulting to the Divine Majesty. They assign to the Creator the very thing they consider inferior.

The phrase “your Lord” reminds them that He is their own Lord, the One who provides for them, yet they ascribe to Him what they detest for themselves. The verse forces a moment of introspection: they must either admit that daughters are not shameful—which contradicts their entire culture—or admit that their attribution of daughters to Allah is a monstrous falsehood. Either way, their idolatry stands exposed.

For the believer, this verse trains us to recognize logical inconsistencies in false beliefs and to worship Allah in a way that is worthy of His perfection. We must never attribute to Allah anything we would not consider perfect.

=150=

The surah follows up the first question with a second, even more pointed one. “Or ask them if We created the angels as females right before their eyes.

” This is a challenge to their claim of knowledge about the angels. The pagans asserted that the angels were female, as if they had witnessed their creation. Allah asks: were you present when We created the angels to know their gender? Did you see them being formed? The obvious answer is no. They have no eyewitness testimony, no revelation, and no evidence whatsoever. Their claim is pure speculation and a fabrication inherited from their forefathers.

The phrase “right before their eyes” highlights the absurdity of their certainty. They speak with such conviction about the unseen, yet they were never there.

This is a recurring Quranic method: to challenge baseless claims by demanding evidence that the claimant logically cannot provide. It exposes that the pagan belief system is built on nothing but assumption and blind following.

The mention of “We created” also establishes that the angels are created beings, not divine offspring. Allah is the sole Creator; the angels are His handiwork, submissive servants who are neither male nor female in the sense of having gender as a biological function, or they are a creation whose reality is known only to Allah, but the crucial point is that assigning them a specific gender and calling them Allah’s children is a fabrication without any foundation. For the Meccans, this question stripped away any pretense of divine authority for their idolatry.

For the believer, this verse underscores the principle that matters of the unseen must be based on revelation, not on speculation or inherited myths. We accept what Allah tells us about the angels—that they are honoured servants—and we reject what has no evidence. It also instills humility about the unseen; we cannot speak of it without knowledge from the Creator.

=151=

After asking the rhetorical questions, the Quran now issues a direct verdict on their claims. “Indeed, it is one of their outrageous fabrications to say.

” The word “indeed” (a-lā) is an emphatic particle drawing attention to the seriousness of the statement. The Arabic word “ifk” translated as “fabrications” denotes a severe lie, a slander, something completely made up and contrary to reality.

The phrase “outrageous” captures the additional nuance that this is not a small mistake but a monstrous falsehood. The pagans’ claim that angels are Allah’s daughters is labelled as pure ifk.

This is a harsh condemnation, but it is justified by the gravity of slandering the Creator. Fabricating lies against Allah is one of the greatest sins. By calling it a fabrication, the Quran dismisses any possibility that there is truth or even a reasonable misinterpretation behind it. It is not a matter of scholarly difference; it is a lie.

The verse also notes that this is “one of” their fabrications, implying they have many other false beliefs, but this one is particularly egregious. The directness of the language is meant to shock the pagans into realizing what they are actually doing: they are inventing lies about the Lord of the worlds. For the believers who were constantly bombarded with these pagan claims, this verse provided clear, authoritative language to refute them. It also serves as a timeless warning against attributing to Allah anything that He has not revealed. In our time, people may fabricate ideas about God based on philosophy, mysticism, or cultural myths; all such fabrications are condemned by the Quran. The truth about Allah is only what He has taught us through His messengers. The verse sets up the exact quote of their lie, which follows immediately, leaving no doubt about what is being denounced.

=152=

The specific fabrication is now quoted: “Allah has children.” This concise statement sums up the claim that the angels are Allah’s daughters, and by extension it includes any claim of divine offspring, such as the Christian claim that Jesus is the son of God, or the Jewish claim that Ezra is the son of God. The Quranic rejection is absolute: “They are simply liars.” There is no softening of the language.

The word “simply” (innahum la-kādhibūn) uses an emphatic structure, affirming without any doubt that they are deliberate liars. The claim that Allah has children is a falsehood that strikes at the very heart of tawheed. It implies that the Creator has a need, a partner, or a lineage, all of which are impossible for the Self-Sufficient, Eternal One. The Meccan pagans did not necessarily believe that Allah had children in the biological sense through procreation, but they called the angels His daughters as a term of honour, yet the Quran refutes even that metaphorical usage because it implies division in the divine essence and leads to worship of the creation. The accusation of lying is severe because the pagans had no evidence for their claim; they were merely repeating what their forefathers said.

Furthermore, they themselves were inconsistent, as the next verse will highlight. The verse reinforces that truth is not determined by majority opinion or cultural heritage but by revelation and reason. For the believers, this clear declaration fortified their faith against the pressure to compromise with polytheistic beliefs. It also teaches that when it comes to the fundamental doctrines of faith, there is no room for ambiguity or polite hedging; falsehood must be called out as falsehood. The starkness of “they are simply liars” is a divine judgment that should inspire a healthy fear of inventing anything about Allah without knowledge.

=153=

The surah continues to dismantle the pagan claim by exposing its sheer irrationality. “Has He chosen daughters over sons?

” The question is rhetorical and loaded with sarcasm aimed at the pagans’ own values. As mentioned earlier, the pagan Arabs regarded daughters as inferior and shameful for themselves, while sons were a source of pride, strength, and honour. If they themselves prefer sons over daughters, how can they believe that the Lord of the worlds, the Most High, would choose for Himself what they deem to be the lesser, inferior option, leaving the superior for them? Is Allah, in their imagination, picking the daughters and leaving the sons for the humans? This would imply either that Allah is foolish or that they are more discerning than Allah—both conclusions being blasphemous.

The question forces them to confront the contradiction between their cultural values and their theological claims. It also subtly defends the dignity of women: the Quran does not say that daughters are inherently inferior, but it uses the pagans’ own prejudice against them. If they insist on their prejudice, their doctrine is absurd. If they abandon their prejudice, then the whole foundation of their idolatry collapses because they no longer have a reason to worship what they consider equal.

The question is a masterful use of logic to corner the opponent. For the believer, it shows the importance of consistency in one’s worldview. False beliefs often contain internal contradictions that, when exposed, reveal their bankruptcy. It also teaches that Allah’s attributes are perfect and beyond any comparison with human attributes. He is far above having children, needing nothing, and His choices are based on infinite wisdom, not on human biases. The verse is part of a sustained attack on the idea of divine offspring, leading to the next challenge to use reason.

=154=

Following the exposure of their contradictory logic, the verse expresses astonishment at their thought process. “What is the matter with you? How do you judge?

” This is a sharp rebuke, a rhetorical expression of bewilderment at their irrational judgment. The phrase “What is the matter with you?” (mā lakum) is used in Arabic to express amazement at someone’s strange behaviour or faulty reasoning. It suggests that there is something deeply wrong with their mental or spiritual state that has led them to such a preposterous conclusion. Then “How do you judge?” questions the very basis of their decision-making. On what criteria are they basing their judgment that Allah has daughters, that these daughters are worthy of worship, and that they are intercessors? Their judgment is completely skewed because it is not based on evidence, revelation, or sound reason, but on blind imitation and whims. The verse calls them to step back and critically evaluate their own cognitive process.

This is an appeal to the innate human intelligence (fiṭrah) that recognizes contradiction and falsehood. The pagans were not unintelligent people; they were merchants, poets, and orators. But their intelligence was clouded by tradition and self-interest. The verse essentially says: use your minds! How can you come to such a verdict? For the believers, this verse is an encouragement to be people of sound judgment who base their beliefs on clear evidence, not on cultural pressures. It teaches that we must constantly audit our own judgments: are we judging according to the Quran and Sunnah, or according to what is popular and inherited?

The question “How do you judge?” is timeless, challenging every individual to examine their standards and to abandon any judgment that contradicts divine guidance. It leads naturally into the next verse, which asks if they will not be mindful, appealing to the same faculty of reason and conscience.

=155=

After the sharp rebuke, the verse softens into an invitation to reflection. “Will you not then be mindful?

” The Arabic word “tadhakkarūn” comes from dhikr, meaning remembrance, mindfulness, and taking heed. The question suggests that the truth is already imprinted in their souls and that the arguments presented should be enough to awaken that dormant knowledge. Mindfulness means connecting the dots: you know that you prefer sons; you claim Allah has daughters; you have no evidence; you realize the contradiction; so will you not then be mindful and correct your belief? It is a merciful pause in the relentless logical assault, offering them a chance to save themselves by simply being mindful and returning to the truth. The Quran frequently uses this approach: after presenting proofs and exposing falsehood, it appeals to the heart and conscience. The faculty of mindfulness is what separates the guided from the heedless. The pagans had become so engrossed in their idolatrous traditions that they had stopped being mindful of the simple truths of reason and the innate knowledge of Allah’s oneness. This verse calls them back to that mindfulness.

For the believer, it is a reminder that the Quran is full of signs that should increase our mindfulness. Reading these verses should not be a mechanical recitation but a catalyst for deep reflection on the greatness of Allah and the absurdity of any belief that detracts from His perfection.

The question also implies that if they do not become mindful, there is something blocking their fitrah—perhaps arrogance or vested interest. The believer must guard against such blocks by constantly engaging in dhikr and self-evaluation. The progression from “What is the matter with you?” to “How do you judge?” to “Will you not then be mindful?” is a perfect psychological and spiritual trajectory: exposing the error, questioning the judgment, and inviting the healing of mindfulness. It is a model of how daʿwah should progress, combining logical rigor with heartfelt appeal.

=156=

The surah now issues a direct challenge: produce your evidence. “Or do you have any compelling proof?

” The word “compelling proof” translates “sulṭān mubīn,” which literally means a clear authority or a manifest warrant. This refers to a definitive proof, whether rational or revealed, that can justify their claims about Allah having daughters or angels being female. It is an open invitation to bring forth their strongest argument. The rhetorical structure “Or do you have” implies that after all the logical contradictions exposed, the only thing that could possibly salvage their position is a solid proof. But the challenge is laced with the certainty that they possess no such proof. If they had any scripture from a previous prophet, any authentic tradition, any rational demonstration, they would have presented it. Their silence is deafening.

This verse establishes a fundamental Islamic principle: beliefs, especially those concerning the divine, must be based on proof, not on conjecture, blind imitation, or cultural heritage. Allah Himself, through the Quran, provides proofs for tawheed, while the polytheists have nothing but “ifk” (fabrications). The challenge also highlights the mercy of Allah, who does not demand belief without evidence but rather provides clear proofs and then asks the deniers to bring their own if they are truthful. For the Meccan pagans, this was a humiliating exposure of their intellectual poverty. They prided themselves on their eloquence and wisdom, yet they could not produce a single compelling proof for the core of their religion.

For the believer, this verse instills confidence that Islam is a faith rooted in clear proofs—the Quran, the miracles, the rational arguments for tawheed—and it is not a blind faith. It also serves as a methodology for evaluating any claim: demand a “sulṭān mubīn. ” If a person makes a religious claim, ask for their proof from divine revelation. If they cannot produce it, their claim is worthless. This protects the faith from innovations and superstitions.

=157=

The challenge is intensified by specifying the type of proof they might claim to have. “Then bring us your scripture, if what you say is true!” The pagans had no revealed book, but the challenge is framed as a taunt and a demonstration of the impossibility of their task. If they were truthful in claiming that their idolatrous beliefs were sanctioned by God, they must have received some divine scripture. Allah demands that they produce this supposed scripture.

The word “your scripture” (kitābakum) implies a book from Allah that authorizes their polytheism. The very idea is absurd because all revealed scriptures, from the scrolls of Abraham to the Torah and the Gospel, preach pure monotheism. There is no scripture that says Allah has daughters or that angels are female and should be worshipped. By asking them to bring such a book, the Quran highlights that their religion is entirely man-made, devoid of any heavenly origin. This also indirectly vindicates the Prophet Muhammad, who came with a clear Scripture—the Quran—which consistently condemns shirk. The challenge “if what you say is true” is a standard Quranic phrase that puts the burden of proof on the claimant. It also implies that their words are false, as they will never be able to meet the challenge. For the Meccans, who were familiar with the concept of scripture from the Jews and Christians, this argument was devastating because they had always admitted they had no book.

For the believer, this verse underscores the centrality of scripture as the source of authentic religious teaching. Any belief or practice that contradicts the Quran and Sunnah is baseless.

The verse also encourages the study and preservation of the Quran, as it is the true Scripture that refutes all falsehood. The challenge to bring their scripture is ultimately an invitation to abandon their fabricated religion and embrace the divine book that has been sent.

=158=

The surah now adds another dimension of the pagans’ false beliefs. “They have also established a marital relationship between Him and the jinn.” Some factions of the pagan Arabs, particularly from certain tribes, claimed that the jinn were related to Allah, even that He had taken spouses from among the jinn and that the angels were the offspring of this union. This was an even more grotesque fabrication, attributing to the Lord of the worlds a marital bond with a creation.

The word “marital relationship” (nasab) signifies kinship through marriage or lineage. By claiming such a thing, they were anthropomorphizing Allah in the most degraded way, imagining Him as a being who takes a spouse and begets children, similar to pagan mythologies where gods marry and have divine offspring. The verse declares that the jinn themselves know well that those who say this will be brought for punishment. The jinn are the very beings they are slandering, yet the jinn, who are accountable creatures, know that this claim is a lie and that those who spread it will face divine judgment.

This is a powerful refutation: even the jinn they supposedly revere disown this belief and know its consequences. It indicates that the truth about Allah is known among the unseen creation, and the pagans are far more astray than the jinn in this regard.

The phrase “will certainly be brought” echoes the surah’s earlier language about the disbelievers being brought to punishment. The jinn are witnesses against them on the Day of Judgment. For the believers, this verse reinforces that shirk can take many vile forms, and all of them are far removed from the majesty of Allah. It also reminds us that the unseen beings, like the jinn, are not to be worshipped or elevated; they themselves are servants of Allah who will be judged. The verse serves to completely dismantle the interconnected pagan myths, leaving no shred of legitimacy to any of their claims about divine progeny or partnership.

=159=

After exposing the fabrications, the absurdities, and the contradictions, the surah breaks into the declaration of tanzīh (divine transcendence). “Glorified is Allah far above what they claim!

” The Arabic “Subḥāna Allāh” is a profound phrase meaning that Allah is utterly perfect, free from any flaw, defect, or association. This glorification is the natural and necessary response to hearing such vile claims about the Creator. It is as if the entire discourse leads up to this moment of clearing Allah’s name from all the slander they have uttered.

The phrase “far above” (ʿammā yaṣifūn) indicates a complete, unbridgeable distance between His perfect reality and their false descriptions. Whatever they claim—daughters, sons, spouses, partners, weaknesses—Allah is elevated far above it all, in His essence, attributes, and actions. This glorification is not just a statement of belief; it is an act of worship. By saying “Subḥāna Allāh,” the believer purifies his heart from any improper thought about Allah and affirms His absolute perfection.

In the context of the surah, which began with angels glorifying Allah and lining up in ranks, this glorification brings the thematic arc full circle. The angels themselves glorify Allah, and now the believers are taught to do the same in response to the pagans’ blasphemies. For the Meccans, this was a direct refutation of their beliefs and a call to join in the glorification of the One true God.

For the believer, this verse is a constant reminder to maintain the purity of tawheed in the heart and on the tongue. Whenever we encounter ideas that compromise Allah’s oneness or perfection, we should immediately resort to tasbīḥ, seeking refuge in the glorification of our Lord.

The verse also carries a tone of reassurance: no matter what lies people invent, Allah’s majesty remains untouched and undiminished. It is the lie that will perish, and the truth of Subḥāna Allāh will abide forever.

=160=

The surah inserts the crucial exception once more, but here its meaning is slightly nuanced by the context. “But not the chosen servants of Allah.” This phrase has appeared as a refrain throughout the surah, indicating that the chosen servants are saved from punishment and granted honourable mention. In this immediate context, scholars offer two interconnected interpretations. First, it means that the chosen servants of Allah—the prophets, the angels, and the sincere believers—do not ascribe such false claims to Allah. While the pagans fabricate lies about Allah having children or consorts, the chosen servants glorify Him and declare Him far above such things. They are the ones who say “Subḥāna Allāh” sincerely. Second, it can be seen as a continuation of the refutation: the pagans who are brought for punishment do not include the chosen servants, who are exempt from that fate. Both meanings are harmonious. The verse thus draws a clear dividing line between the liars and the truthful, between those who slander Allah and those who glorify Him. The chosen servants are the ones with pure hearts, like Abraham, who came to his Lord with a sound heart, and like the angels who are lined up in ranks glorifying Allah. They are the ones who know the truth about their Lord and speak it. The Meccan Muslims, by reciting and believing these verses, are counted among these chosen servants, as they disown the pagan claims. The verse encourages the believer to ensure they are on the side of the chosen servants in both word and deed, actively glorifying Allah and refuting falsehood. It also beautifully sets up the following verses where the angels themselves will speak, declaring their stations and their glorification, proving that they are indeed the chosen servants who would never claim divinity or kinship with Allah. The exception provides a moment of clarity and hope amidst the condemnation, reaffirming that the path of safety and truth is the path of sincere worship and transcendence.
