=61=

After declaring the reward of the chosen servants to be the ultimate triumph, the Quran now issues a powerful call to action: “For such honour all should strive.” The word “honour” points back to the entire description of Paradise—the known provision, the fruits, the honour, the Gardens of Bliss, the thrones, the pure wine, the companions, and the eternal security from death and punishment.

This is not a passive wish; it requires active effort. The verb translated as “strive” comes from the Arabic root that implies a vigorous competition, a race in which every participant pushes themselves to the utmost to win the prize. It is the same concept found elsewhere in the Quran, where believers are encouraged to “vie with one another in good deeds” and to “race towards forgiveness from your Lord.” The honour is described as something that must be competed for because it is the most valuable prize imaginable, and not everyone will attain it without sincere effort. In the Meccan context, the pagans competed in worldly matters—wealth, poetry, tribal glory—but this verse redirects human ambition towards the eternal. The Prophet (peace be upon him) and his companions were enduring hardship and mockery; this call reminded them that the struggle itself was part of the race.

The verse also implies that the opportunity is open to all who are willing to strive. No one is excluded by birth or status; the only requirement is sincere effort in faith and righteousness.

For the believer today, this verse is a daily motivator. Every prayer, every act of charity, every resisted temptation is a stride in that competition.

The word “all” (al-mutanāfisūn) grammatically designates those who engage in this competition, and it suggests that there is an elite group of people who take the Hereafter seriously enough to make it their primary ambition. The verse connects beautifully with the preceding scene of the believer looking at his companion in Hell and realizing the value of divine grace. That realization should push every listener to compete for the honour that will never perish. The ultimate triumph is not awarded casually; it is earned through a lifetime of striving, and the Quran here sounds the starting gun for all who wish to run.

=62=

Having described the bliss of Paradise and urged people to strive for it, the surah now poses a stark rhetorical question to the deniers: “Is this bliss a better accommodation or the tree of Zaqqûm?” The “bliss” refers to the detailed picture of Paradise just painted—the honoured seating, the delicious drinks, the eternal peace. The “accommodation” is the place of dwelling and hospitality. In Arabic custom, honouring a guest with a fine dwelling and generous food was a mark of nobility. So Allah asks the Meccan pagans, who prided themselves on their hospitality, to compare that heavenly hospitality with the “tree of Zaqqûm.” Zaqqûm is a specific tree in Hell, described in terrifying detail in the coming verses. Its very name sounded strange and ominous to the Arab ear. The disbelievers mocked the mention of Zaqqûm when it was first revealed. Abu Jahl, the arch-enemy of Islam, once heard the verses about Zaqqûm and sarcastically called for dates and butter, telling his companions to “eat zaqqûm,” insinuating that Muhammad was fabricating nonsense. But the Quran responds by taking the mockery head-on, asking them to seriously consider: which accommodation is truly better?

The question is rhetorical because no sane person would choose the tree of Zaqqûm over the Gardens of Bliss. Yet the disbelievers, by their actions and rejection, were effectively making that choice. The verse forces the listener to engage in a mental comparison that leads to an inescapable conclusion. It is a teaching method that appeals to reason: look at the two options laid before you, one full of delight and honour, the other full of horror and pain, and then reflect on which one you are working towards.

For the believer, this question reinforces gratitude for guidance and a renewed determination to remain on the path leading to the blissful accommodation. It also serves as a da‘wah tool: we can ask ourselves and others, what is our eternal accommodation going to be?

=63=

The verse immediately explains the deeper purpose behind the terrifying tree: “We have surely made it a test for the wrongdoers.” The tree of Zaqqûm is not merely a punishment in the Hereafter; its very mention in the Quran serves as a trial for the disbelievers in this world. When the verses about a tree growing in the midst of fire were recited, the wrongdoers scoffed. They argued, how can a tree grow in fire? Fire burns trees. Muhammad must be crazy. This reaction exposed their materialistic mindset and their refusal to accept that Allah, who created the laws of physics, can make fire cool and safe for a tree if He wills, just as He made the fire cool and safe for Abraham. Their mockery of the unseen descriptions of Hell became a criterion separating the sincere from the arrogant. The test lies in the response: the believers say, “We believe; it is all from our Lord,” while the wrongdoers mock and reject, thereby increasing their own burden of sin.

The phrase “for the wrongdoers” indicates that the test is specifically targeted at those who already incline towards oppression and disbelief. The description of Zaqqûm, instead of convincing them, becomes a stumbling block that confirms their rejection.

This is a consistent pattern in the Quran: the same verses that are a healing and mercy for believers become a loss and misguidance for wrongdoers. For the believer, this verse teaches the proper attitude towards unseen realities described in revelation: we accept them as Allah describes them, without subjecting them to the limited standards of our worldly experience. The fire of Hell and the tree within it operate by different rules, governed by the Creator’s command. The early Muslims faced ridicule for these beliefs, yet they held firm, and their faith was strengthened by the test. Today, believers still face tests when divine descriptions seem beyond scientific comprehension; the correct response is humble submission and trust in the One who created all laws.

=64=

The surah now begins the actual description of Zaqqûm, starting with its origin: “Indeed, it is a tree that grows in the depths of Hell.” The word “depths” (aṣl) means the very bottom, the root, the foundation of Hell. This tree is not a superficial plant at the edge of the fire; it springs from the deepest, most intensely blazing core of Jahannam. Its roots are nourished by the molten fire and the foul substances that fuel Hell’s torment. This origin symbolizes the essence of evil and punishment. The tree draws its life from the place of ultimate suffering, and it becomes a manifestation of that suffering in a tangible form that the damned must interact with. The fact that it is a “tree” is itself a mockery of the trees of Paradise. In Paradise, trees provide shade and delicious fruits; in Hell, this tree provides agony. The contrast with the earlier “Gardens of Bliss” is deliberate and sharp. Some scholars, including Ibn Abbas, explained that Zaqqûm is a tree whose roots are in the bottom of Hell and whose branches rise up through its various levels, so that the inhabitants of different levels will encounter its fruits.

The phrase “grows” indicates that it is a living organism, created by Allah for the specific function of punishment, renewing itself to provide a continuous supply of torment. For the Meccan pagans who were so attached to the date palms and lush gardens of their valleys, this image served as a horrifying antithesis. The believer reflecting on this verse feels a healthy fear that pushes them to seek refuge in Allah from Hell, and a renewed appreciation for the trees and fruits of this world, which are signs of mercy, not instruments of torture.

=65=

After describing the tree’s location, the verse depicts its fruit: “bearing fruit like devils’ heads.” This is one of the most striking and terrifying similes in the Quran. The fruit of Zaqqûm is not round, pleasant berries or dates; it resembles the heads of shayateen. The Arabs had a mental image of devils as hideous, grotesque beings with ugly, deformed heads. Some early commentators said that the fruit looks like the heads of serpents, which were commonly associated with evil. Others said it resembles a particularly ugly and thorny plant found in the desert. The point is that the fruit is visually repulsive, inspiring immediate disgust and terror.

Yet, the damned will be driven by extreme hunger to approach and eat it. The comparison to devils’ heads also carries a symbolic meaning: just as the devils are the source of misguidance and evil whisperings, the fruit of Zaqqûm represents the consequence of following those whisperings. Those who took the devils as allies in the world will now find that their sustenance in Hell takes the very shape of those allies, as if to mock them.

This verse also connects to the earlier passages about the devils being pelted with flames when they try to listen to the heavenly assembly. Now their image becomes the food of those who followed them. The detailed, almost nightmarish description is meant to shock the listener out of complacency.

For the believer, it serves as a vivid deterrent: the temporary illicit pleasures of this world, which the devils beautify, will transform into something monstrous in the Hereafter if not repented from. The verse is a testament to the power of Quranic imagery to convey spiritual truths in a way that penetrates the heart and leaves a lasting impression.

=66=

After the visual horror of the fruit, the verse moves to the actual consumption: “The evildoers will certainly be left to eat from it, filling up their bellies with it.” The phrase “be left to” carries a sense of being compelled or forced. The inhabitants of Hell are not sitting down to a meal by choice; extreme, unbearable hunger will drive them to eat this abomination. It is reported in hadith that the people of Hell will experience such starvation that they will have no option but to consume the filth and torment provided. They will eat the fruit of Zaqqûm, and instead of satisfying their hunger, it will cause more agony. The verse says they will fill their bellies with it, indicating that they will eat greedily, perhaps because the hunger is so intense that they desperately try to stuff themselves, yet the food brings no relief. As they eat, it will boil in their stomachs like molten metal. Another verse in the Quran describes that Zaqqûm will boil in their bellies like the boiling of scalding water. So the act of eating, which is normally associated with pleasure and sustenance, becomes an act of self-torment. The “evildoers” here are specifically those who committed crimes of disbelief and oppression.

The word “certainly” assures that this is not a possibility but a guaranteed outcome. For the Meccan pagans who took great pride in their feasts and hospitality, this reversal is profound: the generous hosts of the world will become the desperate, humiliated eaters of poison-fruit. The believer is reminded of the value of hunger experienced in voluntary fasting; the temporary pangs of fasting in obedience to Allah are infinitely better than the eternal, forced eating of Zaqqûm. This verse should motivate a careful watch over what we spiritually consume in this world, ensuring we fill our souls with Quran and remembrance rather than the poison of sin that may foreshadow the fruit of Hell.

=67=

The torment does not stop with the solid food; it is compounded with a liquid punishment. “Then on top of that they will be given a blend of boiling drink.” After they have eaten the fruit of Zaqqûm and their stomachs are churning with its heat, they will be seized by a raging thirst. In response, they will be offered a drink, but it is not water to cool them down. It is a “blend” (shūb), a mixture of various foul and scalding fluids. Other Quranic verses describe this drink as ḥamīm—boiling water—and ghassāq—a festering pus. The blend indicates that several types of tormenting liquids are combined into one cup.

The phrase “on top of that” shows the layered nature of the punishment: one agony piles upon another. The drink will burn their faces and tear their intestines as it goes down. In the world, the disbelievers enjoyed blending fine wines and creating delicious beverages; in Hell, the blend they receive is unimaginably horrific.

The mention of being “given” the drink suggests that just as the people of Paradise are served with honour, the people of Hell are served with contempt. The angels of punishment will force them to drink, adding humiliation to the physical pain. The contrast with the “crystal-white, delicious to drink” wine of Paradise, passed around in flowing streams, is complete. The Meccan pagans who boasted about their drinking sessions are being warned that their next drink will be from the streams of Hell if they persist in disbelief.

For the believer, this verse highlights the mercy of Allah in forbidding intoxicants in this world; He wants to save us from even the faintest reminder of the drink of the damned. The torment described is not sensationalism but a merciful warning meant to steer us towards the pure drinks of Paradise.

=68=

After the cycle of eating and drinking, the verse says, “Then they will ultimately return to their place in Hell.” The word “return” indicates that this is a continuous, cyclical torment. They are in Hell, then they are perhaps driven out to certain stations for additional punishment like eating from Zaqqûm and drinking the boiling blend, and then they are herded back into the core of Hell. The “place” is their assigned abode where the fire continually engulfs them.

The phrase “ultimately return” suggests that no matter what momentary variation the punishment takes, the constant underlying reality is the Fire. Some scholars mention that the inhabitants of Hell will be moved between different levels and types of punishment, but they always return to the primary blaze. This cyclical nature adds a psychological dimension to the suffering: there is no escape, no final destination of relief, only a return to the baseline torment.

The Arabic word ma‘ād, translated as “return” or “place of return,” can also mean the final destination. It signals that Hell is their permanent home. The verse ties the previous descriptions together: the tree, the eating, the drinking, all are part of the journey within Hell that ultimately circles back to the heart of the fire. For the Meccan merchants who were used to travelling out on trade caravans and then returning home, this image would resonate; but their true return will be to Hell, not to the comforts of Mecca, if they die in disbelief. The believer reflects on the concept of “return” in a positive light: our ultimate return should be to the mercy and gardens of our Lord.

The verse teaches us to prepare for our final return now, ensuring that our deeds lead us to the blissful accommodation rather than the repetitive torment.

=69=

The surah transitions from describing Hell’s hospitality to diagnosing one of the root causes of disbelief that leads people there. “Indeed, they found their forefathers astray.” The “they” refers to the Meccan pagans and, by extension, to all who follow the same pattern. Their primary justification for their idolatry was that they were following the religion of their ancestors. They inherited a set of beliefs and practices without ever subjecting them to critical examination in the light of truth.

The word “found” indicates a passive discovery; they were born into a society where misguidance was the norm, and they accepted it as the comfortable default. The Quran repeatedly identifies blind following of forefathers as one of the greatest obstacles to accepting divine guidance. When the Prophet called them to tawheed, their immediate response was, “We found our fathers upon a religion, and we are following in their footsteps. ” This is a universal human tendency: traditions and ancestral customs carry immense emotional weight, and breaking away from them feels like a betrayal of family and identity.

However, the Quran insists that truth is not determined by majority vote or antiquity; it is determined by revelation. The forefathers being described as “astray” means they were objectively misguided, regardless of their sincerity or the respect they command. This verse sets up a critical lesson: every individual is responsible for their own guidance. One cannot hide behind the mistakes of previous generations on the Day of Judgment. The Meccan pagans are being warned that their ancestors are not a valid excuse; rather, their ancestors’ misguidance should have been a reason for them to search for the truth. For the modern believer, this verse is a call to examine cultural and familial practices in the light of Quran and Sunnah, and to have the courage to abandon inherited errors.

=70=

Not only did they find their forefathers astray, but they actively and eagerly followed them. “So they rushed in their footsteps!” The verb “rushed” indicates haste, enthusiasm, and lack of deliberation. They did not pause to think or evaluate; they simply sped along the path their ancestors had laid out, as if racing to catch up. This depicts a kind of mindless imitation that goes beyond passive acceptance into active, energetic emulation. The footsteps suggest that they walked exactly where their forefathers walked, replicating their errors in precise detail. There is a tragic irony here: the same energy and commitment they should have used to seek the truth, they instead used to entrench themselves in falsehood.

The verse also reflects the Meccan society where tribal pride was paramount. To question the ancestors was to question the tribe’s honour. So they rushed headlong into defending the ancestral ways against the Prophet’s message. For the believers, this verse is a warning against the psychology of the crowd and the pull of tradition. It reminds us that the path to Hell is often crowded with those who simply followed others without critical thought. The speed of their following indicates that they did not want to hear counter-arguments; they plugged their ears and accelerated. The lesson is to be a person of insight and deliberation, who weighs every tradition against the scale of divine guidance, even if that means walking against the current. The rushing also amplifies the tragedy: they were not slowly misled; they were sprinting towards their own destruction, thinking they were honouring their heritage.

=71=

The verse broadens the historical perspective to show that this phenomenon is not unique to the Quraysh. “And surely most of the earlier generations had strayed before them.” Throughout human history, the pattern has repeated: the majority of people in nation after nation fell into misguidance, worshipping idols, rejecting their messengers, and following their whims.

This is a sobering reality check. The Quraysh might have felt secure in their majority status, thinking, “How could so many of our ancestors and so many nations be wrong?” The Quran answers that the majority is not a proof of truth; in fact, the majority throughout history have been astray.

This is a crucial principle: truth is not established by popularity. The earlier generations include the people of Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, and the people of Lot, whose stories will soon be recounted in this very surah. Their misguidance and destruction serve as powerful signs.

The verse also provides comfort to the Prophet and the small community of believers who might have felt isolated. Being a minority does not mean being wrong. The “earlier generations” (al-awwalīn) had their messengers, their warnings, and their chance, but they chose misguidance. The Quraysh are now following the same script. The believer should understand that standing for truth often means standing with the few, while falsehood enjoys the numbers. The verse encourages us to seek the company of the righteous, even if they are few, and to take warning from the fate of the misguided multitudes who came before. History is a teacher for those who reflect; the ruins and the stories are lessons that the majority path often leads to ruin when it deviates from revelation.

=72=

To eliminate any possible excuse, the verse adds, “although We had certainly sent warners among them.” The earlier generations were not left in ignorance; Allah, in His mercy, sent messengers to warn them about the consequences of their misguidance. The “warners” are the prophets who came with clear signs and teachings, calling their people to monotheism and righteous living. The Quraysh themselves had received a warner—the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him)—whose very presence among them was a fulfilment of this divine pattern.

The phrase “certainly sent” emphasizes the consistency and justice of Allah’s approach. No nation is punished without first receiving a clear warning. This refutes any claim that the disbelievers might make on the Day of Judgment that they were unaware. The warners were sent “among them,” meaning from their own communities, speaking their language, understanding their culture, and living as examples. This made the message accessible and undeniable. The rejection of such warners is thus an act of extreme arrogance and ingratitude.

The verse also serves as a reminder of the high status of prophets; they are the lifelines extended by Allah to humanity. When a people reject their warner, they sever their own connection to salvation. For the Meccan pagans, this was a direct rebuke: you have your warner among you, yet you rush after your astray forefathers.

For the believer, it is a call to deeply appreciate the Prophet Muhammad and to respond to his warnings with obedience and love. The verse also indicates that the destruction of previous nations was not arbitrary but followed their deliberate rejection of the warners. This clarifies the justice behind the ruins the Quraysh passed by on their trade journeys.

=73=

The verse invites reflection on history with a direct command: “See then what was the end of those who had been warned.” The address is to the Prophet, the believers, and by extension the disbelievers who are being challenged to look at the evidence. The “end” refers to the final outcome and punishment that befell the previous nations after they rejected their warners. The ruins of the people of ʿĀd, Thamūd, and the towns of Lot were visible to the Meccan travellers. They would pass by these decimated remains on their journeys to Syria and Yemen. The command to “see” can be both a physical seeing and an intellectual seeing—that is, to observe and then ponder and take heed. History is not a series of random events; it is filled with divine signs showing the pattern of how Allah deals with those who reject His messengers after clear warnings. The verse directly challenges the notion that the ancestors should be blindly followed; their end was often destruction, so why emulate those who met a terrible fate?

For the believer, this instills a sense of the importance of learning from history. The stories in the Quran are not mere tales but lessons to be internalized. The surah will soon recount several of these historical examples—Noah, Abraham, Moses, Lot, and others—to make the lesson concrete. The verse asks us to “see” the pattern: warnings were given, rejection followed, and then punishment descended. The only exception were those who believed. This pattern is unchangeable. It applies to individuals and nations.

Thus, the believer must constantly evaluate their own response to the warnings they receive through the Quran and Sunnah. Are we heeding the warnings, or are we repeating the mistakes of those who saw the truth and turned away, only to face a terrible end?

=74=

Once again, the surah inserts the crucial exception to the grim historical pattern. “But not the chosen servants of Allah.” Among those earlier generations who received warners and were destroyed for their rejection, there was a group that was saved: the chosen servants. These are the prophets themselves and the sincere believers who followed them. When the flood drowned the people of Noah, the chosen servant Noah and the believers on the Ark were delivered. When fire was sent upon the people of Lot, the chosen servant Lot and his family—except his wife—were saved. This exception is a constant refrain in the surah, a thread that runs through the narratives of the prophets. It emphasizes that while divine punishment is certain for the rejecters, divine mercy and salvation are equally certain for the faithful. The “chosen servants” are those who purified their worship for Allah alone and held fast to the guidance brought by the messengers. This verse serves to prevent despair among the believers who live in corrupt societies. Even if the majority rushes after misguidance, the sincere minority are seen, valued, and will be delivered by their Lord. The Meccan Muslims, small in number and persecuted, could cling to this promise: they were the chosen servants of that era, just as the followers of Noah were in his. The exception also clarifies that the earlier verses about “most of the earlier generations” straying do not mean that everyone went astray without exception; Allah always preserved a remnant of truth.

This is a source of hope and a call to be part of that remnant. The verse connects seamlessly to the upcoming stories, where the pattern of warning, rejection, punishment, and the salvation of the chosen servants will be illustrated in detail. It reminds the believer that the path of the chosen servants is the path of safety, and we should strive to be among them by emulating their sincerity and obedience.

=75=

With this verse, the surah begins a series of concise, powerful narratives about several prophets, starting with Noah. “Indeed, Noah cried out to Us, and how excellent are We in responding!” Noah is one of the greatest messengers, the first major prophet sent to a disbelieving people. He called his nation for 950 years with patience and persistence, yet they persisted in mockery and rejection.

Finally, after centuries of abuse and seeing that no more would believe, Noah turned to Allah with a heartfelt supplication. He called out, asking for divine help and for the destruction of the disbelievers who had become a source of corruption. The verse does not detail the supplication here, but elsewhere in the Quran we hear his words: “My Lord, do not leave upon the earth from among the disbelievers an inhabitant.

” The phrase “cried out to Us” indicates a direct, desperate, and intimate plea. It was a call born of exhaustion and complete reliance on Allah after exhausting all human effort.

The second part of the verse, “how excellent are We in responding,” is a magnificent praise that Allah gives Himself for the quality of His response. It assures that Allah not only hears but answers in the best possible way. The response was swift, decisive, and perfectly suited to the situation. This teaches the believer the immense value of supplication, especially when all worldly means seem futile. Noah’s victory did not come through military might or majority support; it came through turning to Allah. The “excellent” response includes the careful planning of the Ark, the rescue of the believers, and the cleansing of the earth from rampant disbelief. The verse sets up the narrative with a focus on Allah’s attribute of perfect responsiveness, encouraging the listener to always turn to Him in times of distress, knowing that He is the best of responders.

=76=

The response to Noah’s cry is now summarized. “We delivered him and his family from the great distress.” The “great distress” (al-karb al-ʿaẓīm) refers to the overwhelming situation of the flood, but also to the long years of psychological distress Noah endured while being rejected and mocked by his people. When the flood waters rose and covered the mountains, it was a catastrophe of unimaginable scale. In that moment of universal drowning, Allah’s deliverance of Noah and the believers aboard the Ark was a spectacular manifestation of mercy.

The mention of “his family” includes his believing wife and his three believing sons—Shem, Ham, and Japheth—and the small group of followers who believed with them. It does not include his other son who refused to board the Ark and was among the drowned, nor his wife if we consider some narrations that she was not among the saved (though the Quran does not explicitly state that about Noah’s wife; it is mentioned about Lot’s wife and Noah’s son). The main point is that Noah’s household of faith was saved. The deliverance from “great distress” echoes the recurring theme of the surah: the chosen servants are rescued from the collective punishment. This deliverance was a physical reality, but it also holds a spiritual lesson: faith in Allah is the true Ark that saves one from the floods of worldly and otherworldly punishment. The Meccan pagans, facing the Prophet’s warning of a coming judgment, are being told to board the Ark of faith before the flood of divine wrath arrives. The believer is reminded that no matter how severe the distress, Allah’s deliverance is near for those who remain faithful.

The phrase “We delivered” emphasizes that salvation is entirely in Allah’s hands; human planning is only a means.

=77=

After the flood, a new phase of human history began, and Allah highlights a critical outcome: “and made his descendants the sole survivors.” The flood was not merely a local disaster; it was a global reset in which all disbelievers were wiped out. Only Noah’s believing lineage remained. The verse states that his descendants (dhurriyyah) became the sole survivors, meaning that all human beings after the flood trace their lineage back to Noah.

This is why Noah is often called the “second father” of humanity. The fact that the survivors were limited to his descendants shows that Noah’s faith became the seed of all future humanity. This was a tremendous honour granted to him in addition to his physical deliverance. It also signifies the victory of monotheism in that epoch: the earth was cleansed of idolatry, and the new civilization began on a foundation of tawheed, even though later generations gradually deviated. The Meccan pagans, who prided themselves on their noble lineage tracing back to Ishmael and Abraham, were actually descendants of Noah as well, so they were being reminded that their very existence was a product of a believer’s salvation. This placed an obligation on them to follow the faith of their forefather Noah.

The phrase “sole survivors” underscores the totality of the destruction; no other group survived the flood. This magnifies the severity of the punishment that befell the rejecters and the exclusivity of the mercy for the believers.

For the believer, this verse inspires the hope that even a small group of faithful can become the foundation for a righteous future. It also serves as a warning that pride in lineage is meaningless if one’s beliefs and actions contradict those of the righteous ancestors.

=78=

The surah now speaks of the legacy granted to Noah after his death. “And We blessed him with honourable mention among later generations.

” This is a profound gift: Allah ensured that Noah would be spoken of with reverence, praise, and respect by all future nations. When anyone among Jews, Christians, or Muslims mentions Noah, they speak of him as a prophet, a righteous man, a saviour of humanity.

The phrase “honourable mention” (lisāna ṣidq) literally means a truthful and praiseworthy reputation. No matter how much his enemies mocked him during his life, his name is eternally honoured.

This is a comfort for all who are ridiculed for their faith: your reputation among people is not what matters; the lasting honour granted by Allah is what counts. The Meccan pagans were calling the Prophet a mad poet, trying to tarnish his name, but Allah is promising him a similar, if not greater, honourable mention. Today, millions of believers around the world send peace and blessings upon Muhammad every day.

This is the fulfillment of that pattern. The verse teaches that a person’s true legacy is not in the momentary praise of their contemporaries but in the enduring recognition that Allah bestows. The later generations include all the prophets who came after Noah and all the believers who read his story.

This is part of the “excellent response” to Noah’s supplication; his name was elevated instead of being erased. For the believer, the lesson is to focus on earning Allah’s lasting praise rather than the fleeting approval of people.

=79=

The honourable mention takes a specific, beautiful form: the divine greeting of peace. “Peace be upon Noah among all peoples.

” This is the very salutation that Allah Himself bestows, and that the angels and believers extend. The phrase “Peace be upon Noah” is a timeless blessing that echoes through the heavens and the earth. It means safety, honour, and divine mercy are invoked for him forever. The addition “among all peoples” (fī al-ʿālamīn) indicates the universal scope of this peace. It is not limited to one nation or one era; it embraces all nations and all times. Noah’s peace and noble remembrance transcend every boundary. This greeting is not merely a historical note; it is part of the Islamic creed. Muslims are taught to say “peace be upon him” whenever they mention any prophet, including Noah. By sending peace upon Noah, Allah elevates him and consoles him for all the abuse he suffered during his long mission.

The verse also indirectly instructs the believers to send peace upon the prophets, as Allah Himself does. In the context of the surah, which repeatedly emphasizes the distinction between the chosen servants and the wrongdoers, this peace is the ultimate gift for the chosen servant Noah. The deniers and mockers of his time perished in disgrace, while Noah sails eternally on a sea of divine peace. This should inspire the believer to seek the company of those upon whom peace descends.

The verse also creates a beautiful link to the later verses in the surah that will conclude with “Peace be upon the messengers,” showing that Noah is part of this blessed brotherhood.

=80=

The narrative of Noah concludes with a principle that echoes throughout the surah: “Indeed, this is how We reward the good-doers.” The deliverance, the honour, the lasting peace—all of it is the standard reward from Allah for those who excel in goodness (muḥsinīn). The “good-doers” are those who perfect their faith and their actions, who worship Allah as if they see Him, and who strive with patience and excellence in the face of trials. Noah epitomized such excellence through his 950 years of unwavering da‘wah and his ultimate trust in Allah. The verse assures that Noah’s case is not an exception; it is the rule. Every good-doer will receive a reward proportional to their sincerity and effort, both in this world and the Hereafter. The principle “this is how We reward” speaks of an unchanging divine sunnah, a law of mercy and justice that applies to all. This connects perfectly to the earlier verses about the chosen servants and the ultimate triumph. It tells the Meccan believers that their good deeds, no matter how small they seem under the shadow of persecution, are seen and will be rewarded in the same magnificent pattern. It also warns the oppressors that the opposite pattern applies: just as the good-doers are rewarded with salvation and honour, the evildoers are rewarded with destruction and disgrace. The verse serves as a transition, setting up the stories of the other prophets that follow—Abraham, Moses, Elias, Lot, and Jonah—all of whom will receive the same refrain, emphasizing the consistency of Allah’s reward.

For the believer, this verse is a powerful motivator: become a muḥsin, a good-doer, and the same Lord who rewarded Noah will reward you in ways that far exceed your imagination.
