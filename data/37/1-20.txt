=1=

The surah opens with a powerful oath in which Allah swears by certain aspects of His creation to draw attention to a fundamental truth. When the verse begins with “By those lined up in ranks,” the expression points specifically to the angels.

The Arabic word used here for “lined up in ranks” is ṣâffât, a description of orderly rows, much like soldiers standing in perfect formation or worshippers arranged in prayer lines. The angels are presented as beings who stand in such disciplined rows before their Lord, ever ready to carry out His commands without hesitation or disorder.

This image is not merely poetic; it carries a profound message about the nature of servitude and the absolute sovereignty of Allah. In the Meccan context, the pagans believed that angels were daughters of God and sometimes even worshipped them. By swearing an oath upon these very angels described as humble servants standing in ranks, the Quran immediately corrects that misconception. They are not divine offspring; they are created beings assigned their stations, displaying the highest form of obedience. The Prophet (peace be upon him) once told his companions that angels in the heavens line up in rows just as believers do in prayer, continuously glorifying Allah and waiting for His decree. This reminds us that the entire cosmos is structured upon submission to the One Creator. The oath also prepares the listener for the conclusion in the coming verses: that the God who commands such organized hosts must be One and Unique. When a person reflects on the perfect discipline of the angels, it becomes unreasonable to associate partners with the Lord who created and arranged them.

Thus, even this opening phrase dismantles idolatry by presenting a glimpse of the unseen world where all beings willingly bow to Allah’s authority.

=2=

After mentioning the angels in stationary worshipful ranks, the oath moves to another group of angels: “those who diligently drive the clouds.” The word translated as “diligently drive” conveys a strong sense of forceful, determined pushing or herding. These angels are tasked with steering the clouds across the sky according to Allah’s precise command. Rain does not fall randomly; it is part of a meticulously managed system in which angels propel clouds to specific locations, sometimes bringing mercy and sometimes, by divine order, punishment. The polytheists of Mecca witnessed rain, yet they attributed natural phenomena to idols or impersonal forces. By swearing by the angels who drive the clouds, the Quran directs attention to a hidden reality behind what people see every day. The rain that revives barren land is not an autonomous process but a sign of the living God’s continuous involvement in His creation. This connects beautifully with the first oath: the same angels who stand in disciplined ranks in the heavens also actively execute Allah’s will in the physical world. Their tireless movement and diligence underscore that all natural occurrences are ultimately acts of worship performed by submissive creatures. The believer, therefore, should look at a rain-bearing cloud and perceive not a mechanical cycle but the obedient angels fulfilling their role, driving the cloud as a shepherd drives a flock. This awareness shifts one’s entire perspective, turning the sight of gathering clouds into a reminder of the unseen realm and a reason to thank the Lord who sends His servants to provide life-giving water. The oath links the hidden angelic realm with the tangible world, so that even a simple weather event becomes a visible link between the heavens and the earth, all of which declare the Oneness of their Maker.

=3=

The third category in this opening oath consists of angels who “recite the Reminder.” The Reminder refers to the Quran itself or, more broadly, to the revelation and words of Allah that angels are honoured to recite. This shows that angels are not only beings of action, driving clouds and standing in ranks, but also beings of worship through recitation. They constantly engage with divine speech, glorifying and praising Allah without fatigue. Some scholars explain that the angels bring down the Reminder from the Preserved Tablet to the messengers, while others see them as reciting the scriptures in the heavens. Either way, this places the Quran, which was being revealed to the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) in Mecca, within a cosmic context. The very angels who form ranks and direct the clouds are the same ones intimately involved with the divine word. For the Meccan disbelievers who mocked the Quran as poetry or magic, this oath draws a stark contrast: the words they dismiss are so exalted that angels themselves recite them in the celestial realm.

The phrase “recite” implies a continuous, melodious reading, indicating that the heavens are filled with the sound of revelation being proclaimed by pure beings. This naturally elevates the status of the Quran in the heart of the believer. It is not merely a book but the very Reminder that resonates through the highest assemblies of angels. By grouping the reciters of the Reminder with those who stand in ranks and drive the clouds, the oath unites the angelic functions of worship, administration, and transmission of knowledge. All of these duties harmoniously serve the ultimate purpose of declaring Allah’s Oneness. The listener is thus invited to recognize that the message brought by the Prophet is the same heavenly truth that the angels themselves honour.

=4=

After three solemn oaths describing the angels in their various functions, the central point of the surah is delivered: “Surely your God is One!” The oath serves as a buildup, and now the conclusion is stated with absolute clarity. The address “your God” speaks directly to the Prophet and his community, but it extends to all humanity.

The word “One” does not merely mean numerically one; it means unique, singular in His essence, attributes, and actions, having no partner, equal, or rival. Everything the previous oaths portrayed—the perfect ranks of obedient angels, the precise driving of clouds, the heavenly recitation of the Reminder—all of it originates from a single divine will. Such an organized universe cannot have multiple conflicting authorities. This verse is a declaration of tawheed, the central doctrine of Islam, which the Meccan idolaters vehemently rejected. They acknowledged Allah as the Supreme Creator but associated other gods with Him, claiming these deities could intercede or bring them closer to Him. In response, the Quran establishes that the Lord of such magnificent angels and cosmic order is One alone, and He requires no partners.

For believers, this short verse becomes a powerful affirmation. It reminds us that the angels in their breathtaking discipline, the clouds in their precise movement, and the revealed scriptures in their wisdom all point back to the same single source. When a person truly absorbs that “your God is One,” it transforms life: worship is directed solely to Him, hope and fear are attached to none other, and the entire existence is seen as a unified sign pointing to the One Creator.

The use of “surely” intensifies the certainty, leaving no room for doubt after such grand witnesses have been presented.

=5=

After establishing that God is One, the surah expands on His lordship. The verse begins by identifying Him as “the Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between.” This phrase encompasses the entire cosmos, from the vast celestial spheres above to the ground beneath our feet and all that lies between them—the air, the clouds, the birds, the unseen creatures. Lordship means ownership, authority, nurturing, and governance. So the One God is not a distant, uninvolved creator; He actively sustains and rules over every particle of existence. Then the verse adds a specific description: “and the Lord of all points of sunrise.

” The mention of “points of sunrise” in the plural is remarkable. The sun does not rise at the same exact spot each day from an earthly perspective; it shifts throughout the year, creating many different points of sunrise. Some commentators also understand it as referring to the many different places on earth from which the sun rises over various lands and peoples. By declaring Himself the Lord of all these rising points, Allah draws attention to His dominion over time, seasons, and geography. The pagan Arabs worshipped various local deities tied to specific regions, but here the Quran emphasizes that the same One Lord controls every sunrise everywhere. The daybreak that illuminates Mecca is under the same authority as the daybreak that shines on the farthest lands. This universal lordship refutes any notion that different gods manage different territories. It also reminds human beings of their complete dependence. We plan our days, but we cannot make the sun rise. That daily miracle is in the hands of the Lord of all points of sunrise.

Thus, the verse builds a logical bridge from the angels’ worship described earlier to the visible creation, inviting reflection on the fact that the One who orders the heavens and the earth must be the sole object of worship.

=6=

The surah now shifts attention to the visible cosmos as a sign of divine power and beauty. The verse states, “Indeed, We have adorned the lowest heaven with the stars for decoration.” The “lowest heaven” refers to the nearest celestial sphere, the sky that is directly observable to human eyes. The stars are described as an adornment, a dazzling decoration that fills the night with beauty.

This is a profound shift in perspective: what people often take for granted as distant burning balls of gas is presented here as deliberate ornamentation placed by the Creator to beautify the sky. The purpose of this adornment is not merely aesthetic; it invites contemplation. When a person looks up at a star-filled sky, the natural response should be awe at the artistry and majesty of the One who arranged it.

The use of “We” in the verse is the royal plural of majesty, not an indication of multiple gods. The verse counters the pagan practice of worshipping stars or considering them as having influence independent of Allah. By stating that the stars are a decoration of the lowest heaven, the Quran makes it clear that they are created objects, placed under Allah’s command, and subservient to His will.

Moreover, this adornment is directly linked to the previous context of angels; the lowest heaven is the very realm where the angels descend with commands and where they are engaged in their duties. So the beauty above us is not empty; it is a domain populated by obedient servants.

For the believer, the starry night becomes a reminder of Allah’s subtle artistry and a call to glorify Him. The concept of adornment suggests that Allah beautified creation as an act of generosity, providing humans with sights that bring tranquillity and inspire reflection, yet all this beauty is merely the lowest level of the heavenly realm.

=7=

The stars do not only serve as decoration; the verse adds a second function: “and for protection from every rebellious devil.” This reveals a spiritual dimension to the cosmos that was hidden from the disbelievers. The “rebellious devils” are the shayateen from among the jinn who attempt to ascend to the heavens to eavesdrop on the conversations of the angels. Allah has stationed the stars—or meteoric flames produced from them—as missiles to repel these intruders. This protective function connects the visible night sky directly with the unseen battle between good and evil. For the Meccan pagans who often consulted soothsayers and believed that jinn had access to heavenly secrets, this verse dismantles that notion by showing that any attempt by devils to snoop is fiercely guarded against. The “protection” is not merely a wall but an active defense system driven by the same divine power that decorated the sky. It teaches that the heavens are secure; no unauthorized entity can penetrate the assembly of the angels. This has immediate implications for the Prophet’s message: the revelation he brings is pure and uncorrupted because no devil can intercept it. The words of the Quran are protected from above, and the Prophet is not a soothsayer receiving whispers from jinn, as his enemies accused him. Instead, he receives revelation through the trustworthy angel Gabriel.

This verse also comforts the believer by showing that Allah’s control over the universe extends to safeguarding spiritual knowledge. The shooting stars we occasionally see are not random cosmic events but signs of devils being pelted and driven away. The same sky that looks tranquil and adorned is also a battlefield where Allah’s commands are enforced against the arrogant rebels who once refused to bow to Adam.

=8=

The description of cosmic protection continues with the statement that the devils “cannot listen to the highest assembly of angels.” The “highest assembly” (al-mala’ al-a‘la) refers to the noble gathering of angels in the heavens where divine decrees are discussed and commands are issued. The rebellious devils strive to overhear snippets of this celestial discourse to use that stolen information for misleading humans. But the verse declares that such eavesdropping is impossible because “they are pelted from every side. ” The imagery is that of a fortress under complete siege; the devils are assailed from all directions by flaming meteors whenever they try to approach.

This is a direct refutation of the belief held by some Arabs that soothsayers had informants among the jinn who brought them news of the unseen. The Quran clarifies that even if a devil manages to snatch a word, a piercing flame pursues it immediately. The “every side” phrase emphasizes total coverage; there is no blind spot in the heavens where a rebellious devil can hide. The lesson for humans is that the unseen is entirely under divine control. Whatever knowledge of future events or hidden matters reaches people through true revelation is only that which Allah permits the angels to convey to His prophets. This also reinforces the integrity of the Quranic message. The Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) is not receiving confused, half-stolen information; he receives clear, guarded revelation delivered by Gabriel, who is part of that highest assembly. The verse thus subtly comforts the Prophet, whose opponents accused him of being a madman or a poet inspired by jinn, by showing that the real channel of heavenly knowledge is secure and the devils are utterly barred from it, pelted away in disgrace.

=9=

The devils are not only pelted and repelled but are also described as “fiercely driven away.” The repulsion is forceful and humiliating, indicating that they are expelled from the heavenly precincts with contempt. This immediate punishment in the sky is followed by a warning of something more severe: “And they will suffer an everlasting torment.” The verse links the present defensive measure with the future eschatological punishment. The rebellious devils are already experiencing the disgrace of being chased away from the light of divine assembly, but this is only a foretaste of the eternal suffering awaiting them in the Hereafter. This dual punishment demonstrates the consistency of Allah’s justice across both the unseen and the seen worlds. The devils, who were once honoured and then rebelled out of arrogance, now face continuous rejection and will ultimately be cast into Hell. For the human disbelievers who align themselves with the whisperings of these devils, there is a grave warning: if the devils themselves cannot escape the flames that pursue them now and the eternal fire later, how can those who follow them hope to be safe? The Meccan pagans who worshipped jinn or sought their aid are being alerted that their supposed allies are themselves hunted fugitives.

The concept of “everlasting torment” also lays the groundwork for the later discussions in the surah about the fate of the wicked. It reminds the listener that this life’s hidden struggles are connected to final outcomes. The believer is reminded that the forces of evil, no matter how deceptive, are ultimately defeated and under constant divine assault. The sky, beautifully adorned, is simultaneously a place of relentless judgment against those who overstep their bounds, reflecting the perfect balance of mercy and justice in Allah’s creation.

=10=

After establishing that the devils are pelted from all sides, the verse adds a specific detail: “But whoever manages to stealthily eavesdrop is instantly pursued by a piercing flare.” This acknowledges that occasionally a devil may snatch a fragment of sound from the heavenly discourse. The verb “stealthily eavesdrop” suggests a sneaky, desperate attempt to grab something quickly.

However, the response is immediate: a piercing flare, a shining meteor, chases and burns it. The word “piercing” indicates a flame that penetrates and overcomes, leaving no chance of escape. This precise imagery served a powerful purpose among the pagan Arabs, many of whom believed soothsayers received their information from jinn who overheard the angels. The Quran does not deny that a snatch of sound might occur, but it clarifies that such stolen information is partial, confused, and intercepted before the thief can benefit. The soothsayers then mix that single fragment with a hundred lies, making their predictions unreliable. The Prophet (peace be upon him), by contrast, receives the pure revelation through Gabriel without such interference. This effectively discredits the entire soothsaying profession. For the modern reader, this verse reinforces the reality that the unseen realm is not a chaotic spiritual free-for-all. It operates under strict laws and surveillance. The fact that a “piercing flare” pursues the eavesdropper demonstrates the speed and precision of divine enforcement. It also subtly answers those who question why bad things happen or why evil seems to have temporary success: even the devils, with all their cunning, can only snatch whispers before being burned. The forces of falsehood are always on the run, pursued by the truth.

This image should fill the believer with confidence that whatever misinformation and deception circulate in the world, the ultimate source of truth remains guarded and uncorrupted by divine protection.

=11=

The discussion now turns from the cosmic scene to a direct challenge issued to the deniers of resurrection. Allah instructs the Prophet to ask them, “which is harder to create: them or other marvels of Our creation?” This rhetorical question targets the disbelievers who found the idea of being resurrected after death impossible. The logic is clear: if Allah created the vast heavens, the stars, the angels, the devils, and all the marvels mentioned earlier, is recreating human beings from their original substance really more difficult?

The question uses their own reasoning against them. They accepted that Allah created the universe, yet they balked at the idea of a second creation.

The phrase “other marvels of Our creation” could refer to the angels, the heavens, the earth, or even the enormous creatures and forces that Allah has brought into existence. Then the verse grounds this argument in human origin: “Indeed, We created them from a sticky clay. ” The original creation of the human race was from a humble, sticky, mouldable clay. This origin is not a source of pride but a reminder of lowly beginnings. The one who fashioned a living, intelligent being from such simple material is certainly capable of bringing that being back to life.

The mention of “sticky clay” also echoes the creation of Adam and reminds the Meccan idolaters that their own substance is from the earth they walk upon. This dual argument—comparing the resurrection to the more immense creation of the cosmos and pointing to the first creation from clay—leaves no intellectual escape. If someone acknowledges the greater act of creating the heavens and the earth, they cannot logically deny the lesser act of reconstituting bones and dust.

=12=

Having presented the logical argument about resurrection, the verse addresses the Prophet directly, but its message also flows to the believers. “In fact, you are astonished by their denial, while they ridicule you.” The Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) was amazed and perhaps bewildered that people could reject such clear reasoning. The evidence for Allah’s power was visible all around them, from the starry sky to the creation of human beings from clay, yet the disbelievers continued to mock the idea of life after death. The verse acknowledges the Prophet’s astonishment but contrasts it with the reaction of the deniers: they are not astonished; they simply ridicule. They do not engage intellectually; they turn to mockery. This dynamic is common throughout history: when truth is presented with compelling proofs, those who are determined to reject it often resort to belittlement and laughter rather than honest consideration. The verse consoles the Prophet by assuring him that his surprise at their stubbornness is natural, but their ridicule stems from arrogance, not from any weakness in the message. It also serves as a caution for believers: do not expect that every logical argument will be met with acceptance. Some hearts are sealed, and their response will be sarcasm. The Prophet is told, in effect, to recognize this pattern and not let their mockery distress him. The shift from the cosmic oaths and resurrection arguments to this intimate description of reactions makes the text deeply personal for the one delivering the message. It reinforces that the struggle between truth and falsehood involves emotional fortitude. The believers are reminded that they stand on the side of wonder at Allah’s signs, while the deniers stand on the side of scoffing.

=13=

This verse explains the deeper cause behind the ridicule. “When they are reminded, they are never mindful.” The reminder here refers to the Quranic verses, the teachings of the Prophet, and the signs in creation that remind people of Allah and the Hereafter. The disbelievers receive these reminders, yet they refuse to allow them to penetrate their hearts.

The phrase “never mindful” indicates a persistent state of heedlessness. It is not that they fail to understand; they choose not to reflect and take heed.

The word used in Arabic conveys the idea of actively paying attention and deriving spiritual benefit. They turn away from that.

This is a severe critique of their attitude. Reminders are meant to awaken the innate human disposition that recognizes truth. Every human soul has a primordial covenant with its Creator, and a reminder should stir that memory. But when a person consistently blocks out the truth, they become desensitized. The verse thus diagnoses the spiritual disease of the disbelievers: they are unmindful by habit.

The earlier mention of the Prophet’s astonishment is now explained; their denial is not based on intellectual lack but on willful negligence. For the believer, this verse is a warning to safeguard the heart against heedlessness. When we hear reminders, we should actively engage our minds and hearts, lest we fall into the same pattern of ignoring what could save us. The surah has already established immense signs—angels, stars, creation from clay—so to be unmindful of such reminders is to be truly lost.

=14=

The verse moves from hearing reminders to seeing signs, completing the picture of their rejection. “And whenever they see a sign, they make fun of it.” A sign (ayah) can be a verse of the Quran, a miracle, or a natural phenomenon pointing to Allah. The Meccan idolaters witnessed the Prophet’s character, the Quran’s eloquence, and the splitting of the moon, among other signs. Their reaction was not contemplation but mockery. They turned these profound proofs into objects of jest. This behaviour is the defensive mechanism of pride. When faced with something that challenges their worldview, instead of submitting, they seek to belittle it to preserve their own ego and social standing. By making fun of the signs, they also pressure the Prophet and the early Muslim community, hoping to discourage them. The verse exposes the moral bankruptcy of such an attitude. It links back to the previous statement about being astonished versus ridiculing: the believers see in the signs a cause for awe; the disbelievers see a cause for laughter.

This contrast continues throughout the surah, preparing for the scenes of the Day of Judgment when the mockers will be in utter regret. The practical lesson for believers is clear: never treat the signs of Allah with levity. When encountering a verse or a miracle, one should approach with humility and a readiness to learn.

The verse also teaches that sometimes rejection is not due to lack of clarity but due to a rotten attitude that must be recognized and countered with patience and continued delivery of the message.

=15=

Now the verse quotes their mockery directly: “saying, ‘This is nothing but pure magic.’” When the disbelievers saw the Quran’s effect on people and the signs the Prophet brought, they could not deny its power. So they resorted to labelling it “magic.” The addition of “pure” (or “clear”) intensifies the claim, as if to say it is obviously sorcery and nothing else. In their minds, this label served to discredit the Prophet and explain away the transformation they witnessed in his followers. Magic, in their cultural context, was understood to be a means of overwhelming and deceiving people through supernatural aid, often associated with jinn. By calling the Quran magic, they simultaneously admitted its extraordinary impact but refused to attribute that to divine origin. This accusation was not new; earlier prophets like Moses were also accused of sorcery when they brought clear signs. The verse thus places the Prophet in the company of previous messengers, normalizing his experience of rejection.

For the believer, this teaches an important lesson about the nature of truth: when falsehood cannot match the evidence, it often tries to rename it. A person of sound intellect should look at the content and results of the message, not the labels thrown at it. The Quran invites this examination by itself.

The verse also highlights the illogical nature of the accusation. Magic is associated with deception and harm, yet the Quran brings moral purification, justice, and monotheism. The very purity of its message testifies against the claim of magic.

=16=

The disbelievers’ mockery takes a specific form: they question the possibility of resurrection. “When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected?” This rhetorical question drips with incredulity and sarcasm. They present the stages of decomposition—first becoming dust, then scattered bones—and ask if life can possibly return to such remains. To their materialistic minds, the idea was absurd. The verse captures their mindset exactly, showing that the core of their denial was not just a rejection of the Prophet’s message but a deep-seated inability to conceive of divine power beyond what they saw in the physical world.

The question “will we really be resurrected?” uses a grammatical form that expects a negative answer, revealing their certainty that such a thing cannot happen. This attitude is the root of much of human rebellion: if there is no accountability after death, one can live without moral restraint. The Quran frequently quotes this argument because it is the psychological barrier that must be broken for faith to enter the heart. The verse follows the pattern of presenting their speech verbatim, making the subsequent refutation more powerful. It also exposes that their earlier mockery and accusations of magic were all just smokescreens to avoid confronting the reality of the Hereafter.

The mention of forefathers in the next verse will show how tradition reinforces this denial.

=17=

They extend their question to include previous generations. “And our forefathers as well?” This addition is meant to make the idea of resurrection seem even more preposterous. If it is hard enough to imagine their own decomposed bodies being revived, how could people who died centuries ago, whose remains have completely disappeared, possibly be raised? This question also reveals a psychological dimension: the disbelievers hid behind the authority and customs of their ancestors. They thought, “If our ancestors lived and died without any resurrection happening to them, then they must have been on the correct path, and this talk of a Day of Judgment is just a new innovation.” The Quran repeatedly confronts the blind following of forefathers as a major obstacle to accepting truth. The specific mention of forefathers here ties the denial of resurrection to tribal pride and the desire to maintain the status quo. It shows that their rejection is not purely intellectual but deeply cultural and emotional. They were reluctant to admit that their beloved ancestors might have been wrong and might face punishment. This verse thus sets the stage for later passages in the surah that show how misled people will turn on those they followed.

The question also allows for a comprehensive answer: Allah, who created the first human and has recorded every atom, can reassemble all generations just as easily as He created them initially. The mention of forefathers makes the question more personal and rooted in lineage, which the coming verses will address with a firm command to answer.

=18=

The command to the Prophet is direct and unapologetic: “Say, ‘Yes! And you will be fully humbled.’” The affirmation “Yes” provides a blunt, clear answer to their sarcastic query. There is no hesitation or softening of the message. Yes, you will indeed be resurrected. The certainty of this declaration contrasts sharply with the doubt and mockery of the previous verses. Then the verse adds a crucial detail about the state in which they will rise: “you will be fully humbled.

” The Arabic word used here (dākhirūn) means subdued, humiliated, and brought low. They had arrogantly scoffed at the idea of resurrection, so their rising will be in a state of abject humiliation, shattering their pride.

This is not just a factual statement but a prophetic warning. The resurrection is not a neutral event for the deniers; it is the beginning of their disgrace.

The answer addresses both the physical possibility and the moral consequence. The mention of being humbled also reassures the Prophet and the believers that the mockery they endured will be answered with divine justice.

In the context of the surah, this short verse serves as a powerful pivot. It ends the dialogue of denial and asserts the truth, preparing the listener for the vivid scenes of the Day of Judgment that will follow. The Prophet is instructed not to engage in prolonged argument but to deliver this crisp, decisive response that combines affirmation of resurrection with a warning about its reality for the arrogant.

=19=

After the decisive “Yes,” the surah describes how easily the resurrection will occur. “It will only take one Blast, then at once they will see it all.” The “one Blast” refers to the blowing of the Trumpet by the angel Isrāfīl. A single, immense sound will be enough to bring the entire creation back to life and assemble them. This underlines the absolute power of Allah. What the disbelievers considered impossible—the reviving of decayed bodies across all ages—requires from Allah nothing more than a single command, a single blast.

The phrase “at once they will see” indicates the suddenness and the immediacy with which the reality they denied will become visible to them. There will be no gradual process; in an instant, they will be standing, witnessing the truth they mocked.

The word “see” implies not only physical sight but also sudden, shocking realization. All their scepticism will evaporate the moment that Blast sounds. This verse, therefore, functions as a vivid corrective to the materialist mindset that measures Allah’s power by human limitations. It also inspires a sense of urgency in the believer. If the Day can come with a single sudden blast, then one must always be prepared. The imagery connects to the earlier oaths about the angels’ ranks; the same organized hosts will be involved in executing this event. The surah thus weaves together the unseen order and the eschatological climax, showing that the cosmos is moving according to a precise divine plan that culminates in a single, devastatingly swift moment of awakening.

=20=

When that moment arrives, the deniers will not remain silent. The verse captures their desperate cry: “Oh, woe to us!

This is the Day of Judgment!” The exclamation “Oh, woe to us!” is a phrase of extreme grief and self-reproach. It is the sound of a person who has suddenly realized the magnitude of their error and the horror of their situation. They recognize the Day for what it truly is, calling it by its right name: “the Day of Judgment.” In their worldly life, they had mocked this very term, but now they utter it with their own tongues in terror. The verse is deeply ironic: the people who once asked sarcastically if they would be resurrected are now the ones crying out in despair upon seeing that very Day. Their acknowledgment does not benefit them; it only compounds their regret.

The word “Judgment” (dīn) also carries the meaning of recompense, indicating that they immediately understand this is a day when every deed will be weighed. The surah presents this scene not just as a prophecy but as a direct rebuke to the contemporary Meccan mockers. In effect, it tells them: “This is the cry you will utter. ” For the believer, this verse evokes a mix of awe and a desire to be among those who will be joyful on that Day, not among those wailing in regret. It leads naturally into the descriptions of the fate of the wrongdoers that follow in the next verses, completing the passage’s movement from oath, to argument, to prophetic command, and finally to the vivid portrayal of the Day they refused to believe in.
