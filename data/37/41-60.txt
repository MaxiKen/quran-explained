=41=

The surah now begins detailing the reward of the chosen servants who were exempted from the painful torment. The verse opens by stating, “They will have a known provision.” This provision is “known” in a profoundly reassuring sense. It is not a vague hope or an uncertain promise but a defined, guaranteed, and specific sustenance that Allah has prepared for them.

The term “known” implies that it has been measured, apportioned, and designated by divine knowledge, and the believers in Paradise are aware of what awaits them. Some scholars explain that the provision is known in its timing, its variety, and its continuous delivery without interruption. In the worldly life, provision is often a source of anxiety; a person may not know if they will have enough tomorrow. In Paradise, this anxiety is removed. The provision is constant and predictable in the most joyful way—every moment brings the delight they already anticipate. This also connects to the concept that the people of Paradise will have whatever they desire, and more. The provision includes all forms of sustenance: food, drink, clothing, companionship, and spiritual joy. The fact that it is “a provision” (rizq) ties back to the Quranic theme that Allah is the Provider; in the Hereafter, His provision for the chosen servants reaches its ultimate form. The psychological comfort for the believer is immense. When one hears about the horrors of judgment, this verse is like a gentle hand on the shoulder, whispering that a prepared, guaranteed, blissful provision awaits those who remain faithful. The Meccan pagans who hoarded wealth and prided themselves on their generous provisions in this life are being told that true, lasting provision is exclusively for Allah’s sincere servants. It also reminds us that our striving in this life is to earn that known provision through faith and righteous deeds, not through amassing worldly goods.

=42=

Among the details of that known provision, the first mentioned is “fruits of every type.” The inclusion of fruits symbolizes abundance, variety, and the fulfillment of desires without limit. In the harsh desert environment of Arabia, fresh fruits were a luxury, often available only seasonally and for the wealthy. The promise of “every type” indicates that in Paradise, every kind of fruit imaginable—and even those beyond human imagination—will be readily available. This demonstrates that Paradise is a place where scarcity does not exist and where the believer’s longing for diverse delights is completely satisfied. Importantly, the verse does not stop at physical provision; it immediately adds, “And they will be honoured.” The honour (ikrām) is a spiritual and social elevation. The disbelievers in Mecca sought honour through tribal status, wealth, and the mockery of the Prophet, but true honour belongs solely to those whom Allah honours. In Paradise, this honour manifests in various ways: the angels greet them, their stations are elevated, their faces radiate with joy, and they are received with divine welcome. The combination of fruits and honour shows that the reward is holistic—it addresses the body’s pleasures and the soul’s dignity.

The word “honoured” also implies that they are treated as VIP guests, not merely as inhabitants. For the believer who might have been humiliated, mocked, or oppressed in this world for their faith, this is a powerful consolation. The provision of fruits and the granting of honour together teach that the Hereafter compensates fully for any deprivation or disgrace suffered for Allah’s sake. The verse paints a picture of dignified satisfaction, far removed from the arrogant feastings of the world that were accompanied by boastfulness and neglect of the poor. In Paradise, honour is coupled with humility and gratitude.

=43=

The honoured provision and companionship are located “in the Gardens of Bliss.” The Arabic term “Jannāt an-Naʿīm” carries a depth of meaning. “Gardens” indicates lush, shaded, expansive spaces full of trees, rivers, and beauty. “Bliss” (naʿīm) refers to a state of complete well-being, comfort, and happiness that touches every dimension of existence. It is not merely physical pleasure but an all-encompassing felicity that includes peace of mind, security, and the absence of any distress. The Gardens of Bliss are the specific dwelling of the chosen servants, distinguished from other parts of Paradise perhaps by their exceptional delight.

This description directly contrasts with the “painful torment” and the “path of Hell” mentioned earlier in the surah. While the wrongdoers are being dragged and detained in humiliation, the chosen servants are settled in the Gardens of Bliss.

The word “Bliss” also negates the possibility of any negative emotion: no sadness, no regret, no boredom, no fear of losing the blessing. The Meccan disbelievers, who revelled in their worldly gardens and vineyards, are being told that the real, eternal Gardens belong to the believers they oppressed.

Moreover, the phrase connects to the broader Quranic portrayal of Paradise as a place of ultimate return, where believers will hear the greeting “Peace” and dwell forever. The believer reading this verse is encouraged to visualize not just a physical garden but a state of existence where Allah’s pleasure is manifest in every detail. The Gardens of Bliss are the final home, and the journey of faith is the path leading to them, a path that many earlier messengers and their followers walked.

=44=

Inside the Gardens of Bliss, the social aspect of Paradise is highlighted: “facing each other on thrones.” The image is that of a convivial gathering where people sit on elevated, comfortable seats, arranged so that they can see one another and converse. There is no turning of backs, no segregation due to animosity. In the worldly life, people might sit in circles of friendship, but even the closest gatherings can harbour hidden jealousies or boredom. In Paradise, Allah removes any ill feeling from the hearts of the believers, so their facing each other is a sign of pure love, mutual respect, and shared joy. The “thrones” (surur) indicate luxury and authority; they are not mere benches but seats of honour, suggesting that every believer is treated like a king or queen. The fact that they face each other shows that the social pleasure of Paradise is communal, not isolated. The believers will enjoy each other’s company, reminiscing about the worldly life with gratitude and discussing the blessings they have received. This scene is a beautiful reversal of the Hellfire scene earlier, where the misleaders and misled turned on each other with blame. There, they faced each other in mutual accusation; here, they face each other in love.

The verse also reflects the culture of Arabian hospitality and social gatherings, but elevated to perfection. No one is left out; all are included in this circle of honour. The believer learns that Paradise is not merely individual enjoyment but a perfected community where the bonds of faith are forever celebrated. The thrones signify stability and permanence: they are seated, at rest, with no need to toil or move on.

=45=

The description of delights continues with a specific drink. “A drink of pure wine will be passed around to them from a flowing stream.” The cup is passed around, indicating a generous, shared hospitality where servants or attendants circulate among the seated believers, offering them this exquisite beverage. The drink is identified as “pure wine,” yet its source is “a flowing stream. ” This indicates that the wine of Paradise does not come from fermented fruits in limited barrels but flows continuously and freely, unpolluted and inexhaustible.

The phrase “passed around to them” (yuṭāfu ʿalayhim) suggests a scene of celebration where goblets are brought again and again, yet without the negative consequences associated with worldly drinking. The Meccan pagans were familiar with wine gatherings, often accompanied by poetry, boasting, and sometimes indecency. Here, the Quran reclaims the symbol of wine and purifies it, presenting a heavenly version that brings only pleasure and no harm. The flowing stream is a direct contrast to the stagnant, murky water sources in the desert, and even to the limited supplies of earthly luxury. The drink being from a stream means it is ever-fresh, never running out. The believer listening to this verse understands that the lawful pleasures of this world are just a faint shadow of the perfect pleasures of the next, and that abstaining from intoxicants here for Allah’s sake leads to this incredible reward. The social dimension—passing the cup around—reinforces the themes of fellowship and mutual honour established in the previous verse.

=46=

The wine’s qualities are then beautifully described: “crystal-white, delicious to drink.” The colour is a radiant white, unlike the usual dark red or golden earthly wines. “Crystal-white” suggests purity, clarity, and a luminous quality that appeals to the eye before it touches the lips. It resembles a pristine, gleaming liquid that reflects light perfectly. This visual appeal is the first level of enjoyment. Then the taste is described as “delicious to drink.

” The Arabic term used conveys extreme pleasantness of taste, a smoothness that glides down the throat without any harshness or bitterness. There is nothing in it that would make a person wince; it is perfection in flavour. The combination of sight and taste encapsulates the complete sensory satisfaction. In the worldly life, even the most expensive drinks may have an aftertaste or a tinge of unpleasantness; this heavenly drink has none.

The verse also implies that the drinkers will savour it consciously, with full appreciation, unlike the dulled senses of earthly intoxication. For the Arab pagans who were proud connoisseurs of wine, this description both challenges and transcends their experience. It tells them that what they indulge in is a defective, harmful imitation of the pure delight reserved for the righteous.

The emphasis on “delicious to drink” is an invitation to reflect on the Reward’s perfection. The believer is reminded that every lawful pleasure given up in this world for the sake of Allah is replaced by something infinitely better in the Hereafter. This verse builds anticipation and strengthens the resolve to remain obedient. The white, delicious wine is a symbol of the purified delights that await, free from any imperfection or sin.

=47=

Immediately after describing the wine’s appealing qualities, the verse clarifies what it is not: “It will neither harm them, nor will they be intoxicated by it.” This is the critical difference that sets heavenly wine apart from its worldly counterpart. Earthly wine causes physical harm—headaches, liver damage, stomach sickness—and it clouds the mind, leading to loss of self-control, foolish speech, and regrettable actions. The wine of Paradise is completely free from these evils. It does not cause any bodily ailment or pain; it is a pure pleasure that nourishes without any negative side effects. Even more importantly, it does not intoxicate. The drinkers will not lose their intellect or consciousness. They will experience a delightful, euphoric sensation—some scholars mention a feeling of exhilaration and joy—but their minds remain clear, and their capacity to converse, remember, and appreciate remains fully intact.

This is crucial because Paradise is a place of awareness and nearness to Allah; to be intoxicated would detract from that. The verse directly answers any criticism that a paradise including wine seems lowly or similar to sinful worldly behaviour. It shows that the substances in Paradise are of a completely different nature, created specifically to give pure pleasure without consequence. It also serves as an admonition to those who justify their drinking of earthly wine by imagining that since Paradise has wine, it cannot be so bad. The earth’s intoxicants are forbidden precisely because they harm and intoxicate, while the heavenly drink is sanctified. The believer finds in this verse a compelling reason to abstain now: the fleeting, harmful buzz is not worth forfeiting the eternal, harmless, and divine delight. The clarity and safety of the heavenly wine reflect the absolute perfection of Allah’s reward.

=48=

The description of the blissful gathering expands to include the companions of Paradise. “And with them will be maidens of modest gaze and gorgeous eyes.” The maidens, often referred to as ḥūr, are beings created for Paradise, perfectly beautiful in form and character. Their first described quality is “modest gaze. ” This means their glances are restrained, directed only towards their spouses with love and devotion, never looking elsewhere with desire or causing any jealousy. Modesty here is not shyness rooted in fear but dignified, loving reserve that enhances intimacy and trust.

The second quality is “gorgeous eyes.” The Arabic term ʿīn refers to eyes that are wide, with a stark, beautiful contrast between the intensely black pupil and the brilliant white sclera. Such eyes were considered the height of beauty in Arabian culture. These maidens are not merely physical companions; they represent perfect, pure, and faithful partnership. In the worldly life, relationships can be marred by wandering gazes, distrust, and disappointment. In Paradise, Allah provides companions who are a source of complete contentment and honour.

The phrase “with them” indicates that these maidens are present in the same gatherings, sharing in the joy, and adding to the social bliss. This promise would resonate with the early Muslims, many of whom had left behind spouses or died unmarried for their faith. It assures them that their sacrifice will be compensated with unimaginably beautiful and loyal companions.

The mention of modesty is significant: it shows that true beauty in Allah’s sight includes character and purity, not just appearance. This elevates the concept of companionship to a spiritual as well as physical level.

=49=

The beauty and purity of these maidens are further emphasized by a striking simile: “as if they were pristine pearls.” The comparison to pearls is deeply evocative. A pristine pearl is one that has been hidden within its shell deep in the ocean, untouched by human hands, dust, or pollution. It retains its original lustre, smoothness, and perfection. In Arab culture, the pearl was a treasured, rare, and precious gem, often used as a metaphor for something extremely valuable and beautiful.

The image of a pearl also conveys a sense of delicate, glowing whiteness, aligning with the purity of the maidens’ characters and appearance. Moreover, a pearl is formed through a process of patience and time within the oyster, hidden from view, which metaphorically reflects the preservation of these companions from any prior defilement or blemish. They are presented to the believers as untouched treasures, exclusive and sublime.

The word “pristine” (maknūn) further means well-protected, guarded, and preserved in the best state. This simile silences any crude or base imaginations that might arise from the mention of female companions; it elevates the description to one of refined, artistic appreciation. The believer is invited to conceive of companions who are like hidden gems, now revealed for their delight in a setting of absolute purity. This contrasts sharply with the illicit relationships of this world, often characterized by exploitation, disappointment, and regret.

The verse teaches that Allah’s reward includes aesthetic and relational beauty in a form that is completely clean, honouring both the giver and the receiver. For the Meccan pagans who devalued women and relationships, this presents a vision of paradise where purity and honour define even the most intimate joys.

=50=

After establishing the general atmosphere of honour, bliss, and companionship, the surah moves to a specific scene of conversation. “Then they will turn to one another inquisitively.” The people of Paradise, seated on their thrones and enjoying their provisions, will begin to converse with one another.

The word “inquisitively” indicates a friendly, curious manner of questioning and sharing. They are deeply interested in each other’s past experiences in the worldly life and how they came to this glorious destination. There is no envy, no competition, only loving curiosity and the joy of storytelling. This turning to one another signifies the complete openness and intimacy of the community of Paradise. They are not isolated in their bliss but actively engaged in a rich social life. The fact that they “turn” physically suggests that they are in a circle, facing one another as previously mentioned, and now they lean in to talk, creating an atmosphere of warmth and close friendship. This scene follows naturally from the honour and provisions; once the initial delight is savoured, they reflect and converse, adding a layer of intellectual and emotional fulfillment. The conversations in the subsequent verses will reveal that they remember their earthly life, the doubts they faced, and the grace that saved them. This remembrance is not painful but profoundly gratifying, as it magnifies their gratitude.

For the believer, this is a beautiful vision of the afterlife: not just physical pleasure but meaningful connection with fellow believers, where stories of faith are shared and the mercy of Allah is celebrated. It also serves as encouragement to cultivate such pure, faith-based friendships in this world, for they may continue into the next in the most delightful form.

=51=

The conversation begins with one of the believers sharing a memory. “One of them will say, ‘I once had a companion in the world.’” The speaker is a person of Paradise who recalls a close associate he knew during his earthly life.

The word “companion” (qarīn) can mean a close friend, a business partner, or someone constantly in one’s company. This companion was evidently a disbeliever who used to challenge and mock the believer’s faith.

The use of “once” indicates that this friendship was limited to the world; it no longer exists in the Hereafter. The believer is not expressing sorrow but rather setting the stage for a story of divine rescue. This reflection will make the believer appreciate his salvation even more.

The verse shows that the people of Paradise will remember the trials they faced, including the negative influences of certain friends. Their memory is complete, but any pain associated with it is removed; instead, it becomes a testimony to Allah’s grace.

This is deeply relatable: many sincere believers have friends or family members who mock their faith or try to lead them astray. The verse assures that enduring such pressure and remaining steadfast is a path to a moment of profound gratitude in Paradise, where one can look back and see the disaster that was averted. The friendly, storytelling tone (“I once had a companion”) indicates the relaxed and happy state of the inhabitants, who can speak of past dangers without fear because they are now eternally safe.

=52=

The believer now quotes his former companion’s words. “Who used to ask me, ‘Do you actually believe in resurrection?’” The phrase “used to ask” shows that this was a recurring theme. The companion would repeatedly challenge and ridicule the believer’s faith, attempting to sow doubt.

The question “Do you actually believe?” is loaded with incredulity and sarcasm; the disbeliever found the idea of resurrection so absurd that he could not fathom a rational person accepting it. This directly mirrors the earlier verses in the surah where the Meccan pagans mocked the Prophet about being raised from dust and bones. The quotation connects the micro-level interpersonal pressure with the macro-level opposition faced by all messengers. It reveals that the denial of resurrection is not just a theoretical stance but can become a tool of social pressure against believers. The companion’s question was designed to make the believer feel foolish, isolated, and perhaps waver in his conviction.

However, the believer held fast, and now in Paradise, he recounts this with a sense of triumph and relief. The verse also demonstrates that Allah is fully aware of the whispered doubts and mockeries that believers endure; these conversations are being recorded and will become part of the blessed remembrance in the Hereafter. The believer listening to this surah should recognize that the same questions thrown at them today by sceptical friends will one day be merely a story they tell in Paradise as they look upon the fate of those who asked them.

=53=

The companion’s scepticism is quoted in full, echoing the exact words of the deniers earlier in the surah. “When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be brought to judgment?” This detailed description of decomposition—dust and bones—was their supposed scientific proof that resurrection is impossible.

The phrase “brought to judgment” is significant because it shows they understood the resurrection was not merely a return to life but a summons to account for deeds. The companion’s question was thus a rejection of moral accountability, not just a biological puzzle. By quoting this in Paradise, the believer highlights the flawed reasoning that nearly ensnared him. The irony is now complete: the companion who found the idea of judgment laughable is himself in the midst of that very judgment, suffering its consequences, while the believer who was mocked is enjoying the bliss of the Gardens. This verse is a powerful reminder that the truth does not depend on the number of people who believe it or the confidence with which falsehood is proclaimed. The companion was utterly convinced of his own rightness, yet his certainty did not change reality. The believer who listens to this verse today can find solace in the fact that the arrogant scepticism of the world will be reduced to a pitiful memory, while the simple, steadfast faith will be crowned with eternal honour and the opportunity to tell the story of one’s deliverance.

=54=

After recounting his companion’s words, the believer turns to his fellow inhabitants of Paradise with a suggestion. “He will then ask, ‘Would you care to see his fate?’” The believer, now curious about the outcome of his former companion, invites the others to share in looking. This indicates that the people of Paradise have the ability, by Allah’s permission, to glimpse the state of the inhabitants of Hell. This seeing is not for the purpose of gloating in a malicious way, but to increase their gratitude and to witness the perfect justice of Allah. The phrasing “Would you care to see?” is gentle and inclusive; it turns this moment into a shared experience of reflection. The “fate” is the final destination of the disbeliever. The fact that the believer asks implies that he does not already have a constant view of Hell; rather, a means is provided upon request. This aligns with hadith literature describing that a door will be opened or a view granted so that the believer can see what his companion’s end became. The psychological effect is profound: the very person who tried to lead him astray is now a visual proof of Allah’s justice, and the believer feels an even deeper sense of mercy that he was saved. This also serves as a deterrent for those still in the world: the friendships we cultivate can become either a source of shared bliss or a subject of dreadful observation. The verse encourages believers to choose their close companions wisely and to worry less about the mockery of those who will one day be merely a cautionary sight.

=55=

The invitation is accepted, and the viewing occurs. “Then he and the others will look and spot him in the midst of the Hellfire.” The believer, along with his companions in Paradise, gazes into the depths of Hell and recognizes his former friend.

The phrase “in the midst of the Hellfire” indicates that the disbeliever is completely engulfed by the punishment, not on the periphery but deep within it. The act of spotting him suggests that despite the vastness of Hell, the believer’s sight is enabled to locate this specific individual. This recognition is instantaneous and certain. The believer sees the horrifying reality that his companion is suffering, which starkly contrasts with his own surroundings of bliss. This moment is not described with any gleeful malice; rather, it is a moment of awe and realization. The believer fully comprehends what he was saved from. The surah’s earlier descriptions of the wrongdoers being gathered, detained, and led to Hell now find a concrete, personal illustration. The Meccan pagans who mocked the Prophet as a mad poet are being warned that those who follow them might one day be viewed in this way by the believers they oppressed. For the modern believer, this verse underscores the seriousness of disbelief and the immense value of guidance. It encourages us to keep our own faith strong and to be grateful for the environment and companions that help us stay on the path. It also reinforces that all pretenses will be stripped away; the true worth of people will be seen in their final abode.

=56=

Upon seeing his former companion in such a state, the believer exclaims, “By Allah! You nearly ruined me.” This oath “By Allah” emphasizes the sincerity and intensity of the emotion.

The phrase “you nearly ruined me” is a direct address to the disbeliever, although it may be spoken across a veil or simply uttered as an exclamation. The believer acknowledges how close he came to being led astray. The “ruin” refers to spiritual destruction leading to eternal punishment. The companion’s persistent mockery and arguments had almost shaken his faith.

This is a powerful testimony to the danger of bad company. The believer was not passively immune; he felt the pressure and the pull of doubt, but Allah’s grace protected him. This exclamation does not express hatred as much as profound relief and a sober recognition of what could have been.

The verse teaches that friendships can be a test, and even strong believers can be nearly ruined by persistent negative influence. The fact that the believer addresses him directly shows that the barrier between Paradise and Hell does not prevent a form of communication; the disbeliever might hear this statement, adding to his regret. For those of us still in the world, this verse is a wake-up call to evaluate our closest relationships. If we have companions who mock our faith or constantly challenge the truths of the Quran in a destructive way, we must guard our hearts and, if necessary, distance ourselves. The “nearly ruined me” is an honest admission that no one is completely safe from the effects of a bad environment, and only through holding tightly to Allah can one be saved.

=57=

Immediately after acknowledging the danger, the believer attributes his salvation entirely to Allah. “Had it not been for the grace of my Lord, I too would have certainly been among those brought to Hell.

” This is the pinnacle of humility and gratitude. Even though the believer had faith and did righteous deeds, he does not boast of his own strength. He knows that his ability to remain steadfast and ultimately enter Paradise was a gift of Allah’s grace.

The phrase “grace of my Lord” encompasses the guidance that was given to him, the protection from misguidance, the faith that was made beloved to his heart, and the final pardon that admitted him to Paradise. This recognition is exactly what the earlier verse meant by “the chosen servants of Allah”—their status is due to divine election and grace.

The phrase “those brought to Hell” (muḥḍarūn) echoes the earlier command to the angels to gather and lead the wrongdoers. The believer realizes he could have been among that group. This moment perfectly captures the spirit of Paradise: the inhabitants are not arrogant about their achievement; they are eternally grateful. The exclamation also serves as an indirect rebuke to the disbeliever’s arrogance. The companion thought he was on a higher intellectual ground, but the reality shows he is in Hell while the one he mocked is in Paradise, and the one in Paradise credits it all to Allah. For the listener, this verse instills a deep reliance on Allah’s grace. While we must strive, we can never rely solely on our own deeds; we must constantly ask for Allah’s grace to complete our faith and save us from a terrible end.

=58=

The believer now turns away from the sight of Hell and addresses his fellow believers in Paradise with a joyful reflection. “Can you imagine that we will never die?

” The question is rhetorical and filled with wonder. The people of Paradise have been granted eternal life, and they are marvelling at this reality. Death, which was an inescapable fact of worldly existence, has been removed from them permanently.

The phrase “Can you imagine” expresses a delightful astonishment, as if the blessing is so immense that they are still savouring its reality. In the world, death is the ultimate source of anxiety, cutting short all pleasures and separating loved ones. In Paradise, this fear is abolished. The believer is not just told he will not die; he is given the consciousness of eternal life to enjoy forever.

The question is shared among the group, enhancing their collective joy. This moment also links to a hadith where the Prophet (peace be upon him) described that after the judgment, death will be brought in the form of a ram and slaughtered before the inhabitants of Paradise and Hell, and it will be announced: “O people of Paradise, eternity for you and no death; O people of Hell, eternity for you and no death.” This will increase the delight of the people of Paradise and the despair of the people of Hell. The believer’s question, “Can you imagine that we will never die?” is a direct reflection of that announcement, savoured among friends.

=59=

The believer continues his reflection, adding two more astounding blessings. “Except our first death, nor be punished like the others?

” The first part, “except our first death,” acknowledges the single death they experienced in the world. That death was the passage from the life of trial to the life of eternal reward. Now that death is permanently behind them. Some scholars note that even that first death will become a distant, non-painful memory, completely overshadowed by the eternal life.

The second part, “nor be punished like the others,” is a statement of relief and gratitude. They see the punishment of the disbelievers and realize they have been spared from it. They are exempt from the torment that they are witnessing in Hell. This awareness makes their bliss even more profound, as contrast heightens appreciation.

The phrase “like the others” refers to the disbelievers and wrongdoers whose fate they just glimpsed. The believer’s tone is not one of arrogant self-praise but of humble gratitude and wonder. He acknowledges that his own deeds could have led him there if not for Allah’s mercy. The verse encapsulates the complete security of Paradise: no death, no punishment, no fear, no sorrow.

This is the final realization of the promise made to the chosen servants. For the living believer, these verses paint a picture of Paradise where one’s past trials are discussed with loving friends, and every difficulty of the world is remembered only to magnify the joy of the ultimate triumph. It encourages a life oriented towards that day when we can marvel together, “We will never die, nor be punished.”

=60=

The passage concludes this reflection with a powerful, emphatic statement: “This is truly the ultimate triumph.” The scene of the believers in Paradise, with all its honours, provisions, companions, and the joyful realization of eternal safety, is declared the supreme success.

The word “triumph” (fawz) in Arabic implies victory, achievement, and the attainment of one’s most desired goal. It is the prize that every human being should strive for. The addition of “ultimate” (al-ʿaẓīm) emphasizes that no other success in any world compares to this. Worldly triumphs—wealth, power, fame, even conquest—pale into insignificance when measured against the eternal triumph of entering Paradise and being saved from Hell. This verse acts as the climax of the description of the reward. It answers the earlier mocking question “Should we really abandon our gods for a mad poet?” with a resounding yes, because the result of abandoning false gods and believing in the truth is this unimaginable triumph. The Meccan disbelievers thought they were the successful ones, with their trade, their idols, and their tribal pride. The Quran redefines success entirely. The believer is invited to meditate on this verse and realign their ambitions. If one truly believes that this is the ultimate triumph, then the sacrifices of this life—avoiding forbidden things, enduring mockery, maintaining prayer—become not just bearable but eminently worthwhile.

This verse also sets up the next section, which contrasts the bliss with the hospitality of Hell and warns that for such honour all should strive. It is a call to action: the triumph is described so vividly that any listener should be moved to earn it.
