=21=

As the disbelievers cry out in anguish, recognizing the Day of Judgment, a voice responds to their wailing. They are told, “This is the Day of Final Decision which you used to deny.

” The term “Final Decision” translates the Arabic word “faṣl,” which implies a decisive separation between truth and falsehood, right and wrong, the obedient and the rebellious. Throughout their worldly life, the deniers were warned repeatedly that a day would come when all matters would be settled conclusively, yet they consistently rejected that warning. Now, that very day has arrived, and they are reminded of their previous denial.

The phrase “which you used to deny” is not just information; it is a rebuke that intensifies their regret. They are forced to confront the fact that their suffering is the direct result of their own arrogance and refusal to accept what messengers brought. The Meccan pagans used to mock the Prophet when he spoke of the Day of Decision, but now they stand in the middle of its unfolding and cannot escape.

This statement also reveals the justice of Allah: He does not punish without making the truth clear beforehand. The denial was an active, repeated choice, and so the outcome is just.

The verse teaches believers that the warnings of the Quran are not empty threats but an inevitable reality. When one hears about the Day of Decision, the heart should tremble and turn to obedience. The reply to the disbelievers also cuts off any hope of argument or appeal; it is the pronouncement that the time for debate is over and the time for recompense has begun. This sets the stage for the detailed scenes of judgment, where every alliance and false god will be shown to be utterly useless. It connects back to the oath at the beginning of the surah: the angels lined in ranks are now part of the cosmic tribunal that brings about this decisive day.

=22=

The scene shifts from the disbelievers’ cry to the divine command initiating the sorting of humanity. Allah instructs the angels, “Gather all the wrongdoers along with their peers, and whatever they used to worship.” The command to “gather” indicates an authoritative, irresistible summoning. The angels, those same beings who were described at the surah’s opening as standing in obedient ranks, now become the executors of this enormous task. They are to assemble not just individuals, but groups. The “wrongdoers” are all those who committed shirk and oppression, and “their peers” refers to those who were like them in evil—partners in crime, leaders of misguidance, and followers who shared their mindset. This grouping signifies that on the Day of Judgment, associations and friendships based on sin will not help but will rather become a source of collective punishment.

The command also includes “whatever they used to worship” besides Allah. Idols, false gods, jinn, and even revered humans will be physically gathered—according to many scholars, given a form of life—so that they can be seen by their worshippers and then cast into Hell along with them. This demonstration utterly humiliates the idolaters by proving that their objects of worship have no power to help or even defend themselves. For the Meccan pagans, who took pride in their ancestral idols and deities, this is a horrifying revelation. Their gods are not intercessors but co-defendants in the same punishment.

The verse shows that no one will be left out; the gathering is comprehensive. This divine order also highlights the angels’ absolute obedience; they do not question but carry out the command instantly. The believer should reflect on the company they keep in this life, because those bonds may become a source of ruin if based on shared disobedience.

=23=

The command continues from the previous verse, adding a second step after the gathering: “then lead them all to the path of Hell.” The phrase “instead of Allah” clarifies that these worshipped entities were taken as deities in place of the One true God.

This is the essence of their crime. Now they are to be led, like cattle being herded, towards Hell.

The word “path” implies that Hell is a destination with a road leading to it, a road that they are now being forced to march upon. The inclusion of the false gods themselves in this parade to damnation is a profound display of humiliation. The Meccan idolaters expected their gods to intercede for them or save them. Instead, they find themselves and their idols being driven together towards the fire.

The Arabic phrasing “lead them” carries a sense of driving and pushing, indicating forceful escort without any possibility of resistance. Scholars mention that the angels tasked with this are the same angels of punishment, fearsome in appearance, who will bind and drag the wrongdoers. The scene is terrifying, yet deeply logical: those who walked a path of misguidance in the world are now being led along the path to its final destination.

The verse also completes the overturning of the pagan worldview. They thought their idols brought them closer to Allah; in reality, those idols are the very reason they are being thrust into destruction.

For the believer, this is a stark reminder that whatever one places above Allah in love, fear, or obedience will become a source of ruin unless it is brought into alignment with His commands. Even permissible attachments, if they become idols in the heart, can lead one astray. The “path of Hell” is paved with the choices made in this life, and the only safe path is the straight path of sincere devotion.

=24=

The final part of the divine command adds, “And detain them, for they must be questioned.” Before they are cast into the fire, the wrongdoers and their false gods will be stopped and held in place. Detention here implies a forced pause in their march to Hell, not to offer hope but to add to their terror and accountability. The questioning is not to gain information—Allah knows all—but to establish guilt through their own confession or through the testimony of their own limbs and the angels. This interrogation will leave them with no excuses. In the worldly courts, a criminal may escape due to lack of evidence or clever arguments, but on that Day, the questioning is perfect and irrefutable. The Meccan pagans who mocked the idea of being held accountable will find themselves standing helplessly before their Lord, being asked about every deed and every false belief.

The question will likely revolve around their worship: “Why did you worship other beings besides Me?” and “What answer do you have to the messengers who were sent to you? ” The verse emphasizes that no one will be punished without a thorough establishment of their guilt. This is a manifestation of Allah’s perfect justice. The detention also serves as a moment of reflection—though too late for benefit—where they see the horror ahead and the pointlessness of their past arrogance. The believers should take this as a warning to constantly hold themselves accountable before that ultimate questioning arrives. The Prophet (peace be upon him) advised that a wise person is one who takes account of himself and prepares for death. The detention in the Hereafter is the unavoidable reality; the questioning we do of our own souls now can save us from its severity then.

=25=

After being detained, the wrongdoers face a humiliating question: “What is the matter with you that you can no longer help each other?” In the world, they used to form alliances, support one another in wrongdoing, and boast about their collective strength. Their tribes, their wealth, their mutual backing gave them a sense of invincibility. Now, they stand powerless and isolated, even though they are gathered together.

The question is deeply sarcastic; it reminds them of their former claims. They helped each other in defying Allah, they encouraged each other to mock the Prophet, and they promised each other that their gods would intercede.

Yet in this decisive hour, none of that mutual assistance functions. They cannot lift a finger to aid one another, nor can their idols offer the slightest help. This sudden paralysis demonstrates that all worldly power is borrowed and vanishes when divine judgment arrives. The verse echoes the earlier argument about resurrection: just as they could not conceive of being raised, they also never imagined a day when their social bonds would dissolve into nothing. For the Meccan pagans who prided themselves on tribal solidarity, this scene dismantles that pride. Leaders and followers, rich and poor, will all be equally helpless. The believer learns that the only bond that will benefit on that Day is the bond of faith and righteous deeds. The mutual help we should cultivate is helping one another towards obedience to Allah, not in defiance of Him.

The question “What is the matter with you?” also exposes the shock and confusion they experience; they themselves cannot comprehend why their previous strategies have failed so completely. The answer, which the verse does not state but is obvious, is that Allah’s power now dominates and all false supports have evaporated.

=26=

The verse provides the stark reality behind their helplessness: “In fact, on that Day they will be fully submissive.” The word “submissive” translates a term that means surrendering completely, humbling oneself, and yielding without resistance. The very people who strutted arrogantly on earth, refusing to bow to Allah’s commands, now are brought to their knees. Their submission, however, comes too late to benefit them. It is not the voluntary submission of faith that earns reward, but the forced submission of encountering undeniable power. This reflects the Quranic principle that while Allah desires willing worship, the entire creation ultimately submits to Him by His irresistible authority. The disbelievers who mocked the call to prayer and the concept of prostration will find their bodies and hearts submitting involuntarily on the Day of Judgment. The addition of “fully” emphasizes the totality of their surrender; there is no defiance left, no eye that dares to lift, no voice that can argue. This humiliation is the direct consequence of their chosen arrogance. The believer is reminded that the voluntary submission of daily prayers and obedience spares one from the forced submission of that dreadful day. The scene also connects with the earlier verses where the deniers were scoffing and arguing; now they have no argument, only submissive silence. This transformation illustrates the complete reversal of fortunes. The surah consistently contrasts the state of the believers and the disbelievers, and this verse shows the ultimate end of the latter: a surrender stripped of any dignity. The lesson is profound: do not let your pride prevent you from submitting now, when submission brings mercy, for the Day will come when submission can no longer avert punishment.

=27=

The helplessness and submission do not create solidarity among the wrongdoers; rather, they dissolve all bonds of false friendship. “They will turn on each other, throwing blame.” Those who were allies in sin become enemies, each pointing the finger at the other to shift responsibility. The atmosphere in the place of detention becomes one of bitter recrimination. Followers blame leaders, and leaders blame followers. This mutual accusation is itself a part of their torment, because it shatters any illusion they had of loyalty and companionship in the worldly life. In their dunyā, they might have said, “We were together, we are in this together,” but now that the punishment looms, self-preservation takes over, though it is futile.

The verse shows that alliances built on anything other than faith and truth are ultimately fragile. The blaming is not a calm discussion but an emotional, heated exchange of “you misled me,” “no, you chose your own path.” The scene echoes the Quranic theme that Satan himself will disown his followers on the Day of Judgment, telling them he merely invited and they followed. Here the human misleaders and misled follow the same pattern.

For the believer, this is a strong warning against blindly following people of influence without examining their teachings against revelation. It also encourages sincere believers to cultivate relationships based on mutual advice to truth and patience, relationships that will endure in the Hereafter rather than becoming occasions of mutual cursing. The verse paints the psychological torment of regret, where one realizes that the very people they loved and trusted in this world were the cause of their eternal ruin.

=28=

The dialogue of blame begins with the followers, the misled, addressing those who led them astray. “It was you who deluded us away from what is right.” The misled here are the common people who followed the leaders of disbelief, the chiefs, and the instigators. They try to offload their culpability onto their leaders by claiming they were deluded.

The word “deluded” implies being deceived, led on with false promises and clever arguments. They argue that they were not inherently inclined to reject truth, but the leaders wrapped falsehood in attractive packages, using persuasive speech, threats, and incentives to keep them away from the message of the prophets. They claim that the leaders blocked their access to the right path by sowing doubts and creating obstacles. This confession reveals that in their hearts, even during their worldly life, they might have recognized some glimmer of truth, but the social pressure and the influence of the powerful figures kept them from embracing it. The verse is a powerful lesson about the responsibility of those in positions of influence—scholars, politicians, celebrities, and community elders—who use their platform to discourage people from true faith. It also reminds the common person that excuses based on being misled will not absolve them if they chose to follow without sincere seeking. The misled are not innocent victims; they made the decision to listen and obey these leaders.

The next verses will make that clear. This accusation sets the stage for the counter-response from the misleaders, creating a dramatic courtroom scene in the Hereafter where all parties try to escape blame, yet all will be held fully accountable.

=29=

The misleaders immediately reject the accusation. “No! You disbelieved on your own.” They flatly deny that they forced the followers into disbelief. Their defense is that the followers possessed their own free will and intellect, and they chose disbelief independently. The misleaders are essentially saying, “We may have invited you, but we did not compel you. You had the same prophets, the same scriptures, the same signs in creation that we did. You made your own decision to reject. ” This response reflects a core Quranic principle: no soul bears the burden of another, and guidance or misguidance ultimately goes back to individual choice, even when external influences are strong. While the leaders are certainly guilty for their misleading invitations, the followers are also guilty for accepting those invitations without critical reflection. The verse dismantles the common psychological crutch of blaming the environment, society, or bad friends for one’s own disbelief. The leaders’ reply, though self-serving, contains a grain of truth that the Quran elsewhere affirms—that each person is accountable for their own response to truth. The mutual blaming reveals the breakdown of the false unity they shared in the world. They had stood together against the Prophet, unified in mockery, but now they tear each other apart. The believer should understand that on the Day of Judgment, following a misguided crowd will not be an acceptable excuse. Allah gave every person an innate disposition to recognize truth and sent messengers to clarify, so the individual cannot simply point to others. This interrogation scene serves as a mirror for us to examine our own willingness to take personal responsibility for our faith and actions.

=30=

The misleaders continue their defense by stating, “We had no authority over you.” They point out that they were not rulers who could physically coerce belief. Their influence was merely verbal and social, not an irresistible force. Since the followers could have chosen to reject their invitations, the leaders claim innocence of direct compulsion.

This statement is actually an admission that they themselves had no real power; it was all a bluff of arrogance. Then they turn the accusation back, saying, “In fact, you yourselves were a transgressing people.” They assert that the followers had a predisposition to transgression and eagerly embraced disbelief. They argue that the followers were not innocent lambs but willing participants who exceeded limits, enjoyed sins, and showed hostility towards the truth.

The word “transgressing” suggests a deliberate crossing of boundaries set by Allah. This defense, while futile in absolving the leaders, exposes the true nature of both parties: the leaders misled, and the followers transgressed willingly. The verse provides insight into the psychology of collective disbelief. Often, corrupt leaders and corrupt followers create a symbiotic relationship where each reinforces the other’s deviance. The leaders enjoy power and adulation, the followers enjoy the license to indulge their desires under the banner of a respected authority.

Thus both are guilty. The believer learns that following a leader in matters of faith requires verifying that leader’s conformity with divine guidance. No human authority is absolute. The fact that the misleaders and misled are now trapped in a blame game shows that none of them can produce a valid excuse. The “authority” they lacked over each other is now overshadowed by the absolute authority of Allah over them all.

=31=

The argument between the misleaders and the misled reaches a grim conclusion as they jointly acknowledge a higher reality. They say, “The decree of our Lord has come to pass against us all: we will certainly taste the punishment.” After all the mutual blame, they realize that it is futile because Allah’s word of judgment has already been pronounced. The “decree” refers to the divine warning given through all prophets that disbelievers would be punished. Now that decree has materialized, and both groups are included in it without distinction.

The phrase “we will certainly taste the punishment” is an admission of inevitable doom. The word “taste” gives a sensory immediacy to the suffering; they will directly experience it, not just observe it. This shared confession ends their dispute in a moment of bleak unity.

The verse shows that on the Day of Judgment, the debate is finally silenced by the overwhelming reality of divine justice. In the world, they might have debated with the Prophet, but now they see the decree unfolding and can only acknowledge its truth. The scene is tragic because it demonstrates that recognition of truth came when it no longer profited them. The believer should take this as a motivation to accept the decree of Allah now, in faith and obedience, so that the decree of mercy is written for them rather than the decree of punishment.

The verse also underlines the absolute sovereignty of Allah: His word cannot be overturned, and His warning is always fulfilled. The mutual blame game ends not because one side convinced the other, but because the Judge’s sentence silences all arguments.

=32=

After jointly acknowledging the decree, the misleaders make a candid admission, perhaps to their followers or to themselves: “We caused you to deviate, for we ourselves were deviant.” This is a moment of raw honesty extracted by the terror of the situation. They finally confess what they had denied before: yes, we misled you, but it was because we were already lost. It is not an excuse but an explanation of the chain of deviance. Misguidance propagates itself; a deviant person naturally leads others into deviation. The statement reveals the contagious nature of falsehood. The misleaders were not enlightened people consciously choosing to destroy others; they were themselves trapped in the darkness they spread. This recognition, however, does not lessen their crime; it only clarifies the mechanism of how evil multiplies in society. For the followers, this confession is devastating: they entrusted their eternal fate to people who now openly admit they were lost. The verse serves as a severe warning against following anyone who is not firmly guided by divine revelation. A teacher, a leader, or an influencer who is deviant will inevitably cause others to deviate, sometimes sincerely thinking they are on the right path. The test for believers is to measure everything against the Quran and authentic Sunnah.

The phrase also echoes the Quranic portrayal of Satan, who admits he misled humans but also acknowledges his own deviation. This parallel suggests that the human misleaders are imitating the archetypal deceiver. The scene in the surah thus weaves a consistent narrative of shared guilt and shattered illusions, pushing the listener to evaluate their sources of guidance in this life before the day when all masks fall away.

=33=

The mutual blame and confessions lead to the final verdict on this group: “Surely on that Day they will all share in the punishment.” The word “share” indicates that the punishment is a common lot; they are partners in suffering just as they were partners in sin. No one’s attempt to shift blame succeeds. The leaders and followers, the misleaders and the misled, are all immersed in the torment together. This verse reinforces the principle of individual responsibility while also recognizing collective accountability for collective crimes. The misleaders receive punishment for their misleading, and the misled receive punishment for their willing followership. The sharing of punishment adds a social dimension to the torment; they will see each other suffering, which may increase their remorse and mutual hatred.

The phrase “on that Day” stresses the inevitability and specificity of the judgment. It is not a vague future but a definite appointed time. The verse ties a knot on the preceding dialogue, confirming that all the arguments are now over and only the consequences remain. The Meccan pagans who were united in opposing the Prophet will find themselves united in the fire, their worldly camaraderie transformed into eternal regret.

For the believer, this is a sobering reminder to choose friends and associates wisely. The company we keep either leads us to share in Allah’s mercy or to share in His punishment. The surah will later contrast this with the believers who share in bliss and honourable mention. The starkness of “surely” leaves no room for doubt or hope of escape for those who die upon associating partners with Allah.

=34=

Following the vivid scene of the misleaders and the misled sharing punishment, the verse delivers a universal principle: “That is certainly how We deal with the wicked.” The phrase “that is how We deal” establishes a divine pattern or sunnah that is consistent and unchanging. The punishment described is not an arbitrary act of anger but a deliberate, just recompense for wickedness. The “wicked” includes all those who commit kufr, shirk, and oppression, and who reject the messengers.

The use of “We” emphasizes the majesty and authority of Allah in executing this justice. This verse serves as a transition from the specific story of the Meccan deniers and the past nations to a general law. It tells the Prophet and the believers that what they have just heard about the Day of Judgment and the punishment is not an exception; it is the standard operating procedure of divine justice. This should both terrify the stubborn disbelievers and comfort the oppressed believers. For the believers, it means that the apparent success of the wicked in this world is temporary and does not reflect their ultimate fate. The day of reckoning will certainly arrive, and Allah’s way with the wicked is humiliation and punishment.

The verse also functions as a warning to anyone who might think they can escape accountability by clever arguments or by hiding behind influential leaders. The “deal” is a settlement of accounts, and no wicked person will be overlooked. It connects to the earlier rhetorical question about which is harder to create; the One who can create the heavens and earth can just as easily bring the wicked to justice. The finality of the statement reinforces the surah’s central theme of resurrection and judgment.

=35=

The surah now zooms in on a particular trait of the wicked that sealed their fate. “For whenever it was said to them in the world, ‘There is no god worthy of worship except Allah,’ they acted arrogantly.” The verse identifies the call to tawheed, the declaration of faith, as the core message they rejected.

The phrase “There is no god except Allah” (lā ilāha illā Allāh) is the essence of Islam. When this call reached them, whether from the Prophet or from the earlier messengers, their immediate reaction was not contemplation but arrogance.

The word “acted arrogantly” describes a psychological and behavioural state of considering oneself too grand, too important, or too sophisticated to submit to this simple truth. The Meccan pagans were particularly guilty of this: they perceived that accepting tawheed would mean abandoning the traditions of their forefathers and losing their social status as custodians of the idols. Their arrogance was a shield protecting their pride and interests.

The verse teaches that arrogance is the primary barrier between a person and true faith. It is not usually lack of evidence that prevents belief, but an inflated ego that refuses to bow to a higher authority. The call to monotheism demands a revolution in one’s life—a transfer of ultimate allegiance from self, tribe, or nation to the Creator alone. The arrogant find this humiliating. The verse’s use of “whenever” shows that the call was repeated again and again, yet each time they persisted in pride. This demonstrates that their punishment is fully earned; mercy was offered repeatedly but rejected. The believer should examine their own heart for traces of this same arrogance when they hear reminders to obey Allah.

=36=

The verse quotes their specific argument, which encapsulates both their arrogant attachment to ancestral worship and their ridicule of the Prophet. “Should we really abandon our gods for a mad poet?

” The question is rhetorical and dripping with contempt. They frame the call to tawheed as an outrageous demand to “abandon our gods”—deities they inherited from their forefathers and around which their social, economic, and religious life revolved. To abandon them was unthinkable, a betrayal of their very identity. Then they belittle the source of the message by calling the Prophet “a mad poet.” The accusation of madness was a common slander against prophets. They could not attribute his eloquence and the Quran’s impact to normal human ability, so they labelled it either sorcery or the ravings of a madman. By adding “poet,” they tried to categorize the Quran as imaginative literature, denying its divine origin. The surah will refute this later by emphasizing that the Prophet came with the truth and confirming earlier messengers. For now, the verse simply records their speech to expose its foolishness. A “mad poet” does not produce a book of law, wisdom, and consistent monotheistic theology that transforms a society. The absurdity of their claim would be evident to any fair observer, but their arrogance blinded them. The believer learns from this that truth is often met with character assassination. When people cannot counter the message, they attack the messenger. Patience is required, as the Prophet himself endured such insults.

The verse also highlights the emotional aspect of disbelief: they were not just intellectually unconvinced; they felt a deep personal attachment to their gods, making the call to abandon them feel like a loss.

=37=

The immediate response to their claim is a divine assertion: “In fact, he came with the truth, confirming earlier messengers.” The verse does not engage in a lengthy debate but simply states the reality. The Prophet brought the truth—this is a definitive judgment from Allah, who knows the nature of the message. The truth he brought is the message of pure monotheism, the resurrection, and accountability, which aligns perfectly with what all previous messengers taught. The Quran is not a new, strange doctrine invented by a madman; it is a continuation and confirmation of the chain of prophethood stretching back through history.

The phrase “confirming earlier messengers” serves multiple purposes. It validates the continuity of divine revelation, showing that Islam is not a break from the past but its fulfillment. It also refutes the pagans’ assumption that their forefathers were upon truth; the true forefathers in faith were the prophets like Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, whom the Quran confirms. For the Christians and Jews who may hear this, it calls them to recognize the truth in Muhammad’s message as consistent with their own scriptures’ original teachings. For the Meccan pagans, it undermines their claim that the Prophet invented something new.

The verse also comforts the Prophet himself: despite being called a mad poet, Allah testifies that he is on the path of all noble messengers. The believer takes from this verse a clear criterion for evaluating any claim to truth: does it confirm the core message of all prophets? Any teaching that contradicts the fundamental principles of tawheed and resurrection is falsehood. The truth is unified across time, and the Prophet’s way is the final and complete expression of it.

=38=

After affirming the truth of the Prophet’s message, the verse pivots to address the deniers directly with a solemn warning: “You will certainly taste the painful torment.” The address “You” points sharply at the Meccan pagans and, by extension, all who follow their path of arrogant denial.

The word “taste” gives the torment a personal and experiential emphasis; it will not be a distant observation but a direct, internalized suffering. The adjective “painful” underscores that this is not a metaphorical or light punishment but an intense, agonizing reality. The certainty expressed by “certainly” leaves no room for the scepticism they displayed in the world. Just as they were certain in their mockery that resurrection would never happen, now the Quran gives them a contrary certainty: punishment will happen and they will experience it. This verse brings back the surah’s theme of reversal: what they denied becomes their unavoidable fate.

The verse also serves as a direct answer to their rhetorical question about abandoning gods for a mad poet. The consequences of not abandoning those gods are this painful torment. The warning is an act of mercy even in its severity, because it is delivered in this world through the Quran, giving them a chance to repent. The torment is described as something they will “taste,” which implies a sampling of the consequences they have fully earned, with the full meal of eternal punishment to follow. Believers who hear this verse should feel a healthy fear that drives them to stay far from the actions and attitudes that lead to such an end.

=39=

The verse adds a critical principle of justice to the warning: “and will only be rewarded for what you used to do.” The word “rewarded” here is used in the context of recompense, whether good or bad, but in this context it clearly refers to the punishment being the exact requital for their deeds.

The phrase negates any possibility of injustice. They will not receive anything beyond what their own hands earned. Allah does not wrong anyone; the torment they taste is the direct product of their own disbelief, mockery, arrogance, and oppression.

The phrase “what you used to do” reminds them of their habitual actions in the world. Their disbelief was not a one-time mistake but a pattern of behaviour that they repeated and persisted in. This cuts off any argument that they are being punished for something minor or unintentional.

The verse also emphasizes individual moral responsibility, a theme the Quran consistently highlights. The recompense is tied strictly to deeds, which is both a warning and a comfort: a warning to those who do evil, and a comfort to those who do good that their good deeds will not be wasted.

In the context of the surah, this verse forms a bridge to the next section, which contrasts the fate of the wicked with the reward of the devout servants of Allah. The divine justice system is perfectly balanced; punishment is earned, and so is reward. The believer is encouraged to focus on the quality of their deeds, knowing that every action, whether seen or hidden, will be presented on that Day. The logical outcome of such belief is a life of careful, conscious devotion.

=40=

With this short verse, the surah introduces a massive shift in tone and content. “But not the chosen servants of Allah.” After detailing the accusations, the blame game, the warning of painful torment, and the principle of just recompense, Allah makes a crucial exception. The “chosen servants” are those whom Allah has purified, guided, and selected for His mercy.

The term “chosen” (mukhlaṣīn) can also be understood as those who have made their worship and devotion purely for Allah, i.e., the sincere ones. This group is completely exempted from the painful punishment described earlier. The verse serves as a ray of hope and a clear demarcation. It tells the listener that although the general pattern for the wicked is destruction, there is a category of people who are saved. The Meccan pagans thought they were the chosen ones due to their lineage or their custodianship of the Kaaba, but Allah clarifies that chosenness is based on sincere servitude, not tribal identity. The verses that follow will beautifully elaborate on what these chosen servants will receive in Paradise.

For the believer, this verse is a lifeline. It reminds us that amidst all the terrifying scenes of judgment, there is a safe haven for those who purify their faith. It also connects back to the beginning of the surah, where the angels were described as lined up in ranks, reciting and driving clouds—they too are among the chosen servants. The exception “but not” is a small phrase with enormous implications; it separates humanity into two distinct destinations. It makes one eager to learn the description of these chosen ones and their reward, which the surah will now unfold in vivid detail, beginning with their known provision and honoured status.
