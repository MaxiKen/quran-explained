=81=

After declaring that this is how Allah rewards the good-doers, the surah adds a final commendation of Noah that explains why he deserved such a magnificent reward. The verse states, “For he was truly one of Our faithful servants.

” The word “truly” emphasizes the certainty and depth of this quality. Noah did not merely perform acts of worship; he was a servant through and through, in every dimension of his being.

The phrase “faithful servants” translates the Arabic “ʿibādinā al-muʾminīn,” which combines servitude with faith. Faithfulness here means he was a true believer whose belief permeated his actions, his patience, and his complete reliance on Allah.

This description is the highest honour a human can receive. It signifies that Noah’s entire life was oriented around obedience to his Lord. He was not a servant of his own desires, nor of public opinion, nor of his tribe. When his people mocked him for building an ark on dry land, his faithfulness kept him steady. When his own son refused to believe and was drowned, Noah’s faithfulness led him to accept Allah’s decree without protest.

The verse teaches that the ultimate distinction is not lineage, wealth, or worldly success, but being counted among the faithful servants of Allah. This title connects Noah directly to the “chosen servants” mentioned repeatedly in the surah, those who are exempted from punishment and granted paradise. For the believers of Mecca, who were being told by the pagans that they had abandoned the religion of their forefathers, this verse reassures them that their new identity as faithful servants of Allah places them in the noblest lineage of all—the lineage of Noah, Abraham, and all the prophets.

The verse also prepares the ground for the story of Abraham, who is described in the next verses as one who followed Noah’s way. Faithfulness is thus shown to be a continuous chain linking all true believers across history. Every believer should ask themselves whether their life reflects this quality of being a truly faithful servant, not merely a cultural or nominal one. The reward of such faithfulness is not just deliverance in this world, but eternal honour.

=82=

The verse closes the account of Noah with a brief, stark statement that contrasts sharply with the honour given to the faithful servant. “Then We drowned the others.” There is no lengthy description here, as the surah’s focus is on the pattern and the lesson, not on graphic detail of the punishment itself, which has already been touched upon. “The others” refers to all those who rejected Noah’s call, including his own son who chose to seek refuge on a mountain instead of boarding the ark. The drowning was a total, comprehensive punishment that wiped out an entire civilization of disbelievers. The simplicity of the phrase, set against the elaborate praise of Noah, drives home the reality of divine justice. Those who are not among the chosen servants, who persist in disbelief and mockery, will face an end that is swift and final. The water, which could have been a source of life and mercy, became an instrument of destruction by Allah’s command.

In the context of the Meccan pagans who were being warned of a similar punishment—perhaps a drought, a military defeat, or the ultimate Day of Judgment—this brief verse carries a heavy threat. They are the “others” of their time unless they embrace the message brought by the Prophet.

The verse also highlights a recurring Quranic theme: the separation between truth and falsehood. The flood physically separated Noah and his followers from the disbelievers, and that separation became eternal. The believer learns that there is no middle ground; one is either with the faithful servants or among the others. The drowning of the others was not an act of cruelty but the necessary consequence of their sustained rebellion and their rejection of the clear warning. Noah called them for centuries, but they chose their path. The lesson is to never assume that Allah’s patience means indifference. The punishment, when it comes, is decisive.

The verse also serves as a transition to the next prophet, Abraham, who lived in a later generation and faced his own people’s idolatry with the same faith that Noah embodied.

=83=

The surah now introduces the next great prophet, Abraham, linking him directly to Noah. “And indeed, one of those who followed his way was Abraham.

” The phrase “his way” can be interpreted in two beautiful ways. The pronoun “his” refers to Noah, meaning that Abraham was a follower of Noah’s religious path—the path of pure monotheism, patience, and calling to Allah. Alternatively, some scholars read it as referring to the Prophet Muhammad, meaning Abraham was of his spiritual party, a predecessor who shared his core message. Both interpretations are valid and harmonious. The point is that the struggle for tawheed is a single continuous movement across history. Abraham is not presented as an innovator of a new religion but as a link in the chain of faithful servants. This was a powerful argument against the Meccan pagans who claimed Abraham as their ancestor and the founder of their religion. The Quran corrects them: Abraham’s true way was the worship of Allah alone, not the idolatry they practiced. By saying “one of those who followed his way,” the verse also indicates that there were other believers between Noah and Abraham whose names are not mentioned but who kept the flame of monotheism alive. This continuity is a source of comfort for the believers: they are not a lonely minority in history but part of a vast brotherhood of faithful servants. The verse elevates Abraham’s status while humbling him as a follower of a previous messenger, showing that all prophets are united in their mission.

For the believer, this teaches the importance of seeing oneself as part of an ummah that stretches back to the beginning of humanity. We should feel a deep connection with Noah’s patience and Abraham’s uncompromising faith. The introduction of Abraham also prepares the ground for the detailed scenes of his debate with his people, his destruction of the idols, and his great sacrifice, all of which will be recounted in the following verses to illustrate the pattern of a chosen servant facing a hostile, idolatrous society.

=84=

The narrative now focuses on Abraham’s personal spiritual state. “Remember when he came to his Lord with a pure heart.” The command “Remember” is addressed to the Prophet and the believers, calling them to reflect on Abraham’s exemplary approach to Allah.

The phrase “came to his Lord” does not refer to a physical journey but to the orientation of his entire being towards Allah. It is a coming of devotion, sincerity, and submission. Abraham turned away from the falsehood of his people and directed his soul wholly towards his Creator. The quality that defined this approach is “a pure heart” (qalb salīm). A pure heart, in Quranic terminology, is one that is free from shirk, doubt, hypocrisy, and love of evil. It is a heart that loves what Allah loves and hates what Allah hates, a heart that is untainted by the diseases of arrogance, envy, and worldly attachment. Abraham’s heart was pure because he had cleansed it from the idolatry of his father and people through his deep contemplation and his innate recognition of the One God. The purity of his heart was the foundation for all his subsequent actions, from his fearless debate with his people to his willingness to sacrifice his son.

This verse teaches that Allah looks at hearts, not outward appearances. The journey to Allah begins with cleansing the heart. For the Meccan pagans whose hearts were filled with tribal pride and attachment to idols, this was a call to purify themselves. For the believers, it is a reminder that the ultimate goal of all worship is to present oneself before Allah with a heart that is sound and pure.

The concept of qalb salīm is so central that the Quran describes the Day of Judgment as a day when neither wealth nor children will benefit, except one who comes to Allah with a pure heart. Abraham achieved this, and thus he became a beloved friend of Allah, worthy of mention in the divine scripture forever.

=85=

With a pure heart, Abraham could not remain silent about the falsehood around him. The verse describes his da‘wah: “and said to his father and his people, ‘What are you worshipping?’” Abraham began with his own father, Azar, who was a maker and seller of idols, and then extended his call to the wider community.

The question “What are you worshipping?” is a rhetorical challenge designed to make them reflect on the absurdity of their practices. It is not a request for information; Abraham knew very well what they worshipped.

The question invites them to articulate their beliefs so that their irrationality becomes evident. By asking this, Abraham places the burden of proof on the idolaters. He forces them to consider the worthiness of the objects they devote themselves to. In the Meccan context, the Prophet was doing the same: asking his people what they worshipped and why, when the Lord of the heavens and earth is the only One deserving of worship.

The mention of “his father” shows that the call to truth must start with one’s closest relatives, even if they are deeply entrenched in falsehood. It also demonstrates that blood relations do not override the obligation to speak the truth. Abraham’s approach is both compassionate and bold; he desires to save his father and his people from the fire, so he confronts their core error directly.

For the believer, this verse is a model of da‘wah: begin with what is closest, ask thought-provoking questions, and address the fundamental issue of worship. The pure heart of Abraham compelled him to speak, and his speech was not aggressive but reasoned, as the next verses will show. It is a lesson that true faithfulness often requires challenging the deeply held beliefs of one’s own family and community with wisdom and courage.

=86=

Abraham continues his questioning, sharpening the focus. “Is it false gods that you desire instead of Allah?

” The phrase “false gods” translates a word that implies fabricated deities, things that have no true divinity but are given divine status out of human desire and falsehood. The question contrasts these false gods with Allah, the One true God. The key word is “desire” (tamānan). It exposes the psychological root of idolatry: it is not based on reason or evidence but on whim, desire, and wishful thinking. They desire these gods either because their forefathers worshipped them, or because they serve their worldly interests and lusts. There is no intellectual basis for their worship; it is simply what they prefer.

This is a profound insight into human psychology. Often, people reject the truth not because of a lack of evidence but because accepting it would require them to abandon their desires and the social structures that benefit them. The Meccan pagans profited from the pilgrimage to the idols, and their tribal identity was tied to the idols. Their “desire” was for the status quo. Abraham’s question strips away all pretenses and exposes the emptiness of their worship. For the modern believer, this verse is a mirror: what are we truly desiring and prioritizing in our lives? Do we have false gods—money, status, ego—that we desire more than Allah? Idolatry is not limited to stone statues; anything that takes the place of Allah in one’s heart becomes a false god. Abraham’s challenge is timeless: examine your priorities and see if your desires have led you away from the pure worship of the Creator. The rhetorical nature of the question also implies that no reasonable person would openly admit they desire falsehood over truth, thus cornering them into self-reflection or defensive arrogance.

=87=

Having challenged their worship, Abraham shifts their attention to the true God with another powerful question. “What then do you expect from the Lord of all worlds?

” This is an appeal to their innate sense of justice and consequence. Abraham is saying: if you reject the true God, the Lord of all creation, what do you think His reaction will be? Do you expect Him to ignore your rebellion? Do you think He will not hold you accountable?

The phrase “Lord of all worlds” emphasizes Allah’s absolute sovereignty over everything. He is not a tribal deity or a local god; He is the Master of the entire universe. To worship others besides Him is a direct affront to His lordship.

The question implies that a terrible reckoning awaits those who persist in such an affront. It is a warning wrapped in a question, inviting them to consider the consequences of their actions. Abraham’s approach is logical: first establish the absurdity of idol worship, then establish the reality of the Creator, and then point to the inevitable judgment. This matches the structure of the entire surah, which begins with oaths to establish the truth of monotheism, then argues for resurrection and judgment, and then presents the fates of the believers and disbelievers. Abraham, in his brief speech, summarized the entire Quranic message.

For the believer, this verse inspires a sense of accountability. We should constantly ask ourselves: what do we expect from the Lord of all worlds? If we disobey Him, do we not fear His punishment? If we obey, do we not hope for His reward? The Lord of all worlds is not indifferent; He is just and powerful. Abraham’s question is left hanging in the air, unanswered by his people, because they have no coherent answer. Their silence reveals their intellectual bankruptcy.

This verse teaches the da‘wah technique of turning the attention of the heedless towards their Lord and His impending judgment.

=88=

The surah now recounts a specific incident in Abraham’s life that demonstrated both his wisdom and his strategy. “He later looked up to the stars in contemplation.” This occurred after his debate with his people, likely during a festival when they were preparing to leave the city. Abraham, who was known for his intelligence, looked up at the sky.

The phrase “in contemplation” indicates that he was not merely gazing but thinking and planning. Some scholars explain that his people were astrologers and star-worshippers, so Abraham used their own frame of reference as a strategy. He looked at the stars and then, as the next verse says, declared that he was sick, using the observation of the stars as a pretext that they would understand. His looking at the stars was not an act of worship or divination; it was a legitimate means of deduction and planning, using the knowledge of the celestial bodies that Allah created. Others interpret that his looking at the stars was a sign of his natural contemplation of the heavens, leading him to realize the Creator, but in this context, it was part of a ruse to stay behind while his people went to their festival, so he could destroy their idols. This event shows that sometimes wisdom requires using strategies that are permissible and intelligent to achieve a greater good. Abraham did not lie about being sick; he made a statement that was ambiguous but true in a certain sense, or it was a form of ma‘āriḍ (indirect speech) to avoid direct lying while still achieving a righteous aim.

The verse teaches that a believer should be thoughtful and clever, using his intellect in the service of faith, while never compromising on truth and sincerity. Abraham’s pure heart did not prevent him from being strategic; rather, his pure heart guided his strategy for the sake of Allah alone.

=89=

Following his contemplation of the stars, Abraham made a statement to his people: “I am really sick.” This declaration was part of his plan to avoid going with them to their pagan festival, thus giving him the opportunity to confront their idols alone. Scholars have debated the nature of this statement. It was not a lie. Some say he meant he was sick at heart due to their idolatry, as the Quran elsewhere describes the hypocrites as having a disease in their hearts. Others say he was physically unwell, perhaps with a fever, and that was true. Still others note that he used a form of speech that was technically true but intended to be understood by them as a physical illness that prevented him from attending the celebration. The pagan custom was to go out to a feast, and Abraham claimed weakness so he could stay behind. The key lesson here is not that deception is permissible in all cases, but that in the face of idolatry and the potential to eradicate falsehood, a believer may use legitimate indirect means that do not involve committing a sin. Abraham’s goal was to demonstrate the powerlessness of the idols by destroying them, thereby making a profound point to his people. Staying behind was necessary for that. The statement “I am really sick” must be understood in its context. The most cautious scholarly view is that he was genuinely sick in some sense—perhaps sick of their shirk—and the words he chose conveyed a truth while serving his righteous purpose. This incident is a lesson in balancing truthfulness with wisdom. The believer should always speak truth, but sometimes the full context allows for statements that serve a higher good without harming anyone. Abraham’s pure heart and his desire to guide his people justified his action, and Allah recorded it in the Quran without censure, showing divine approval of his overall conduct.

=90=

Abraham’s statement had its intended effect. “So they turned their backs on him and went away.” His people, thinking he was physically sick or weak, left him behind in the city while they went off to their festival.

The phrase “turned their backs on him” indicates that they abandoned him without care or concern. They did not investigate his condition further; they simply left him alone. This neglect gave Abraham the perfect opportunity to execute his plan. The verse highlights how the disbelievers were heedless and arrogant; they did not take Abraham seriously. They probably thought he was just a sick young man with strange ideas. This underestimation is a recurring theme: the forces of falsehood often dismiss the believers as weak or irrelevant, only to find that truth has a power they cannot comprehend. The turning of their backs also symbolizes their turning away from the truth Abraham represented. They were more interested in their festival and their idols than in listening to his message.

For the believer, this verse offers hope: sometimes, when it seems that opponents are ignoring you or walking away, that very neglect may be part of Allah’s plan to give you space to strengthen your faith and take positive action. Abraham used the solitude to confront the idols directly. The lesson is to be patient and perceptive; Allah can create openings in unexpected ways. The Meccan pagans similarly turned their backs on the Prophet, mocking him and going about their business. This verse foreshadows that their neglect will not last forever, and the truth will eventually confront them when they least expect it. Abraham’s solitary stand is a model for all believers who find themselves alone in a society dominated by falsehood.

=91=

With the people gone, Abraham took action. “Then he stealthily advanced towards their gods, and said mockingly, ‘Will you not eat your offerings?’” The stealth indicates that he went quietly and deliberately, perhaps to avoid any remaining guards, though the temples were likely empty. He approached the idols that had been set up, with food offerings placed before them as a sign of devotion. The pagans believed their gods consumed or blessed the offerings. Abraham, knowing these idols were lifeless stone and wood, addressed them with biting sarcasm. He asked them why they were not eating the food sitting right in front of them. The mockery was not for the sake of mockery itself; it was a pedagogical method to expose the absurdity of idol worship. By speaking to the idols as if they were alive, he highlighted the contradiction: their worshippers treat them as gods, yet they cannot perform the simplest act of a living being, like eating. This method of using irony and rhetorical questions is a legitimate form of da‘wah when dealing with clear falsehood, as it forces the audience to think. Abraham’s pure heart did not prevent him from using sharp wit; rather, his wit was a tool in service of truth. The Meccan pagans similarly placed offerings before their idols in the Kaaba. The Prophet later destroyed those idols, following in the footsteps of his father Abraham.

For the believer, this verse teaches that confronting falsehood sometimes requires bold, dramatic actions that capture attention and challenge the status quo. However, such actions must be guided by wisdom and the right timing, as Abraham’s was. His question, “Will you not eat?” remains unanswered, highlighting the lifelessness of false gods.

=92=

Abraham continued his mock interrogation of the idols. “What is wrong with you that you cannot speak?” After asking them about eating, he now questioned their muteness. The inability to speak is a clear proof of lack of life, intellect, and power. A god who cannot answer a simple question or defend itself is no god at all.

The question “What is wrong with you?” is a rhetorical device that exposes the defect of the idols. It suggests that there is something fundamentally broken about these so-called deities. Abraham knew they could not speak, but by publicly asking them, he was building a case that would later be presented to his people. The silence of the idols would condemn them and their worshippers. In the Quranic account in Surah Al-Anbiya, when the people returned and found their gods smashed, they asked Abraham if he did it. He replied, “Rather, this one, the biggest of them, did it. Ask them, if they can speak. ” That beautiful retort used the same principle: if the idols can speak, they can identify the culprit; if not, why worship them? The lesson for the believer is clear: anything worshipped besides Allah is mute, deaf, and powerless. Whether it is an idol, a human leader, wealth, or desire, none of them can truly speak, guide, or benefit. They are silent in the face of real need. Abraham’s question is a timeless challenge to every false object of worship. It also demonstrates the use of logic and humour in da‘wah. Rather than delivering a dry lecture, Abraham set up a scenario that made the absurdity of idol worship vividly obvious. The Meccan pagans hearing this would recognize the force of his argument and the shame of their own similar practices. The believer is encouraged to use reason and clarity when explaining tawheed.

=93=

After verbally mocking the idols, Abraham escalated his action to a physical demonstration of their helplessness. “Then he swiftly turned on them, striking them with his right hand.

” The word “swiftly” indicates a sudden, decisive movement. He did not hesitate or waver; he acted with full conviction. He struck the idols, likely using an axe or a tool, breaking them into pieces.

The use of “his right hand” is significant: the right hand symbolizes strength, honour, and righteous action. Abraham employed his strongest hand for this act of destroying falsehood, showing his complete commitment. He left only the largest idol untouched, as we know from other surahs, to later use it as a lesson. The act of striking the idols was not mere vandalism; it was a prophetic act of iconoclasm, a physical removal of spiritual corruption. Abraham, as a prophet, had the authority to do this, guided by divine wisdom. His action demonstrated that idols are not just false in theory but vulnerable in practice; they cannot defend themselves against even a single man. This visual proof was far more impactful than a hundred speeches. The Meccan believers, who were longing to see the Kaaba cleansed of idols, could find in Abraham’s action a powerful precedent. The lesson for believers is not to go around destroying property in contemporary pluralistic societies, as the legal and prophetic context differs, but to understand the principle: falsehood must be challenged actively, not just passively. The idols in our lives—the ideologies, the material attachments, the ego—must be “struck” with the right hand of sincere faith and decisive action. Abraham’s courage is a model: once the truth is clear, one should act upon it with strength, not remain in a state of passive acceptance of surrounding falsehood.

=94=

News of the destroyed idols reached the people, and they reacted with predictable rage. “Later, his people came rushing towards him furiously.” The festival ended, and they returned to find their sacred idols shattered into pieces, save for the largest one. The sight filled them with anger and humiliation.

The word “rushing” indicates a chaotic, emotional stampede. They did not come calmly to investigate; they came in a fury, ready to punish the one who had insulted their gods. Their anger reveals the deep psychological hold the idols had on them, despite their utter powerlessness. They had been publicly embarrassed, and their instinct was to retaliate with violence rather than to reflect on the truth that Abraham’s action demonstrated.

The phrase “his people” shows that the entire community was united in their opposition to Abraham. He stood alone against a furious mob.

This is the station of the prophets: solitary truth-bearers facing the collective wrath of misguidance. The verse reflects the experience of the Prophet Muhammad in Mecca, where the Quraysh would rush to persecute him and his followers whenever their idols were criticized.

For the believer, this verse is a reminder that speaking truth and dismantling falsehood often provokes intense backlash. One should not expect a calm, rational response from those whose vested interests and deep-seated beliefs are challenged. The rush of the people shows that they had no rational counter-argument; their only response was emotional rage. Abraham’s courage is contrasted with the mob’s irrationality. The believer must prepare psychologically for such reactions and remain steadfast, trusting that the truth will ultimately prevail, just as it did for Abraham.

The verse also shows that the destruction of idols was the catalyst that forced the issue, moving the confrontation from debate to a decisive showdown that would eventually prove Abraham’s prophethood.

=95=

When the furious people confronted Abraham, he did not panic or retreat. Instead, he seized the moment to deliver his ultimate argument. “He argued, ‘How can you worship what you carve with your own hands?’” This is the crowning logical blow. Abraham pointed out the simple, undeniable fact that the idols were man-made. They were products of human craftsmanship, carved from wood or stone by the very hands of the worshippers themselves. A thing that is created by human hands cannot possibly be the creator of humans or the controller of the universe. The argument is devastating in its simplicity: a maker is superior to what he makes. If you fashion an idol with your own hands, how can you then bow down to it and expect it to benefit or harm you?

The word “carve” emphasizes the artistic, manual process, reducing the idols from objects of reverence to mere human artefacts. This verse exposes the absurdity of all forms of human-made religion, where people create concepts or objects and then enslave themselves to them. For the Meccan pagans who intricately carved their idols from dates or stone, Abraham’s argument was directly applicable. It stripped away any mystical awe surrounding the idols and revealed them as lifeless lumps of matter.

For the believer, this argument is a timeless proof of tawheed: Allah alone is the Uncreated Creator, while everything else, including the most impressive human constructions, is created and subordinate. Abraham’s rational, fearless debate in front of a hostile crowd is an inspiration. He used a single, undeniable premise—you carve these things—to demolish their entire belief system. It teaches that the truth is inherently powerful and simple, requiring no complex philosophy, just clear thinking and the courage to state it.

=96=

Abraham extends the argument from the specific to the universal. “When it is Allah Who created you and whatever you do?” Not only are the idols made by them, but the very people who made the idols, along with all their actions, are created by Allah.

The phrase “whatever you do” can be understood as including the carving of the idols themselves. Thus, even the act of making the false gods is under Allah’s creative power and permission. The Creator of the universe is the one who gave humans the hands, the intelligence, and the materials to carve. So how can they take what ultimately belongs to Allah and use it to defy Him?

This verse establishes the ultimate basis of worship: gratitude and recognition of the Creator. Worship should be directed only to the One who originated our existence and sustains it. Abraham’s logic connects the creation of the heavens and the earth, the creation of human beings, and the creation of their actions, all pointing to the absolute sovereignty of Allah. It is a profound theological statement that refutes not only idol worship but also any belief that humans are fully autonomous. Everything we do, including our so-called creations, is within the domain of Allah’s creation and will. This humbles human pride and directs all praise to the true Doer. For the Meccan pagans, who acknowledged Allah as the supreme Creator but associated partners with Him, this argument closed any loophole. If Allah is your Creator and the Creator of your deeds, He alone deserves your worship, not the pieces of stone you shape.

For the believer, this verse fosters a deep awareness of Allah’s encompassing power, inspiring reliance on Him and humility. It also serves as a response to modern forms of idolatry where human achievements are glorified, forgetting that the ability to achieve itself comes from Allah. Abraham’s speech is the essence of tawheed al-rubūbiyyah leading necessarily to tawheed al-ulūhiyyah.

=97=

Confronted with an argument they could not refute, the people resorted to brute force. “They said to one another, ‘Build him a furnace and cast him into the blazing fire.’” The logical debate was over; their only response was to kill Abraham. They conspired among themselves, inciting each other to take this drastic action. The “furnace” (bunyān) here refers to a great structure or pit in which a massive fire would be kindled. This was to be a public execution, a spectacle to deter anyone else from challenging their gods.

The phrase “blazing fire” emphasizes the intensity of the punishment they planned. This is a classic reaction of falsehood when cornered: it turns to violence to silence the truth. The Quraysh listening to this would see a direct parallel to their own treatment of the Prophet, whom they also tried to kill. The verse reassures the believers that the threat of fire is nothing against the protection of Allah. Abraham’s people thought they could eradicate his message by burning him, but they forgot that the God Abraham called upon is the Lord of the fire and everything else. Their conspiracy shows that they recognized the existential threat Abraham posed to their system; they had no answer, so they sought to eliminate the questioner.

For the believer, this verse is a lesson that the path of truth often leads through extreme danger, and the forces of falsehood will not hesitate to use violence. Yet, the outcome is always in Allah’s hands.

The verse also highlights the wickedness of a society that would rather burn a man alive than reconsider its inherited beliefs. Abraham’s calm in the face of this sentence, not recorded here but known from tradition—he said, “Allah is sufficient for me, and He is the best disposer of affairs”—demonstrates ultimate trust. The believer should cultivate such trust, knowing that when all human means fail, Allah’s help is certain.

=98=

The verse summarizes the outcome of their plot with majestic brevity. “And so they sought to harm him, but We made them inferior.” They intended to burn Abraham and thereby prove their gods’ superiority and his weakness. Their plot was elaborate and their conviction was strong.

However, Allah’s intervention turned the tables completely. The fire was kindled, and Abraham was cast into it. But Allah commanded the fire, “O fire, be coolness and safety upon Abraham.” The fire lost its nature and became a garden of peace for him. The people watched in astonishment as their greatest effort to destroy him resulted in a miracle that exposed their impotence.

The phrase “We made them inferior” means that Allah humiliated them and gave victory to Abraham. They were the ones who were shown to be weak and defeated, not Abraham. Their attempt to assert dominance backfired, and Abraham emerged with even greater honour and credibility.

This is the divine sunnah for all who oppose the prophets: they plot, but Allah plans, and Allah is the best of planners. The verse serves as a direct message of hope to the Prophet and the believers in Mecca, who were facing the Quraysh’s plots. It reassures them that no matter how powerful the opposition seems, Allah can make them inferior in an instant. The fire of persecution cannot harm a believer unless Allah wills it.

For the believer, this verse is a source of immense comfort and courage. When one is faced with threats, bullying, or oppression for the sake of faith, one should remember Abraham and know that the harm intended by the enemies can be transformed into a source of coolness and safety by the One who controls all elements. The inferiority of the disbelievers is not just a future event in the Hereafter; it can manifest in this world as a sign of divine support for His faithful servants.

=99=

After his miraculous deliverance from the fire and the continued rejection of his people, Abraham made a momentous decision. “He later said, ‘I am leaving in obedience to my Lord. He will guide me.’” Abraham chose hijrah—emigration for the sake of Allah. When a land becomes hostile to faith and its people persist in disbelief despite clear signs, the prophets and righteous are permitted to leave. Abraham’s departure was not an act of cowardice but of obedience. He recognized that his mission in that particular place had reached its limit, and Allah had a greater plan for him elsewhere.

The phrase “in obedience to my Lord” shows that his migration was an act of worship, done at Allah’s command or in pursuit of a place where he could freely worship Allah. He did not leave out of frustration or despair; he left with complete trust in divine guidance. The statement “He will guide me” reflects his certainty that Allah would direct him to a land of blessing and a community of righteousness. Indeed, he journeyed from Ur or Babylon to the blessed land of Syria and Palestine. This hijrah paved the way for the birth of a great nation of monotheists from his descendants. For the Prophet Muhammad in Mecca, this verse was a clear parallel and a prophecy of his own upcoming hijrah to Medina. The Meccan believers were also later commanded to emigrate from the persecution of the Quraysh. The lesson for believers is that sometimes the best form of worship is to physically remove oneself from an environment of sin and oppression in order to preserve faith and build a new, God-conscious community. Abraham’s emigration was not the end; it was a new beginning. The trust that “He will guide me” is the motto of the muhājir. It teaches us to step into the unknown for Allah’s sake, confident that the One who commands the migration will also provide the destination and the path.

=100=

Upon his departure, or perhaps during his journey or settlement, Abraham turned to his Lord with a heartfelt supplication. “My Lord! Bless me with righteous offspring.

” This is the prayer of a man who, despite his old age and his barren wife Sarah, hoped in Allah’s mercy. He asked not just for any child, but for a righteous one—a child who would be among the ṣāliḥīn, those who are virtuous and obedient to Allah. Abraham’s primary concern was not lineage or personal comfort but the continuation of the message of tawheed. He wanted an heir who would carry the banner of monotheism after him. This supplication reflects the deep foresight of a prophet. He had left his people and his father; he now needed a new family of believers. This prayer was answered with the gift of Ishmael and later Isaac. The story of the sacrifice will soon follow, showing how that righteous offspring proved his worth.

The verse teaches the importance of prioritizing righteousness in our children over worldly success. Many people pray for children who are healthy, wealthy, or successful, but the most important quality is righteousness. Abraham’s prayer is a model for all parents. It also demonstrates that while one must take means, the ultimate source of righteous children is Allah’s blessing. Abraham was a prophet, yet he still supplicated humbly for this gift.

The word “bless me” (hab lī) implies a gift, something freely bestowed by divine grace. The believer learns that even the most pious cannot guarantee the righteousness of their offspring; they must constantly turn to Allah in prayer. This verse sets the stage for the beautiful narrative of Ishmael’s birth, his father’s dream, and the great test of sacrifice that both father and son would undergo, demonstrating the fruit of that righteous offspring.
