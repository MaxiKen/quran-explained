=61=

The narrative now moves from Abraham's encounter with the angels to their arrival at the dwelling of Lot. The transition is marked by the word "so," indicating the direct consequence of the mission they disclosed to Abraham. The angels, having delivered the glad tidings to Abraham and calmed his concern for Lot's believing family, depart and reach the cities of the plain.

The phrase "the family of Lot" here refers to Lot and his household, the small band of believers residing in Sodom. Lot, like Abraham, was a prophet and a man of immense hospitality and moral courage. He had been sent to a people whose wickedness had reached a level unprecedented in human history, as the Quran mentions elsewhere that they committed abominations no other nation had practiced before them. The angels arrived in the form of handsome young men, a form that would immediately test the depravity of the city's inhabitants. Lot, unaware of their true identity, saw them as human travelers in need of shelter and protection. The arrival of these guests at Lot's home is the spark that ignites the final confrontation between the prophet and his people, a confrontation that will end with the salvation of the believers and the total destruction of the transgressors.

This verse also serves as a narrative bridge, linking the mercy shown to Abraham with the justice about to descend on Sodom. The same angels who brought laughter to an elderly couple now bring terror to a corrupt society. It demonstrates that the divine messengers execute Allah's will in all its dimensions, carrying both reward and punishment to their respective destinations without partiality or hesitation.

=62=

When Lot saw the angels, he immediately recognized that these were strangers. The phrase "unfamiliar people" translates qawmun munkarun, which can mean people who are unknown, whose appearance or manner is unfamiliar, or people who bring with them a sense of something out of the ordinary. Lot had never seen these men before, and in a city as dangerous as Sodom, the arrival of unknown, strikingly handsome strangers was a cause for immediate alarm. Lot's statement is not a greeting of welcome but an expression of anxiety. He knew his people. He knew what they would do if they discovered the presence of these guests. His heart immediately became heavy with the burden of protecting them from the inevitable assault. This reaction reveals Lot's deep sense of responsibility and his understanding of the moral depravity that surrounded him. He was a stranger to his own people in terms of his righteousness, and now strangers had arrived who would be vulnerable to that people's evil. The tension in this verse is palpable. Lot is caught between his duty of hospitality, a sacred obligation in the ancient Near East and in the Abrahamic tradition, and his knowledge that extending hospitality to these guests would put them in mortal danger. He does not yet know that these are angels who need no protection. He sees them as human beings, and his prophetic conscience cannot bear the thought of handing them over or failing to shield them. This verse captures the psychological pressure under which Lot lived daily. He was a righteous man in a city that had normalized the worst of sins, and each new arrival of innocent travelers brought a fresh crisis. The verse sets the stage for the confrontation that follows and highlights the courage of Lot, who despite his fear, chose to protect his guests rather than surrender them to the mob.

=63=

The angels, perceiving Lot's distress, immediately reveal the true nature of their visit. They have not come as ordinary travelers in need of protection. They have come bearing the very torment that the people of Sodom had persistently doubted and mocked. Lot had warned his people for years about the consequences of their sins. He had called them to repentance, to decency, to the natural order of marriage that Allah had established. They responded with ridicule, with threats of exile, and with the chilling demand that he hand over his guests for their gratification. They did not believe that any punishment would ever come. The angels now declare that what was doubted has arrived.

The word "torment" here translates 'adhab, the same word used throughout the surah for divine punishment. The people doubted its reality, treating Lot's warnings as the rantings of a madman or a self-righteous moralist. The angels' statement is direct and without euphemism. They do not negotiate. They do not offer a final chance for repentance. The time for repentance had passed. The appointment that Abraham had been told about in verse fifty-eight has now reached its hour. This verse is a chilling example of how divine punishment can arrive when it is least expected and when the opportunity for turning back has been exhausted. For the believers reading this, it is a reminder that the warnings of the prophets are not empty threats. They are news of a reality that will unfold on a timeline known only to Allah. The mockery of the disbelievers does not delay the decree. It only makes the arrival of the punishment more devastating because it finds them utterly unprepared.

=64=

The angels reinforce their previous statement by emphasizing the absolute certainty and truthfulness of their mission. The phrase "with the truth" translates bil-haqq, which means with the established reality, with the decree that cannot be altered, with the just and inevitable outcome.

This is the same phrase they used with Abraham when they told him they brought good news in truth. The angels are not speaking from their own authority. They are conveying a divine decree, a truth that is as fixed as the laws of existence. Then they add, "and we are certainly truthful." This second affirmation addresses any remaining human hesitation in Lot's heart. Lot might have thought that the situation, though terrible, could still be resolved, that perhaps the people might be given more time, that his years of struggle might yet bear fruit. The angels make it clear that the matter is settled. They are truthful in what they say, and what they say is that the destruction is now imminent.

The repetition of the concept of truth underscores the Quranic theme that Allah's promises and threats are always true. The disbelievers may live as if the Day of Judgment and divine punishment are fables, but the angels who execute Allah's commands know otherwise.

This verse also serves a subtle function in the narrative: it distinguishes Lot's reaction from that of the disbelievers. Lot believes the angels. He does not argue, does not mock, does not demand additional signs. He accepts the truth when it is presented to him.

This is the mark of a prophet and a believer, immediate recognition of and submission to the truth, even when it brings news of a catastrophic upheaval. The angels' truthfulness is a guarantee that the believers will be saved and the wicked will be destroyed, a guarantee that will be fulfilled to the letter in the verses that follow.

=65=

The angels now give Lot precise instructions for his escape. The plan is detailed and urgent. First, they are to travel "in the dark of night," under the cover of darkness. This timing is for their safety, to avoid detection by the people of the city, and also because the destruction is scheduled for the early morning, as verse sixty-six will reveal. Night is the time when the city sleeps and when movement is least likely to be noticed. Second, Lot is instructed to "follow closely behind them." He is to be the last in the group, ensuring that everyone under his care proceeds ahead of him.

This is the posture of a shepherd who watches over his flock, making sure that no one falls behind or turns back. It reflects Lot's pastoral responsibility and his love for his family. Third, the command is given: "Do not let any of you look back." The prohibition against looking back is not merely a practical instruction to keep moving forward. It carries a deep symbolic meaning. Looking back would indicate attachment to the place of sin, lingering doubt, or a desire for what was being left behind. The destruction that was about to befall Sodom was so catastrophic that even witnessing it could be harmful, and the angels wanted the believers to be fully oriented toward their destination, not toward the past. This same command was given to Lot's wife, and she disobeyed. She looked back, and she was consumed by the punishment. Fourth, they are to "go where you are commanded," a direction that would lead them to safety, away from the blast zone. The destination was specified by Allah and communicated through the angels. This verse is a masterclass in obedience. Salvation requires following divine instructions precisely, without modification, delay, or personal rationalization. The believers were saved because they did exactly as they were told, when they were told, in the manner they were told.

=66=

Alongside the practical instructions for escape, Allah also revealed to Lot the fate of the people he was leaving behind. The verse uses the word "revealed" translating qadayna, which means We decreed, We communicated as a definitive judgment. The decree is stark: the sinners will be uprooted in the morning.

The word "uprooted" translates maqtu', which literally means cut off, severed at the root. The image is of a plant that is completely removed from the ground, leaving nothing behind. The people of Sodom, with all their numbers, their buildings, their wealth, and their power, would be cut off from the land of the living as if they had never existed.

The phrase "in the morning" specifies the exact time of the destruction. The night was for the escape of the believers. The dawn would bring the end for the transgressors. This timing is precise and meaningful. Morning is the time when the world awakens to a new day, when people typically feel safe, when the darkness of night gives way to light. But for the people of Sodom, that particular morning would not bring light and safety. It would bring a blast that would end everything. The revelation of this decree to Lot served multiple purposes. It reinforced the urgency of the night escape. It gave Lot the knowledge that his long struggle was about to be resolved. And it was a mercy to him, confirming that the justice he had called for during years of patient preaching was now arriving. For the believers of Makkah and all later generations, this verse is a reminder that Allah's decrees are precise and unalterable. The destruction of a people is not a random event but a scheduled occurrence, timed to the exact hour according to divine wisdom. The morning came, and the decree was fulfilled.

=67=

This verse describes the arrival of the mob at Lot's house, but with a profoundly disturbing detail: they came rejoicing. The Arabic word yastabshirun means they were delighted, exultant, celebrating in advance. They had heard about the handsome guests, and they were arriving not with shame or hesitation but with excitement and glee.

This is the depth of their moral corruption. Sin had become so normalized, so celebrated, that the prospect of committing a heinous crime was a source of joy. They did not skulk in the shadows or feel any sense of wrongdoing. They rushed to Lot's door openly, as if to a festival.

This detail is significant for understanding why the punishment was so severe. When a society reaches the point where it not only commits evil but celebrates it, when the collective conscience is so dead that atrocity is a cause for rejoicing, that society has passed the point of no return. The men of the city were not unaware of Lot's warnings. They had heard him preach, forbid their behavior, and call them to decency. But their response was not merely to ignore him. They actively sought to humiliate him and to violate the guests he was protecting. The rejoicing indicates that they saw this as an opportunity to assert their power and to mock the prophet in his own home.

This verse also evokes the earlier verse of the surah where Allah said the disbelievers would enjoy themselves for a while and then come to know. The men of Sodom were rejoicing in their final moments of respite, unaware that the morning would bring their annihilation. The contrast is stark and intentional. Their joy is the prelude to their ruin. The verse serves as a powerful warning that moral complacency and the celebration of sin are signs of a society heading toward its doom.

=68=

As the mob clamored at his door, Lot stepped out to confront them with words of desperate appeal. He began by asserting his right to protect those under his roof: "Indeed, these are my guests." In the culture of the ancient Near East, the obligation to protect a guest was sacred. A host was expected to defend his visitors with his life if necessary. Lot invoked this principle, hoping that even these hardened criminals would respect the age-old custom of hospitality.

The word "guests" translates dayf, which also means visitors, those who have placed themselves under one's protection. By emphasizing that they are his guests, Lot was making a moral claim on his people, appealing to whatever remnant of decency might still exist in their hearts. He then said, "so do not embarrass me. " The word for embarrass is tafdahun, which means to disgrace, to shame, to bring scandal upon someone. Lot was not merely asking them to refrain from a crime. He was asking them not to bring shame upon him in his own home, not to violate his honor as a host. This shows that Lot framed his plea in terms that his society could understand. He appealed to their concepts of honor and shame, even though those concepts had been twisted and perverted.

This verse reveals the painful position of a prophet who lives among a people whose moral compass is broken. Lot had to argue on their terms, using their language, while knowing that the deeper issue was their rebellion against Allah. His plea is both brave and tragic. He is one man standing against a mob, using words against brute force, honor against depravity.

Yet, as the next verses will show, his words fell on deaf ears. The mob had come with a specific intention, and no appeal to hospitality, honor, or decency would deter them.

=69=

Lot intensifies his appeal by shifting from the social argument of honor to the religious argument of fearing Allah. He commands them to fear Allah, to have taqwa, to be conscious of the divine power that watches over all actions and will hold every soul accountable.

This is the fundamental call of every prophet: to reconnect human beings with their Creator and to instill in them a sense of reverent awe that restrains them from evil. Lot had been delivering this message for years, and now, in this final confrontation, he repeats it one last time. He then adds a personal dimension: "and do not humiliate me.

" The word for humiliate is tukhzun, which means to disgrace, to bring low, to cause shame. Lot is speaking as a prophet who has been sent to these people, and their actions reflect on him. If they succeed in violating his guests, it would be a profound humiliation for Lot, a public demonstration that his message had no effect and that his authority as a prophet was disregarded. This personal appeal adds emotional weight to his plea. He is not just a preacher delivering an abstract sermon. He is a human being, standing in front of his own home, facing the imminent violation of his protected guests, and begging his people to spare him this degradation. The combination of the religious command "Fear Allah" and the personal plea "do not humiliate me" is a powerful rhetorical strategy. Lot is using every possible argument, from the highest spiritual principle to the most personal emotional appeal, to turn the mob away from their evil intent.

Yet the mob's response, recorded in the next verse, will show that neither the fear of Allah nor the bonds of human decency held any sway over them. They had sunk below the reach of both divine warning and human compassion.

=70=

The mob's answer to Lot's desperate pleas is chilling in its arrogance. They do not engage with his arguments. They do not deny that his guests deserve protection. Instead, they throw back at him their own previous threats. They remind him that they had already forbidden him from protecting anyone, from offering shelter, from interfering with their desires.

The word "protecting" translates from the root meaning to shelter, to guard, to give refuge. The people of Sodom had established a society in which the fundamental human act of offering protection to a vulnerable stranger was outlawed. They had inverted the moral order so completely that hospitality was a crime and predation was the norm. Lot had presumably been warned before, perhaps many times, that he must not interfere with their activities. They had told him to stop his preaching, to stop his moralizing, and above all to stop providing refuge to travelers. Now they were reminding him of that prohibition, with the clear implication that if he persisted in protecting these guests, he would face the consequences.

This verse reveals the psychology of a society in the final stages of moral collapse. It is not enough for the corrupt to practice their corruption. They must also silence and punish anyone who dares to stand against them. Lot's righteousness was an offense to them. His very existence as a man who said "no" to their evil was a rebuke, and they sought to eliminate that rebuke by forbidding him from acting on his conscience. This dynamic repeats throughout history. When evil becomes dominant, it does not tolerate goodness. It seeks to make virtue illegal and vice mandatory. The people of Sodom are the archetype of this tyrannical inversion of values, and their response to Lot is the classic argument of the oppressor: we have forbidden you from doing good, so comply or suffer.

=71=

In a final attempt to divert the mob from their evil intent, Lot offers an alternative that appeals to the natural and lawful order. He points them toward his daughters, meaning the women of his community or his own daughters as some commentators explain, and suggests that they marry them instead of pursuing their illicit desires.

The phrase "if you wish to do so" indicates that Lot was presenting a lawful outlet for their desires, the institution of marriage between men and women as ordained by Allah. This was not a casual offer but a prophetic plea pointing to the path of purity. Lot's offer has been the subject of much discussion in tafsir. By "my daughters," Lot was referring to the women of his nation, as a prophet is a father to his people, or to his own daughters, whom he offered in lawful marriage as a means of reforming their behavior. This was not an offer to commit sin but an invitation to the natural, divinely sanctioned relationship. The mob, however, had no interest in marriage. Their depravity had gone so far that what is lawful held no appeal for them. They wanted what was forbidden.

This is a common feature of unchecked lust. The lawful no longer satisfies because the thrill of transgression has become an addiction. Lot's offer was an act of desperate love for his people. He wanted to save them from the punishment he knew was coming. He was willing to bind his own family to them in marriage if that would turn them from their path. But the mob's refusal, implicit in what follows, shows that they were beyond rehabilitation.

This verse also demonstrates that the messengers of Allah exhaust every possible means of reform before punishment descends. Lot did not give up on his people even when they were at his door demanding his guests. He sought a last-minute opening, a final chance for them to choose the lawful over the forbidden. Their rejection of even this sealed their fate.

=72=

This verse contains a unique and powerful oath. Allah swears by the life of the Prophet Muhammad, a distinction not given to any other messenger in the Quran.

The phrase "By your life" is la'amruka, an oath by the Prophet's life and duration of his existence. This is an extraordinary honor, indicating the elevated status of the Prophet in the sight of Allah. Allah could swear by any of His creation, but choosing to swear by the life of Muhammad is a declaration of his immense worth and the special position he holds. The oath is made to emphasize the truth of what follows: that the people of Lot were wandering blindly, intoxicated by their lust.

The word "wandered blindly" translates la-fi sakratihim ya'mahun. Sakrah means drunkenness or intoxication, but here it refers to the stupor of unrestrained desire, a state in which reason and moral judgment are completely suspended.

The word ya'mahun means they were wandering, stumbling, moving without direction or purpose. This is a precise psychological description of what happens when a society gives itself over to lust. Reason becomes clouded. The ability to distinguish right from wrong fades. People stumble through life chasing desires that never satisfy, losing all sense of higher purpose. The oath by the Prophet's life also serves to console him. In the midst of the rejection he faced in Makkah, Allah swears by his very existence, affirming his truthfulness and the certainty of the divine narratives he conveys. The story of Lot is not a fable. It is a historical reality, as true as the life of the Prophet himself.

The verse also serves as a warning to the Makkans and to all who would follow the path of Sodom. Unchecked lust leads to a blindness that makes destruction seem impossible until it arrives. The people of Sodom were staggering in their intoxication, unable to see the divine judgment that was already at their doorstep.

=73=

The consequence of the people's moral blindness arrives with devastating precision. The word "blast" translates sayhah, which means a mighty shout, a thunderous sound, an explosive cry. This was the divine punishment that came suddenly and overwhelmingly. Some commentators say it was the terrifying scream of the angel Gabriel, a sound so powerful that it shattered their hearts and leveled their structures. The timing is specified as sunrise, the moment when light begins to spread across the land. The people who had spent the night in their intoxication, who had rejoiced at the prospect of violating Lot's guests, were now facing the dawn of their annihilation. The choice of sunrise is deeply symbolic. Light normally represents hope, guidance, and a new beginning. For the people of Sodom, the sunrise brought none of these. It brought the blinding light of divine wrath. The punishment began at the time when they might have expected the day to proceed as usual. This sudden reversal is a common feature of Quranic narratives of destruction. The punishment comes when the disbelievers feel most secure, when they least expect it, when they are going about their ordinary business. The blast that overtook them did not give them time to flee, to repent, or even to understand what was happening. It was swift, final, and absolute. This verse brings the narrative of Lot's people to its climax. The long period of warning, the years of patient preaching, the final night of escape for the believers, all led to this single moment at sunrise when the city and its inhabitants were wiped from the earth. The verse is a stark reminder that divine punishment, when it comes, is not a gradual process but a decisive act that leaves no room for second chances. The morning that the sinners had greeted with rejoicing became the morning of their utter ruin.

=74=

The destruction of Lot's people is described in even more graphic detail in this verse. Not only were they struck by a blast, but their entire physical environment was transformed into an instrument of their punishment. Allah turned the cities upside down.

The phrase "turned upside down" indicates a complete geological inversion. What was high became low, and what was low became high. The region where these cities once stood, near the Dead Sea, is characterized by unusual geological features consistent with a massive upheaval. The destruction was not a random earthquake but a deliberate act of divine justice that literally flipped the landscape.

In addition to the inversion, Allah "rained upon them stones of baked clay. " The stones are described as being of sijjil, baked clay or stones marked with specific designations. These stones fell from the sky like a deadly hail, targeting the criminals individually even as their cities were being overturned. The combination of these two forms of punishment, from below and from above, ensured that no one would survive. The earth rose against them, and the sky fell upon them. The four elements of nature, which normally sustain human life, became the agents of their destruction. The stones of baked clay are particularly significant. The people of Sodom had prided themselves on their sophistication, their urban civilization, their material achievements. They were destroyed by something as simple as hardened clay, a material that is formed from earth and fire, reminiscent of the very elements of creation that Allah mentioned in the verses about the creation of humanity and jinn.

This is a profound demonstration that Allah can use any means to execute His will. The most advanced civilization is helpless against the simplest elements when Allah commands those elements to serve as soldiers of His justice.

=75=

After recounting the destruction, Allah draws the listener's attention to the lessons embedded in the narrative. The word "signs" translates ayat, the same word used to describe the verses of the Quran itself. The historical event and the Quranic account of it are both signs, pointing toward deeper truths about Allah, about human nature, and about the consequences of moral choices.

The phrase "for those who contemplate" translates lil-mutawassimin, which is a particularly rich Arabic term. It comes from the root meaning to observe carefully, to scrutinize, to read the signs and draw conclusions. It refers to people who do not merely glance at events but study them, probe their meaning, and allow the lessons to penetrate their hearts. This verse is a call to intelligent reflection. The ruins of Sodom and Gomorrah are not merely archaeological curiosities. They are messages from the past, reminders written in stone and ash of what happens when a society defies its Creator and normalizes abomination. Those who look at such sites with a contemplative eye will see not just ruins but warnings. The verse implies that the signs are there, but they benefit only those who have cultivated the capacity to read them. A person who approaches the world with a closed heart or a dismissive attitude will see nothing but rocks and dirt. The person who approaches with faith and reflection will see the fingerprints of divine justice.

This principle applies to all of the destroyed nations mentioned in the Quran. Their stories are preserved not for entertainment but for edification. Every believer is called to be among the mutawassimin, those who contemplate and draw the right conclusions. The sign is present.

The question is whether we have the eyes to see it and the wisdom to apply its lesson to our own lives and societies.

=76=

This verse adds a specific historical and geographical detail to reinforce the reality of the narrative. The ruins of Lot's people were not lost to history or buried in some inaccessible location. They lay along a "known route," a path that the Arabs of the Prophet's time traveled regularly. The trade caravans of the Quraysh passed through the region of the Dead Sea on their journeys to Syria. They could see with their own eyes the desolate landscape, the strange rock formations, and the evidence of a once-thriving civilization that had been obliterated. The ruins were not hidden. They were visible, accessible, and unmistakable.

This detail serves as a powerful argument against those who dismissed the Prophet's warnings as myths or fables. Allah is essentially saying: go and look for yourselves. The evidence is along the road you travel twice a year. You do not need to undertake an archaeological expedition. The ruins are right there, in plain sight, along the well-known route that your grandfathers and great-grandfathers used. This appeal to empirical observation is consistent with the Quran's method of engaging the human intellect. Faith is not a blind leap. It is supported by evidence that can be seen, examined, and verified. The ruins of Sodom and Gomorrah are a standing testimony that the Quran's account is true.

The verse also carries a lesson about the impermanence of worldly power. The people of Lot were prosperous, settled, and secure. Their cities were major urban centers. Today, those cities are a wasteland, a cautionary tale for travelers who pass by and wonder what happened. The known route that once facilitated their trade now facilitates the contemplation of their ruin. This reversal of fortune is a recurring pattern, and those who travel the roads of the world with open eyes can see similar lessons everywhere. The verse is an invitation to learn from history, to recognize that the fate of past nations is not random but the operation of unchanging divine laws.

=77=

This verse echoes verse seventy-five but with a subtle and important difference. Whereas the previous verse said the signs are for "those who contemplate," this verse says the sign is for "those who believe," al-mu'minin. The two descriptions are complementary. Contemplation is the process, and belief is the outcome. Those who contemplate the ruins with sincerity and intellectual honesty will arrive at belief. The sign is present for all to see, but only those who have the quality of iman, of faith, will fully benefit from it. For the believers, the destruction of Lot's people is not just an ancient story. It is a confirmation of everything the Quran teaches about Allah's justice, about the reality of prophethood, and about the consequences of rejecting divine guidance. It strengthens their faith because it shows that the warnings they believe in are not abstract theories but verified historical facts.

The verse also makes a distinction between different levels of engagement with the signs. The contemplative person sees the sign and begins to think differently. The believer sees the sign and feels their faith deepen and their resolve strengthen. The ultimate goal of the Quranic narratives is to produce and nurture iman, and this verse explicitly states that the story of Lot is a sign that serves exactly that purpose. For the believers in Makkah who were suffering under the persecution of the Quraysh, this verse was a source of immense comfort. It reminded them that the forces of evil, no matter how dominant they appear, have an expiration date. The people of Lot seemed invincible, their society seemed permanent, their rejection of the prophet seemed secure. But the morning came, and they were no more. The believers who trust in Allah and follow His guidance will be saved, just as Lot and his family were saved. The sign is for them, to reassure their hearts and to anchor their faith in the certainty of divine justice.

=78=

The surah now moves to another example of a destroyed people, the residents of the Forest. The Arabic word is al-Aykah, which refers to a thicket, a dense cluster of trees, a forested area. These were the people of the prophet Shu'ayb, who is mentioned in several surahs. They lived in the region of Madyan, near the Gulf of Aqaba, in a fertile and well-watered area that supported extensive vegetation, hence the name. They were known for their commercial activities and their dishonest business practices, particularly their habit of giving short measure and cheating their customers in trade. The verse describes them as "truly wrongdoers," emphasizing the depth and persistence of their injustice.

The word for wrongdoers is zalimun, which comes from the root meaning to put something in the wrong place, to transgress, to violate rights. This description encompasses all their crimes: economic exploitation, rejection of their prophet, and worship of false gods. The story of Shu'ayb is recounted in detail in Surah Ash-Shu'ara and other surahs. He called his people to worship Allah alone and to deal justly in their business transactions. He warned them not to shortchange people, not to spread corruption, and not to threaten those who believed. They responded with mockery and arrogance, telling Shu'ayb that if not for his family's status they would have stoned him. They challenged him to bring down the punishment he was warning about. This verse condenses their entire history into a single identifying phrase. They were the people of the forested region, and their defining characteristic was wrongdoing.

The mention of them here, immediately after the story of Lot, reinforces the pattern of surahs like this one that recount multiple destroyed nations in rapid succession. Each people had its prophet, its specific sins, and its specific form of destruction, but the underlying pattern is the same: rejection of divine guidance leads to ruin.

=79=

Allah states that He inflicted punishment upon the people of the Forest, just as He did upon the people of Lot. The specific form of punishment given to Shu'ayb's people is described elsewhere in the Quran. They were seized by a terrible earthquake or a blast that left them lying dead in their homes, faces down. Some reports also mention a cloud of fire that rained down upon them after they had experienced an intense heat wave. The punishment was sudden, comprehensive, and final, leaving their thriving commercial centers in ruins.

The verse then draws an interesting geographical parallel. Just as the ruins of Lot's people lie along a known route, so do the ruins of both nations.

The phrase "both nations" refers to the people of Lot and the people of Shu'ayb. Both sets of ruins were visible to the Arab travelers of the time. The route to Syria passed by the Dead Sea region where Sodom and Gomorrah once stood. The route to Yemen or within the Hijaz passed through or near the old territories of Madyan. The Quraysh, in their winter and summer journeys, would have been familiar with both sites. This geographical detail is a powerful argument from divine mercy. Allah did not only send prophets with warnings. He left behind physical evidence of the truth of those warnings. The ruins are like open books, written not in words but in stones and sand, telling the story of what happens when human arrogance meets divine justice. The "well-known road" indicates that these are not remote or hidden locations that require special effort to find. They are along the paths that people travel regularly. The travelers can see them, reflect on them, and draw the correct conclusions, if they have the will to do so. This accessibility of the evidence removes any excuse. No one can claim that the warnings were delivered in secret or that the evidence of past punishments was unavailable. The ruins are there, on the well-known road, a silent but eloquent testimony to the truth of the Quranic message.

=80=

The surah now introduces the third and final example in this sequence: the residents of the Stone Valley, known as the people of al-Hijr. This is the community from which the surah takes its name. They were the people of Thamud, the nation to whom the prophet Salih was sent. They lived in a region of northwestern Arabia characterized by massive rock formations, and they were famous for their skill in carving elaborate homes and palaces directly into the mountainsides.

The phrase "denied the messengers" indicates their rejection of Salih, and by extension the entire concept of prophethood and divine message. The denial of one messenger is equivalent to the denial of all, because the core message is the same. The people of Thamud were successors to the civilization of Aad, and they inherited great power, wealth, and technological skill. Their arrogance led them to believe they were invincible. They demanded that Salih bring them a miraculous sign, and Allah sent them a she-camel as a test. They were commanded to allow the camel to drink from the well on specific days and to share the water. Instead, they hamstrung and killed the camel, openly defying the prophet and challenging Allah to bring the punishment they had been warned about.

The term "Stone Valley" is a direct reference to the physical environment they inhabited and the very mountains they carved with such pride. Their skill in stonework gave them a false sense of security. They thought that their carved homes, their fortresses in the rock, would protect them from any calamity.

The following verses will describe their destruction and the futility of their defenses. By beginning with "Indeed," the verse signals that this is an established fact, a well-known piece of history that the Arabs were familiar with. The ruins of Thamud were located along the trade route to Syria, and the Quraysh passed by them regularly. This verse sets up the final historical example in the surah, which will be developed in the next verses and which serves to drive home the warning to the Makkan disbelievers that their own rejection of the messenger places them in the same category as those who came before and who were destroyed.
