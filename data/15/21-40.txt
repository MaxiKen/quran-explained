=21=

After describing the sustenance placed in the earth for human beings and countless other creatures, Allah now makes an even more comprehensive statement about His absolute ownership and control over all provision. The verse begins with a sweeping negation: there is not a single thing that serves as a means of sustenance whose treasuries, whose reserves, are not held exclusively by Allah.

The word translated as "reserves" is khaza'in, the plural of khizanah, meaning storehouses or treasuries. This is a powerful metaphor. Every resource, every form of provision, every source of life and livelihood exists within the vast storehouses of Allah's power and generosity. The rain that falls, the crops that grow, the minerals beneath the earth, the energy of the sun, the oxygen in the air, the knowledge and skills that enable human productivity, all of these originate from divine treasuries that never diminish and never deplete. This understanding fundamentally reorients the human relationship with provision. We often imagine that our sustenance comes from our jobs, our businesses, our investments, or our social connections. These are merely means, channels through which Allah delivers what He has already decreed. The true source, the reservoir from which all provision flows, is with Allah alone.

The verse then adds the crucial qualifier: "only bringing it forth in precise measure." The Arabic word for measure here is qadar, which carries the sense of a determined amount, a precise decree. Allah does not release provision haphazardly or randomly. Everything that reaches any creature has been measured, apportioned, and sent down according to a wisdom that encompasses all of creation. Some receive abundance, some receive scarcity, and both are tests. The one given plenty is tested with gratitude and the responsibility of spending correctly. The one given little is tested with patience and trust. Neither condition is an indication of Allah's pleasure or displeasure by itself. This precise measure also extends to the natural world. The rain falls in specific quantities. The seasons bring their cycles of growth and dormancy in exact patterns. The ecological balance is maintained through countless measured provisions that interact with one another. The verse is both a comfort and a warning.

For the believer, it is a comfort to know that one's provision is guaranteed by the One who owns all storehouses. Anxiety about sustenance loses its grip when the heart truly understands that no soul will miss what was decreed for it. For the arrogant, it is a warning that their wealth and resources are not truly theirs. They are merely temporary custodians of what belongs to Allah, and He can withdraw it as easily as He granted it.

=22=

This verse draws attention to two specific and intimately connected manifestations of divine provision: the winds and the rain. The winds are described as "fertilizing," translating lawaqih, which literally means impregnating or fecundating.

This description is remarkably precise. Winds carry pollen from plant to plant, enabling the reproduction of countless species. They carry moisture-laden clouds across continents. They stir the atmosphere and facilitate the mixing of air masses that produce weather patterns. The modern scientific understanding of wind as a vector for pollination, cloud formation, and atmospheric circulation is encapsulated in this single word. The pre-Islamic Arabs understood the practical importance of winds for their agriculture and their lives, but the Quran elevates this understanding by linking the winds directly to Allah's creative and sustaining action. The winds are not independent natural forces. They are sent by Allah as agents of His mercy, carrying out a fertilizing function that prepares the way for rain. Then comes the rain itself, brought down from the sky for human beings to drink.

The phrase "for you to drink" highlights the direct benefit, but the rain's purposes extend far beyond quenching thirst. It sustains rivers, lakes, and underground aquifers. It irrigates crops, supports livestock, and maintains the entire freshwater ecosystem on which human civilization depends.

The verse then closes with a statement that echoes the previous verse's theme of divine ownership: "It is not you who hold its reserves." Human beings do not control the storehouses of rain. We cannot summon a storm when we want it or hold back a flood when it threatens. Despite all our technological advances in weather prediction and water management, we remain utterly dependent on the natural cycles that Allah has established and that He alone sustains. This humbling reminder is particularly relevant in an age of technological hubris. We can build dams, desalination plants, and irrigation systems, but we cannot create water from nothing. The reserves are with Allah, and He releases them according to His wisdom and measure. The verse connects seamlessly to the previous one, where Allah stated that all storehouses are with Him. Here He provides a concrete, observable example that every human being can witness. The rain that falls on the just and the unjust alike is not the product of human effort but of divine mercy. The appropriate human response is not pride in our water management systems but humble gratitude to the One who sends the fertilizing winds and brings down the life-giving rain.

=23=

This verse presents a direct and emphatic declaration of Allah's exclusive power over life and death. The Arabic construction uses inna for emphasis, leaving no ambiguity. Life and death are not autonomous processes, not the results of natural selection, not accidents of biology, and not within the domain of any other deity or force. They are the direct and deliberate acts of Allah. Every living thing that comes into existence does so by His creative command. Every living thing that dies does so at His decree. The embryo that forms in the womb, the seed that germinates in the soil, the microbe that divides in the water, all receive life from the same source. And when the appointed term arrives, that life is taken back. Between the giving and the taking, there is no independent power that can add a moment or subtract one. This dual power, life and death, is one of the central proofs of Allah's uniqueness. The idols that the Makkans worshipped could neither create a fly nor prevent its death. The powerful rulers who oppressed the believers could not escape their own mortality. The Prophet's opponents who mocked him would one day face the death they refused to contemplate. The verse functions as both a comfort for the believers and a warning to the rejecters. The believers who feared persecution could take solace in knowing that their lives and the lives of their enemies were both in Allah's hands. The timing of victory and defeat, of life and death, was not determined by human power but by divine wisdom.

The verse then concludes with a profound declaration: "And We are the Eternal Successor." The Arabic word is al-Warithun, from the root of inheritance. Allah is the Inheritor, the One who remains after everything else has perished. All human possessions, all kingdoms, all wealth, all relationships, all achievements, will eventually be left behind. Their owners will die, and they will pass to others or crumble into dust.

Ultimately, everything returns to Allah, the only Being who is eternal and self-subsisting. This title provides the ultimate perspective on life and death. Human beings spend their lives acquiring, building, and competing for things they will inevitably leave behind. The only thing that endures is what is done sincerely for Allah. Everything else becomes part of what Allah inherits. This understanding transforms the believer's relationship with worldly possessions and ambitions. They are means to serve Allah, not ends in themselves, because ultimately everything goes back to Him.

=24=

After declaring His power over life and death and His status as the Eternal Inheritor, Allah now affirms His comprehensive knowledge of humanity across all time. The verse states that He knows those who have passed away, the earlier generations, and He equally knows those who are yet to come, the later generations.

The Arabic uses the verb form for intensive and complete knowledge. This is not a general awareness but a detailed, specific, and exhaustive knowledge of every individual, their circumstances, their actions, their intentions, and their ultimate fate. The verse can be understood on multiple levels simultaneously. On one level, it addresses the Prophet and his community directly. Allah knows those who believed and passed away before the Prophet's mission, and He knows those who will believe after him until the end of time. The struggle the Prophet was engaged in was not isolated in history. It was part of a continuous chain of faith stretching back to the earliest human beings and forward to the final generation. On another level, the verse speaks of Allah's knowledge of all human beings in relation to the Day of Gathering mentioned in the next verse. When the time comes to assemble all of humanity for judgment, there will be no confusion, no missing records, and no need for investigation. Every soul is already known to Allah, its entire history recorded, its precise place in the sequence of generations established.

The verse also provides a subtle but powerful argument against the Makkan disbelievers' mockery. They acted as if they were the arbiters of truth and that the Prophet was a marginal figure who would be forgotten. Allah reminds them that He knows every generation, the ones who came before and the ones who will come after. The arrogant people of Makkah were just one small link in a vast chain of humanity, and their significance was not measured by their wealth or status but by their response to the truth. For the individual believer, this verse offers a sense of significance and accountability. Each human life is known to Allah. No one is lost in the crowd. No one is forgotten by the Creator. The generations that came before us lived their lives and faced their judgment. The generations after us will do the same. Our own lives unfold under the watchful knowledge of the One who knows all who have gone and all who will come. This awareness should inspire both humility and a sense of purpose. We are part of a larger human story, and our chapter matters.

=25=

This verse provides the conclusion to the sequence that began with the declaration of Allah's ownership of all sustenance and moved through His power over life and death and His comprehensive knowledge of all generations. Now Allah states that He alone will gather all of humanity together for judgment.

The word "gather" translates yahshuruhum, which carries the meaning of assembling, collecting, and bringing together from all directions. This gathering encompasses every human being who has ever lived, from the first to the last, across all times and places. The verse specifies that this gathering is the exclusive act of "your Lord." The disbelievers of Makkah might have imagined that their idols would intercede for them or that they would somehow escape accountability. This verse firmly closes that door. The gathering is Allah's alone, and no one else has any role or authority in it. The two divine names that conclude the verse, al-Hakim and al-Alim, the All-Wise and the All-Knowing, explain why the gathering is both certain and just. Allah's knowledge ensures that nothing is forgotten, no deed is overlooked, and no injustice occurs in the accounting. Every action, every intention, every secret thought is fully known to Him. His wisdom ensures that the gathering serves a perfect purpose. It is not an arbitrary display of power but the necessary culmination of human existence. A world in which good and evil, belief and disbelief, justice and oppression all ended in the same nothingness of death would be a world devoid of ultimate meaning. Divine wisdom requires that there be a day when accounts are settled, when the oppressed receive their justice, and when the distinction between those who lived for Allah and those who lived in rebellion becomes manifest.

This verse also connects directly to the preceding verses about life, death, and knowledge. Allah gives life, causes death, knows every generation, and will gather them all. The cycle is complete. Creation, life, death, and resurrection are all in His hands. For the Prophet and the believers facing persecution, this verse was a source of strength. The apparent triumph of the disbelievers was temporary.

The final gathering would reveal the true outcome. The mockers who seemed powerful in Makkah would stand before the same Lord as the believers they oppressed, and the judgment would be based on truth, not on worldly status. This perspective transforms the believer's patience from passive endurance into active trust. The delay in justice is not denial. The gathering is certain, and the Judge is All-Wise and All-Knowing.

=26=

The surah now transitions into the extended narrative of human creation and the rebellion of Iblis, a story that provides essential context for understanding the nature of human existence, the source of evil, and the ongoing struggle between obedience and temptation. The verse begins with the affirmation that Allah created man, using the Arabic word insan, the human being, from a specific material: sounding clay moulded from black mud. The description is remarkably detailed and sensory. The clay is described as "sounding," translating salsal, which indicates dry clay that produces a ringing sound when struck, like pottery that has been fired. It is "moulded," indicating that it was shaped and formed with deliberate artistry. It originates from "black mud," hama'in masnun, which conveys the image of dark, smooth, sticky mud that has been altered over time, perhaps fermented or aged. This origin story carries profound implications. The human being is created from the earth, from the most humble and common of materials. Mud is something people walk on without a second thought. It is the substance of the ground, trodden underfoot. By reminding human beings of this origin, Allah cuts through all arrogance and pretension. The human who struts proudly on the earth, who oppresses others, who considers himself self-sufficient, was created from mud. His body, no matter how strong or beautiful, is ultimately made of the same elements as the soil. At the same time, the careful description of the clay as "sounding" and "moulded" points to the artistry of the Creator. The same substance that is lowly in its raw form becomes a marvel of design in Allah's hands. The human body, with its intricate systems, its delicate balance, and its capacity for thought, creativity, and worship, is a masterpiece crafted from the humblest of origins. This dual message, humility about our origin and wonder at our design, pervades the Quran's treatment of human creation. We are not to worship the mud from which we came, as some materialists effectively do by denying any higher purpose. Nor are we to deny our lowly origin and act as if we are self-existent deities. We are to recognize that a magnificent Creator took humble material and shaped it into a being capable of knowing and worshipping Him. This recognition naturally leads to the response of gratitude and submission. The One who shaped clay into a human being deserves to be worshipped by that human being.

=27=

This verse provides crucial information about another category of conscious beings, the jinn, and it does so by contrasting their creation with that of humanity. The jinn were created earlier, before human beings. The Quran does not specify exactly how much earlier, but the sequence indicates that their existence predates the creation of Adam. This chronological priority is significant for understanding the story of Iblis that follows. Iblis was among the jinn, a being who had worshipped Allah for ages before the creation of Adam, and his long history of worship became the ground for his later arrogance when he was commanded to prostrate to the new creation. The material from which the jinn were created is described as "smokeless fire," translating the Arabic phrase min marijin min nar.

The word marij refers to a smokeless, pure flame, a fire that is intense and translucent, without the dark smoke that accompanies ordinary combustion. This is a form of creation entirely different from the clay-based creation of human beings. The jinn, being created from fire, possess qualities associated with that element: they are subtle, invisible to human eyes under normal circumstances, quick, and capable of assuming different forms.

However, their fiery origin does not imply superiority. Fire, for all its intensity and brilliance, can be destructive and consuming. The clay of human creation, for all its humility, is the substance from which life springs, the medium in which seeds germinate and grow. This verse is important for correcting common misconceptions. Some people imagine that the jinn are fallen angels or that Iblis was an angel who disobeyed. The Quran is clear that the jinn are a separate creation, distinct from angels. Angels were created from light and are incapable of disobedience. The jinn were created from fire and, like humans, possess free will. Among them are believers and disbelievers, the righteous and the wicked. Iblis was a jinn who had reached a high station through his worship, but his essential nature as a creature of fire with free will set the stage for his eventual rebellion.

The mention of the jinn here also expands the scope of the narrative. The struggle between good and evil is not limited to human beings. There is an entire unseen realm of conscious beings facing similar tests of obedience. This awareness broadens the believer's understanding of the cosmos and reinforces the message that the Quran's guidance addresses not just human concerns but the fundamental realities of existence that affect all created beings with free will.

=28=

The narrative now moves to the dramatic moment when Allah announced His intention to create a human being to the angels. The instruction to "remember" is directed to the Prophet, and through him to all who hear the Quran, calling them to reflect on this primordial event as if they were witnessing it. The scene takes place in the heavenly realm, before the creation of Adam, when Allah informed the angels of His plan. The description of the human being repeats the exact words from verse twenty-six: "sounding clay moulded from black mud. " This repetition is deliberate. The angels are being told that this new creation will be made from humble earthly material. They are being prepared for the fact that this creature, despite its lowly origin, will be honored with a special status, including the command to prostrate that will follow. The angels' response is not recorded here, but elsewhere in the Quran we learn that they expressed concern, asking whether Allah would place on earth a being that would cause corruption and shed blood. Their question was not an objection but an inquiry born from their knowledge of the jinn's previous history on earth, where they had caused great turmoil. The angels, who are beings of pure obedience, could not understand why a creature with free will, capable of disobedience and bloodshed, would be given authority on earth. Allah's response, recorded in Surah Al-Baqarah, was "I know what you do not know," indicating that the wisdom behind human creation transcended the angels' understanding. This background enriches the verse before us. The announcement to the angels was not merely informational. It was the beginning of a divine plan that would unfold across human history, a plan that included prophethood, revelation, guidance, testing, and ultimately the reward of Paradise or the punishment of Hell. The angels were being enlisted as witnesses and, as the next verse will show, as participants in honoring the new creation. The clay of Adam's origin is emphasized because it highlights the contrast between what the human being is made of and what the human being is destined for. From mud comes a creature that will carry the divine spirit, that will be taught the names of all things, that will be the vicegerent of Allah on earth, and that will be honored with the prostration of angels.

=29=

This verse contains the divine command that became the test separating the obedient angels from the arrogant Iblis. Allah instructs the angels that once He has completed the fashioning of the human being and breathed into him from His spirit, they are to fall down in prostration.

The sequence of actions is significant. First comes "fashioning," the shaping and forming of the physical body from clay.

This is the biological creation, the intricate crafting of the human form with its bones, muscles, nerves, and organs. The word "fashioned" translates sawwaituhu, which implies making something straight, balanced, and complete. The human form is not a rough sketch but a perfected design.

The second stage is the breathing of the spirit. The phrase "a spirit of My Own" is highly significant.

The Arabic is min ruhi, literally "from My spirit." This does not mean that a portion of Allah's essence entered the human being, a concept that would compromise divine transcendence. Rather, it is an honorific attribution. The spirit that was breathed into Adam was a spirit created by Allah, owned by Him, and specially honored by being attributed to Him directly. This breathing of the spirit transformed the clay form into a living, conscious being. It is the divine gift that makes human beings unique among earthly creatures, endowing them with reason, moral awareness, and the capacity to know and worship Allah. Only after this dual creation, physical and spiritual, does the command to prostrate come. The prostration is not worship of the human being. It is prostration to Allah, with the human being as the direction or the occasion of the prostration. It is an act of honoring the new creation and acknowledging the divine wisdom that brought it into existence. The prostration of the angels was an act of obedience to Allah that also demonstrated their acceptance of the human being's honored status.

This command became the dividing line. The angels, created from light and inherently obedient, prostrated without hesitation. Iblis, a jinn of fire who had risen to their company through his worship, refused. The command was simple but the test was profound. Would these created beings accept Allah's choice to honor a creature of clay, or would their pride in their own origin, whether light or fire, prevent them from submitting to the divine will?

=30=

This verse reports the angels' immediate and complete obedience to the divine command. The prostration was universal among the angels, as indicated by the phrase "all together," translating ajma'un. There was no hesitation, no discussion, no questioning of the wisdom behind the command. The angels, by their very nature, do not disobey Allah. Their existence is defined by perfect submission to whatever Allah orders.

The word for prostration here is sajada, the same word used for the act of worship that human beings perform in prayer. This prostration was a physical act of lowering themselves, placing their faces on the ground in humility. For the angels, it was an act of worship directed to Allah, with Adam as the qiblah, the direction of the prostration. This moment is of immense significance in the cosmic drama. It represents the formal recognition by the highest created beings of the special status that Allah had granted to humanity. The angels, who had initially expressed concern about the potential for human corruption, now submitted to Allah's wisdom and honored the new creation without reservation. Their prostration was an acknowledgment that Allah's knowledge and plan were perfect, even if they could not fully comprehend them. The fact that the prostration was collective is also noteworthy. The angels acted as a unified community in their obedience. There was no dissent among them, no faction that held back, no one who suggested that a creature of clay was unworthy of such honor. This unity in obedience stands in sharp contrast to what happens in the next verse with Iblis, whose individual rebellion breaks the harmony of the heavenly assembly.

This verse also serves as a model for human believers. The angels are presented as exemplars of immediate, unquestioning obedience to Allah's commands. When a clear divine instruction comes, the proper response is to say "we hear and we obey," not to delay, negotiate, or rationalize a way out. The angels' prostration was a perfect act of worship precisely because it was done exactly as commanded, when commanded, without adding or subtracting anything from the divine order.

For the believer reading this verse, the lesson is clear: true submission means doing what Allah asks, even when the wisdom behind it is not immediately apparent.

=31=

The harmony of the angelic prostration is shattered by a single exception: Iblis. He refused to join the others in their act of obedience.

The Arabic verb aba carries a strong sense of willful refusal, of stubborn rejection. This was not an oversight, a delay, or a misunderstanding. It was a deliberate, conscious choice to disobey a direct divine command. The verse identifies the refuser by name: Iblis. His name is derived from the root meaning to despair or to be confounded, and it is fitting because his rebellion led him to despair of Allah's mercy and to become a source of despair for others. The verse specifies that he refused to prostrate "with the others," emphasizing that his disobedience was not merely a private act but a public separation from the community of the obedient. While the angels acted collectively, Iblis stood apart. This introduces the Quranic theme that evil often begins with an individual choice to separate from the community of faith, to assert one's own judgment over the collective obedience to Allah.

The question naturally arises: was Iblis an angel? The Quran answers this explicitly elsewhere. Iblis was from the jinn, as stated in Surah Al-Kahf. He had, through his extensive worship and devotion, been elevated to the company of the angels, but his essential nature as a jinn, a being of fire with free will, remained. When the command to prostrate came, the flaw in his character was exposed. His long history of worship had not purified his heart from pride, and the test brought that hidden pride to the surface.

This is a sobering lesson for every believer. Outward devotion, even of the highest degree and longest duration, does not guarantee a sound heart. A person can spend years in worship, prayer, and spiritual discipline, yet harbor within themselves a seed of arrogance that will reveal itself when a particular test comes. Iblis had knowledge, he had experience, he had a history of obedience, but he lacked humility. When Allah honored a creature of clay over him, his pride would not allow him to accept it. The verse marks the moment when the greatest enemy of humanity was created. From this single act of refusal, an entire history of enmity, temptation, and spiritual warfare would unfold. The surah will now explore the dialogue between Allah and Iblis that followed this refusal, revealing the arguments of the arrogant and the justice of the divine response.

=32=

This verse records the divine interrogation of Iblis following his refusal. Allah's question is not a request for information that He does not already possess. Allah's knowledge encompasses all things, including the hidden thoughts and intentions of every creature.

The question serves multiple other purposes. It gives Iblis the opportunity to articulate his reason, thereby exposing the true nature of his disobedience and making it a matter of public record in the heavenly assembly. It also demonstrates divine justice. Allah does not punish without first giving the offender the chance to explain himself, even though the explanation, when given, only confirms the guilt.

The question is phrased with a tone of rebuke. "What is the matter with you" translates ma laka, an expression used to express disapproval and to demand an account. Allah is calling Iblis to account for his singular act of defiance.

The phrase "that you did not join others in prostration" highlights the communal nature of the obedience that Iblis rejected. It was not that he failed to perform an isolated act. He separated himself from the congregation of the obedient. His refusal was a public dissent, a visible rebellion that set him apart from the angels who had all prostrated together. This divine questioning contains an important lesson about how Allah deals with disobedience. The door to repentance is opened even in the moment of questioning. Iblis is being asked to account for himself, and the very asking implies that a different response, an admission of wrong and a plea for forgiveness, is possible.

Yet Iblis will not take that path. His response in the next verse will not be repentance but justification. His pride will not allow him to say "I was wrong, forgive me." Instead, he will offer a reason that reveals his contempt for the human creation.

This verse also teaches believers about the importance of being able to give an account of one's actions. Every soul will be questioned by Allah about what it did and why. The wise person lives with an awareness of that coming interrogation and ensures that their actions are such that they can be explained with honesty and humility, not with the arrogant self-justification that Iblis will now display.

=33=

Iblis's response to Allah's question is recorded here, and it reveals the core of his rebellion. He does not claim that the command was unclear. He does not claim incapacity. He does not ask for forgiveness. Instead, he offers a justification rooted in his perceived superiority over the human being. The essence of his argument is that his own origin, fire, is superior to the human origin, clay, and therefore it is not fitting for a superior being to prostrate to an inferior one.

The phrase "it is not for me" expresses a sense of entitlement and pride. Iblis is effectively setting his own standard of what is appropriate, judging the divine command against his personal assessment of relative worth. He acknowledges that Allah created the human from clay, but he fails to acknowledge that it was Allah who commanded the prostration. In his pride, he elevates his own judgment above the clear command of his Lord. The description of the human as created from "sounding clay moulded from black mud" is Iblis's scornful restatement of what Allah Himself had said. He uses the exact words to emphasize the lowliness of the material. His tone is one of contempt. "How can I, a being of pure, smokeless fire, bow down to this creature of dark, stinking mud?

" This is the logic of racism, of caste, of every form of prejudice that judges worth by origin rather than by piety. Iblis looked at the outer form and the material composition, and he missed the inner reality. He saw clay, but he did not see the divine spirit that had been breathed into that clay. He saw a body of mud, but he did not see the vicegerent of Allah, the being taught the names of all things, the creature honored above even the angels. This verse exposes the root of all satanic temptation: the refusal to accept Allah's wisdom and the insistence on one's own flawed standards of value. Iblis's reasoning sounds logical to the arrogant ear. Fire is indeed lighter, subtler, and more powerful than clay in certain respects. But the error lies in assuming that material origin determines spiritual worth, and that the creature has the right to question the Creator's assignments of honor. This error has been replicated throughout human history whenever individuals or groups have considered themselves inherently superior to others based on race, lineage, wealth, or any other worldly criterion.

=34=

Iblis's arrogant justification is met with an immediate and decisive divine judgment. Allah commands him to leave Paradise, the abode of honor and nearness from which he had previously benefited. The command "get out" translates ukhruj, an imperative of expulsion. The honor of being in the company of the angels in the heavenly realm is revoked. Iblis is demoted, cast out from the station he had attained through his long worship. This expulsion is a direct consequence of his pride. He considered himself too elevated to bow to a creature of clay, and now he is being made lower than that creature. The one who refused to lower himself in obedience is now lowered in status, removed from the sacred presence. The reason for the expulsion is then stated clearly: "for you are truly cursed.

" The word "cursed" translates rajim, which means expelled from divine mercy, driven away, pelted. It is the same word used in verse seventeen to describe the devils from whom the sky is protected. Iblis, who once enjoyed a station of honor, has now become one of the accursed, an object of divine rejection. This transformation from honored worshipper to accursed outcast is one of the most dramatic falls in the history of creation. It demonstrates that past righteousness, no matter how extensive, provides no guarantee of future standing. A single act of arrogant disobedience, unrepented and persisted in, can undo a lifetime of apparent devotion. The test that Iblis faced was not of intellectual understanding but of humble submission. He knew the command was from Allah. He knew prostration was an act of worship due to Allah. But his pride in his own essence prevented him from accepting that Allah could honor a creature of clay above him. This verse is a warning to every believer. The moment we begin to think that our worship, our knowledge, our lineage, or our achievements make us inherently superior to others, we are walking in the footsteps of Iblis. The only proper response to divine command is "we hear and we obey," without adding our own assessment of what is fitting or appropriate. The cursed status of Iblis is not an arbitrary punishment but the natural consequence of separating oneself from divine mercy through arrogance.

=35=

The judgment upon Iblis is extended and clarified in this verse. The curse is not temporary or probationary. It is a fixed decree that will remain upon him "until the Day of Judgment," meaning the Day of Resurrection, the final reckoning.

The word translated as "condemnation" is la'nah, which carries the meaning of being expelled from Allah's mercy, being deprived of all good, and being the object of divine wrath. This is a comprehensive state of ruin. To be deprived of Allah's mercy is to be deprived of hope, of guidance, of peace, and of any ultimate good. The specification of the time period, "until the Day of Judgment," is significant. It establishes that Iblis has a limited period during which his enmity and his tempting activities will be permitted. He is not granted an eternal reprieve. His time is fixed and will end with the judgment of all creation. This means that evil, despite its apparent power and persistence in this world, is not eternal. It has a terminal point. The Day of Judgment will mark the end of Iblis's activities and the beginning of his final punishment.

For the believer, this specification of a time limit is a source of hope. The trials and temptations of this world, no matter how intense, have an expiration date. The enemy of humanity has been given time, but that time is finite. Every day that passes brings the end of his reign closer. The struggle against satanic temptation is not an endless war but a battle with a known conclusion.

The verse also establishes a fundamental difference between the fate of Iblis and the fate of those humans who follow him. Iblis's condemnation is already decreed. Those humans who repent can still attain Allah's mercy. The door remains open for the descendants of Adam to return, but Iblis himself has locked himself into permanent rebellion. He knows his fate is sealed, and this knowledge fuels his desperate determination to drag as many human beings as possible into the same ruin.

This is the psychology of the enemy: having despaired of mercy for himself, he works tirelessly to make others share his despair. The believer's defense is to remember that Allah's mercy is vast and that sincere repentance can overcome even a lifetime of following the accursed one.

=36=

Despite the severity of the judgment just pronounced upon him, Iblis does not repent. Instead, he makes a request. He addresses Allah as "My Lord," a recognition of Allah's sovereignty that he does not deny even in his rebellion.

This is a strange and disturbing feature of satanic psychology. Iblis believes in Allah. He knows Allah's power and lordship. He does not deny the truth of divine existence or attribute partners to Allah. His sin is not atheism but arrogance. He is a theist whose pride makes him the worst of creation. The request is for respite, for his death to be delayed until the Day when human beings are resurrected. He is not asking for forgiveness or a reduction of punishment. He is asking for time to execute a project of revenge. The destruction of his own standing has filled him with malice toward the creature whose creation was the occasion of his fall. He wants to live until the Day of Judgment so that he can devote himself to leading human beings astray. This request reveals the depth of his enmity. He does not see the human being as a fellow creature to be pitied or guided. He sees humanity as the cause of his humiliation, and he seeks to prove, in his twisted reasoning, that these creatures of clay are not worthy of the honor they received. He wants to demonstrate, through the widespread disobedience of humans, that Allah's choice to honor them was misplaced.

This is the project that the Quran calls the work of Satan: beautifying evil, whispering temptations, and leading humans step by step toward the same ruin that befell him. Allah's response, which will follow, grants the request but with clear limitations. The granting of this respite is part of the divine wisdom. Human beings are placed on earth to be tested. The presence of a dedicated enemy who works tirelessly to mislead them is part of that test. Will they remember their Lord and resist? Will they follow the guidance of the prophets? Or will they succumb to the whispers of the one who has declared open war on their species? The request also shows that Iblis acknowledges the reality of resurrection. He does not ask to be spared from death entirely but to be given reprieve until the day of resurrection. This confirms that even the chief of the devils knows that the Day of Judgment is real, even as he works to make human beings doubt it.

=37=

Allah's response to Iblis's request is brief but definitive. The request is granted. Iblis will be among those who are given respite, whose death is delayed until the appointed time.

The word "delayed" translates from the same root as the word used in Iblis's request, indicating that Allah is granting exactly what was asked: a postponement of his end. However, the simple affirmative "you will be delayed" carries within it layers of meaning that become clear in the following verse. The granting of this request is not an indication of divine approval or a sign that Iblis has any power to negotiate with his Creator. It is rather an element of the divine plan that was already decreed. The test of humanity required the presence of an adversary. The honor that Adam received required a context in which that honor would be challenged and in which human beings would have to choose between following the guidance of Allah and following the temptations of the enemy. Iblis's request was already known to Allah, and the granting of it was already part of the cosmic script. This teaches an important lesson about the relationship between divine decree and creaturely requests. Sometimes a creature asks for something, and Allah grants it, but the granting is not because the creature has any claim or power but because the request aligns with what Allah had already wisely decreed. The permissive will of Allah allows many things that He does not love or command, including the continued existence and activity of Satan.

This is part of the mystery of divine wisdom. Evil exists in the world not because Allah is unable to eliminate it but because its temporary existence serves a purpose in the larger scheme of testing and spiritual growth. The believer who struggles against temptation and overcomes it reaches a spiritual station that could not be attained without the struggle. The victim of satanic whispers who repents and returns to Allah experiences the sweetness of divine mercy in a way that the one who never sinned cannot know.

This is not to glorify evil but to recognize that Allah's wisdom encompasses even the existence of His enemy. The respite granted to Iblis is ultimately a component of the mercy shown to believing humans, for it is through resisting him that they earn their place in Paradise. The verse, in its stark simplicity, marks the moment when the cosmic battle between Iblis and humanity is formally set in motion.

=38=

The respite granted to Iblis is qualified with a precise time limit: "until the appointed Day." The word "appointed" translates ma'lum, meaning known, specified, determined. The Day referred to here is the Day of Judgment, the Day when all accounts will be settled and the current order of testing will come to an end. This qualification is crucial because it establishes that Iblis's freedom to operate has a definite expiration. He is not immortal. He is not an eternal principle of evil existing in perpetual opposition to a principle of good. He is a created being with a limited span of activity, after which he will face the full consequences of his rebellion. The "appointed Day" is known to Allah but hidden from all creatures, including Iblis. He knows that his time is limited, but he does not know exactly when the limit will be reached. This uncertainty is part of his punishment and also part of what drives his frantic efforts to lead humanity astray. He is working against a deadline he cannot see.

The concept of the appointed Day ties this verse back to the earlier verses of the surah that spoke of every community having a destined term. Just as nations have their appointed times, so too does the great enemy of humanity have his appointed end. The precision of divine timing governs all things. Nothing in creation, not even the most rebellious creature, operates outside the boundaries of Allah's predetermined schedule. This knowledge is a source of comfort for believers. The enemy seems powerful, his whispers seem persistent, and his influence appears widespread, but his time is running out. Every day that passes brings closer the moment when his power will be stripped away forever. The believer is engaged in a struggle with an opponent who has already been given his sentence and whose defeat is certain. The only question is whether individual human beings will be among those who overcome his temptations or among those who follow him to his ultimate destination. The appointed Day thus functions as both a warning and an encouragement. For the heedless, it is a warning that the pleasures of sin are temporary and the accounting is certain. For the struggling believer, it is an encouragement that the battle has a fixed endpoint and that perseverance will be rewarded when the Day finally arrives.

=39=

Having received the respite he requested, Iblis now reveals his intentions with stark clarity. He swears an oath of enmity against humanity, declaring the program he will pursue for the remainder of his time.

The verse begins with Iblis addressing Allah again as "My Lord," the same recognition of sovereignty that makes his rebellion all the more perverse. He acknowledges that Allah is his Lord even as he declares war on Allah's honored creation.

The phrase "For allowing me to stray" is significant and requires careful understanding. Iblis attributes his misguidance to Allah, saying essentially that because Allah decreed or permitted him to go astray, he will respond by leading others astray.

This is an attempt to shift blame and to justify his actions by pointing to divine decree. It is the argument of every sinner who says "it was my destiny" as an excuse for their choices. The Quran does not endorse this reasoning. Allah's decree encompasses all things, including Iblis's misguidance, but Iblis chose his path through his own free will and his own arrogance. The divine decree does not negate personal responsibility. Iblis's statement is recorded to expose the psychology of the unrepentant sinner, who blames Allah for his own evil choices. His declared program has two components. First, "I will surely tempt them." The verb used means to beautify, to make attractive, to adorn falsehood so that it appears desirable. Iblis will not force human beings to sin. He will make sin look appealing. He will work on their perceptions, their desires, and their imaginations so that the path of disobedience seems more attractive than the path of obedience. Second, he will "mislead them all together." His ambition is comprehensive. He is not content with leading a few individuals astray. He wants to corrupt the entire human race, to prove that these creatures of clay are unworthy of the honor they received.

This is the voice of pure malice, of a being whose only remaining satisfaction is to see others share his ruin. The believer who reads this verse should feel a sense of alertness. The enemy has openly declared his intentions. There is no ambiguity, no hidden agenda. He wants your destruction, and he will use every means at his disposal to achieve it. This knowledge is a defense. When temptation comes, the believer can recognize it as the fulfillment of an ancient threat and respond with the shield of faith.

=40=

After declaring his comprehensive plan to mislead all of humanity, Iblis makes a crucial exception. He acknowledges that there is a category of human beings whom he will not be able to corrupt: Allah's chosen servants.

The word "chosen" translates mukhlasin, which can be read with a slightly different vocalization to mean either "chosen" or "sincere" or "purified." Both meanings are complementary. The servants whom Iblis cannot touch are those whom Allah has chosen for His mercy and who have purified their devotion to Him, making it sincere and free from the mixtures of pride and desire that Iblis exploits. This admission by Iblis is of immense significance. The enemy himself testifies that his power has limits. He is not an all-powerful force of evil that no human can resist. There are human beings, fallible creatures of clay, who are beyond his reach. This testimony should fill the believer with hope and determination. If other human beings have attained the status of being protected from Iblis, then it is possible for us as well. The path to becoming among the chosen servants is the path of sincerity, of purifying one's worship for Allah alone, of struggling against the ego and its desires. Iblis's admission also clarifies the nature of his power. He does not control human beings. He whispers, he beautifies falsehood, he presents temptations, but the final decision to sin or to obey rests with the human soul. Iblis cannot breach the fortress of a heart that has sincerely turned to Allah.

This is affirmed elsewhere in the Quran when Iblis himself says to the wrongdoers on the Day of Judgment that he had no authority over them except that he called them and they responded. They followed him by their own choice. The exception of the chosen servants is a direct challenge to Iblis's pride. He had declared that he would mislead all humans, but he is forced to admit that there is a group he cannot touch. His project of proving humanity unworthy will fail. The chosen servants will enter Paradise, and their very existence will testify for all eternity that Adam's children, despite their clay origin, were capable of the most sublime devotion and that Allah's honor of them was fully justified.
