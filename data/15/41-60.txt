=41=

After Iblis declared his intention to mislead humanity and admitted his inability to touch the chosen servants, Allah responds with a definitive statement that establishes the unchangeable reality. The verse begins with Allah saying "This is the Way," and the word translated as "Way" is sirat, the straight path, the clear course of truth and justice. It refers to the path of guidance, devotion, and protection that Allah has laid down for His sincere servants. Iblis had just drawn a line between those he could tempt and those he could not, and now Allah confirms that this distinction is indeed a fixed path, a binding principle that governs the entire human struggle.

The phrase "binding on Me" is of immense theological significance. Allah is not bound by any external force, but in His infinite wisdom and mercy He has decreed upon Himself certain things. The Quran mentions elsewhere that Allah has prescribed mercy upon Himself. Here He affirms that the protection of His sincere servants from Iblis's authority is something He has committed to, not because He must but because His perfect justice and mercy make it a certainty. The believers can take absolute comfort from this. The enemy who declared war on humanity is already constrained by a divine decree that he cannot overcome. Those who truly seek Allah, who purify their faith and follow the straight path, will find that the path itself is a fortress. The "Way" also implies a journey, a direction of travel. Those who walk this path are moving toward Allah, and as they move, they receive His protection.

The image is not of a static state but of an active striving, a conscious walking on the path that Allah has made binding upon Himself to safeguard. This verse thus answers the question that might arise in the believer's heart: can I truly be safe from the whispers of Satan?

The answer is yes, by taking the path of sincerity, by walking the straight way that Allah has guaranteed to protect, you will be among those over whom the enemy has no authority. It is a promise from the Lord of all worlds, and His promise is truth.

=42=

Allah continues the declaration that began in the previous verse, now addressing Iblis directly and explicitly limiting his power. The word translated as "authority" is sultan, which in Arabic denotes not just raw power but compelling authority, the kind of dominance that leaves the subject with no ability to resist. Allah tells Iblis that he will not have this kind of authority over "My servants." The possessive "My" is deeply significant. These servants belong to Allah, and their connection to their Lord is the very thing that shields them. It is not their own strength or intelligence that protects them but their relationship of servitude and devotion to the Creator.

The verse then makes the crucial exception clear: "except the deviant who follow you." The word for "deviant" is ghawin, which comes from a root meaning to go astray willfully, to err after knowing the truth. These are not people who were simply uninformed or who made honest mistakes. They are those who chose to deviate, who heard the call of truth and turned away, who found the whispers of Iblis more attractive than the guidance of Allah. The authority that Iblis exercises over them is not one of force but of invitation. He calls, and they respond. They follow him, and by following him they place themselves under his influence.

The phrase "follow you" indicates a voluntary alignment. No one is dragged into Iblis's camp against their will. The believer who reads this verse should understand that the protection from Satan is not a magical immunity but a spiritual reality tied to sincere devotion. As long as a person remains a true servant of Allah, actively striving to obey and avoiding what He has forbidden, the enemy's weapons are blunted. The danger comes when the servant begins to deviate, when the heart turns away from remembrance of Allah, when sins become habitual and unrepented. It is then that Iblis finds an opening and his authority begins to take hold.

The verse also clarifies a common misconception about spiritual warfare. Satan is not an equal opponent to Allah. He has no power over anyone except those who give it to him by their own choices. The ultimate control remains with the human soul and its Creator.

=43=

Having defined the two groups, the sincere servants who are protected and the deviant who follow Iblis, Allah now states the final destination of the followers. Hell is their "destined place," translating the Arabic maw'id, which means an appointed meeting place or a promised rendezvous.

The use of this word carries a heavy irony. Iblis and his followers will meet in Hell. The appointment was set long ago, and it will be kept with absolute precision. There will be no missed connections, no excuses, no alternative arrangements.

The word also echoes the "appointed Day" mentioned earlier when Iblis was granted respite. That Day is the time of the appointment, and Hell is the place.

The verse adds the phrase "all together," ajma'in, which is the same word used in verse thirty to describe the angels prostrating all together. The deliberate parallel is striking. The angels came together in unified obedience to Allah's command, and their reward was eternal nearness to Him. Iblis and his followers will come together in unified punishment. The community of obedience is contrasted with the community of damnation. This also implies that the followers of Iblis, despite their different sins and different levels of worldly status, will share a common fate. The leaders of disbelief and the ordinary people who followed them, the active sinners and the passive accepters, will all find their appointment in Hell. There is a lesson here about the danger of following the crowd. The fact that many people are doing something, that one is part of a large group, provides no safety if that group is headed toward destruction. The only community that matters in the end is the community of the faithful.

This verse also serves as a warning that the respite given to Iblis does not mean escape. He was granted time, but the end of that time is a confirmed appointment in Hell. His project of leading humanity astray will not save him from his own fate. On the contrary, every soul he misleads will add to his burden and to the severity of his punishment. The appointment is certain, and it will not be broken.

=44=

This verse provides a vivid detail about the structure of Hell, revealing that it is not a single undifferentiated abyss but a realm with seven distinct gates, each gate assigned to a specific category of its inhabitants. The number seven is significant in the Quran and in Arabic usage, often indicating completeness, multiplicity, and a full range. The seven gates suggest that Hell encompasses every type of sin and every level of punishment, with each gate serving as the entrance for a particular group defined by the nature of their wrongdoing. Islamic scholarship has discussed the names and assignments of these gates based on various narrations. Some reports mention that the gates include Jahannam for the sinful among the Muslims, Laza for the Christians, Hutama for the Jews, Sa'ir for the Sabians, Saqar for the Magians, Jahim for the polytheists, and Hawiya for the hypocrites. Other scholars have arranged the categories differently. What matters is the principle that divine justice is precise and proportionate. Punishment is not dispensed in a haphazard manner. Each group enters through the gate that corresponds to its specific type of deviation, and the torment they experience is calibrated to what they earned.

The word "designated" translates maqsum, meaning divided, apportioned, assigned. The division is not random but based on a divine decree that takes into account the exact nature of each person's disbelief and sins. This precision demonstrates that Allah's knowledge encompasses every detail of human behavior and that His judgment is perfectly fair. The existence of multiple gates also serves as a rhetorical device to emphasize the vast capacity of Hell. It is not a small prison that might become overcrowded. It has seven great gates, each capable of receiving a massive influx of condemned souls. This was a warning to the Makkan disbelievers who dismissed the Prophet's warnings as fanciful. Hell is real, it is vast, it is organized, and it has a designated entrance for every group that chose to follow the path of Iblis rather than the path of the prophets.

The verse also calls to mind the contrast with Paradise, which according to other Quranic verses has eight gates. The gates of Paradise are for the righteous, and the gates of Hell are for the followers of Iblis. The choice of which gate one will eventually enter is being made in this life, with every deed and every decision.

=45=

After the detailed description of Hell's gates and the fate of the deviant, the Quran immediately shifts the focus to the reward of the righteous, maintaining the balance between warning and glad tidings that characterizes the entire surah. The word "righteous" translates muttaqin, those who have taqwa, who are conscious of Allah, who guard themselves against sin, who live with an awareness of divine accountability. These are the same as the "chosen servants" whom Iblis admitted he could not mislead. Their destination is described as "Gardens and springs. " The word for Gardens is jannat, which evokes the image of lush, verdant orchards, places of shade, fruit, and beauty that were the ideal of comfort in the Arabian environment. The springs, 'uyun, signify flowing water, a constant, effortless supply of the most precious resource in a desert land. The combination of gardens and springs speaks of perfect provision, a place where every need is met and every sense is delighted.

The verse emphasizes their location by saying they are "amid" these blessings, meaning they are surrounded by them, immersed in them, not merely observing from a distance. This is their permanent environment. The contrast with the preceding verses is stark and intentional. The followers of Iblis enter through the seven gates of Hell into a realm of torment, while the righteous enter Gardens with flowing springs. The two destinations could not be more different, and yet the path that leads to each begins in the same world, with choices made in the same human heart. The verse serves as both comfort and motivation. For the believers in Makkah who faced persecution and hardship, the promise of Gardens and springs was a reminder that their current difficulties were temporary and that an eternal reward awaited them. For anyone hesitating between the path of Iblis and the path of righteousness, the imagery of the verse paints a picture of what is at stake. The choice is between the flames and the gardens, between the cursed company of Iblis and the blessed company of the righteous. Every soul is making that choice moment by moment, and the destination is being built from the sum of those choices.

=46=

This verse describes the moment of entry into Paradise, capturing the greeting that the righteous will receive as they cross its threshold. The phrase "It will be said to them" indicates that this is an announcement, a welcome delivered by the angels who guard the gates of Paradise. The words "Enter in peace and security" are rich with meaning.

The word "peace" is salam, the greeting of Islam, the word that signifies not merely the absence of conflict but a state of wholeness, well-being, and harmony. The righteous are being invited into a realm where everything is as it should be, where no discord exists, and where their relationship with their Creator, with themselves, and with others is perfectly ordered.

The word "security" is aminin, from the same root as iman, faith. It means safety, freedom from fear, the complete absence of any threat or danger. In the earthly life, even the most powerful and wealthy individuals live with some level of anxiety about the future, about their health, about their possessions, about their enemies. In Paradise, that anxiety vanishes forever. The righteous will know with absolute certainty that their bliss is permanent, that no harm can reach them, and that their Lord is pleased with them. This greeting of peace and security is the fulfillment of what the believers experienced in a limited way in this world when they submitted to Allah and found inner peace, but now it is complete and unending.

The verse also has a legal dimension. The command "Enter" is a formal admission. The gates of Paradise are opened to them, and they are authorized to take possession of their eternal home. It is the culmination of a life of faith, the moment when hope becomes reality and the promises of Allah are fulfilled in the most direct and personal way imaginable. For the listener who is still in the world of testing, this verse is an invitation to imagine that moment and to let that imagination fuel the determination to be among those who hear these blessed words.

=47=

This verse describes one of the most profound blessings of Paradise: the purification of the heart from all negative feelings. The word translated as "bitterness" is ghill, which refers to deep-seated resentment, malice, envy, grudges, and ill-will. In this world, even the most righteous people sometimes struggle with these feelings. Relationships between believers can be strained by misunderstandings, by competition, by perceived slights. The hearts of the best among us are not entirely free from the whisperings of ego and the residues of conflict. But in Paradise, Allah will remove all of this. The hearts of the righteous will be cleansed so thoroughly that not a trace of bitterness remains. They will meet one another with pure love, with genuine joy at each other's company, with no memory of any past disagreement that might mar their fellowship. This purification is an essential preparation for the social life of Paradise.

The verse goes on to describe that condition: "In a friendly manner, they will be on thrones, facing one another." The word translated as "in a friendly manner" is ikhwanan, which literally means as brothers. The relationship between the inhabitants of Paradise is one of brotherhood, a bond of sincere affection and mutual regard that surpasses even the closest friendships of this world. They are seated on thrones, indicating honor and comfort, and they are facing one another, indicating intimate and open social interaction. No one is sitting in a corner alone. Paradise is a place of joyful community.

The image of facing one another also suggests that there is no hierarchy that separates them, no king looking down on subjects, but mutual respect and enjoyment. This verse directly answers the psychology of Iblis. His downfall was caused by the bitterness in his heart, the resentment he felt toward Adam. The righteous are saved from that same poison. Their hearts are made pure by Allah's grace, and they inherit what Iblis lost: eternal fellowship in a state of peace and love.

=48=

This verse adds two further assurances about the nature of life in Paradise. First, "no fatigue will touch them there.

" The word for fatigue is nasab, which refers to weariness, exhaustion, the draining of energy that accompanies labor and effort in this world. Human life on earth is a constant cycle of exertion and rest. Even the most enjoyable activities eventually tire us. In Paradise, this law is abolished. The blessings are experienced without any depletion of energy. The joy is constant, the delight is unflagging, and the body and soul do not experience the diminishing returns that characterize worldly pleasures. There is no need for sleep to recover, no sense of having had enough, no creeping boredom.

This is part of the perfection of the next life. The second assurance is equally profound: "nor will they ever be asked to leave.

" The Arabic word mukhrajin means expelled, evicted, forced out. This statement carries a deep resonance with the story of Adam. Adam and Eve were placed in the Garden and then, after their slip, were told to descend to the earth. Their stay in Paradise was conditional, and the condition was broken. For the righteous in the eternal Paradise, there is no such condition and no such expulsion. Their residence is permanent, irrevocable, and eternal. The fear of losing what one loves is one of the deepest anxieties of the human heart. In Paradise, that fear is gone. The righteous possess their joy forever. This verse thus closes the description of Paradise with the ultimate security. The peace they entered, the purified hearts they enjoy, the brotherhood they share, all of this is not a temporary state but an everlasting reality. The believer who reads this verse should feel a surge of hope and a renewed commitment to the path that leads to such an abode. The hardships of this world, the fatigue of worship, the pain of resisting temptation, are temporary. The rest, the eternal home, is coming.

=49=

The surah now pivots from the extended narrative of Iblis and the descriptions of Hell and Paradise to a direct instruction to the Prophet, and through him to all who convey the message. Allah tells him to inform His servants of two fundamental truths.

The first is that Allah is truly the All-Forgiving, the Most Merciful. The name Al-Ghafur, the All-Forgiving, means that Allah covers sins, wipes them away, and does not hold them against the one who repents. No sin is too great for His forgiveness if the servant turns back sincerely.

The name Ar-Rahim, the Most Merciful, means that His mercy is vast, continuous, and encompasses all things. It is the mercy that manifests as blessings, guidance, and ultimately the admission into Paradise. These two names together convey a message of immense hope. The Prophet is instructed to proclaim this to the servants of Allah, not to hide it, not to make the religion appear as a burden of impossible demands without a path of redemption.

The emphasis "truly" translates inna, indicating that this is an established truth, not a speculation or a mere hope. The believers can rely on Allah's forgiveness and mercy with full confidence.

This verse also subtly addresses the psychology of Iblis. Iblis despaired of Allah's mercy, and his despair led to his eternal ruin. The believers are called to the opposite response: to recognize the vastness of divine mercy and to run toward it rather than away from it. The verse is a remedy for the despair that Satan tries to instill in the hearts of sinners. No matter what one has done, the door of forgiveness is open. The condition is sincere repentance, a turning back to Allah with a heart that regrets the past and resolves to amend the future.

The verse also balances the severe warnings that came before. The seven gates of Hell are real, but so is the mercy that can save a person from entering any of them. The message of Islam is not a message of doom alone but a message of hope grounded in the reality of divine mercy. The Prophet is told to inform, to announce, to make known this joyful truth.

=50=

The instruction to the Prophet continues with the second part of the message he must convey. Alongside the announcement of Allah's forgiveness and mercy, he must also inform them that Allah's torment is the most painful. The two truths must be communicated together. A message that only speaks of mercy can lead to complacency and a disregard for divine commands. A message that only speaks of punishment can lead to despair and a sense that striving is futile. The Quranic method is to hold both in balance, presenting the hope of mercy and the warning of punishment side by side so that the believer lives between fear and hope.

The word for "torment" is 'adhab, which implies a pain that is not just physical but also psychological and spiritual. The word for "painful" is alim, an adjective of intensity. The verse declares that this painful torment is indeed from Allah, and it is a reality that must not be ignored. The conjunction "and that" ties this verse directly to the previous one. The same Prophet who tells the people "My Lord is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful" must also tell them "and My Lord's torment is the most painful.

" This is not a contradiction but a complete picture of divine justice. Allah's mercy does not negate His justice, and His justice does not overwhelm His mercy. Both attributes are perfect and operate in full harmony. The sinner who repents finds a Lord who is ready to forgive. The sinner who persists and dies without repentance finds a Lord whose punishment is severe. Neither outcome contradicts the divine nature.

The verse also serves as a warning to those who might misinterpret the previous verse as a license to sin. The fact that Allah is forgiving does not mean that one can sin carelessly with the intention of repenting later. The pain of His torment is described as the most painful, and no intelligent person would risk such an outcome by delaying repentance. The balance of these two verses is the essence of prophetic preaching. The prophets brought glad tidings and warnings, and this surah exemplifies that methodology with precision and power.

=51=

After the general proclamation of mercy and punishment, Allah now instructs the Prophet to convey a specific story that illustrates both of these realities in action. He is told to inform the people about the guests of Abraham.

The word "guests" translates dayf, which in Arabic can refer to a single guest or a group. Here it refers to the angels who visited Abraham in human form. This story is told in several surahs, each time with different details suited to the context.

In this surah, the story will highlight the mercy shown to Abraham through the good news of a son, and the punishment that befell the people of Lot, demonstrating the two messages of verses forty-nine and fifty in a concrete historical event. The instruction to "inform them" places this story within the broader mission of the Prophet. He is not merely recounting ancient tales for entertainment. He is providing evidence from history that Allah's mercy and punishment are real, that they descend upon human beings in this world as well as the next, and that the pattern is consistent. Abraham is chosen as the central figure of this narrative because he is the father of prophets, the friend of Allah, and a model of hospitality, faith, and concern for others. The Makkan disbelievers claimed descent from Abraham through Ishmael and honored him as their forefather. By telling a story in which Abraham receives angels, believes in their message, and shows concern for the fate of a wicked people, the Quran challenges the Makkans to examine whether their own response to divine messengers matches the response of their revered ancestor. Abraham recognized the messengers and treated them with honor. The Makkans mocked their messenger and called him insane. The contrast is deliberate and powerful. The verse sets the stage for a narrative that will reinforce every major theme of the surah so far: the rejection of messengers, the certainty of divine punishment, the protection of the righteous, and the operation of Allah's mercy and justice in human history.

=52=

The narrative begins with the angels arriving at Abraham's dwelling and greeting him with the word "Peace," the same greeting that the righteous will receive upon entering Paradise as mentioned earlier in the surah. This greeting of salam is the mark of the believers and of the angels, a sign of goodwill and divine blessing. Abraham responded to their greeting, but his response is not immediately recorded. Instead, the verse moves to a later moment when Abraham, after observing something unusual in his guests' behavior, expresses fear.

The phrase "He later said" indicates a shift in the situation. According to the detailed accounts in other surahs, Abraham quickly prepared a roasted calf for his guests, as was the custom of generous hospitality. But when he placed the food before them, he noticed that their hands did not reach toward it. They did not eat. In the ancient Near Eastern culture, refusing to eat the food of one's host was a sign of hostile intent. A guest who would not share a meal was suspected of coming with harmful purposes. Abraham, who had initially assumed they were human travelers, now became alarmed. He said, "Surely we are afraid of you," expressing his sudden fear that these strangers might intend harm to him and his household.

The use of "we" indicates that the fear was shared, likely by his wife Sarah as well. This verse captures a very human moment. Abraham, the great prophet and friend of Allah, experiences the natural emotion of fear when faced with an uncertain and potentially dangerous situation. This does not diminish his status but makes him relatable. Even the most faithful servants of Allah can feel fear, and that fear is not a sign of weak faith but of normal human prudence.

The verse also builds suspense in the narrative. The reader or listener wonders: who are these guests, why do they not eat, and what will happen next? The angels' response in the following verses will both calm Abraham's fear and reveal the dual purpose of their mission.

=53=

Seeing Abraham's fear, the angels immediately moved to calm him. Their first words were "Do not be afraid," a reassurance that has been given by angels to prophets throughout history. When Gabriel appeared to Mary, he told her not to fear. When the angels visited Lot, they told him not to fear. The presence of angels can be overwhelming, and the circumstances of their visits are often extraordinary, so reassurance is the necessary prelude to communication. After calming his fear, the angels delivered their first piece of news: the glad tidings of a knowledgeable son.

The word for "good news" is nubashshiru, which means we bring you tidings that should make you rejoice, news that will bring joy to your heart. The news was that Abraham would have a son, despite his old age and his wife's lifelong barrenness. This was the fulfillment of a deep and long-held longing. Abraham had prayed for a righteous child, and now the answer to that prayer had arrived. The son is described as "knowledgeable," ghulamun 'alim. A ghulam is a boy who has reached the age of youth and vigor, indicating that the son would live to maturity.

The word 'alim means knowledgeable, wise, possessing understanding. This points to the future prophethood of Isaac, who would be granted wisdom and knowledge by Allah and would carry the line of prophethood forward. The tidings were not just of a child but of a child with a special destiny. This verse illustrates the first part of the angelic mission: the delivery of mercy and blessing to a beloved prophet. It demonstrates that Allah's mercy is personal and specific. It addresses the deepest hopes of the individual believer. Abraham's hospitality and righteousness are rewarded with news that changes his life and the course of history. The knowledgeable son would become a prophet, an ancestor of many prophets, and a link in the chain that ultimately leads to the final Prophet, Muhammad, peace be upon them all.

=54=

Abraham's response to the glad tidings is recorded in this verse. He expresses wonder and astonishment, not disbelief.

The phrase "Do you give me good news despite my old age" reveals the source of his amazement. He is fully aware of the natural impossibility of the situation. He is old, well past the age of fathering children. His wife Sarah is also old and has been barren throughout her life. From every human perspective, the birth of a son is impossible. Abraham acknowledges this reality openly. His question is not a challenge to the angels' truthfulness but an expression of joyful surprise at the unexpected working of divine power. He is essentially saying: how can this be, when all the normal means have ceased? His wonder is the wonder of a person who knows the laws of nature and suddenly sees them suspended by the command of the One who established those laws.

The phrase "What unlikely news" translates the Arabic fabima tubashshirun, which carries a tone of amazed inquiry. He is not rejecting the news but asking about its basis. His mind is grappling with the gap between what is naturally possible and what divine power can accomplish.

This is a healthy spiritual response. The believer acknowledges the reality of natural causes but also knows that Allah is not limited by them.

The verse also highlights Abraham's humility. He does not say "I am a great prophet, of course I deserve such a miracle." He focuses on his own human limitations, his old age, his frailty. This humility is part of what made Abraham the friend of Allah. He did not let his high spiritual station make him forget that he was a mortal human being dependent entirely on divine grace.

The verse teaches that it is permissible to express wonder at Allah's blessings and to acknowledge the gap between human capacity and divine power. Such wonder is a form of praise, recognizing that what is impossible for creation is easy for the Creator.

=55=

The angels respond to Abraham's wonder with a firm affirmation. They tell him that they bring the good news "in all truth," bil-haqq, meaning with absolute certainty, grounded in reality, and issued from the decree of Allah.

This is not a guess, a hope, or a metaphor. It is a fact that will come to pass as surely as they are standing before him. The reassurance is necessary because the news is so extraordinary that a human heart might waver between hope and doubt. By anchoring the promise in truth, the angels eliminate the space for doubt. Then they add a gentle caution: "so do not be one of those who despair.

" The word for "despair" is qanitin, which means those who have lost hope, who have given up on the possibility of good. The angels are telling Abraham that his expression of wonder could, if carried too far, border on despair of Allah's mercy. But they quickly clarify that they are not accusing him of despair. They are warning him away from it. This nuance is important. Even a prophet is reminded to guard against the subtle entry of hopelessness into the heart. If a man of Abraham's stature needs such a reminder, then ordinary believers should be all the more vigilant. This verse connects directly to the earlier declaration that Allah is the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful, and that His mercy must never be doubted. The angels are embodying that message. They bring a miracle to an old man and tell him not to lose hope.

The verse teaches that despair of Allah's mercy is a dangerous state. It is, as the next verse will explicitly state, the characteristic of the misguided. The believer must always maintain hope, even when the situation appears impossible. The power of Allah is not constrained by human calculations. The child that was promised to Abraham would indeed be born, and from that child would come a great nation. This was the truth that the angels delivered, and Abraham's heart was being prepared to receive it fully.

=56=

Abraham's response to the angels' gentle admonition is swift and emphatic. He declares that no one would ever despair of the mercy of their Lord except those who are misguided. In saying this, Abraham clarifies that his earlier expression of wonder was not despair but simple human amazement at the extraordinary power of Allah. He distances himself completely from any suggestion of hopelessness.

The word "misguided" translates dallun, which means those who are lost, who have strayed from the right path, who are in a state of error. To despair of Allah's mercy is, in Abraham's understanding, a hallmark of being spiritually lost. The person who truly knows Allah knows that His mercy is vast, that His power is unlimited, and that He can create whatever He wills regardless of natural impossibilities. Despair is therefore not a minor emotional state but a sign of a deeper problem in one's knowledge of Allah. This verse has resonated throughout Islamic spirituality. It is cited as a proof that a believer must never lose hope, no matter how great the sin or how difficult the circumstance. If an elderly, childless couple can be given a son, then any problem can be solved, any sin can be forgiven, and any prayer can be answered in the way Allah sees best. The statement also contains a subtle warning. By saying "who would despair," Abraham implies that no reasonable person who understands Allah's attributes would ever fall into despair.

Therefore, if one finds oneself despairing, one should recognize that as a symptom of a misunderstanding that needs to be corrected. The cure is to learn about Allah's names and attributes, to reflect on His mercy, and to remember the stories of those who received His blessings after long waiting and against all odds.

This verse also ties back to Iblis's despair. Iblis despaired of mercy and became the archetype of the misguided. Abraham, by contrast, is the archetype of the hopeful believer who trusts his Lord completely.

=57=

Having received the glad tidings and affirmed his unwavering hope in Allah's mercy, Abraham now turns his attention to the broader purpose of the angelic visit. He addresses them as "messenger-angels," recognizing their true nature. By this point, the combination of their supernatural greeting, their refusal to eat, and the extraordinary news they delivered has made it clear that these are no ordinary travelers. They are messengers sent by Allah, and messengers come with specific tasks. Abraham's question reflects his spiritual insight and his concern for the affairs of Allah's creation. He understands that the angels who brought him good news are also carrying out a larger mission, and he wants to know what it is.

This is characteristic of Abraham, who is portrayed throughout the Quran as someone deeply concerned with justice and the fate of others. He argued with the angels about the people of Lot, seeking to avert punishment if possible. His question here is the prelude to that intercession.

The phrase "O messenger-angels" translates ayyuha al-mursalun, which is a respectful and formal address. Abraham treats the angels with the honor due to their station as envoys of the Almighty.

This verse also serves a narrative function. It moves the story forward from the private good news given to Abraham to the public mission of punishment that the angels were primarily sent to execute. The good news of a son was a secondary purpose, a mercy granted along the way. The primary mission was the destruction of the people of Lot. By having Abraham ask the question, the Quran draws the listener's attention to this shift in focus. The angels are about to reveal the terrible purpose of their journey, and Abraham's question sets the stage for that revelation.

For the believer, there is a lesson in Abraham's attentiveness. He did not become so absorbed in his own joy that he forgot to ask about the larger purposes of Allah. Personal blessings should not make one indifferent to the state of the world and the unfolding of divine justice.

=58=

The angels answer Abraham's question with a direct and sobering statement. They have been sent to a "wicked people.

" The word used is qawmin mujrimin, a people characterized by crime and wrongdoing. The same word mujrimin was used earlier in the surah, in verse twelve, to describe those into whose hearts disbelief is threaded. It refers to those who have gone beyond the bounds of decency and morality, who have made sin their habit and identity, and who have rejected the warnings of the prophet sent to them. This people is the nation of Lot, who lived in the cities of Sodom and Gomorrah near the Dead Sea. The angels do not immediately name the people, but Abraham, who knows the region and knows his nephew Lot, understands exactly who is being referred to. The angels' statement is deliberately blunt. They do not offer a detailed list of the people's sins at this moment. They simply characterize them as wicked, a sufficient description given the gravity of what is about to befall them.

The word "actually" translates inna, providing emphasis. There is no ambiguity about their mission. They have been sent with a specific command from Allah, and that command is to execute punishment. This verse illustrates the second part of the message the Prophet was told to convey: that Allah's torment is indeed the most painful. The angels who brought mercy to Abraham in the form of a promised son are the same angels who will bring destruction to the people of Lot. Mercy and punishment are two sides of the same divine justice. The believers are protected and blessed, while the wicked are held accountable. Abraham, a prophet who knows both the mercy and the justice of Allah, now faces the tension between his joy at the good news and his concern for Lot and his family. The verse sets up that tension, which will unfold in the following verses as Abraham seeks to understand the scope of the coming punishment.

=59=

The angels now provide Abraham with the crucial exception to the destruction they are about to unleash. The family of Lot will be saved.

The word "deliver" translates la-munajjuhum, which means to rescue, to save, to bring safely out of danger. The emphasis in the Arabic construction is strong, using the lam of emphasis and the pronoun hum, meaning all of them. The angels want Abraham to know that his nephew Lot and those who believe with him will not be touched by the punishment.

This is a direct expression of divine mercy. Even in the midst of a terrible judgment, Allah distinguishes between the wicked and the righteous. The destruction is not indiscriminate. It targets the criminals and spares the believers. Abraham's concern for Lot is addressed before he even voices it. The angels know that Abraham's heart will immediately turn to the fate of his relative, and they provide the reassurance without being asked. This demonstrates the care with which Allah treats His prophets. Abraham is not left in suspense. He is told clearly that the righteous will be saved.

The phrase "the family of Lot" includes his believing household members, those who accepted his message and followed his guidance. According to the Quranic account in other surahs, the number of believers in Lot's community was very small. Some reports say it was only his daughters and perhaps a few others. But however small the group, they are known to Allah and they will be delivered. This verse reinforces the principle that Allah does not punish a people without saving the believers among them. The presence of a small righteous minority can delay punishment, but when the time comes, that minority is extracted before the destruction descends.

The verse also serves as a comfort to the Prophet Muhammad and the believers in Makkah. They were a small minority surrounded by a hostile majority. The story of Lot reminds them that numbers do not determine outcome. The few who believe are the ones who are saved, while the many who reject are the ones who perish.

=60=

The comprehensive deliverance promised for Lot's family has a single, painful exception: his wife. The angels state clearly that she is excluded from the rescue and will be among those who are destroyed.

The word "doomed" translates al-ghabirin, which literally means those who remain behind, those who are left in the destruction, those who do not go forward with the saved group. Lot's wife is decreed to be among them. This exception is significant for several reasons. Lot's wife was not merely a passive bystander. According to the Quranic account in Surah At-Tahrim, she betrayed her husband, not in a marital sense but in matters of faith and community. She sympathized with the wicked people of the city. She is said to have informed them when guests arrived at Lot's house, acting as a spy and an accomplice to their evil intentions. Her heart was with the criminals, not with the prophet. The fact that she was the wife of a prophet did not save her. The Quran is emphatic on this point across multiple surahs. On the Day of Judgment, no familial relationship will benefit anyone unless it is accompanied by true faith. The wife of Noah and the wife of Lot are both cited as examples of disbelievers who were married to righteous men but perished with their people.

This is a sobering warning against the assumption that one can ride to salvation on the piety of one's family. Every soul stands alone before Allah.

The phrase "We have determined" translates qaddarna, which means We have decreed, We have ordained. The fate of Lot's wife was not an accident or an oversight. It was a specific divine decree based on her own choices. She had made herself one of the criminals, and she would share their destiny. This verse completes the dual lesson of the angelic mission. Abraham receives mercy and good news, while the wicked are warned of punishment, and even a prophet's wife is not exempt if she aligns herself with evil.

The surah continues to weave its themes of personal responsibility, divine justice, and the uncompromising nature of true faith.
