=1=

The surah opens with a profound rhetorical question that sets the tone for the entire message: “Is there not a period of time when each human is nothing yet worth mentioning?” The Arabic “Hal ata ‘ala al-insani heenun min al-dahri lam yakun shay’an madhkura” draws the listener’s attention to the forgotten past. “Heenun min al-dahr” refers to an immense span of time, an age stretching back before the human being came into existence. For countless eons, the universe existed, the earth turned, the stars burned, but the individual human was not present at all. He was not even a thought, not a name, not a memory. “Lam yakun shay’an madhkura” means he was not a thing worth mentioning. He was a non-entity, utterly absent from the register of existence. This reflection is profoundly humbling. The human being who walks the earth today with so much pride, who denies the Day of Judgment, who busies himself with his own importance, was for an unimaginably long period simply nothing. No one spoke of him, no one knew him. He was not a forgotten hero; he was a pure absence. The verse invites the human to look back and realize his total dependence on Allah for his very existence. The fact that he now is “a thing mentioned”—that he has a name, a life, a consciousness—is entirely due to Allah’s creative will.

This reminder is the foundation for the argument that follows. If Allah brought him from non-existence into existence once, can He not do it again? The resurrection is easier to comprehend when one remembers the original creation.

The verse also implicitly answers those who question why they should be accountable. They are accountable because they were given existence as a gift, and that gift comes with a purpose. The “period of time” is a sign of Allah’s power over time itself.

For the believer, this verse evokes a deep sense of gratitude. The life I have now is a brief flicker after an eternity of nothingness. I must use it well before I return to the earth and face what comes next. It also cures arrogance. No matter how great one becomes, one’s origin is nothingness. The entire surah flows from this humbling beginning toward the choices that determine the eternal future.

=2=

After reminding humanity of its original non-existence, the surah now describes the biological beginning of every human being: “For indeed, We alone created humans from a drop of mixed fluids.” The Arabic “nutfatin amshajin” refers to the mingled fluids of the male and female.

This is a remarkably precise description of the moment of conception, when the sperm and the ovum unite. The word “amshaj” means mixed, combined. The human being originates from a tiny, humble drop that is a mixture of two different fluids.

This detail was not known to the science of the seventh century in the way it is now understood, and it stands as a sign of the Quran’s divine origin. The phrase “in order to test them” explains the purpose behind this creation. The human was not created for aimless play. Life is a test (ibtila’).

The Arabic word implies a trial that reveals the true nature of a person. Allah gave the human being life and then equipped him for the test: “so We made them hear and see.” Hearing and sight are two of the most precious faculties. They are the primary gateways of knowledge and communication. Without them, a person is cut off from much of the world’s information and from the guidance that comes through revelation. By mentioning these two senses, Allah is reminding the human that the ability to hear the Quran and see the signs in creation is a trust. The test is precisely this: will the person use these faculties to recognize the truth and submit, or will he use them to pursue vanities and deny his Creator? The verse directly links the physical creation to the moral responsibility. The faculties are not for entertainment; they are tools for the test. The believer understands that every sound he listens to, every sight he allows his eyes to dwell upon, is part of his examination. The surah will now explain that the test is not a blind one; Allah has provided the guidance. The hearing and seeing are meant to lead the human to the “Way” mentioned in the next verse. The drop of mixed fluids is a reminder of humble origin, and the hearing and seeing are reminders of high responsibility. The combination of lowly origin and lofty faculties points to a Creator who wants His creation to rise above their base beginnings through obedience.

=3=

After establishing that humans are created for a test and given the faculties of hearing and sight, the surah declares the fairness of the test: “We already showed them the Way.” The Arabic “inna hadaynahu al-sabeel” means We guided him, We made the path clear. This guidance is of two types. First, the universal guidance embedded in the human conscience and in the signs of the natural world. Every soul has an innate sense of right and wrong, a fitrah that recognizes the Creator. Second, the specific guidance sent through the prophets and scriptures. The path of monotheism, righteousness, and accountability has been clearly marked. No one can claim they were left in the dark. The verb “showed” indicates that the way is illuminated and visible, not hidden or obscure. Then the verse presents the binary outcome of the test: “whether they choose to be grateful or ungrateful.

” The Arabic “imma shakiran wa imma kafuran” uses two strong terms. “Shakir” is the grateful one, who acknowledges the favors of Allah—especially the guidance—and responds with worship and obedience. “Kafur” is the intensely ungrateful one, who covers up the favors, denies the truth, and rejects the guidance. The human being has free will. The path is shown, but the choice is his.

The use of “kafur” instead of “kafir” intensifies the meaning: it is not just a passive disbelief but an active, extreme ingratitude. The entire life is a response to the divine guidance. Will the person be thankful, which leads to Paradise, or will he be ungrateful, which leads to Hell? The verse echoes the opening of the surah: the one who was nothing, then created, then guided, now decides his eternal fate. It is the ultimate expression of human dignity and responsibility. The believer reads this and renews his commitment to be among the grateful, not just with the tongue but with all the faculties that Allah gave.

The surah now proceeds to describe the rewards for each group, beginning with a brief mention of the punishment for the ungrateful, followed by a lavish description of the bliss of the grateful. The stage is set for the magnificent portrait of the virtuous that fills much of this surah.

=4=

The surah briefly but firmly mentions the fate of those who chose ingratitude and disbelief: “Indeed, We have prepared for the disbelievers chains, shackles, and a blazing Fire.” The word “Indeed” emphasizes the certainty of this preparation. The punishment is not an afterthought; it is ready and waiting. The “chains” (salasil) are long, heavy iron chains used to bind the inmates of Hell. The “shackles” (aghlal) are iron collars that encircle the necks and hands, restricting all movement. The “blazing Fire” (sa‘eeran) is the furious, flaming inferno that consumes without mercy. This triad of punishments covers restraint, humiliation, and burning. The disbelievers, who walked arrogantly on earth, refusing to be bound by the laws of Allah, will find themselves physically bound and thrown into the fire. They used their limbs in rebellion, so their limbs will be chained. They used their freedom to mock the truth, so their freedom will be utterly revoked. The verse serves as a sharp contrast to the lengthy, beautiful description of the rewards of the grateful that follows.

The mention here is brief, while the rewards are detailed at length, highlighting that Allah’s mercy predominates, but His justice is also severe. The brevity of the punishment description also allows the reader to focus on the hope and motivation of Paradise, rather than dwelling excessively on the fear, though the fear is a necessary goad. The chains and shackles are a direct answer to the deniers’ claim that the Day of Judgment is a myth. They will find these instruments of punishment very real.

The word “prepared” (a‘tadna) indicates that Allah has already created these things in His justice. The verse connects to the earlier surahs like Al-Ma‘arij and Al-Muddaththir, which described the shackles and the raging Fire. It is a consistent warning throughout the Quran.

For the believer, this brief verse is enough to make the heart tremble and to renew the determination to be among the grateful, who are about to be described in exquisite detail. The contrast between the two destinies is the engine of choice.

=5=

Having given the brief warning, the surah launches into a long, beautiful description of the reward of the grateful. “Indeed, the virtuous will have a drink of pure wine—flavoured with camphor.

” The Arabic “inna al-abrara yashrabuna min ka’sin kana mizajuha kafuran” identifies the recipients as “al-abrar.” The abrar are the righteous, the pious, those who consistently did good and fulfilled their duties. They are the grateful ones who chose the right path. Their reward is described in sensory, delightful terms that appealed to the Arab imagination and still captivate the soul. They will drink from a “ka’s,” a cup or goblet filled with a pure beverage. Unlike the wine of this world, which intoxicates and causes impurity, this heavenly drink is pure, delicious, and without any negative effects. It does not cloud the mind, cause headache, or lead to foolish behavior. It is a drink of pure pleasure and purity. The drink is “flavoured with camphor,” or “kafur.” Camphor in this world is a white, fragrant substance known for its cooling and soothing properties. In Paradise, it will be something far more exquisite, whose true nature only Allah knows.

The mention of camphor evokes a sense of freshness, coolness, and a beautiful aroma. It is a drink that pleases the senses and refreshes the spirit.

The phrase “a drink” is an indefinite noun, suggesting a special, rare, and wonderful beverage. The abrar will be served this drink as a welcoming gift, perhaps right at the beginning of their entry into Paradise. The verse sets the tone for the lavish hospitality that awaits the believers. It transforms the familiar pleasures of this world into a perfected, eternal form. The believer reads this and feels motivated to be among the abrar, to earn that drink from the cup flavored with heavenly camphor.

The description continues to unfold the source of this drink and the nature of the servants who will drink it, painting a scene of sublime beauty.

=6=

The source of the heavenly drink is now revealed: “from a spring where Allah’s servants will drink, flowing at their will.” The Arabic “‘aynan yashrabu biha ‘ibad Allah” describes a natural spring, a fountain of Paradise, from which the devoted servants of Allah will drink.

The word “‘ibad Allah” (servants of Allah) is an honorific title that elevates the abrar. They are not just any servants; they are those who have truly realized their servitude to the Most Merciful. They are the ones who were grateful and obedient. The spring is theirs to enjoy. The verb “yashrabu biha” can be understood as “they drink from it” or “they cause it to flow,” as the next phrase indicates. “Yufajjirunaha tafjiran” means they make it gush forth abundantly, at their will, wherever they desire. In this world, a spring is fixed in one location, and one must go to it. In Paradise, the believers will have the authority to cause the springs to flow wherever they wish. They can direct the streams through their palaces and gardens, creating their own rivers of pure drink.

This is a symbol of the complete autonomy and honor they will enjoy. They are not passive recipients; they are active masters of their environment, all under the permission of Allah. The flowing spring also represents the never-ending nature of the blessings. The water (or wine) is not stagnant; it is perpetually renewed, fresh, and abundant.

The image of a spring evokes life, purity, and coolness. The ability to cause it to flow at will is a delight in itself, a form of pleasurable activity for the blessed. The verse deepens the picture of Paradise as a place where every desire is fulfilled with ease and grace. The believer who reads this is encouraged to strive in servitude, to be among those “‘ibad Allah” who will not only drink from the heavenly spring but will be able to summon its flow with a mere gesture. The surah will now describe the specific qualities of these servants in the world, explaining what deeds made them worthy of such honor.

=7=

The surah now begins to list the characteristics of the abrar, the virtuous ones who will enjoy these delights. “They are those who fulfil their vows and fear a Day of sweeping horror.

” The first quality is “yufuna bil-nadhr,” they fulfill their vows. A vow (nadhr) is a solemn promise to do something good for the sake of Allah, often conditional on something, but also voluntary. In pre-Islamic Arabia, people made vows to idols. The believers redirect this impulse to Allah alone, and they take their vows seriously. Fulfilling a vow is a sign of integrity and God-consciousness. It teaches that a Muslim’s word is a binding commitment. Many of the companions would make vows to fast, pray, or give charity, and they would never break them.

The verse then mentions a deeper motivation: “and fear a Day of sweeping horror.” The Arabic “yakhafuna yawman kana sharruhu mustateera” describes a Day whose evil and terror spread far and wide, enveloping everything. The Day of Judgment is a day of pervasive calamity, where no one is exempt from the initial terror. The believers, even while doing good, live with this fear in their hearts. Their fear is not a paralyzing terror but a cautious awe that drives them to prepare. They fulfill their vows and do all other righteous deeds precisely because they are afraid of that Day. This fear is one of the qualities of the faithful mentioned in Al-Ma‘arij, where the believers fear the punishment of their Lord.

The phrase “sweeping horror” emphasizes that the Day is not a localized event; it is a cosmic catastrophe that sweeps across all creation. The believers keep this Day in view, and it shapes their actions. The verse combines a positive act (fulfilling vows) with a negative emotion (fear) that serves as a motivator. It teaches that true righteousness is not merely doing good but doing it with a heart that is conscious of accountability. The surah will continue to list other deeds, each one adding a layer to the portrait of the abrar.

=8=

The next quality of the abrar is their selfless generosity. “And give food—despite their desire for it—to the poor, the orphan, and the captive.

” The Arabic “wa yut‘imuna al-ta‘ama ‘ala hubbihi” means they feed others even though they themselves love the food and are in need of it. The phrase “‘ala hubbihi” (despite their love for it) indicates that they are not giving away leftovers or things they dislike. They give from what they themselves cherish, from food that they desire to eat.

This is the highest level of charity: to give when you are in want, not just from surplus. This echoes the Quranic standard in Surah Al-‘Imran, “You will not attain righteousness until you spend from that which you love.” The recipients are specifically named: the poor (miskeen), who have nothing; the orphan (yateem), who has lost his father and support; and the captive (aseer), who is a prisoner of war or anyone in bondage. The orphan and the poor are obvious categories of vulnerability.

The mention of the captive is striking. In the early Meccan context, there were no Muslim prisoners of war; captives were typically polytheists captured by other tribes, or slaves.

Yet the Quran commands kindness to them. This shows that Islamic charity transcends religious and tribal boundaries. The captive is fed simply because he is a human being in need. Later, after Badr, the Muslims treated their captives with remarkable kindness, sharing their own food with them. This verse is often associated with a famous incident in the household of the Prophet. According to many commentators, including Ibn Abbas, the verses were revealed concerning Ali ibn Abi Talib and Fatimah, may Allah be pleased with them. They had vowed to fast for three days if their sick sons Hasan and Husayn recovered. The children recovered, and the family fasted. On the first evening, a poor man came to their door asking for food; they gave him their entire meal and broke their fast with water. On the second evening, an orphan came, and they did the same. On the third evening, a captive came, and again they gave away their food. The Prophet saw their weakened state, and Jibril descended with this surah, praising their selfless act. Whether this specific occasion is the sole cause or a prime example, the lesson is timeless: the abrar are those who give food to the needy even when they themselves are hungry. Their generosity is rooted in a fear of something greater than hunger, as the next verse explains.

=9=

The surah reveals the pure, inner intention of the abrar when they perform their acts of charity. They say within their hearts, or perhaps they express this sentiment, “We feed you only for the sake of Allah, seeking neither reward nor thanks from you.

” The Arabic “innama nut‘imukum li-wajh Allah” means our feeding is exclusively for the Countenance of Allah, for His pleasure alone. This is the secret of their generosity. They do not expect the poor, the orphan, or the captive to repay them in any way. They do not even want a thank you.

The phrase “seeking neither reward nor thanks” uses “jaza’an” (recompense) and “shukuran” (gratitude). They are not doing a favor to be returned later. They are not looking for the recipient to sing their praises. Their eyes are fixed on Allah alone. This purity of intention is what transforms a simple act of feeding into a ticket to Paradise. It is a severe warning against the sin of “mann,” reminding others of one’s charity, or doing deeds for show. The abrar have so purified their souls that they do not even let the thought of praise enter their minds. They see the needy person as a gift from Allah, a means to attain His pleasure.

The phrase “for the sake of Allah” (li-wajh Allah) is a profound expression. It means they seek Allah’s Noble Face, His attention and approval.

This verse teaches the believer to constantly examine his motives. Even the greatest acts of charity lose their value if they are contaminated by the desire for recognition. The abrar are invisible heroes; their left hand does not know what their right hand has given.

The verse also demonstrates the dignity of the poor in Islam. The giver does not feel superior; he feels grateful for the opportunity to serve.

This is the ultimate liberation of the soul from the shackles of ego. The surah continues to show how this pure intention is coupled with a deep fear of the Hereafter.

=10=

The abrar complete the expression of their inner state by revealing the motivation behind their selfless charity: “We fear from our Lord a horribly distressful Day.” The Arabic “inna nakhafu min rabbina yawman ‘abusan qamtareeran” uses two powerful adjectives for the Day. “‘Abus” means frowning, scowling, grim. It describes a day so severe that it is as if the Day itself has a fierce, terrifying expression. “Qamtareer” means exceedingly long and distressful, a day that stretches on in unrelenting hardship. The believers feed the poor and fulfill their vows not to earn human praise but because they are terrified of this Day. Their fear of the Day of Judgment is the engine of their righteousness. This verse repeats and reinforces the theme of fear mentioned earlier, but here the fear is directly linked to the charity. They see a connection between feeding a hungry person today and being saved from the horrors of that frowning Day. The Lord is described as “our Lord,” which is intimate and personal. They fear Him, but they also know He is their Rabb, their Sustainer. Their fear is not of a tyrannical unknown but of a Just Master whose punishment is severe but whose mercy is hoped for.

The word “‘abusan” personifies the Day with a frowning face, making the terror almost tangible. Everything about that Day will be harsh for the disbelievers. The believers, by acknowledging this fear and acting upon it, are promised deliverance.

The verse teaches that a healthy combination of fear and hope is necessary. The fear stops one from sin, and the hope keeps one from despair. The abrar did not just do good; they did good with trembling hearts, knowing that their deeds might not be perfect and that only Allah’s mercy can grant salvation. This combination of pure intention and profound fear is what made their simple act of feeding a captive so immensely valuable in the sight of Allah. The surah will now describe the reward they receive because of this inner state.

=11=

The result of their fear and their charitable deeds is now announced: “So Allah will deliver them from the horror of that Day, and grant them radiance and joy.” The Arabic “fa-waqahum Allahu sharra dhalika al-yawm” means Allah protected them, shielded them, saved them from the evil of that very Day they feared. Their fear was not in vain; it was the very means of their salvation. The one who fears the Day and prepares for it is rescued from it.

The word “waqa” implies a protective shield. Allah Himself becomes their guardian on that Day when no other guardian exists. Then, in addition to deliverance, they are given positive gifts: “and grant them radiance and joy.

” The word “nadrah” means a shining brightness, a radiant glow on their faces, as was described in Al-Qiyamah for the people of the right. “Surur” is a deep happiness, a delight that fills the heart. They are not merely saved from pain; they are elevated to a state of visible honor and inner bliss. The radiance is the light of faith and the reward for their sincerity. The joy is the eternal contentment of Paradise. The verse captures the complete transformation. In the world, they may have been hungry, tired, and poor, but on that Day, they will be glowing and joyful. The fear they felt in private is now replaced with public honor. The verse connects beautifully to the earlier description of the gloomy faces of the deniers in Al-Qiyamah. The abrar have the opposite destiny. This verse is a message of hope for all believers who strive in charity and devotion, even in the face of their own need. The reward is not just a distant promise; it is a certain reality. The deliverance from the horror is immediate and complete.

The surah now begins to detail the specific elements of that joy in the Gardens, starting with their eternal home.

=12=

The reward is further elaborated: “and reward them for their perseverance with a Garden in Paradise and garments of silk.” The Arabic “wa jazahum bima sabaru jannatan wa hareeran” explicitly states that the reward is in direct recompense for their “sabr,” their patience, perseverance, and steadfastness.

The word “sabr” encompasses all their struggles: their patience in fasting, their patience in giving away their beloved food, their patience in obeying Allah and avoiding sin, their patience in the face of poverty and hardship. The reward is “a Garden” and “garments of silk. ” The Garden is the ultimate abode of bliss. The silk garments are a specific luxury that was forbidden for men in this world. In Paradise, silk will be a reward for the righteous, both men and women, indicating the removal of all worldly prohibitions and the granting of the finest things. In this world, wearing silk was a sign of arrogance and extravagance for men, which is why it was prohibited. But in Paradise, where there is no arrogance and no competition, it becomes a pure adornment. The silk garments also symbolize comfort, softness, and honor.

The verse shows that patience pays off. Every moment of hunger endured while feeding the captive, every pang of sacrifice, is now compensated with the soft touch of silk and the eternal pleasure of the Garden.

The word “reward” (jaza) indicates a fair exchange. The believers gave food, and they receive a Garden. They were patient, and they receive silk. The transaction is infinitely in their favor, yet it is framed as a just reward. The verse encourages the believer to remain steadfast, no matter how difficult the path. The silk is not just a physical garment; it is a robe of honor, signifying their elevated status.

The surah continues to describe the luxurious details of their life in the Garden, inviting the listener to visualize and yearn for it.

=13=

The scene inside the Garden is now painted with vivid strokes. “There they will be reclining on canopied couches, never seeing scorching heat or bitter cold.

” The Arabic “muttaki’eena fiha ‘ala al-ara’ik” describes the posture of complete ease and relaxation. The believers are reclining, leaning back on “ara’ik,” which are raised, luxurious couches, often with canopies or curtains.

This is the posture of kings and honored guests, not of laborers or servants. The reclining indicates that there is no toil, no worry, no need to work. Everything is served to them. The atmosphere is one of perfect comfort.

The phrase “never seeing scorching heat or bitter cold” uses “la yarawna fiha shamsan wa la zamhareeran.” They will not see the sun—meaning they will not experience the oppressive, burning heat of a blazing sun. The Gardens are shaded, the climate is perfectly temperate. They will also not experience “zamhareer,” which is an intensely cold, bitter chill. In this world, people suffer from extremes of weather, from the scorching desert sun to the freezing winter winds. In Paradise, the environment is perfectly regulated. There is no discomfort of any kind. The canopied couches provide elegant shelter, and the absence of harsh elements ensures perpetual enjoyment. This verse appeals to the human desire for physical comfort and safety. The Arab who lived in the harsh desert sun and the cold desert nights could especially appreciate a place without these extremes.

The verse also subtly hints at the removal of all suffering. The sun and the cold are symbols of the challenges of the worldly life. Their absence means the absence of all forms of pain and hardship. The believers’ patience in the world is rewarded with a place where the very concept of discomfort is unknown. The reclining posture also suggests that they are engaged in pleasant conversation, enjoying each other’s company in this serene environment.

The surah continues to detail the delights of this Garden.

=14=

The description of the Garden’s comforts continues with two specific blessings: “The Garden’s shade will be right above them, and its fruit will be made very easy to reach.” The Arabic “wa daniyat ‘alayhim dhilaaluha” means the shade of the trees will be close, hanging over them, providing permanent, pleasant shelter. Shade in the hot climate of Arabia is a precious luxury, a symbol of relief and tranquility. In Paradise, the shade is not a fleeting patch but a constant, encompassing canopy that is perfectly positioned.

The second blessing is “wa dhullilat qutufuha tadleela,” meaning its fruits are made submissive, brought low, and easily pluckable. The trees of Paradise bend their branches to the inhabitants, offering their ripe fruits without any effort. There is no climbing, no stretching, no thorns. The fruit comes to the hand willingly. Some commentators mention that even if a person is reclining, the branch will lower itself so he can pick the fruit without rising.

This is a symbol of the complete ease and honor of the believers. In this world, obtaining food requires labor, sweat, and sometimes danger. In Paradise, all that toil is replaced with effortless abundance. The fruits are not only easy to reach but are perpetually fresh, delicious, and varied. This verse illustrates the fulfillment of desire before it is even fully expressed. The believers are served by the very environment. The shade and the fruit together represent the two key elements of a garden: protection from the sun and the provision of delicious sustenance. They are the external, physical manifestations of Allah’s pleasure. The verse invites the believer to imagine the peace of resting under a perfect shade, reaching out a hand and finding the most exquisite fruit already placed within grasp.

This is the reward for the hunger they endured in the world, when they gave their food to the poor and the captive. Every pang of hunger is now replaced with effortless, eternal satisfaction.

The surah continues to build the scene of their hospitality.

=15=

The scene shifts to the service and the utensils of the heavenly feast: “They will be waited on with silver vessels and cups of crystal.” The Arabic “wa yutaafu ‘alayhim bi-aaniyatin min fiddatin wa akwaabin kaanat qawaareer” describes the attendants (the eternal youths who will be mentioned later) circling among the believers, serving them. The “aaniyah” are large platters, vessels, and containers of food and drink, all made of silver. Silver in this world is a precious metal, a sign of wealth. In Paradise, it is the common material of their tableware, indicating the immense richness of the place. The “akwaab” are cups, beakers, or goblets, which are described as “qawaareer,” meaning made of crystal, glass, something transparent and sparkling. The combination of silver vessels and crystal cups suggests a setting of luminous elegance. The vessels are not merely functional; they are objects of beauty that add to the pleasure of the feast. The fact that they are “waited on” indicates that the believers are served like honored royalty. They do not fetch their own food or pour their own drinks. The service is continuous and graceful. The crystal cups allow the drinker to see the beautiful color and purity of the beverage inside, enhancing the sensory delight. This verse appeals to the human appreciation for fine dining and beautiful tableware. In the world, such luxury is often associated with arrogance and waste, but in Paradise, it is purified and made a pure reward. The believers are not distracted by the vessels; they simply enjoy them as part of the overall bliss.

The mention of these details demonstrates that Paradise is a physical, tangible place with real objects, not just a spiritual abstraction. The senses are fully engaged and delighted. The surah will further describe the special nature of these crystal cups.

=16=

The description of the cups becomes even more wondrous: “crystalline silver, filled precisely as desired.” The Arabic “qawaareera min fiddatin qaddaruha taqdeeran” resolves a seeming contradiction. The cups were described as crystal (glass), but now they are said to be “of silver.” This means they are made of silver that has the transparency and brilliance of crystal. In this world, silver is opaque and glass is transparent. In Paradise, Allah combines the qualities of both into a new, unimaginable material: the cups are silver in substance but clear as glass.

This is one of the marvels of the heavenly creation, showing that the things of Paradise, while bearing familiar names, are of a completely different and higher nature. The phrase “qaddaruha taqdeeran” means the cupbearers, or perhaps the cups themselves, have measured the drink in exact accordance with the desire of the drinker. The quantity is perfectly suited to the individual’s wish—not a drop more, not a drop less. The drink is poured to the precise level that the person finds most satisfying. This attention to detail reflects the personalized nature of the bliss in Paradise. Every desire is met with perfect precision. The cup is a work of art, and the drink inside is perfectly proportioned.

The verse shows that the pleasures of Paradise are not random or haphazard; they are meticulously designed by the All-Wise. The believer who reads this feels that his every wish is known and will be fulfilled exactly as he wants. The combination of the transparent silver cup and the precisely measured drink is a symbol of the fusion of beauty and function. The believer’s heart is drawn to the One who prepares such hospitality.

The surah continues to describe the drink itself, adding another flavor to the pure wine.

=17=

The heavenly drinks continue to be described. “And they will be given a drink of pure wine flavoured with ginger.

” The Arabic “wa yusqawna fiha ka’san kana mizajuha zanjabeela” introduces another goblet. Earlier, the drink was flavored with camphor (kafur), which is cooling and soothing. Now, it is flavored with ginger (zanjabeel). In this world, ginger is known for its warm, spicy, and aromatic qualities. It adds a pleasant heat and a distinct, invigorating flavor. The contrast between the cool camphor and the warm ginger shows the variety of delights in Paradise. The believers can enjoy both according to their mood and desire. The drink is still described as “ka’s,” a cup of pure wine that is non-intoxicating, bringing only pleasure and no harm.

The mention of ginger also evokes the idea of a refreshing, stimulating drink that aids digestion and adds to the enjoyment of the feast. The Arabs were familiar with ginger as an exotic, valuable spice imported from distant lands. Its presence in Paradise indicates the finest and most sought-after flavors are abundantly available. The drink is not a single, uniform beverage; it is a vast selection of exquisite tastes. The believer is invited to imagine the joy of being served a perfectly prepared drink, its flavor carefully calibrated, in a cup of luminous silver-crystal, while reclining on a canopied couch in eternal shade.

The verse adds another layer to the sensory tapestry of Paradise. The variety also suggests that the believers will never become bored; there will always be new pleasures and new flavors to explore.

The surah now reveals the name of the fountain from which this ginger-flavored drink is drawn, adding to the sense of a unique, special source.

=18=

The source of the ginger-flavored drink is a special spring: “from a spring there, called Salsabeel.” The Arabic “‘aynan fiha tusamma salsabeela” identifies a fountain within Paradise named Salsabeel.

The name itself is beautiful and meaningful. “Salsabeel” is derived from a root that implies a smooth, easy flow, a pleasant swallow, and a sweet taste. It is a drink that goes down easily, delightfully, without any harshness.

The word has a musical, flowing quality in Arabic. It evokes the image of a gentle, sparkling stream. The fact that the spring has a name indicates its special, distinguished status. It is not just any fountain; it is a renowned, exclusive source of a particular drink. The believers will be given drinks from this spring, perhaps as a reward for their specific acts of patience and charity. The naming of the spring also shows the attention to detail and the personal nature of the blessings. Paradise is not a generic place; it has landmarks, named rivers, and special features that the inhabitants will know and love. The believers may ask, “Let us go to Salsabeel,” and they will direct its flow to themselves. The spring is “there” (fiha), within the Garden, accessible and ever-flowing.

The name Salsabeel has been interpreted by some as “the drink that is easy to swallow,” combining “salasa” (smooth) and “sabeel” (way), meaning the way of smoothness. Others say it simply means a drink that is delicious and wholesome. The verse inspires a longing for that named fountain, a place that awaits the abrar. It personalizes the reward and makes it feel like a real destination.

The surah now introduces the servers who will bring these drinks.

=19=

The attendants serving the believers in Paradise are described with breathtaking imagery: “They will be waited on by eternal youths. If you saw them, you would think they were scattered pearls.

” The Arabic “wa yatoofu ‘alayhim wildanun mukhalladoon” describes boys, young servants, who are “mukhalladoon,” meaning they are eternally young, never aging. They remain in a perpetual state of youthful beauty, energy, and freshness. They circle around the believers, carrying the silver platters and crystal cups, attending to every need. Their presence adds to the honor and delight of the believers.

The second part of the verse gives a glimpse of their beauty: “If you saw them, you would think they were scattered pearls.” The Arabic “itha ra’aytahum hasibtahum lu’lu’an manthura” creates an image of exquisite, glowing pearls that have been scattered across a surface. Pearls are pure, luminous, and precious. The scattered pearls suggest abundance and the delightful randomness of beauty. The youths are not standing in rigid lines; they are dispersed throughout, attending to the guests, each one a gem of perfection. Their skin is radiant, their features are flawless, and their movements are graceful.

This description is not meant to evoke any inappropriate desire but to express the perfect aesthetic harmony of Paradise. Everything in Paradise, including the servants, is a source of visual pleasure. The believers will be surrounded by beauty that pleases the eye and the soul. The eternal youthfulness of the servants also indicates that Paradise is free from the decay of time. The believers themselves will be eternally young, and their attendants will mirror that. The scattered pearls image also implies that they are numerous, and each one is uniquely beautiful. This verse is a continuation of the theme of honor; the believers are served by beings of angelic or specially created nature, who are themselves treasures of the Garden.

The surah now turns to the overall view of the Kingdom that the believer will possess.

=20=

The surah now invites the listener to take in the panoramic view of Paradise: “And if you looked around, you would see indescribable bliss and a vast kingdom.” The Arabic “wa itha ra’ayta thamma ra’ayta na’eeman wa mulkan kabeera” addresses the Prophet directly in the first instance, but it is a general address to any observer.

The word “thamma” means “there,” in that place. If you were to cast your eyes around Paradise, wherever your gaze fell, you would see “na’eem,” which is an all-encompassing term for bliss, delight, comfort, luxury, and happiness. This includes all the sensory pleasures described so far and much more that is beyond description. Then you would see “mulkan kabeera,” a magnificent, immense kingdom. The believers are not just guests; they are kings and queens in their own right. Each believer is given a kingdom whose vastness is unimaginable. The lowest person in Paradise will have a kingdom ten times the size of this world, according to a hadith. The kingdom includes palaces, gardens, rivers, servants, and treasures beyond counting.

This verse reveals the ultimate reward: the believers, who were often poor and powerless in the world, become the rulers of a grand, eternal domain. The phrase “indescribable bliss” translates “na’eeman,” which captures both the physical and spiritual joys. The “vast kingdom” signifies authority, autonomy, and honor. The believer will not be a passive inhabitant; he will be a sovereign in his own realm, with his desires fulfilled instantly. The vision of this kingdom inspires the believer to work for it. Every sacrifice made in the world, every restriction observed, was the price of this kingdom.

The verse also answers the taunts of the disbelievers who thought they were the powerful ones. On the Day of Judgment, the true kingdom will belong to the righteous. The surah has now painted a complete picture of the reward: from the drink to the garments, the couches, the shade, the service, and the overarching kingdom. The believer who reads these verses feels a surge of motivation and a deep gratitude to the Lord who promises such generosity.
