=21=

The surah continues to lavish detail upon the rewards of the abrar, the virtuous ones, by describing their splendid attire and adornments. “The virtuous will be dressed in garments of fine green silk and rich brocade.

” The Arabic “'aliyahum thiyabu sundusin khudrun wa istabraq” paints a picture of exquisite clothing. Sundus is a fine, thin, luxurious silk, while istabraq is a thick, heavy brocade silk, often interwoven with gold or silver threads. The combination of the two indicates a wardrobe of immense richness and variety. The colour green is specifically mentioned. Green is the colour of life, freshness, and beauty; it is the colour of the vegetation of Paradise. It is also, according to many narrations, the colour of the garments of the people of Paradise, and the colour of their silken couches and the cushions they recline upon. The green silk signifies vitality and eternal youthfulness. The clothing is not merely for covering but is an adornment and a mark of honour. In this world, silk is forbidden for men, but in Paradise it becomes a pure and permissible delight, signifying the removal of worldly restrictions and the elevation of the believers into a state of refined luxury. Then the verse adds another layer: “and adorned with bracelets of silver. ” The Arabic “wa hullu asawira min fiddah” indicates they will be decorated with silver bracelets. Bracelets are ornaments typically associated with royalty and high status. In pre-Islamic Arabia, gold and silver bracelets were worn by kings and the wealthy elite. The believers, many of whom were poor and without adornment in the world, are now adorned like kings.

The mention of silver here, while gold is mentioned in other surahs, shows the variety of adornments. Every believer is lavishly decorated.

Finally, the verse culminates with an even greater gift: “and their Lord will give them a purifying drink.” The Arabic “wa saqahum rabbuhum sharaban tahura” places the focus on the direct action of the Lord. Allah Himself will serve them a drink.

This is the ultimate honour, far surpassing being served by the eternal youths. The drink is “tahura,” intensely pure. It purifies the soul from any remaining traces of worldly attachments, envy, or impurity, making them absolutely clean and radiant, fit for the eternal presence. Some scholars interpret this as the final drink after they have eaten and drunk, which cleanses their stomachs and bodies, leaving only a fragrant perspiration and a complete internal purity. Others say it is a special wine of Paradise that elevates them to the highest spiritual state. The combination of silken garments, silver bracelets, and the Lord’s personal drink is a breathtaking picture of honour, beauty, and intimate divine hospitality. It shows that the reward is not just a place but a relationship, a nearness to the Most Merciful who personally attends to His beloved servants.

=22=

After describing all these wonders, the surah reveals the words that the believers will hear in Paradise, words that will bring them the deepest satisfaction. “And they will be told, ‘All this is surely a reward for you. Your striving has been appreciated.’” The Arabic “inna hadha kana lakum jazaa’an wa kana sa’yukum mashkura” is a divine commendation. The voice could be that of the angels conveying Allah’s greeting, or it could be a direct address from the Lord.

The phrase “All this is surely a reward for you” confirms that the bliss is not a random gift but a direct compensation for their deeds. The word “jazaa’an” implies a just recompense, a payment due.

Yet it is a payment whose value infinitely exceeds the work, out of divine generosity. The second phrase “Your striving has been appreciated” is perhaps even more touching. “Sa’yukum” refers to their earnest efforts, their running toward good, their hard work in worship, charity, and patience.

The word “mashkura” means it has been accepted, recognized, and thanked. Allah, the Lord of the worlds, is acknowledging and thanking His servants for their tiny, flawed efforts.

This is the ultimate validation. In the world, their efforts were often mocked, ignored, or deemed worthless. They were told their prayers were useless, their charity a waste. Here, the Creator of the universe tells them that their striving was seen, valued, and appreciated.

The term “shukr” (gratitude) is usually from a servant to the Lord, but here the Lord is expressing gratitude to His servant, meaning He has fully acknowledged and rewarded their deeds. This verse is a source of immense hope for every believer who feels that their struggles are unnoticed. Allah sees the quiet charity, the secret night prayer, the tear of repentance, the patient endurance of hardship. On that Day, He will announce His appreciation in front of the angels and the assembled believers.

The verse also teaches the importance of “sa’y,” of striving. Paradise is not attained by wishful thinking but by active, continuous effort. The believers are the people of striving. This divine commendation will erase every memory of fatigue, pain, and sacrifice. The words “your striving has been appreciated” will be the sweetest sound the believers ever hear.

The surah now transitions from the description of the reward to the context of the revelation and the reassurance of the Prophet, linking the glorious promises to the immediate situation in Mecca.

=23=

After the lengthy and beautiful description of the rewards of the righteous, the surah turns directly to address the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. “Indeed, it is We Who have revealed the Quran to you O Prophet in stages.

” The Arabic “inna nahnu nazzalna ‘alayka al-qur’ana tanzeela” uses multiple emphasis: “inna” (indeed), “nahnu” (We), and the verb form “nazzalna” which indicates a gradual, repeated sending down. This is a profound statement of the Quran’s origin. It is not the word of a man, as the deniers alleged in Al-Muddaththir; it is a revelation from the Lord of the worlds, sent down in stages, piece by piece, over twenty-three years.

The word “tanzeel” implies a deliberate, measured descent. The Quran was not revealed as a single book all at once; it came down according to the needs of the moment, guiding the Prophet and the believers step by step. This gradual revelation was a mercy, making it easier to memorize, understand, and implement. The verse serves as a reassurance to the Prophet. The long descriptions of Paradise, the warnings of Hell, the stories of previous nations—all of this is not his own invention. It is from the One who knows the unseen.

Therefore, he should be confident in his message. The verse also connects to the earlier command in Al-Qiyamah where the Prophet was told not to rush his tongue with the Quran; Allah would ensure its collection and recitation. Here, the emphasis is on the fact that the revelation is a deliberate act of Allah. This knowledge strengthens the Prophet’s resolve.

The word “tanzeela” also implies that the Quran is sent from above, from a high, exalted source. It carries the authority of the heavens. For the believers, this verse reinforces their faith in the divine origin of the scripture they follow. It is not a human philosophy but a guided, measured revelation from the All-Wise. This verse acts as a hinge, connecting the doctrinal and eschatological content of the surah with the practical instructions for the Prophet that follow.

The next verses will command patience, worship, and reliance on Allah in the face of the deniers’ opposition, all grounded in the fact that the Quran is from Him.

=24=

Having affirmed that the Quran is a revelation from Him, Allah gives the Prophet a crucial command: “So be patient with your Lord’s decree, and do not yield to any evildoer or staunch disbeliever from among them.” The Arabic “fasbir li-hukmi rabbika” links the patience directly to the preceding fact of revelation. Because the Quran is from Allah and contains the truth, the Prophet must remain steadfast in implementing its teachings and awaiting the fulfillment of its promises, despite the opposition. “Hukmi rabbika” means your Lord’s decree, His command, His judgment. This includes all the religious rulings, the moral instructions, and the divine decision to give the disbelievers respite before punishment. The Prophet is to patiently endure the mockery, the persecution, and the delay of victory, trusting that his Lord’s decree is perfect.

The verse then specifically warns: “and do not yield to any evildoer or staunch disbeliever from among them.” The Arabic “wa la tuti‘ minhum athiman aw kafura” prohibits the Prophet from obeying or giving in to the demands and pressures of two types of people. The “athim” is the evildoer, the one steeped in sin, who actively commits immoral acts. The “kafur” is the staunch, intensely ungrateful disbeliever who rejects the truth with stubbornness. The Quraysh elite were a mix of both. They tried to pressure the Prophet to compromise, to soften his message, to stop criticizing their idols, to give them special treatment. They suggested that if he wanted them to follow him, he should expel the poor believers, or that they should alternate in worship. This verse commands him to refuse all such compromises. He is not to yield an inch to their sinful and disbelieving demands. The command is a protection of the message’s purity. The Prophet’s duty is to obey Allah, not the whims of the powerful evildoers. This verse provides a timeless lesson for all believers who face pressure to compromise their faith. Whether it is in matters of worship, ethics, or doctrine, the believer must not obey the sinner or the disbeliever when they call to something contrary to Allah’s decree. The patience is to be accompanied by firmness.

The surah now provides the spiritual tools to maintain this patience.

=25=

The surah now prescribes the spiritual sustenance that will enable the Prophet to maintain the commanded patience and firmness: “Always remember the Name of your Lord morning and evening.” The Arabic “wadhkur isma rabbika bukratan wa aseela” is a command to engage in continuous dhikr, the remembrance of Allah, throughout the day. “Bukrah” refers to the early morning, the time from dawn to sunrise. “Aseel” refers to the evening, from the afternoon until sunset.

The mention of these two specific times encompasses the beginning and the end of the day, and by extension, all the time in between. The Prophet and the believers are instructed to bookend their days with the remembrance of Allah’s Name. This remembrance includes the formal prayers, the morning and afternoon prayers, as well as the informal glorification, praise, and supplication.

The Name of the Lord is a fortress. When a believer begins the day with “Bismillah” and the morning adhkar and ends it with the evening adhkar, he places a spiritual shield around himself. The remembrance keeps the heart connected to Allah, making it easier to resist the temptations to yield to the evildoers.

The word “always” (implied by the command form) indicates consistency. It is not a one-time act but a perpetual habit. The surah is echoing the command given in Al-Muzzammil to remember the Name of the Lord and devote oneself to Him. The morning and evening are times of transition, when the day shifts from darkness to light and from light to darkness. These transitions remind the believer of the constant change in the world and the permanence of the Creator. By remembering Allah at these times, the believer anchors his soul in the unchanging reality amidst the flux of life.

The verse teaches that patience and firmness are not achieved by mere willpower; they are the fruits of a heart saturated with the remembrance of Allah. The next verse will add the night worship, completing the daily cycle of devotion.

=26=

The daily and nightly worship cycle is completed: “and prostrate before Him during part of the night, and glorify Him long at night.” The Arabic “wa min al-layli fasjud lahu wa sabbihhu laylan taweela” commands the Prophet to perform sujood (prostration) in a portion of the night. Prostration is the highest form of physical submission, the moment when the servant is closest to his Lord. The command specifically mentions prostration, indicating the night prayer, which is characterized by long, deep prostrations.

This is the tahajjud prayer, the prayer that was made obligatory or highly emphasized in the early Meccan period. The second part, “and glorify Him long at night,” uses “sabbihhu laylan taweela” to command extended glorification (tasbeeh) throughout a lengthy portion of the night. This could refer to the recitation of the Quran, which is a form of glorification, or to the specific phrases of SubhanAllah, or to the prayer itself as an act of glorification. The length of the night worship is emphasized. The night is the time for unhurried, intimate communion with Allah. In the quiet of the night, the heart is free from distractions, and the glorification flows more sincerely.

This command is directly linked to the earlier command of patience. The night prayer is the fuel for the day’s patience. The Prophet, peace be upon him, would stand in prayer until his feet swelled, and when asked why he did so when he was already forgiven, he replied, “Should I not be a grateful servant?” This verse demonstrates that the night worship is the training ground for the soul. It builds the spiritual strength to face the hostility of the evildoers and disbelievers during the day. For the believers, this is an invitation to make the night a time of drawing closer to Allah, even if it is a small portion. The glorification “long at night” implies that there is a sweetness in prolonged worship that cannot be found in hurried rituals.

The surah now turns back to the deniers, contrasting their love of the fleeting world with the believers’ devotion.

=27=

After instructing the Prophet in patience and worship, the surah turns to diagnose the core sickness of his opponents. “Surely those pagans love this fleeting world, totally neglecting a weighty Day ahead of them.

” The Arabic “inna ha’ula’i yuhibbuna al-‘ajilata wa yadharuna wara’ahum yawman thaqeela” points directly at the Quraysh. The word “ha’ula’i” (these) refers to the deniers immediately around the Prophet. Their fundamental problem is their love (“yuhibbuna”) for the “ajilah”—the fleeting, immediate, transient world. They are infatuated with its pleasures, its wealth, its status, its immediate gratification. This love consumes their attention and energy. As a direct consequence, they “neglect” (yadharuna) what is “behind them”—meaning the Hereafter, which they have cast aside and left behind their backs as if it does not matter. The Day is described as “thaqeela,” heavy, weighty. It is a Day of immense gravity, laden with terrifying events, severe accounting, and eternal consequences. Their neglect is a willful abandonment. They turn their backs on it, as was described in Al-Muddaththir with the simile of the startled zebras. They focus entirely on the immediate and ignore the ultimate. This verse is a masterful summary of the psychology of the disbeliever. The love of the world and the neglect of the Hereafter are the twin roots of all sin and rejection. The love of the fleeting world makes the heavy demands of faith feel like burdens. The neglect of the weighty Day makes the warnings seem irrelevant. The believer, in contrast, loves the Hereafter and is mindful of its weight, which makes the fleeting world seem light and insignificant.

The verse also contains a subtle warning: the weighty Day is “ahead of them,” meaning they are moving toward it inevitably, even as they neglect it. They cannot escape its gravity.

The surah now delivers a powerful reminder of the Creator’s power, which they are ignoring, linking back to the creation arguments of the previous surahs.

=28=

The surah confronts the neglectful pagans with the reality of their own creation, echoing the arguments from Al-Qiyamah. “It is We Who created them and perfected their physical form.

” The Arabic “nahnu khalaqnahum wa shadadna asrahum” uses the plural of majesty: “It is We.” Allah reminds them that He is their Creator. The verb “shadadna” means to make strong, to tighten, to bind firmly. “Asrahum” refers to their joints, their frame, their physical constitution. Allah created them and then made their bodies strong, well-constructed, with bones and ligaments perfectly tied together, a marvel of biomechanical engineering. They walk on two legs, use their hands, speak with their tongues, all because Allah fashioned them with precision.

This reminder is a call to humility. How can a being whose body is a divine gift use that body to rebel? Then comes the threat: “But if We will, We can easily replace them with others.

” The Arabic “wa idha shi’na baddalna amthalahum tabdeela” means that Allah can, with a single will, remove them entirely and bring another people, similar to them or better, who will worship and obey. This is the same warning given in Al-Ma‘arij: “We are truly capable of replacing them with others better than them.

” The word “tabdeela” emphasizes a complete, effortless substitution. The Quraysh were not indispensable. Their lineage, their status, their wealth meant nothing. If they continued in their love of the fleeting world and neglect of the weighty Day, Allah would simply replace them. This verse serves as a severe warning to the arrogant. It also comforts the Prophet and the believers that the opposition is not permanent or all-powerful. Allah can remove the obstacles and bring forth a generation of sincere worshippers. The verse grounds the threat in the undeniable reality of creation. The One who made them can unmake them and make others. The entire argument of the surah comes full circle: the human who was once nothing worth mentioning, then created from a drop, then guided, is now reminded that his existence is entirely dependent on the Creator’s will.

The surah now offers a final, open invitation and a profound theological conclusion.

=29=

The surah now concludes its message with an open, universal call. “Surely this is a reminder. So let whoever wills take the Right Way to their Lord.

” The Arabic “inna hadhihi tadhkiratun fa-man shaa ittakhadha ila rabbihi sabeela” encapsulates the Quran’s approach to guidance. “This” refers to the entire surah, the Quran as a whole, the revelations that have been sent down. It is a “tadhkirah,” a reminder, an admonition. It is not a new, alien knowledge but a reminder of the truth already embedded in the human soul, the covenant taken before birth, the signs in creation and in the self.

The word “tadhkirah” implies that the human being is forgetful; the Quran comes to awaken the memory. Since it is a clear reminder, the choice is now given: “So let whoever wills take the Right Way to their Lord.

” The phrase “man shaa” (whoever wills) upholds human free will and responsibility. The path, the “sabeel,” is open and illuminated. The destination is “their Lord,” the most beautiful and personal goal. The verse is an invitation, not a coercion. It respects the dignity of the human being to choose his own eternal destiny. The “Right Way” is the path of Islam, of submission, of gratitude, of the abrar whose qualities were described. The verse echoes the similar open invitation in Al-Muzzammil and Al-Muddaththir. It is a signature of the Meccan surahs. The tone is gentle yet urgent. The reminder has been delivered. The consequences have been laid out. Now, the will of the individual is summoned. This verse calls the listener to action. It demands a response. The believer answers by saying, “I will, my Lord. I choose the Way to You. ” The surah then immediately balances this statement of free will with the all-encompassing reality of divine will, preventing any sense of human self-sufficiency.

=30=

After affirming human choice, the surah places it within the ultimate framework of divine decree. “But you cannot will to do so unless Allah wills.

” The Arabic “wa ma tasha’oona illa an yashaa Allah” is a foundational theological statement. Human will is real, but it operates under the sovereign will of Allah. No person can will to take the Right Way, or to do anything at all, unless Allah has willed for him to have that will and to exercise it. This does not negate free will; rather, it establishes that human will is itself a creation of Allah and is encompassed by His will. It prevents arrogance in the believer, who cannot boast that he chose the path by his own power alone. It also explains why some, despite the clear reminder, do not take the way: Allah has not willed it for them, as a consequence of their own stubbornness and rejection of the truth, which is known in His eternal knowledge. The verse provides comfort and humility. The believer is grateful that Allah willed for him to be among the guided. He seeks refuge from being left to his own devices.

The verse ends with two names of Allah: “Indeed, Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise. ” “Aleeman” indicates that Allah’s knowledge encompasses everything: who will choose guidance, who will stray, and what every soul earns. “Hakeeman” indicates that His decree and His will are governed by perfect wisdom. He does not will anything except for a wise purpose.

The placement of these names here is crucial. It answers the question of why some are guided and others are not: it is rooted in Allah’s infinite knowledge and wisdom, not in randomness or injustice. The believer trusts that whatever Allah wills is the best possible outcome. The verse brings the surah to a close with a sense of awe and surrender. The human being chooses, but his choice is embedded in the larger, beautiful tapestry of the divine will.

The surah ends with one final verse that summarizes the outcome of the two wills.

=31=

The final verse of the surah encapsulates the ultimate division of humanity based on the divine will and human choice. “He admits whoever He wills into His mercy.

” The Arabic “yudkhilu man yashaa’u fee rahmatihi” describes the entry into Paradise, which is the “mercy” of Allah. Paradise is not earned by deeds alone; it is entered through Allah’s mercy, as the Prophet, peace be upon him, taught: “None of you will enter Paradise by his deeds alone. ” They asked, “Even you, O Messenger of Allah?” He said, “Even me, unless Allah envelops me in His mercy.

” The phrase “whoever He wills” refers to those whom Allah knows have sincerely chosen the path of faith and righteousness, and upon whom He bestows His merciful acceptance. It is the successful outcome of the grateful ones, the abrar. Then the verse states the opposite destiny: “As for the wrongdoers, He has prepared for them a painful punishment.” The “wrongdoers” (dhalimeen) are those who chose ingratitude, disbelief, and oppression. By rejecting the reminder and using their will to stray, they have wronged their own souls. Allah’s justice has prepared a painful punishment for them.

The word “a‘adda” (prepared) indicates that Hell is already created and waiting, just as Paradise is prepared. The verse perfectly mirrors the structure of the surah. The surah began by mentioning the human being created from nothing, then guided, then given free choice to be grateful or ungrateful. It then briefly mentioned the punishment of the disbelievers, and at length described the reward of the grateful. It now concludes by stating that the final entry into mercy is by Allah’s will, and the final punishment is by His justice. The surah provides no room for complacency. The believer hopes for Allah’s mercy and fears His punishment. The “painful punishment” is the outcome of neglecting the weighty Day. The “mercy” is the culmination of taking the Right Way to the Lord. The surah, which so beautifully detailed the canopied couches, the crystal cups, the silken garments, and the divine drink, now closes with the grave reality that the way to that bliss is through the mercy of Allah, attained by the will to take the path that Allah has shown.
