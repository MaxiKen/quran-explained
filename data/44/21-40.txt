=21=

After presenting the compelling proof and seeking refuge in Allah from the threat of stoning, Moses delivers a final, dignified ultimatum that encapsulates the prophetic approach to rejection. The phrase "if you do not believe me" places the responsibility squarely on Pharaoh and his court. Moses has fulfilled his duty; he has come with clear signs, a trustworthy character, and a divine command. He has removed any possible excuse. The condition he sets out is now simple and final: if after all of this, you still choose not to believe, then there is nothing more I can do for you, and no further argument I can present.

The second part of the verse, "then let me be," or more literally "then leave me alone" or "separate yourselves from me," marks a profound moment of prophetic dissociation. Moses is not declaring war, nor is he threatening them with a personal retaliation. He is declaring a peaceful separation between those who submit to the truth and those who arrogantly reject it.

This is a boundary that must be drawn when calling to faith. The messenger is not a coercer; his duty is only to convey the message clearly. Once the message has been delivered and rejected, the relationship shifts. The believer can no longer be an accomplice in the disbelief of the disbelievers, and his path must now diverge from theirs.

This is a powerful lesson in spiritual integrity. There comes a point where continued debate, constant compromise, or staying in a state of ambiguous association becomes spiritually damaging. The caller to truth must have the courage to say, with all courtesy but with complete firmness, "If you do not believe me, then let me be." It is a declaration that one’s ultimate allegiance is to Allah alone, not to the approval or acceptance of people, no matter how powerful. This dignified separation also serves as a final warning, for it signals the impending divine judgment that will now fall upon those who have been left to their own chosen path.

=22=

The story reaches a critical turning point. After the prolonged mission of Moses, the repeated signs, and the final ultimatum, Pharaoh and his people persisted in their disbelief and transgression.

The verse begins with "Ultimately," indicating that this was the final outcome after all efforts were exhausted. The action is deeply personal: "he cried out to his Lord. " Moses, the noble messenger, the one who conversed directly with Allah, now turns his face away from the creation and towards his Creator in a private, intense supplication.

The Arabic word "da'aa" implies calling out earnestly, with humility and need. The content of his call is a simple, factual report, not a hateful rant: "These are a wicked people.

" The word used, "mujrimoon," denotes deeply rooted criminality and sinfulness, people who have severed their connection with morality and truth. Moses is not making this declaration out of personal frustration but as a truthful testimony before the Lord of the worlds. This cry is an appeal for divine judgment after all human effort has been exhausted. It echoes the words of other prophets, such as Noah, who after centuries of patient calling, finally declared his people's irredeemable state. It marks the moment when divine mercy's call has been definitively rejected, and the sunnah of Allah regarding the destruction of persistent, wicked nations is about to be executed.

This is a deeply sobering moment. It reminds us that the prophets were human beings who felt the pain of rejection and the sorrow of seeing people choose destruction. But it also demonstrates the prophetic balance: they exhausted every possible avenue of mercy and warning before ever reaching this point. The cry is not the first resort but the last, a heartbreaking acknowledgment that the people, by their own free will, have certified their identity as a criminal nation for whom no amount of clear signs will work.

=23=

In immediate response to Moses’s heartfelt call, Allah issues a direct command. The response is not delayed, for the appointed time for judgment has arrived.

The command begins with "Leave with My servants at night." This is a divine directive for an exodus under the cover of darkness.

The phrase "My servants" is incredibly significant. These oppressed, enslaved Children of Israel are not referred to as Moses’s people or a downtrodden ethnic group; Allah claims them directly as His own.

This is the ultimate honor and the ultimate liberation. In the very house of bondage, Allah is declaring them free and declaring His intimate connection to them. The timing, "at night," was a strategic mercy from Allah. Nighttime allows for a covert departure, minimizing the risk of immediate confrontation and providing cover for the mass movement of men, women, and children.

The verse then adds a chilling piece of intelligence from the All-Knowing: "for you will surely be pursued." Allah informs Moses that Pharaoh will not simply let them go. His arrogance will drive him to chase after them with his military might. This foreknowledge serves multiple purposes. It prepares Moses and the Israelites for the trial to come, so they are not seized with terror when they see the dust of the pursuing army. It is also a divine trap being set for Pharaoh. His pursuit is not an act of strength but a final, fatal step into the doom that Allah has decreed. Knowing that you will be pursued but being commanded to march forward into the wilderness at night is a massive test of trust. Moses’s immediate obedience, without arguing about the logistics, demonstrates the unwavering faith of a true prophet. The command to leave is the first step of a two-part miracle; the command to strike the sea will follow.

=24=

This verse continues the divine instructions given to Moses and adds a breathtaking command that defies all human logic. After the Children of Israel have crossed, Allah commands Moses to "leave the sea parted." The miraculous parting of the Red Sea, struck by Moses's staff, created a dry path through walls of water. Now, the natural instinct of a commander leading a fleeing people would be to let the waters come crashing back down immediately after crossing, to create a barrier against the pursuing army. But Allah orders the exact opposite: leave the path open. Do not pray for the sea to return to its normal state. The command is a deliberate act of enticement, a divine trap set for the tyrant. The reason is given immediately: "for they are certainly an army bound to drown. " The Arabic carries a strong emphasis of certainty. Pharaoh's army is not simply a force that might be stopped; they are a "drowned army" in the divine decree. Their fate is sealed. The open path is the bait that will draw Pharaoh and his chariots into the heart of the sea, where their arrogance will reach its full height and their destruction will be absolute. This moment is a powerful illustration of how Allah's plans operate on a level far beyond human strategic thinking. What appears to be a tactical error—leaving the escape route open for the enemy—is actually the perfect execution of divine justice. The tyrant is not overcome by a clever ambush or a superior fighting force; he is destroyed by his own arrogance and blindness. He sees the open path and mistakes it for his own destiny to recapture his slaves, not realizing it is the hallway to his own grave.

For the believer, this verse teaches that one must obey Allah’s commands even when they seem counterintuitive, because the All-Knowing sees the end results that are hidden from human eyes.

=25=

With the destruction of Pharaoh’s army, the surah now shifts the listener's gaze to the aftermath and the moral lesson to be drawn from it. The verse begins with an implied command to reflect: "Imagine how many gardens and springs the tyrants left behind.

" The word "kam" expresses the sheer vast quantity of what was lost. The audience is asked to picture the sophisticated, lush civilization of Egypt. The "gardens" represent carefully cultivated orchards, a sign of wealth, stability, and mastery over the land. The "springs" represent the abundant fresh water, the source of life and luxury in an arid climate. The gardens and springs together paint a picture of a paradise on earth, a testament to a great and powerful civilization. But the operative and devastating phrase is "the tyrants left behind. " All of that meticulously built and enjoyed splendor was left, instantly and eternally. The verb "khalafu," they left behind, implies that they went forward, into death and destruction, and everything they owned remained behind for others. The tyrannical Pharaohs, who believed they owned the earth and even claimed divinity, could not take a single rose or a single cup of water with them into their watery grave.

This is the ultimate exposure of the fleeting nature of worldly power and material wealth. It is a direct warning to the Meccan aristocrats, like Abu Jahl and others, who prided themselves on their gardens in Taif and Mecca, their orchards, and their wells. The verse forces them to look at the ruins and history of a greater power and see their own future. No amount of worldly flourishing can protect a nation from divine punishment when they reject the truth. The gardens and springs are a silent, empty witness against their former owners.

=26=

This verse continues the inventory of what the tyrants of Egypt left behind, adding two more categories that deepen the sense of loss and the vanity of their worldly achievements. The "crops" or "zuroo'" refer to their agricultural produce, the fields of wheat, barley, and other grains that sustained their mighty civilization.

The word used implies variety and abundance, vast golden fields that were a source of economic power and food security. The second category is "splendid residences" or "maqam kareem. " This phrase denotes not just houses but magnificent, noble dwellings, palaces with towering columns, luxurious furnishings, and elaborate architecture that spoke of their status and honor. These were residences fit for self-proclaimed gods. Together with the gardens and springs, these items formed the complete picture of a settled, sophisticated, and seemingly permanent civilization. They had mastered agriculture to feed themselves, and they had built monuments to their own grandeur.

Yet the verse places all of this under the same stark sentence: they "left behind." The unspoken truth is that these crops were harvested by others, and these splendid residences were inhabited by strangers. The tombs and pyramids they built to immortalize their names ironically became tourist stops for later generations. This verse contains a profound psychological lesson about the nature of worldly attachment. The disbelievers of Mecca, like the Pharaohs before them, saw their wealth and status as proof that they were on the right path, favored by the gods or by fate. Allah dismantles this delusion by showing that these worldly goods are merely a test, a temporary grant that can be transferred in an instant. The very crops and palaces that make one feel immortal and secure are, in reality, a mirage that vanishes upon the first breath of divine decree. The true measure of a people is not what they build, but what they believe and how they obey their Lord.

=27=

This verse completes the trilogy of what the tyrants left behind by mentioning the most personal and intimate aspect of their wealth: "luxuries" or "na'mah" and the fact that "they fully enjoyed" them. The word "na'mah" refers to a state of blissful ease, comfort, and pleasure. It encompasses all the things that make life feel soft, delightful, and carefree—exquisite food, fine clothing, entertainment, servants, and all the means of physical gratification that a wealthy empire can provide.

The phrase "which they fully enjoyed" is particularly cutting. The Arabic "fakiheen" implies that they were not just passively possessing these things; they were actively, happily, and indulgently rejoicing in them. Their life was a party, a continuous cycle of pleasure-seeking and amusement. This connects directly back to the earlier description of the Meccans who were "amusing themselves" in doubt. Like the Meccans, the people of Pharaoh filled their lives with the enjoyment of luxuries to the point where the serious call of a messenger was a disruptive, unwelcome noise.

The verse reveals a tragic irony. The very luxuries they fully enjoyed became a barrier to their faith and, ultimately, a witness against them. The intensity of their enjoyment made the idea of leaving it for a life of submission and morality seem absurd to them. But the enjoyment was a loan, not a gift. When the time came, they had to walk away from it all, not just their gardens and palaces, but every sweet taste and every comfortable sensation. The lesson is deeply relevant. It is not a sin to enjoy the permissible good things of this world, but to make that enjoyment the ultimate purpose of life is a fatal error. When pleasure becomes the compass, the truth becomes an enemy, and the end is the sudden, eternal loss of both the pleasure and the soul that worshipped it.

=28=

This verse delivers the final, decisive seal on the story of Pharaoh’s people with a divine pronouncement. The phrase "So it was" translates "kadhalik," meaning "thus" or "like that," confirming that the destruction described was a settled, executed matter. It is the definitive closing of the history book on the tyrants. The event is framed as an established divine pattern, not a random catastrophe.

The second half of the verse reveals what happened to the vast wealth catalogued in the previous verses: "And We awarded it all to another people." The word "awrathna," "We made inherit," indicates a complete transfer of ownership by divine decree. All the gardens, springs, crops, splendid residences, and luxuries were given to a different nation. Scholars of tafsir explain that this refers to the Children of Israel, who, after their wandering in the wilderness, were eventually established in lands and given the spoils of other civilizations. Some also see it as a reference to other groups, but the core lesson remains the same. The Pharaohs toiled to build an empire, believing it was for their own eternal glory and the glory of their descendants. In reality, they were just temporary caretakers, working for the eventual benefit of a people they despised and enslaved.

This is the ultimate exposure of the illusion of ownership. No human being truly owns anything; Allah is the sole Owner of the heavens and the earth, and He gives the inheritance of this world to whomever He wills. This was a direct message to the arrogant Quraysh. They looked at their caravans, their trade routes, and their control of the Kaaba and felt secure. This verse tells them that the God who transferred the empire of Pharaoh to the Children of Israel can just as easily transfer their wealth, their leadership, and their sacred precincts to the believing community of Muslims, which is exactly what eventually happened. The verse instills in the believer a sense of detachment from worldly goods and a firm belief that the earth belongs to Allah and is inherited by His righteous servants.

=29=

After detailing the immense material loss of the tyrants, this verse unveils a far more profound and chilling truth: their absolute spiritual and moral bankruptcy was so complete that the entire cosmos was indifferent to their destruction. The statement "Neither heaven nor earth wept over them" is a powerful metaphor that resonates with the ancient Semitic concept of cosmic mourning when a great and beloved figure dies. The idea that the heavens and the earth would shed tears for a person or a nation signifies that their life was of such immense value, their presence a source of such goodness and blessing, that the very world grieves their departure. For a believer who worships, remembers, and repents to Allah, their place of prayer and the very ground they walk on are said to miss them.

Yet for Pharaoh and his army, not a single tear was shed by the sky that had given them air to breathe, or the earth that had given them soil to walk on. They lived as a burden and a corruption upon the world, and their removal was a relief.

The second clause, "nor was their fate delayed," underscores the swift, decisive nature of their end. The Arabic "wa ma kanoo munzareen" means they were not granted any respite or postponement. When the divine decree for their destruction came, it came with immediate, terrifying precision. There was no final chance, no extension of time. This stands in stark contrast to the merciful respites given to people who might still repent. For the people of Pharaoh, the window of mercy had definitively closed. This verse serves as a terrifying wake-up call for every human being. We should live in such a way that our existence is a blessing to the world around us, that our prayers and good deeds make the earth grateful to its Lord. A life lived for tyranny, arrogance, and rejection of truth is a life so worthless that even the inanimate creation is glad to see it go.

=30=

The narrative now shifts focus from the destruction of the oppressors to the salvation of the oppressed, highlighting the other side of divine justice. The verse begins with powerful emphasis: "And We certainly delivered.

" The Arabic "laqad najjayna" uses multiple particles to stress the certainty and completeness of this deliverance. It is a settled historical fact, a pillar of divine mercy that the believers should recall with gratitude and faith. The ones delivered are "the Children of Israel," the very servants of Allah whom Moses had demanded Pharaoh release. The thing they were saved from is described as "the humiliating torment." This phrase, "al-adhab al-muheen," is extremely precise and deeply revealing. The torment was not just physically painful; it was specifically "humiliating," designed to degrade, dehumanize, and break the spirit. The torment imposed by Pharaoh included the mass slaughter of their infant sons, the use of their adult men in brutal slave labor, and a systemic campaign to erase their identity and dignity as a free people created by God. An "adhab" that is merely painful can be endured by a proud spirit, but a "muheen" torment aims to crush the soul itself. Allah's deliverance, therefore, was not just about saving bodies from death; it was about restoring dignity and freeing spirits from the shackles of humiliation. This delivers a powerful message to all oppressed believers. Even when the tyrant holds all worldly power and seems to have total control over your body and your circumstances, your salvation is in the hands of the One who controls the heart of the tyrant. The deliverance of the Children of Israel was a miracle that no human strategy could have achieved. It was a direct intervention from the Lord of the worlds to replace humiliating torment with honorable freedom. This verse served as an anchor of hope for the persecuted Muslims in Mecca, assuring them that the humiliating torment they were suffering at the hands of the Quraysh was seen by the same God who had seen the suffering of the Israelites, and His promise of deliverance is always true.

=31=

This verse identifies the architect of that humiliating torment by name, title, and devastating description. The deliverance was specifically "of Pharaoh," the title of the kings of ancient Egypt. By naming him directly, the Quran transforms the Pharaoh from a distant historical figure into a timeless archetype of evil. He is not just a dead king but the living symbol of every ruler and every system that sets itself up against Allah's command. The description that follows is a concise, two-part diagnosis of his spiritual disease. First, he is described as "truly a tyrant" or "aaliyan," meaning someone who is high, arrogant, and domineering. This was the root of his public crime. He had elevated himself above the station of a created human being, even making the infamous claim, "I am your lord, most high. " His tyranny was the natural political expression of his inner arrogance. Second, he is described as "a transgressor" or "musrifeen," from the word "israf," which means to exceed all bounds. This refers to his personal and moral corruption. He was not just a political oppressor; he was a man who had lost all restraint in sin, luxury, and the shedding of blood. The two characteristics are linked. A heart that transgresses the limits of morality will inevitably seek to dominate and tyrannize others, and a person who places himself above other humans will inevitably transgress all limits, because he recognizes no external law. This dual diagnosis is critical because it shows that the tyranny of Pharaoh was not an isolated, inexplicable evil but a predictable outcome of a heart devoid of faith and humility. The warning to the Meccan leaders, especially Abu Jahl, was crystal clear. The Quran is holding up a mirror. When you mock the messenger, plot to kill him, and oppress the weak believers, you are walking in the footsteps of Pharaoh, a tyrant and a transgressor, and you should learn from what happened to him.

=32=

Having described their deliverance, the Quran now reveals the exalted status that the Children of Israel were granted after their salvation. The verse begins with an emphasis: "And indeed, We chose the Israelites.

" The word "chose" or "ikhtarna" implies a deliberate, divine selection for a special purpose and honor. This choice was not based on their numerical strength, their wealth, or their worldly accomplishments; it was a pure favor and a divine trust. The purpose of this choice was to make them the bearers of the divine message and the torchbearers of monotheism in a world drowning in idolatry and tyranny for a specific period of history.

The verse ends with a crucial qualifier: "knowingly" or "alaa 'ilm." This means Allah's choice was based on His perfect, foreknowing wisdom. He knew the generations of the Israelites, their strengths and weaknesses, and He knew exactly which of them would live up to the trust and which would betray it.

This choice is declared "above the others" of their time, meaning Allah elevated them over the other nations of their era, granting them prophets, scripture, and miraculous sustenance. However, this verse has often been misunderstood. The honor given was a heavy responsibility, not a certificate of unconditional racial superiority. It was contingent upon their upholding of the covenant. When they later broke the covenant, rejected and killed prophets, and distorted the scripture, they lost that position of honor and were replaced by the final, universal Muslim Ummah. The verse served as a reminder to the Meccans that just as Allah once chose a formerly enslaved and weak people to become a great nation of faith, He could and was now choosing the believers from among them to carry the final message. It also taught the Muslims the proper perspective: honor comes from Allah alone and is tied to the faithful carrying of His message, not to ethnic pride or worldly status.

=33=

This verse continues the account of the blessings bestowed upon the Children of Israel but introduces the concept of the test that accompanied those blessings. The phrase "We showed them signs" refers to the numerous, tangible miracles they witnessed with their own eyes. These signs were not just the nine plagues sent upon Egypt but also the parting of the sea, the gushing of water from the rock, the shading by clouds in the desert, and the sending down of manna and salwa (the heavenly food). These were clear, undeniable proofs of Allah's power and care for them.

The verse then reveals the true nature of these signs: they contained "a clear test." The Arabic word is "balaa'," which can mean both a test and a trial. The signs were a blessing, but they were also a profound test of gratitude and obedience.

The question was: having been delivered from humiliation, chosen for a divine mission, and shown miracle after miracle, would they be faithful to their covenant, or would they, as history records, fall into the worship of a golden calf at the very foot of Mount Sinai? The signs were clear and the path was obvious, but the test was in their free-willed response. This verse serves as a universal principle. Allah's blessings are not merely gifts to be enjoyed; they are always a test. When Allah grants a person or a community knowledge, wealth, miraculous deliverance, or spiritual insight, it is an intensified examination. With every great blessing comes a greater responsibility and a sharper accountability. This was a subtle warning to the Muslim Ummah and to the Meccan pagans who would later embrace Islam. The Muslims are the recipients of the greatest sign, the Quran itself. The test is whether they will be grateful and obedient, or follow the path of those before them who took the signs for granted and failed. The history of the Israelites is a living parable, written not for their honor alone but as a permanent lesson for all who come after.

=34=

With this verse, the surah makes a sharp turn from the historical narrative back to the present reality of the Prophet’s time in Mecca. The word "Indeed" connects the lessons of the past directly to the statements of the contemporary disbelievers.

The phrase "these Meccans say" translates "inna ha'ula'i la-yaquloon," which has a tone of rebuke and amazement. After all the evidence of creation, the narration of the clear Book, the warning of the haze, and the terrifying historical example of Pharaoh, this is the response of the Meccan elite? The verse acts as an exposé. It’s as if Allah is saying: Look at these people, who have heard all of this, who travel past the ruins of destroyed nations, and who witness the signs in their own souls and in the universe, and yet, in the face of all that, here is what they have the audacity to say. This sets up a profound contrast. Moses, a noble messenger, faced the arrogant denial of Pharaoh, a mighty tyrant. Muhammad, a noble messenger, faces the equally arrogant denial of the Meccan chieftains. The historical parallel is complete. The very next verses will quote their specific arguments, which reveal the core of their disbelief: a denial of the Afterlife. This structural move in the surah is a masterclass in divine argumentation. It silences the listener’s potential objection that "our situation is different" by showing that it is, in fact, exactly the same. The same root disease of arrogance, the same empty amusements, and the same flimsy arguments against resurrection that were destroyed before are being recycled. The verse builds suspense and calls the audience to lean in and listen to the hollow arguments that will be systematically demolished in the following verses.

=35=

The Meccan disbelievers' core argument is finally revealed, and it is a flat, absolute denial of the Afterlife. Their statement is presented in two emphatic parts. First, "There is nothing beyond our first death.

" This is a denial of any existence, consciousness, or judgment after the body dies. In their materialistic worldview, death is a simple, biological cessation. The soul does not survive, there is no intermediate realm, no grave, no accounting. "Our first death" implies that the life they are living now is the only life there is.

The second part is a consequence of the first: "and we will never be resurrected." The Arabic uses a strong negation particle to express their absolute conviction that they will not be brought forth again. For them, resurrection is not just unlikely; it is a physical impossibility, a myth from old legends.

This statement gets to the very heart of their rejection of the Prophet's message. Why did they not want to believe? Because belief in the Resurrection necessarily entails belief in accountability. If they truly believed they would be raised and judged for their deeds—their oppression of the weak, their usury, their burying of infant daughters, their worship of idols—it would demand a complete moral overhaul of their lives, their society, and their elite privileges. It is far easier to deny the Judgment Day than to prepare for it. This materialist denial is a timeless human phenomenon. The wealthy and powerful often love the idea that this life is all there is, because it frees them from ultimate responsibility. The verse exposes this psychology brilliantly. Their stated intellectual objection is a mask for a deep-seated moral rebellion. They do not want resurrection to be true, so they declare it to be false, and then seek arguments to justify that desire.

=36=

This verse presents the sarcastic, dismissive demand the Meccans made in an attempt to mock the Prophet and his message of resurrection. It is a direct challenge: "Bring back our forefathers, if what you say is true." The logic they employ is a misguided, superficial one. They argue that if God is truly capable of raising all humanity on a future Day of Judgment, then He should be able to perform a micro-demonstration right here and now by bringing their dead ancestors back from their graves. They imagined this was a clever trap. If the Prophet could not do it, he was a liar. If he could do it, they might deign to believe. This demand reveals several layers of their ignorance. First, it shows their fundamental misunderstanding of the Day of Judgment. The Resurrection is not a cheap magic show performed on demand for the amusement of skeptics. It is a cosmic, pre-ordained event on a day known only to Allah, the "Day of Final Decision," which will happen at its appointed time. Second, their demand is made in bad faith. Even if their forefathers were brought back and testified to the truth of the resurrection, the Quran tells us they would simply find another excuse to disbelieve. Their hearts have a disease that evidence cannot cure. Third, the argument from their forefathers is an appeal to a false authority. They were clinging to the ways of their dead ancestors, who also lived in polytheism and ignorance, as justification for their own disbelief. The demand is not a search for truth but an attempt to put God on trial.

This verse teaches the believer a critical lesson about engaging with certain types of skeptics. When people make bad-faith demands for customized miracles while ignoring the vast, clear signs already all around them, the problem is not a lack of evidence but an unwillingness to submit. The response will come in the next verse, not by fulfilling their childish demand, but by pointing them to the historical evidence of nations stronger than them who were destroyed.

=37=

The Quran responds to the arrogant Meccans not by answering their demand for their forefathers to be brought back, but by turning the tables and questioning their own sense of strength and superiority. The rhetorical question, "Are they superior to the people of Tubba' and those before them?" is a devastating blow to their pride. Tubba' refers to the kings of the ancient Himyarite civilization in Yemen, a powerful and wealthy kingdom known for its advanced irrigation systems, like the famous Marib Dam. The Quraysh were well-acquainted with the trade routes to Yemen and knew the ruined remains of that once-great civilization.

The question implies: you Meccans, with your relatively small city and tribal rivalries, do you consider yourselves stronger, richer, or more firmly established in the land than the mighty empire of the Tubba' people? The answer is an obvious no.

The second part of the verse delivers the cold, hard conclusion: "We destroyed them all, for they were truly wicked." The phrase "We destroyed them" sweeps away the Tubba' and every other powerful nation before them. Their engineering marvels, their formidable armies, and their vast wealth could not save them when their record of wickedness was full and divine punishment arrived. The reason for their destruction is stated plainly: they were "mujrimeen," deeply entrenched in criminal sin.

This is the same word Moses used for Pharaoh's people. The argument is airtight and deeply logical. If destruction for wickedness is the established, observable pattern of history, as seen in the ruins all around you, then it is not your forefathers being brought back that you need to worry about. It is your own impending destruction if you continue in the same wickedness. The verse dismisses their mocking request for a sign and instead gives them a sign they see every day: the empty remains of nations who were stronger than them. History is the witness, and its judgment is terrifyingly clear.

=38=

After refuting their historical arguments, the Quran now presents the fundamental, rational, and universal proof for the reality of the Day of Judgment. It is an argument rooted in the very purpose of creation.

The verse begins with a powerful negation: "We did not create the heavens and the earth and everything in between for sport." This is a declaration of profound cosmic purposefulness. The vastness of the universe—the billions of galaxies, the precise orbits of planets, the intricate balance of ecosystems, and the miracle of human consciousness—is not a random game, a meaningless joke, or an amusement built by a bored Creator.

The word "laa'ibeen" implies play and frivolity, actions without serious purpose. The majesty and perfection of the Creator are utterly incompatible with such a notion.

This is a foundational Quranic argument. The existence of a wise, powerful, and purposeful Creator logically demands that His creation also has a profound purpose. The human sense of justice, which cries out that good should be rewarded and evil punished, is not an illusion or a chemical reaction in the brain. It is a genuine signal placed within us by our Creator, pointing to a reality where this justice will be fully and finally realized. A world where the tyrant dies in his bed after a life of oppression and the innocent victim dies in agony with no justice is a world that is, on a cosmic scale, incomplete and meaningless if it all ends in the grave. The existence of the heavens and the earth as a deliberate, structured system is the first piece of evidence. The conclusion, which will be made explicit in the next verse, is that a purpose must exist, and that purpose requires a final judgment. The verse dismantles the materialist worldview that sees life as a cosmic accident with no beginning purpose and no end goal.

=39=

Having negated the idea of purposeless creation, this verse now states the positive, explicit truth. "We only created them for a purpose" translates the profound phrase "illa bil-haqq," which carries a cluster of interconnected meanings: with truth, for a just purpose, and in accordance with ultimate reality. The creation of the heavens and the earth is fundamentally tied to "Haqq"—truth and justice. The universe is a manifestation of the truth of Allah’s existence, power, and wisdom. It was created so that this truth could be known, witnessed, and responded to. Human beings, as the morally responsible agents within this creation, were placed here to be tested on their recognition of this truth. Will they use their God-given intellect and free will to recognize their Creator and worship Him, or will they live as if the universe were a meaningless accident? This test requires that there be a Day of Judgment where the results are announced. Without the recompense, the test is incomplete. So, the creation for a purpose and the inevitability of the Hereafter are two sides of the same coin.

The verse then ends with a somber note of realism: "but most of these pagans do not know." Their ignorance is not a lack of raw intelligence. They were sharp traders, eloquent poets, and skilled astronomers. But they are blind to this supreme, foundational truth. Their knowledge is fragmented, confined to the material world, and willfully shut off from its spiritual and ultimate purpose. This "not knowing" is the tragic root of their denial. They are like a person who spends their whole life meticulously studying the frame of a painting, discussing the quality of the wood and the nails, but never stepping back to look at the masterful, meaningful picture it contains. The verse is a call to move from superficial knowledge of the world to a deep, holistic knowledge of its Creator and purpose.

This verse is the climax of the logical argument that has been building. If creation is for a purpose and not for sport, then there must be a culmination point where that purpose is realized and justice is established. That point is given a powerful and intimidating name: "the Day of Final Decision," or "Yawm al-Fasl.

" The word "Fasl" means separation, judgment, and decisive settlement. It is the day when truth will be irrevocably separated from falsehood, the believer from the disbeliever, the oppressed from the oppressor, and the people of Paradise from the people of Hell. All the ambiguous, tangled, and unresolved injustices of the world will be cut through with the sharp blade of divine judgment.

The verse then declares that this day is "the time appointed for all," or "meeqaatuhum ajma'een." This means it is a fixed, predetermined appointment in Allah's knowledge from which no human being, from the first to the last, can be absent or excused.

The word "meeqaat" implies a precise time and place set by a supreme authority. Just as the sun and moon run on appointed courses, the entire human story runs towards its appointed final meeting with its Lord.

This is the definitive answer to the Meccans' denial of resurrection. Their denial does not alter the reality of the appointment. The Day of Decision is not a theoretical possibility; it is a "surely" event, a fixed appointment as certain as the arrival of dawn.

For the believer, this verse fills the heart with both awe and hope. It gives meaning to every sacrifice and patience in the face of injustice, knowing that the Day of Final Decision will bring perfect redress. For the one lost in amusement and doubt, it is a dire warning that the clock is ticking towards a meeting they cannot escape and a decision they cannot appeal.
