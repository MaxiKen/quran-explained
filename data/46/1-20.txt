=1=

The surah opens with the two Arabic letters Ḥâ and Mĩm, known among the disconnected letters that appear at the beginning of twenty-nine chapters of the Quran. Their precise meaning is a matter of the unseen, and the soundest position of the early scholars is that Allah alone knows their full reality.

Yet their placement is far from meaningless. These letters immediately seize the attention of the listener, especially an Arab of the Prophet’s time who recognised that the very letters of his own alphabet were being used to compose a Book unlike any human speech. The letters serve as a direct challenge: this Quran is built from the same letters you use in your daily speech, so if you doubt its divine origin, produce something like it. What follows after these letters typically glorifies the Quran itself or declares its source, and that pattern continues here. The presence of Ḥâ-Mĩm also connects this surah to a family of seven consecutive chapters, all beginning with these same two letters, known as the Ḥawâmîm. They were revealed in Mecca during a period of intense opposition, and they share common themes: the truth of revelation, the folly of idolatry, the certainty of resurrection, and the consolation of the Prophet. Opening with Ḥâ-Mĩm reminds the believer that behind the familiar letters lies a divine speech that contains guidance, warning, and mercy, and that the human mind is being drawn into an encounter with the transcendent. The abruptness of the letters, followed by a declaration about the Book, also mirrors how divine revelation broke into human history: sudden, undeniable, and demanding a response. So the very first verse, though consisting of only two letters, is a profound gateway into the message that will unfold, alerting the heart to the weight of what it is about to hear.

=2=

Having captured attention with the disconnected letters, the surah immediately clarifies the source of the Quran: its revelation, or tanzîl, is decisively from Allah. The Arabic word tanzîl conveys the idea of a gradual sending down from on high, highlighting that this Book did not originate in the mind of the Prophet but descended from the Lord of the worlds. Allah is named here by two majestic attributes: al-ʿAzîz, the Almighty, and al-Ḥakîm, the All-Wise.

The pairing of might and wisdom is crucial because it reassures the listener that the revelation carries both irresistible authority and perfect purpose. The Almighty does not send down speech out of need or weakness, and the All-Wise does not reveal guidance that is flawed or futile. For the Meccan idolaters who were being asked to abandon the gods of their forefathers, this verse announces that the call to monotheism is backed by the power and wisdom of the One who controls everything. It also comforts the Prophet, who faced rejection and accusations of fabrication; the Book he recites is not his own, but from the Almighty who will protect it and the All-Wise who knows why it must meet resistance before it triumphs. This opening verse of substance, then, roots the entire surah in the reality of divine authorship, making clear that to reject the Quran is to reject the authority and wisdom of Allah Himself. It also subtly prepares the heart for the themes ahead: a powerful and wise God creates with purpose, commands justice, sends messengers, holds people accountable, and is fully able to resurrect the dead. All of these ideas will flow naturally from the foundational claim that the revelation of this Book is from Allah, the Almighty, All-Wise.

=3=

The verse begins with a definitive statement that the entire universe—the heavens, the earth, and all that exists between them—was not created in vain or as a passing sport. The words “for a purpose” translate the Arabic bil-ḥaqq, which means with truth, justice, and a definitive reason. Creation is therefore an expression of divine wisdom and not a purposeless accident. Adding “and an appointed term” underscores that this cosmic order has a fixed duration; it will one day come to an end, and with that end comes the fulfilment of every purpose for which it was made. This directly refutes both the materialist who thinks life ends with death and the idolater who imagines that the world runs by the whims of false gods. When Allah says “We only created,” it establishes the exclusive creative act of the Almighty, leaving no room for partners.

Yet despite this unmistakable truth, the disbelievers are turning away from what they have been warned about. The warning encompasses the consequences of rejecting tawhid, the reality of resurrection, and the certainty of judgement. Their turning away is not due to lack of evidence; creation itself is a vast, visible sign pointing to its Creator. Rather, it is a stubborn refusal born of arrogance, attachment to ancestral ways, and immersion in worldly desires. The verse, then, frames the entire human drama: a universe full of purpose, a limited lifespan for reflection and action, and a divine warning that many heedlessly ignore. The believer reads this and is reminded to live intentionally, to see every moment as meaningful, and to stay aligned with the purpose for which the heavens and earth themselves were brought into being.

=4=

The Prophet is instructed to pose a direct challenge to the idolaters, forcing them to examine the foundations of their worship. The rhetorical question “Have you considered whatever idols you invoke besides Allah?” demands that they reflect on the beings and objects they call upon in prayer and distress. The challenge then narrows to the ultimate proof of divinity: creation. “Show me what they have created on earth!” is devastating because it highlights the absolute impotence of idols. They have not brought forth a single grain of sand, a leaf, or a drop of water. The follow-up question, “Or do they have a share in the creation of the heavens?” pushes further, asking whether these false gods participated in the grand cosmic order. The heavens, in their vastness and precision, are an even more undeniable testimony to Allah’s exclusive creative power. After the argument from creation comes the argument from revelation. The Prophet is told to say, “Bring me a scripture revealed before this Quran or a shred of knowledge, if what you say is true.” The idolaters cannot produce any earlier divine book that sanctions their polytheism, nor can they offer any remnant of sound, transmitted knowledge that justifies idol worship.

The phrase “a shred of knowledge” translates athâratin min ʿilmin, meaning even a trace of reliable learning. The entire verse dismantles idolatry rationally: if your gods create nothing, own nothing in the heavens, and have never sent a single message commanding their worship, then what basis remains for your devotion? It is an invitation to use the intellect that Allah Himself bestowed, and it exposes idolatry as a tradition devoid of any evidence, built only on inherited assumption.

=5=

This verse builds upon the challenge of the previous one by painting a stark picture of the spiritual condition of the idolater. The question “And who could be more astray” is a rhetorical device implying that no one is further from the truth than such a person. The objects of their supplication are described as utterly unresponsive: they cannot respond to them until the Day of Judgment. That includes wooden idols, stone statues, angels, prophets, or saints whom they call upon besides Allah. Even if the supplicated beings are righteous servants of God who will be honoured in the Hereafter, they are completely unable to hear, let alone answer, any prayer in this world.

The phrase “and are even unaware of their calls” deepens the tragedy. Those being invoked have no knowledge that anyone is calling upon them. They are absent, lifeless, or occupied with their own devotion to Allah. The worshiper cries out in need, in fear, in hope, but the one called upon remains in total ignorance. This exposes the irrationality of idolatry: it attaches the heart to what has no awareness, no power, and no capacity to benefit or harm.

The verse also implicitly contrasts these false objects of worship with Allah, who is ever near, fully aware, and always able to respond. A person who pours out his soul before a deaf and ignorant object has lost the very purpose of his existence, while one who turns to the All-Hearing, All-Knowing Creator finds his call heard and answered according to divine wisdom. The practical lesson is that human desperation needs a responsive God, and only Allah fulfills that need.

=6=

The scene shifts to the Day of Resurrection, when the true nature of idolatrous relationships will be exposed. The verse states that when people are gathered for judgement, the very gods they used to invoke will become their enemies and will disown their worship.

This is a profound reversal of the intimate devotion the idolaters showed in the world. The beings they called upon—whether idols, false deities, or even righteous figures who were wrongly deified—will declare themselves innocent of the worship that was directed to them. Many passages in the Quran describe this disowning: the worshipped will say they never commanded such worship, and the worshipers will be left in shame and regret.

The verse reveals that idolatry is not a neutral mistake; it creates a relationship of enmity on the day when every soul desperately needs an intercessor and ally. Those who invested their love, fear, and hope in false gods will find those same gods standing against them as witnesses to their misguidance. This outcome is the logical consequence of the earlier verses: if the idols cannot create, cannot hear, and cannot respond, then in the Hereafter they will certainly not defend. The Meccan pagans were warned that their ancestral deities, far from bringing them closer to Allah, would become a source of ruin. The believer, reflecting on this verse, understands the supreme importance of purifying worship for Allah alone, because every attachment that takes the place of Allah in the heart will eventually turn into a source of pain and separation on the Day when all veils are lifted.

=7=

The surah now turns to the Meccan response to the Quranic message itself. The phrase “Whenever Our clear revelations are recited to them” indicates persistence: repeatedly, as the Prophet recited the verses, the disbelievers heard the unmistakable signs of divine speech. These are called “clear revelations” because they are lucid, self-evident, and distinguished from human speech.

Yet the disbelievers, rather than contemplating their clarity, immediately label them “pure magic. ” The word used for magic, siḥr, implies a spell that captivates and deceives. By calling the Quran magic, they simultaneously acknowledged its extraordinary power over their hearts while denying its divine source. They could not dismiss the Quran as poetry, soothsaying, or mere stories, so they resorted to an accusation that robbed it of any moral authority.

The phrase “of the truth when it has come to them” exposes the tragedy: truth itself has arrived, clear and undeniable, but they reject it with a label that requires no thinking. It is an emotional defence mechanism to avoid submission. This verse echoes the experience of earlier prophets, whose miracles were called magic by stubborn disbelievers. It also consoles the Prophet that the rejection he faces is not due to any shortcoming in the message, but due to a spiritual disease in the hearers.

The verse teaches that the truth can be manifestly clear and yet still be refused by hearts that are unwilling to relinquish pride, desire, or ancestral tradition. The response required from a sincere person is humble listening and fair reflection, not a preemptive dismissal.

=8=

Another accusation of the disbelievers is that the Prophet himself invented the Quran. The verse begins with “Or do they say,” linking it to the previous slander of magic, showing that the disbelievers fluctuated between contradictory accusations: sometimes magic, sometimes fabrication. The Prophet is instructed to reply with calm and rational argument. He says that if he had indeed fabricated it and falsely attributed it to Allah, then the people could offer him no protection from divine punishment. This shifts the focus from human debate to divine accountability. The Prophet is not seeking their approval; he fears Allah, who knows the truth of the matter fully.

The phrase “He knows best what slurs you indulge about it” acknowledges that the disbelievers are engaging in sustained verbal attacks, but that Allah is aware of every word. “Sufficient is He as a Witness between you and me” means that the ultimate judgement is in Allah’s hands; He will decide who is truthful and who is lying. The verse closes with two beautiful divine names: the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

This is a subtle invitation even for those who slander the Quran. If they would turn from these accusations, repent sincerely, and embrace the truth, they would find Allah’s forgiveness and mercy wide open. So the response to the charge of fabrication is neither anger nor despair; it is a dignified entrustment of the matter to Allah, combined with a window of hope for the accusers. It also underscores that the Prophet’s mission is not a personal project; he stands under the authority of the One who sent him, and his sole concern is fulfilling the trust.

=9=

The Prophet is commanded to further clarify his human nature and the limits of his knowledge, cutting off any exaggerated claims that might later be made about him. Saying “I am not the first messenger ever sent” means his coming is part of a long chain of prophethood; he is not a novelty, and his message is the same core truth brought by Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus. This also implies that the rejection he faces is not unique; previous messengers were similarly accused and denied. The statement “nor do I know what will happen to me or you” is a profound admission of human limitation. The Prophet does not possess independent knowledge of the unseen, of future events, or of his own ultimate fate except what Allah reveals. This reinforces absolute reliance on Allah and shatters any notion that the Prophet is a fortune-teller or a divine being. “I only follow what is revealed to me” defines his mission precisely: he is a follower, not an innovator. The revelation guides his actions, his decisions, and his teachings. The conclusion, “And I am only sent with a clear warning,” sums up his role: a warner whose message is unambiguous, leaving no one in ignorance about the consequences of disbelief and disobedience. The verse instills humility in the believer regarding the Prophet: he is honoured as the final messenger, but he is a servant who follows revelation, not an independent source of knowledge. It also invites the disbelievers to assess the message on its own merit rather than demanding from the Prophet what only Allah can provide.

=10=

The Prophet is told to present a hypothetical but weighty scenario to the Meccan pagans. The verse asks them to reflect on the possibility that the Quran genuinely originates from Allah while they obstinately deny it. The situation is made more serious by mentioning “a witness from the Children of Israel” who attests to the truth of the Quran and believes. This witness is understood by many commentators to be someone knowledgeable in the previous scriptures who recognises the signs of true prophethood described in the Torah and the Gospel and then accepts Islam. The Meccans respected the People of the Book and sometimes consulted them on religious matters, so the testimony of such a witness should have carried weight.

Yet their arrogance prevents them from even considering the evidence. The contrast is sharp: a learned person from a scriptural tradition embraces the truth, while the Quraysh, who prided themselves on their nobility, reject it out of pride.

The phrase “whereas you act arrogantly” pinpoints the root disease: not lack of proof, but arrogance. The verse concludes with a statement of divine principle: “Surely Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people.” Those who persist in wrongdoing, including the arrogance that rejects truth, cut themselves off from divine guidance.

This is not because Allah is unwilling to guide, but because their moral posture makes them unreceptive to the light. The lesson is that humility and openness are prerequisites for guidance, and that even the most unlikely people can become believers when their hearts are sincere.

=11=

This verse captures the social arrogance of the Quraysh leaders when they saw the weak and the poor among the early Muslims accepting Islam. They looked at the likes of Bilâl, ʿAmmâr, and Suhayb and said, “Had it been something good, they would not have beaten us to it.” In their minds, truth should first be recognised by the elite, the wealthy, and the powerful; that the downtrodden embraced the message was, to them, proof of its falsehood.

This is a classic case of judging truth by the social status of its followers rather than by its intrinsic merit. The Quran exposes this mindset and then describes their next logical step: since they have already rejected its guidance based on pride, they now need to justify their rejection. So they follow up with a dismissal, calling it “an ancient fabrication.

” This is contradictory: earlier they claimed the weak beat them to it, suggesting it was a new movement, but now they label it ancient and therefore irrelevant. The inconsistency shows that the rejection is not based on reasoned evaluation but on a stubborn desire to find any label that wards off the call. The verse serves as a warning against the elitism that blinds people to truth. It also comforts the believers that their social standing in the world does not determine their worth before Allah; sincerity, not status, is the criterion.

=12=

The surah now connects the Quran to the earlier revelation given to Moses, the Torah. By mentioning that the Book of Moses was a guide and mercy, the verse establishes a continuity of divine guidance. The Torah was a light for the Children of Israel, and now the Quran comes as a confirmation of the truths that were revealed before.

The phrase “in the Arabic tongue” highlights that this final scripture is in a clear, accessible language for its primary audience, the Arabs. This confirms that divine mercy is not confined to one nation; it flows in the language people understand.

The verse then spells out two functions of the Quran: to warn those who do wrong, and to give good news to those who do good. The warning is directed at the wrongdoers, the idolaters, and the arrogant who persist in sin, while the glad tidings are for the muhsinîn, those who excel in faith and righteous deeds. This dual function permeates the entire Quran, balancing fear and hope. The Meccans, who were familiar with the concept of the Torah and respected Moses, are being told that what the Prophet brings is not a strange new doctrine but a reaffirmation and completion of the same prophetic tradition.

Thus rejecting the Quran is rejecting a message that is essentially the same as the one they claimed to respect in the Torah. The verse reassures the believers that they are part of a long history of guided communities, and it invites the disbelievers to reconsider their stance in light of the clear, Arabic confirmation they now hold.

=13=

After describing the rejection of the disbelievers, the surah turns to a beautiful portrait of the faithful. The verse defines true believers as those who declare, with full conviction, that their Lord is Allah, and then istaqâmû—they remain steadfast. This single word, istaqâmû, carries immense weight. It means they hold firmly to that declaration of monotheism, letting it shape their beliefs, character, actions, and entire life direction. They do not waver with every trial; they do not mix pure tawhid with hidden shirk; they persist in obedience through ease and hardship. The reward for such steadfastness is described in deeply comforting terms: “there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.” Fear relates to what lies ahead, and grief relates to what has passed. So they are safe from terror in the future—the horrors of the grave, the Day of Judgement, and the Fire—and they will not sorrow over missed opportunities or past losses.

This promise echoes the Quranic description of the allies of Allah. The verse is a powerful reminder that the path of faith is not merely a momentary emotion or a verbal utterance; it is a lifelong commitment to stability upon the truth. The absence of fear and grief is the ultimate peace that every human soul seeks, and it is found only in the sincere and sustained recognition that Allah is the Lord.

=14=

The promise of the previous verse is now given a concrete, eternal home. Those who combine the testimony of tawhid with steadfastness are described as “the residents of Paradise.

” The word used implies companionship and permanence; they will be the people of the Garden, belonging to it as their true and everlasting abode. The phrase “staying there forever” removes any fear of termination, decay, or eviction. Paradise is not a temporary resort but an eternal realm of delight. The verse explicitly states that this is “a reward for what they used to do,” which clarifies that while salvation is ultimately by Allah’s mercy, deeds are the tangible expression of faith and the basis for degrees of reward. The Quran consistently ties eternal outcomes to earthly actions, not to empty claims. This linkage is an encouragement to the believer to view every act of goodness, every restraint from sin, and every moment of patience as an investment that will bear everlasting fruit. It also counters the fatalistic idea that deeds are irrelevant. The verse completes the portrait of the blessed: they trust in Allah, they live uprightly, and their final destination is a paradise of unimaginable bliss that they will never have to leave. For the early Muslims facing persecution in Mecca, these verses were a source of immense hope, a reminder that the hardship of the moment was nothing compared to the eternal reward.

=15=

The surah now addresses the relationship between children and parents, a theme deeply connected to the gratitude owed to the Creator. Allah commands humanity to treat parents with ihsân—the most excellent conduct. The verse immediately provides the reason by describing the mother’s unique suffering: she bore the child in hardship upon hardship, and then delivered the child in hardship. The accumulation of weakness, pain, and exhaustion is a powerful motivator for gratitude. The period of bearing and weaning is specified as thirty months. Scholars have deduced from this, along with another verse that prescribes two full years for weaning, that the minimum period of gestation is six months.

The verse then transitions to a beautiful picture of a righteous person who, upon reaching full maturity at the age of forty, makes a heartfelt supplication. Forty represents the completion of physical and intellectual strength, a time when life’s experiences should have deepened wisdom. Such a person prays for inspiration to be truly thankful for the favours bestowed upon him and his parents. The prayer includes a request to perform righteous deeds that please Allah, showing that gratitude is not just a feeling but translates into action. The supplicant also asks Allah to instil righteousness in his offspring, demonstrating concern for the spiritual legacy that continues beyond one’s own life. The prayer ends with a declaration of repentance and submission to Allah. The entire verse thus draws a complete circle: the child who was once carried in weakness grows to recognise the favours of his Lord and his parents, and channels that recognition into a life of devotion, goodness, and concern for the next generation.

=16=

Allah now declares the blessed outcome for those described in the previous verse, the righteous children who honour their parents, reach mature reflection, and dedicate themselves to supplication and good deeds. The verse states that it is from these people that Allah will accept the best of their actions.

The phrase “accept the good they did” translates a concept of graceful reception; Allah does not merely note their deeds, He accepts them with pleasure and multiplies their reward. Alongside this acceptance, Allah will overlook their misdeeds. This overlooking means that the sins and shortcomings that inevitably cling to every human being will be passed over, forgiven, and not held against them.

The verse then places these people in the company of the residents of Paradise, indicating that they are counted among the righteous inhabitants of the Garden. The promise they were given is described as “the true promise,” the waʿd al-ṣidq, which is Allah’s unfailing pledge of eternal reward for the believers. The connection to the previous verse is seamless: the one who honours parents, reflects on divine favours, prays for gratitude and righteous deeds, and cares for the faith of his children, will find his record purified and his place in Paradise assured.

This is a message of immense hope for every believer who strives to balance duties to Allah and duties to parents. It also shows that family relationships, when built on faith and righteousness, become a means of drawing nearer to Allah and attaining His pleasure in the Hereafter.

=17=

In stark contrast to the dutiful child, this verse presents the rebellious one who scolds his believing parents when they remind him of resurrection and accountability. His dismissive words, “Enough with you!”, show irritation and disrespect. He argues that the idea of being brought forth from the grave is absurd because countless earlier generations have perished and never returned.

This is a materialist objection rooted in seeing only the physical world and forgetting the power of the Creator. The parents, who are described as crying to Allah for help, are heartbroken. Their plea, “Pity you. Have faith! Surely Allah’s promise is true,” reveals their deep love and desperation. They use the gentlest expression, waylaka, which means ‘woe to you’ or ‘pity you,’ not a harsh curse, but a sorrowful expression of concern. They appeal to the truth of Allah’s promise, but the denier stubbornly insists that such talk is “nothing but ancient fables.” This replicates the exact attitude of the Quraysh disbelievers towards the Quran itself. The verse thus illustrates a tragic family dynamic: the generational divide is not merely about age but about faith versus disbelief. The child’s arrogance has closed his heart to the most sincere advice. This scene serves as a warning that one’s own family may become a test, and that true loyalty is to the truth, even when it pains those we love.

=18=

The verse identifies the arrogant deniers described in the previous verse as those upon whom the word of punishment has become binding, just as it did for earlier nations of jinn and humankind who rejected their messengers. The phrase “the fate” translates al-qawl, the decree or word of punishment that Allah pronounces against persistent rejecters. This decree is not arbitrary; it is “justified” because they followed in the footsteps of the ruined nations, sharing their rebellion, arrogance, and mockery of divine warning.

The mention of both jinn and humans broadens the scope, indicating that accountability and the consequences of disbelief transcend the human realm. The jinn, like humans, have received guidance and will be judged.

The final clause, “for they were truly losers,” gives the reason for their ruin. They are losers in the most absolute sense: they lost their souls, their opportunities, their eternal happiness, and exchanged the truth for falsehood.

This description is a sobering reminder that no one is immune to the patterns that destroyed earlier peoples. If the same attitudes of denial, mockery, and disrespect for divine reminders are present, the same outcome will follow.

The verse also reinforces the Quranic theme that history is not a random sequence of events; it contains lessons for those who reflect. The fate of past nations is not merely a story but a warning that the sunan of Allah, His established ways, do not change.

=19=

After presenting the contrasting portraits of the righteous and the rebellious, the surah now declares the universal principle of divine justice that will be applied on the Day of Judgement. The two groups—the believers and the disbelievers—will be classified into degrees, or ranks, based entirely on their deeds.

The phrase “according to what they have done” underscores that eternal standing is not determined by lineage, wealth, or worldly status, but solely by the quality and sincerity of one’s actions. The purpose of this ranking is “so He may fully reward all,” meaning that every soul will receive exactly what its deeds have earned, down to the smallest atom of good or evil. The statement “and none will be wronged” is a guarantee of perfect justice. No one’s good deed will be diminished, and no one’s evil will be exaggerated. This verse provides a balanced perspective that encourages both hope and fear. The believer strives for excellence, knowing that every effort is recorded and will contribute to his rank in Paradise. The wrongdoer is warned that his deeds are also being recorded and will determine his level of recompense.

The concept of ranks implies that even within Paradise there are levels, and within the Fire there are levels, all assigned with flawless equity. This principle frees the heart from envy and competition over worldly distinctions; what truly matters is the rank one achieves in the sight of Allah through righteous conduct.

=20=

The scene shifts dramatically to the moment when the disbelievers are confronted with the Fire. The word “exposed” translates yuʿraḍûn, conveying a sense of being brought near and made to face the horrifying reality they denied. The address they receive is piercing: they are told that they already exhausted their share of good and pleasant things during their earthly life, and they fully indulged in them. This does not mean that enjoyment itself is condemned; rather, they made worldly pleasure their ultimate goal, forgetting the Hereafter entirely. They consumed their portion of lawful and unlawful delights without any concern for gratitude, accountability, or the rights of others. The consequence is that today they receive the torment of disgrace as a fitting recompense. Two specific sins are highlighted: arrogance throughout the land without any right, and rebelliousness. Arrogance here means treating themselves as superior, rejecting the truth, and oppressing others. “Without any right” emphasises that there is no legitimate basis for such haughtiness. Rebelliousness, or fusûq, refers to persistent departure from obedience to Allah. The torment is called “the torment of disgrace” because it is accompanied by humiliation; just as they sought honour through wrongful pride, they are now covered in shame. This verse serves as a powerful warning that this life is a season of sowing, not just reaping. Those who take their fill now and ignore the coming harvest will find themselves empty-handed and disgraced when it matters most.
