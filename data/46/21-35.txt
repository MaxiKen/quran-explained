=21=

The surah now transitions from the general warning to a specific historical example that the Meccans knew well: the story of the people of ʿȂd and their prophet Hûd. Hûd is tenderly described as “the brother of ʿȂd,” not a stranger or an outsider, but one of their own kin, whose sincerity and concern for them could not be questioned. He was sent to a people who inhabited the sand-hills, al-Aḥqâf, a region of towering dunes in the southern part of the Arabian Peninsula. This geographical detail grounds the story in a real place, reminding the Quraysh that the ruins they passed on their trade journeys were not legends but remnants of a destroyed civilisation.

The verse adds an important parenthetical remark: “there were certainly warners before and after him.” This situates Hûd within a long chain of prophets. He was not the first to deliver the message of tawhid, nor the last, showing that Allah’s guidance has been a continuous mercy to humanity. Hûd’s message is the unchanging core of all prophetic calls: “Worship none but Allah. ” This is the simple and profound declaration that shattered the idolatrous traditions of his people. Then comes the warning: “I truly fear for you the torment of a tremendous day.” Hûd’s fear is not a personal anxiety but a compassionate dread that his people are heading towards a catastrophic punishment if they persist in their denial. The “tremendous day” could refer to the Day of Judgement or a severe worldly punishment, or both, as was the case with ʿȂd who faced a devastating wind in this life, followed by the reckoning in the Hereafter. The Meccans, hearing this, were meant to see themselves mirrored in the people of ʿȂd: prosperous, powerful, and yet rejecting the call of a sincere brother from among them.

=22=

The response of ʿȂd to Hûd mirrors the arrogance of the Quraysh to Prophet Muḥammad. Instead of contemplating the message of pure monotheism, they accused Hûd of having a hidden agenda: “Have you come to turn us away from our gods?” Their attachment to their false deities was so deep that any call to abandon them was perceived as a hostile takeover of their identity and heritage. They could not imagine that someone might genuinely care for their spiritual wellbeing; they assumed a motive of power or manipulation. Then, in a display of defiant pride, they challenged Hûd to bring on the punishment he threatened.

The phrase “Bring us then whatever you threaten us with, if what you say is true” is not a sincere request for proof, but a sarcastic and arrogant dare. They believed that their strength, their impressive buildings, and their advanced civilisation made them immune to any divine retribution. This attitude reveals a fatal misunderstanding of the nature of divine punishment: it is not something humans can summon on demand, nor is it a sign of weakness if it is delayed. The demand to hasten the punishment is a recurring theme with many nations, showing that when people feel secure in their worldly power, they mock warnings and treat the Hereafter as a distant myth. The verse captures the tragic collision between a sincere warner who fears for his people and a people who mistake his compassion for a threat to their comfortable traditions. The same dynamic was unfolding in Mecca, where the leaders dared the Prophet to bring down the stones from the sky if he was truthful.

=23=

Hûd’s reply is a model of prophetic humility, clarity, and firmness. He does not claim to control the punishment or know when it will descend. He states, “The knowledge of its time is only with Allah.

” This is a crucial corrective: the timing and nature of divine punishment belong exclusively to Allah’s wisdom. A messenger is not a distributor of vengeance; he is a deliverer of a message. Hûd then defines his mission precisely: “I only convey to you what I have been sent with.” He has no personal agenda, no ability to alter the message, and no power to bring about the consequences on his own. His duty is delivery, and whether they believe or not, he has fulfilled his responsibility. His final statement, “But I can see that you are a people acting ignorantly,” is not an insult but a diagnosis. Their ignorance is not a lack of intelligence; ʿȂd were materially advanced and skilled. Rather, it is jahl in its Quranic sense: an impulsive, arrogant, and irrational behaviour that springs from passions and pride rather than knowledge and reflection. By demanding punishment and mocking the warner, they were acting against their own best interests, like a patient who abuses the doctor bringing the cure.

This verse teaches that the role of religious guides is to convey the truth with clarity, not to force results or to know the unseen. It also warns that demanding immediate tangible proof while ignoring the clear signs already given is a sign of deep ignorance.

=24=

The scene of the punishment’s arrival is described with dramatic irony. After a period of drought, the people of ʿȂd saw a dense cloud advancing towards their valleys. Their immediate reaction was one of joy and relief: they said happily, “This is a cloud bringing us rain.” Their minds were fixed on their immediate worldly needs, and they interpreted the natural phenomenon through their hopes and desires. They had no inkling that this cloud was not a bearer of mercy but a vessel of destruction. Then Hûd’s voice cuts through their celebration with a terrifying clarification: “No, it is what you sought to hasten: a fierce wind carrying a painful punishment!” The very thing they had mockingly dared him to bring had now materialised, but in a form they did not expect. The cloud was not carrying water droplets but a screaming, violent wind that would uproot everything. This sudden reversal illustrates how divine punishment can come from directions people least anticipate, and how what appears to be a blessing can, for the guilty, be the vehicle of wrath.

The verse also underscores the folly of demanding punishment as a proof; when it comes, it leaves no room for repentance or escape. The irony is painful: they were rejoicing at the sight of their own doom. For the Quraysh, this was a vivid warning that their mockery of the Prophet’s warnings could end in a similarly unexpected catastrophe. The believer is reminded to never feel secure from Allah’s plan and to receive His signs with humility rather than presumptuous interpretations.

=25=

The wind, acting by the command of its Lord, became an instrument of total annihilation. The verse says “It destroyed everything,” meaning every structure, every possession, and every living being in its path.

The phrase “by the command of its Lord” is crucial: the wind did not act as a random natural disaster; it was a soldier from the soldiers of Allah, obedient to His decree. The destruction was so thorough that nothing remained visible except their ruins, their abandoned dwellings that stood as silent testimony to their former strength. The ruins of ʿȂd’s stone-built homes in the region of al-Aḥqâf were known to the Arabs, serving as a perpetual reminder of what happens to those who defy Allah’s messengers. The concluding sentence, “This is how We reward the wicked people,” establishes a universal principle. It is not merely a historical report; it is an ongoing divine law that wickedness and persistent rejection of truth lead to ruin in both this world and the next. This verse serves as a sobering reflection that human strength, no matter how impressive, is nothing before the command of Allah. The wind, which usually brings life through pollination and rain, becomes a destroyer when carrying a divine decree. The same element that gives life can take it, reminding humanity that everything in the universe is a servant of its Creator and will act as He wills.

=26=

The address shifts directly to the Meccan pagans, drawing an explicit comparison between them and the destroyed people of ʿȂd. Allah tells the Quraysh that He had established the ʿȂd with a power and prosperity that far exceeded what the Meccans enjoyed. ʿȂd had greater physical strength, more advanced architecture, wider dominion, and longer lifespans. Despite this, they were destroyed. If a mightier nation could not escape punishment, how could the less powerful Quraysh expect immunity?

The verse then lists the fundamental faculties that should have guided ʿȂd: hearing, sight, and intellect. They could hear the warnings of Hûd, they could see the signs of creation around them, and they had intellects to process and understand the truth.

Yet these faculties were of no benefit whatsoever. The reason is given: they persisted in denying Allah’s signs. The faculties were sound, but the will was corrupted by arrogance and attachment to false tradition. The outcome was that they were overwhelmed—literally surrounded and destroyed—by the very thing they used to ridicule. They had mocked Hûd’s warnings of a painful torment, and that torment descended upon them exactly as described. The same pattern applied to the Quraysh, who were ridiculing the Prophet’s warnings. This verse is a profound lesson about the misuse of God-given faculties. Hearing, sight, and intellect are trusts; when they are employed to reject truth rather than embrace it, they become witnesses against their owners. The tragedy is not in having weak abilities, but in failing to use strong ones for the purpose they were created.

=27=

Allah reminds the Quraysh of a fact they could not deny: the societies that surrounded them in the Arabian Peninsula—ʿȂd, Thamûd, the people of Lot, and Madyan—had all been destroyed. The ruins of these nations were visible along their trade routes, and their stories were known in oral tradition. The destruction was not arbitrary; it came after Allah had “varied the signs.” This means that the proofs and warnings were presented in diverse, repeated, and compelling ways. The messengers came with clear arguments, miracles, and scripture, all designed to touch the heart and intellect. The purpose of this variety was so perhaps they would return, meaning turn back from their disbelief and sin to the Right Path.

The word “perhaps” here does not indicate uncertainty in Allah’s knowledge, but rather expresses the wisdom of leaving the door open for human choice and transformation. The destruction of previous nations was a manifestation of both justice and mercy: justice for the persistent rejecters, and a merciful warning to subsequent generations like the Quraysh. The Meccans were intended to learn from these nearby examples, to see that the very roads they travelled were built over the graves of arrogant empires, and to realise that the same sunan of Allah applied to them. The verse invites reflection on history as a teacher; those who fail to learn from the past are doomed to repeat its fatal mistakes.

=28=

The surah now returns to the central theme of idolatry, asking a devastating question about the destroyed nations and, by extension, the Meccan idolaters. The false gods they had adopted were not always simple pieces of wood; many were beings like angels, jinn, or righteous humans whom they worshipped hoping they would act as intermediaries to bring them closer to Allah.

The phrase “hoping to get closer to Him” exposes the internal justification of idolatry: they knew Allah was the Supreme Creator, but they believed these lesser deities could intercede and draw them near. Yet when divine punishment descended on ʿȂd and others, these supposed intermediaries and gods were nowhere to be found. “Why then did they not come to their aid?” is the crushing question. They vanished, offered no protection, and could not stop the wind or the earthquake. The verse answers plainly: “Instead, they failed them.” They were utterly lost and absent, leaving their worshipers to face destruction alone. The concluding sentence explains the root cause: “That is the result of their lies and their fabrications.” The entire system of polytheism was built on falsehood, on names and concepts they invented without any sanction from Allah. On the day of reckoning, falsehood collapses, and only truth remains. For the Quraysh, who also claimed that their idols were intercessors with Allah, this verse was a direct and urgent warning that their reliance on such fabricated deities would end in the same catastrophic disappointment.

=29=

After the long segment about human rejection, the surah offers a striking contrast in the receptivity of a group of jinn to the Quran. The Prophet is told to remember the occasion when Allah directed a company of jinn towards him during his recitation. According to authentic reports, this occurred when the Prophet was praying or reciting Quran in a valley, and a group of passing jinn stopped to listen. The jinn are a creation made of smokeless fire, accountable to divine commands, and they have believers and disbelievers among them. Upon hearing the Quran, they immediately recognised its divine origin. Their first reaction was to say to one another, “Listen quietly! ” This command shows their profound respect and eagerness to understand. They silenced one another to absorb the recitation without distraction, a model of sincere listening that contrasts sharply with the Quraysh who would make noise to drown out the Quran. When the recitation finished, they did not keep the treasure to themselves; they returned to their community as warners, carrying the message they had just received. They transformed from mere listeners into preachers of truth. This brief narrative carries powerful lessons: the truth can penetrate hearts that human arrogance has closed, the jinn who are unseen showed more humility than the visible human elite, and the Quran is a guidance for all sentient beings. It also comforted the Prophet, showing him that while his own people rejected him, other creatures of Allah were embracing the message in the unseen.

=30=

The content of the jinn’s preaching is recorded in this and the following verses, giving us a rare insight into their call. They addressed their community with a warm and inclusive “O our fellow jinn,” establishing their shared identity and concern. Their testimony begins with a remarkable statement: they heard a scripture revealed after Moses. This indicates that the jinn had some awareness of earlier revelations, particularly the Torah given to Moses, and they recognised that prophethood had continued. The Quran is described as coming “after Moses,” confirming what came before it. This confirmation of earlier scriptures is a major theme of the Quran, which sees itself as the culmination and guardian of the original truths revealed to all prophets. The jinn then declare that this scripture “guides to the truth and the Straight Way.” The truth here means the correct beliefs about Allah, the afterlife, and the purpose of existence. The Straight Way, aṣ-ṣirâṭ al-mustaqîm, is the path of conduct that leads directly to Allah’s pleasure and Paradise. Their words are a concise and accurate summary of the Quran’s function, which the Meccan disbelievers were unable to articulate because of their prejudice. The fact that the jinn—who have no tribal loyalties to the Prophet, no ancestral pride in Arab paganism—could so readily and clearly articulate the message is a devastating indictment of the human disbelievers who had the same Quran recited to them daily yet persisted in calling it magic or ancient fables.

=31=

The jinn continue their passionate call to their people, urging an immediate and positive response. “Respond to the caller of Allah” identifies the Prophet Muḥammad as Allah’s summoner, the one who invites all creatures to their Lord. The command to “respond” implies not just passive listening but active obedience and acceptance. Following this, they say “and believe in him,” meaning to affirm the message the caller brings and to trust in his truthfulness. The jinn then outline the magnificent reward for responding: forgiveness of sins and protection from a painful punishment. Forgiveness deals with the past, wiping clean the record of transgressions, while protection from painful punishment deals with the future, guaranteeing safety from Hellfire.

This is the core promise of the Quranic message, and the jinn present it with beautiful simplicity, without arguing, debating, or making excuses. Their call is an invitation of mercy, not a threat. The fact that jinn, who are generally more hidden from human interaction, would so clearly articulate the gospel of hope exposes the stubbornness of the human disbelievers who had the same invitation and rejected it.

This verse also emphasises the universality of the Prophet’s mission: he is not merely a messenger to the Arabs or to humans, but to all creatures of Allah who are subject to moral responsibility.

=32=

The jinn balance their glad tidings with a clear warning, demonstrating that true preaching must contain both hope and fear. They declare that whoever fails to respond to Allah’s caller will find himself absolutely helpless.

The phrase “will have no escape on earth” means that no matter where such a person flees, he cannot elude Allah’s grasp. The earth, vast as it is, provides no refuge from divine accountability. The addition “nor will they have any protectors against Him” dismantles the entire basis of idolatry, where people took gods as intercessors and protectors from harm. On the Day of Judgement, there will be no allies, no lawyers, no bribes, and no false gods to shield a person from the consequences of his rejection. The jinn conclude with a definitive judgement: “It is they who are clearly astray.” They are not merely slightly mistaken; they are in manifest, self-evident misguidance because they have turned away from the one true source of guidance and protection.

This warning from the jinn would have been deeply unsettling for the Quraysh to hear. A hidden nation of unseen beings has understood the stakes of the Prophet’s message with more clarity than the human elite of Mecca. The verse reinforces the Quranic message that rejection of the call is not a trivial choice; it is a catastrophic error with eternal consequences, leaving a soul utterly alone and exposed in the face of the Almighty.

=33=

The surah returns to the central Meccan objection: the denial of the resurrection. The disbelievers found it inconceivable that decayed bones and scattered dust could be reanimated.

This verse presents a rational argument from the greater to the smaller. It begins with “Do they not realize,” appealing to the intellect that observes the heavens and the earth. The creation of the entire cosmos, with its vast galaxies, intricate laws, and boundless space, is undeniably a greater feat than reassembling human bodies from the earth.

The phrase “and did not tire in creating them” is a powerful refutation of any notion that the act of creation exhausted or diminished Allah. The idea that a god could become weary is found in some ancient myths, but the Quran categorically rejects it. Allah’s power is infinite and His act of creation was effortless. If He created all of this without fatigue, then bringing the dead back to life is, for Him, an utterly trivial matter. The verse answers its own question with an emphatic “Yes indeed!”, and then declares “He is certainly Most Capable of everything.

” The Arabic word qadîr implies not just ability but boundless, perpetual power that can accomplish anything at any moment. The argument is so clear that only stubbornness can reject it.

For the believer, contemplating the heavens and earth becomes a daily reminder that the same Lord who hung the stars and spread the earth will surely resurrect every soul for account.

=34=

The surah now portrays the confirmation of resurrection in the Hereafter itself. The disbelievers who spent their lives mocking the concept of a Day of Judgement will be exposed to the Fire, brought face to face with the very reality they denied. They will be asked a question that is not for information but for acknowledgement and rebuke: “Is this not the truth?” The truth they ridiculed, the truth they called ancient fables, is now an inescapable, tangible reality before their eyes. Their answer will be immediate and emphatic: “Absolutely, by our Lord!” The oath “by our Lord” is significant because in the world they associated partners with Allah, but now all false gods have vanished, and they address Allah directly as their sole Lord. Their acknowledgment, however, comes too late.

The final pronouncement is chilling: “Then taste the punishment for your disbelief. ” The word “taste” implies a direct, personal, and sensory experience of the torment. It is linked specifically to their disbelief—not just any sin, but the foundational rejection of Allah’s signs and the denial of the meeting with Him. This verse corresponds to the earlier scene in verse 20 where the disbelievers are told they exhausted their pleasures. Here, the bitter truth is swallowed instead. The dialogue form makes the scene vivid and terrifying, a drama where the denier is forced to testify against himself. It serves as an urgent warning that the time for “intellectual objections” to the Hereafter will expire, and what will remain is only the unalterable consequence of one’s chosen path.

=35=

The surah concludes with a powerful exhortation to the Prophet and, by extension, to every believer who carries the message of truth. The command “So endure patiently” comes as a direct consequence of all that has been narrated: the stories of earlier prophets, the rejection of the Quraysh, the arrogance of the powerful, and the certainty of divine judgment. The Prophet is told to model his patience after ulû al-ʿazm, the Messengers of Firm Resolve. These are understood to be a select group of prophets—commonly identified as Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Muḥammad—who faced the most intense trials with unshakeable constancy. Patience is not passive; it is a firm, active perseverance in the face of opposition, mockery, and hardship.

The verse then instructs the Prophet not to seek to hasten the torment for the deniers. It is natural for a human being who is mocked and harmed to wish for a swift punishment upon his oppressors, but the divine timing is based on wisdom and mercy, not human emotion. The day of reckoning will come, and when it does, the disbelievers will perceive their entire worldly life as having lasted no more than an hour of a single day. This metaphor illustrates the fleeting nature of earthly existence and the suddenness with which the Hereafter will dawn. The statement “This is a sufficient warning!” affirms that the Quran contains everything necessary for guidance and warning; no further signs are needed.

The surah ends with a rhetorical question that summarises its entire message: “Then, will anyone be destroyed except the rebellious people?” Destruction is not random; it is the earned outcome of rebellion against Allah. Those who break away from obedience, who persist in transgression and arrogance, are the ones who will be utterly ruined. This closing verse balances divine justice with divine wisdom, offering a final call to reflection and a reassurance to the patient believer that the outcome is certain and the victory belongs to those who remain steadfast.
