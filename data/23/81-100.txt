=81=

After appealing to reason and presenting the undeniable signs of creation, life, death, and the alternation of night and day, the sûrah now exposes the real obstacle: blind imitation. “But they just say what their predecessors said.” The Meccan disbelievers, when confronted with the call to tawḥîd and the resurrection, did not respond with fresh arguments or genuine reflection. Instead, they merely parroted the objections of earlier nations.

The word “just” captures the dismissive and unthinking nature of their response. They had not examined the evidence for themselves; they had inherited a set of doubts and denials from their forefathers and repeated them without scrutiny. This verse diagnoses one of the most persistent diseases of the human heart: taqlîd, the blind following of tradition without understanding. The Quraysh took immense pride in their ancestry and the religion of their fathers. When the Prophet challenged that religion, their defense was simply, “We found our fathers upon a way, and we are following in their footsteps.” They used this as a shield against having to think. The verse strips away the respectability of this position. Simply repeating what predecessors said is not a valid argument. If the predecessors were wrong, then following them is self-destruction. The Qur’ân repeatedly asks, “What if their fathers understood nothing and were not guided?” This verse prepares the listener for the content of what those predecessors actually said, which the next verses quote. It also serves as a mirror for every generation: how much of what people believe and practice is merely inherited, and how much is the result of sincere, personal reflection on the truth? The believer is called to a faith that is rooted in conviction, not in social conformity. The fact that the disbelievers’ only response was to echo the ancients shows the bankruptcy of their position. When reason and revelation are met with nothing but tradition, it is a sign that the heart has closed itself. The verse thus links the Meccan pagans to the long line of those who rejected the messengers before them, all of whom used the same excuse and met the same end.

=82=

The verse now quotes the specific argument that the predecessors passed down and that the Meccans repeated: “They said, ‘Once we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected?’” This is the core objection to the message of the Hereafter. The disbelievers found the idea of resurrection absurd because they could not imagine how a body that had decayed into scattered particles could possibly be reassembled and brought back to life.

The emphasis on “dust and bones” highlights what they saw as the finality of death. Dust represents the complete decomposition of the flesh, and bones represent the last solid remnants that eventually crumble. In their minds, once a person reached that state, they were gone forever, absorbed into the earth.

The question is phrased with incredulity: “will we really be resurrected?” The tone is mocking, as if the very idea is too ridiculous to entertain. This objection was not new; it was the same objection raised by the people of Noah, the people of Hûd, and countless others, as recounted earlier in the same sûrah. The verse deliberately places these words in the mouths of the Meccans to show that they are not original thinkers; they are merely replaying an old script. The Qur’ân answers this objection not by denying the decomposition but by pointing to the greater miracle of the original creation. The One who created the human being from nothing can certainly recreate them after death. The dust and bones are not lost to His knowledge; every particle is under His control.

The verse also reveals the psychological reason behind the objection: it is not really about the physical possibility of resurrection but about the discomfort of facing judgment. If resurrection is denied, then moral accountability can be ignored.

The question, “will we really be resurrected?” is therefore not a neutral scientific inquiry; it is a rejection dressed in the language of doubt. The verse leaves the question hanging, as the next verse will add the weight of tradition to this denial.

=83=

The disbelievers continue their objection by adding the weight of time to their denial: “We have already been promised this, as well as our forefathers earlier. This is nothing but ancient fables!” They argue that the promise of resurrection is not a new, credible warning but an old, worn-out tale. They claim that earlier generations were also threatened with resurrection, and yet nothing happened; the dead remained dead, and no one returned. In their limited view, the very fact that the warning was ancient proved its falsehood. If it were true, they thought, why had it not already occurred? This reveals a profound misunderstanding of divine timing. They measured truth by immediate fulfillment, unable to grasp that the Day of Judgment is a fixed, appointed event that will come at its own time, not according to human impatience.

The phrase “ancient fables” (asâṭîr al-awwalîn) is a dismissive label they frequently used for the Qur’ân itself. By calling the promise of resurrection “fables,” they reduce it to the level of myths and legends—stories told for entertainment or moral instruction but not to be taken as literal fact. This was a deliberate rhetorical strategy to undermine the authority of the revelation. If the Qur’ân’s central promise was just a collection of old fairy tales, then no reasonable person needed to take it seriously. The verse exposes the arrogance of this stance. The very people who prided themselves on having no knowledge of the unseen were confidently declaring that something they could not possibly test was false. The irony is that they mocked the revelation as “ancient fables” while themselves mindlessly repeating the objections of their ancient forefathers. The verse serves as a warning that dismissing truth as outdated is a timeless tactic of disbelief. Every generation of disbelievers has labeled the divine message as old myths, and every generation has learned the truth when the punishment arrived. For the believers, this verse is a reassurance that the mockery they hear is not original; it is the same tired script that the prophets always faced.

=84=

After recording the empty objections of the disbelievers, the sûrah shifts to a powerful series of questions designed to corner them with their own admissions. “Ask them, O Prophet, ‘To whom belong the earth and all those on it, if you really know?’” This is the first of three rhetorical questions that appeal to the innate knowledge and acknowledged beliefs of the Quraysh. The Prophet is instructed to question them about the ownership of the earth and everything dwelling upon it—humans, animals, plants, mountains, and seas.

The phrase “if you really know” challenges them to answer honestly based on the knowledge they already possess. The Quraysh, despite their idolatry, did not believe that their idols created the earth or owned it. They acknowledged that the earth and all it contains belong to Allah alone. The verse therefore uses their own belief against them. If they admit that the earth belongs to Allah, then they must also admit that He alone has the right to command, to prohibit, and to call to account. Ownership implies authority. A king who owns a land has the right to make laws for it; the Creator who owns the earth and all on it has the right to send messengers, reveal scriptures, and establish a day of judgment.

The question is simple yet profound. It forces the disbelievers to connect the dots between what they already know and what they are refusing to accept.

The verse also serves an educational purpose for the believers: it teaches them how to argue with wisdom, starting from common ground and leading the opponent step by step to the logical conclusion. This method of questioning is a hallmark of the Qur’ânic approach to daʿwah. Rather than simply asserting the truth, it invites the listener to think and to answer for themselves, making the conclusion feel like their own discovery.

The next verse records their expected answer.

=85=

The verse confidently predicts their response: “They will reply, ‘To Allah!’” The Quraysh could not deny this obvious truth. They believed in Allah as the supreme Creator and the Owner of the earth. To claim otherwise would have been absurd even by their own standards. Their idols, in their own theology, were not creators or sustainers; they were merely intercessors. So when asked a direct question about the ownership of the earth, they had no choice but to answer honestly.

The verse then delivers the punchline: “Say, ‘Why are you not then mindful?’” The word “mindful” translates tadhakkarûn, which means to remember, to be mindful, to take heed. The argument is devastating in its simplicity. You admit that the earth and all who are on it belong to Allah. You know that He is the Creator, the Owner, the Provider. Then why do you not let this knowledge penetrate your heart and change your behavior? Why do you worship other beings alongside Him? Why do you not fear His punishment and hope for His reward? Why do you live as if you are not going to return to Him?

The question exposes a fundamental disconnect between their intellectual acknowledgment and their practical life. They had the knowledge, but they failed to act upon it.

This is a condition that can afflict believers as well: knowing the truth in theory but not letting it shape decisions and priorities. The verse calls for a mindfulness that transforms abstract belief into living consciousness.

The use of “then” (fa) indicates that the conclusion should logically follow from the premise. If Allah owns the earth, then He alone should be worshipped. If they are not mindful, it is not because the evidence is lacking but because their desires and arrogance have blocked the natural movement of the mind toward its Lord.

=86=

The questioning escalates from the earth to the heavens: “And ask them, ‘Who is the Lord of the seven heavens and the Lord of the Mighty Throne?’” The “seven heavens” refers to the multiple layers of the sky, the vast cosmic order that Allah has created. The Quraysh acknowledged Allah’s lordship over the heavens as well. The addition of “the Mighty Throne” (al-ʿArsh al-ʿAẓîm) elevates the question to the highest level. The Throne is the symbol of Allah’s supreme authority and dominion over the entire creation. It is described elsewhere as encompassing the heavens and the earth, and as being carried by angels. Mentioning the Throne emphasizes that Allah’s rule is not limited to the physical earth but extends over the entire universe, including the unseen realms.

The word “Mighty” underscores the majesty and grandeur of that dominion. By asking about the Lord of the heavens and the Throne, the verse is inviting the disbelievers to reflect on the scale of Allah’s power. If He is the Lord of something as immense and unfathomable as the Throne, then what are their small idols in comparison? What power do they have over the heavens?

The question also connects to the theme of the sûrah: the One who sits upon the Throne in majesty is the One who will judge on the Day of Resurrection. Lordship over the heavens and the Throne implies the authority to command, to send messengers, to reveal books, and to hold all beings accountable.

The question is not merely about labels; it is about recognizing the implications of that lordship. The next verse records the inevitable answer and the sharp follow-up.

=87=

Once again, the disbelievers are forced to concede the truth: “They will reply, ‘Allah.’” They cannot attribute the lordship of the heavens and the Mighty Throne to anyone else. Their idols, no matter how much they revered them, were never believed to be the lords of the cosmic order. The confession is extracted from their own mouths, leaving them with no logical ground to stand on.

The verse then delivers the second penetrating question: “Say, ‘Will you not then fear Him?’” The first question asked, “Why are you not then mindful? ” This second one escalates from mindfulness to fear (tattaqûn), which is taqwâ—the reverent, protective fear that leads to obedience. The logic is plain: if Allah is the Lord of the seven heavens and the Mighty Throne, then His power is absolute, His authority is unchallenged, and His punishment is to be feared. How can you acknowledge such a Lord and yet not fear Him enough to abandon your idols and follow His commands?

The question is a powerful appeal to self-preservation. If a person knows that a mighty king has authority over them, they naturally fear displeasing him. The disbelievers acknowledged the mightiest King of all, yet lived as if He had no claim on their lives. This inconsistency is what the verse exposes.

The word “then” (fa) again links the answer to the consequence. Their own admission should lead directly to taqwâ, but their stubbornness breaks that natural connection.

For the believer, this verse is a call to examine the quality of their own fear of Allah. Do they truly fear the Lord of the Throne in a way that transforms their daily choices?

The verse teaches that true faith is not just a mental acknowledgment; it is a state of the heart that produces vigilant, obedient awareness.

=88=

The third question in this powerful sequence probes the deepest level of divine power: “Ask them also, ‘In Whose Hands is the authority over all things, protecting all while none can protect against Him, if you really know?’” The word “Hands” is a metaphorical expression for complete ownership, control, and dominion. The “authority” (malakût) over all things refers to the ultimate power to govern, manage, and dispose of every affair in the universe. Nothing escapes this authority; every atom, every event, every destiny is under His control.

The verse adds a crucial detail: “protecting all while none can protect against Him. ” Allah is the ultimate protector. He can grant safety, refuge, and security to whomever He wills. No force can harm someone whom Allah protects. Conversely, if Allah decides to punish or afflict someone, there is no power in existence that can shield that person from His decree. No idol, no intercessor, no wealth, no army can protect against the will of the Almighty.

This is a direct challenge to the pagan belief that their idols could intercede for them or protect them. The verse asks them to search their own knowledge and conscience: who truly holds this absolute power? The expected answer, as given in the next verse, is Allah.

The question leaves no room for ambiguity. If Allah’s authority is absolute and His protection is the only real protection, then seeking protection from idols, relying on wealth, or fearing human powers above Him is the height of irrationality. This verse, like the previous ones, builds the case for pure tawḥîd by appealing to what the disbelievers already acknowledge. It also provides immense comfort to the believers: they serve the One in whose Hand is all authority and who protects His servants. No earthly power can touch them without His permission, and no harm can come to them unless He wills it for a greater wisdom.

=89=

The pattern repeats for the third time: “They will reply, ‘Allah.’” They cannot honestly answer the question in any other way. Their own belief system compels them to admit that Allah alone holds the ultimate authority and protection.

Yet this admission stands in stark contradiction to their daily worship and their rejection of the Prophet’s message. The verse then delivers the most pointed question yet: “Say, ‘How are you then so deluded?’” The word “deluded” translates tu’fakûn, which means to be turned away, deceived, or diverted from the truth.

The question is not “why do you disbelieve?” but “how are you so utterly turned away from the obvious?” It expresses amazement at the depth of their self-deception. They admit that Allah is the sovereign owner of the earth, the Lord of the heavens and the Throne, and the holder of all authority and protection. These admissions should make a person the most devoted worshipper of Allah alone, trembling in awe of Him. Instead, they worship idols, deny the resurrection, and mock the messenger. The disconnect is so extreme that it can only be explained by some powerful delusion that has gripped their minds. The Qur’ân elsewhere identifies this delusion as the work of Satan, the love of worldly life, and the hardening of hearts through repeated sin.

The question is both a rebuke and a lament. It is as if the verse is saying, “You have all the pieces of the puzzle, yet you refuse to put them together. How is that possible?” For the believers, this verse serves as a warning to guard against self-deception. A person can know a great deal about Allah and still be deluded if that knowledge does not translate into humble submission. The verse calls every listener to examine themselves: do my actions match what I claim to know about Allah, or am I, too, somehow deluded?

=90=

After the series of questions and answers, the sûrah delivers a final verdict on the disbelievers: “In fact, We have brought them the truth, and they are certainly liars.” The “truth” (ḥaqq) encompasses everything the Prophet brought—the Qur’ân, the message of pure monotheism, the news of the Hereafter, and the moral commands. It is described as something that “We have brought them,” emphasizing that it originates from Allah and has been delivered fully and clearly to the Quraysh. There is no deficiency in the delivery, no lack of clarity, and no hidden agenda. The truth is complete, manifest, and accessible.

The second part of the verse, “and they are certainly liars,” is a definitive judgment on their character. After all their excuses—that the Prophet was insane, that the Qur’ân was ancient fables, that resurrection was impossible—the verse calls them what they truly are: liars. They know deep down that the message is true, but they lie to themselves and to others by denying it. Their rejection is not based on an honest search for truth; it is a willful falsehood. This verse mirrors the opening of the sûrah, which declared the believers successful, and now it labels the disbelievers as liars. The contrast is stark. The believers are those who speak the truth, live the truth, and will inherit the eternal truth of Paradise. The disbelievers are those who fabricate falsehood, cling to lies, and will face the consequences of their lies.

The verse also provides a model for the believer’s relationship with the truth. The truth is not something we create or modify; it is something we receive from Allah and submit to. The disbelievers’ lie is not just a factual error; it is a moral failing, a refusal to honor the truth they have been given. For the Prophet and his followers, this verse was a vindication. They were on the side of truth, no matter how powerful or numerous the liars seemed. The ultimate victory belongs to the truth, and the liars, however loud their voices, will be silenced.

=91=

The sûrah now presents a decisive argument for pure monotheism, refuting the claim that Allah has offspring or partners. “Allah has never had any offspring, nor is there any god besides Him.

” This statement directly addresses the beliefs of various groups: the pagan Arabs who claimed the angels were Allah’s daughters, the Christians who called Jesus the son of God, and others who associated partners with the Creator. The verse negates offspring in the most absolute terms, using “never” (mâ ittakhadha) to emphasize that this is not something that could ever be true of Allah. Offspring imply need, succession, and a likeness between parent and child—all of which are impossible for the Self-Sufficient, Eternal Creator.

The verse then declares “nor is there any god besides Him,” completing the statement of tawḥîd. To reinforce this, the verse presents a brilliant rational argument: “Otherwise, each god would have taken away what he created, and they would have tried to dominate one another.” If there were multiple gods, the universe would descend into chaos. Each deity would want to exercise independent authority, taking away its own creation and competing with the others. The inevitable result would be conflict, disorder, and the ruin of the cosmos. The fact that the universe operates with such precise, harmonious laws is proof that it has a single, unified Controller. This argument, known as the argument from the unity of design, is simple yet profound. A perfectly ordered system points to a single will behind it.

The verse concludes with a declaration of transcendence: “Glorified is Allah above what they claim!” Subḥânallâh—Allah is far exalted above the false descriptions and associations that people invent. This glorification is both a statement of truth and an act of worship, correcting the lies with praise. For the believers, this verse solidifies the foundation of their faith: Allah is One, without partner, without child, without equal. It calls them to reject any doctrine that compromises this pure monotheism and to exalt Allah above all the limitations that human imagination tries to impose on Him.

=92=

The verse continues the glorification of Allah by highlighting one of His perfect attributes: “He is the Knower of the seen and unseen.” The “seen” (ghayb) refers to the realm that is hidden from human perception—the future, the past, the heavens, the angels, the soul, the realities of Paradise and Hell, and the inner thoughts of hearts. The “seen” (shahâdah) is the visible, tangible world that human senses can perceive. Allah’s knowledge encompasses both realms perfectly, without any limitation. Nothing in the unseen is hidden from Him, and nothing in the seen escapes His notice. This attribute is directly relevant to the context of the sûrah, which has been discussing the resurrection, the judgment, and the secret schemes of the disbelievers. The disbelievers mock what they cannot see, but Allah knows the full reality of the Hereafter as completely as He knows the present world. Their mockery is based on ignorance of the unseen; Allah’s knowledge is absolute.

The verse then concludes with another declaration of transcendence: “Exalted is He above what they associate with Him.” The word “exalted” (taʿâlâ) means He is far above, beyond, and greater than anything they try to link with Him. The verse thus combines a positive attribute—perfect knowledge—with a negation of shirk. Because Allah knows the unseen and the seen, He is fully aware of all the false gods that people worship, and He is infinitely above them. The idols they associate with Him know nothing of the unseen; they cannot see, hear, or benefit anyone. The contrast could not be sharper. The verse serves as a warning to those who persist in shirk, and as a comfort to the believers who trust in the All-Knowing. Everything that is hidden from them—their future, their provision, their safety—is fully known to their Lord, who is exalted above every false object of worship. This verse thus provides a fitting close to the section that dismantles polytheism and affirms the absolute oneness and perfection of Allah.

=93=

The sûrah now turns to a personal supplication that the Prophet is taught to make, reflecting his human vulnerability and his complete reliance on Allah. “Say, O Prophet, ‘My Lord! Should You show me what they are threatened with…’” The “what they are threatened with” refers to the punishment that the disbelievers have been repeatedly warned about. The Prophet is instructed to address his Lord with the intimate phrase “My Lord” (Rabbi), expressing his personal connection and dependence. The supplication begins with a conditional: “Should You show me,” meaning if Allah wills that the Prophet should witness the punishment that will befall the Quraysh in his lifetime. The Prophet, peace be upon him, knew that the denial and mockery of his people would bring divine retribution, just as it did for the nations of Noah, Hûd, and others. He did not know whether this punishment would come during his lifetime or after his death. This supplication reveals the Prophet’s deep concern for his people, even those who persecuted him. He does not ask for their punishment eagerly; rather, he turns to Allah with a prayer that reveals his fear of being caught up in the consequences of their wrongdoing, as the next verse shows.

The verse also teaches the believers the proper attitude toward divine judgment. One should not gloat over the anticipated destruction of enemies; instead, one should turn to Allah with humility, seeking His mercy and protection. The Prophet’s example here is one of restraint and spiritual sensitivity. He is not a vengeful leader calling down curses; he is a servant who leaves the matter entirely in the hands of his Lord while praying for his own safety. The conditional “should You show me” acknowledges that the timing of punishment is entirely in Allah’s control, and the Prophet’s role is simply to deliver the message and seek refuge in his Lord.

=94=

The supplication continues with the essential plea: “then, my Lord, do not count me among the wrongdoing people.” The Prophet asks that if the punishment does descend upon the disbelievers during his lifetime, he be protected from it and not be grouped with the wrongdoers. This prayer is remarkable because the Prophet was infallible in his mission and was not one of the wrongdoers in any moral sense.

Yet he prays as if he fears that association with a punished people might somehow encompass him. This reflects the profound humility of the prophets. They never take their salvation for granted; they always see themselves as utterly dependent on Allah’s mercy. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, was taught to pray like this, just as earlier messengers like Noah and Hûd prayed for help and deliverance. The plea also echoes the story of Lot, who was saved from the punishment that befell his people, and the command to Noah that the wrongdoers among his own family would not be spared. The Prophet here distances himself from the wrongdoing of his people and places his trust entirely in Allah’s judgment.

The word “wrongdoing” (ẓulm) refers to the shirk, denial, and persecution committed by the Quraysh. By asking not to be counted among them, the Prophet is reaffirming his complete separation from their evil, while at the same time acknowledging that Allah’s punishment can be sweeping, and only His mercy can grant exemption. For the believers, this supplication is a model of how to think about divine punishment. Even when one is on the side of truth, one should never feel arrogantly secure. The destruction of the wicked is a sobering event, not a spectacle for entertainment. The believer’s response is to turn to Allah with a prayer for safety and to examine one’s own heart to ensure there is no trace of the wrongdoing that invites punishment.

The verse teaches a profound balance: confidence in the truth of the message, combined with personal humility before the Judge.

=95=

In response to the Prophet’s conditional supplication, Allah affirms His absolute power: “We are indeed able to show you what We have threatened them with.” This is a reassurance that the threat of punishment is not empty. Allah is fully capable of bringing it to pass, whether during the Prophet’s lifetime or after.

The emphasis “indeed” (innâ) and “able” (qâdirûn) strengthens the certainty. The Quraysh mocked the idea of punishment, but this verse tells the Prophet that the matter is settled in principle; the only question is timing. Allah could, if He willed, make the Prophet witness the destruction of his enemies just as earlier prophets witnessed the destruction of their peoples.

The verse also contains a subtle comfort. The Prophet’s heart was grieved by the constant rejection and mockery. This verse assures him that the justice he might long to see is completely within Allah’s power. He need not worry that their defiance will go unanswered. The ability to show the punishment implies that the punishment exists, is prepared, and can be revealed at any moment. The fact that it is delayed is an act of mercy and wisdom, not of weakness. For the believers, this verse reinforces their trust in divine justice. Sometimes the triumph of truth seems distant, and the oppressors appear to get away with their crimes.

This verse reminds them that the One who made the promise is absolutely able to fulfill it. The delay is part of the test, but the outcome is certain.

The verse also serves as a warning to the disbelievers who might be listening: do not mistake the delay for inability. The punishment is real, and when it is unleashed, there will be no escape. The Prophet is thus fortified by this declaration of power, knowing that his Lord is not unmindful of the threats against His messenger and can demonstrate His justice whenever He chooses.

=96=

Having been assured of Allah’s power to deal with the disbelievers, the Prophet is now given a crucial moral instruction: “Respond to evil with what is best. We know well what they claim.

” This is a comprehensive ethical principle that governs the believer’s reaction to hostility. The “evil” (sayyi’ah) here includes the verbal abuse, mockery, slander, and persecution that the Prophet and his followers endured. The command is not merely to respond with good, but with “what is best” (aḥsan)—the most excellent, most beautiful response. This means repaying insults with forbearance, harm with forgiveness, and harshness with gentle words and prayers for guidance. The Prophet’s life was a living example of this command. When the people of Ṭâ’if stoned him until he bled, he did not curse them but prayed, “O Allah, guide my people, for they do not know. ” This is the “what is best” that the verse commands.

The second part of the verse, “We know well what they claim,” provides the motivation and the comfort behind this difficult ethical standard. Allah is fully aware of every lie, every slander, and every hurtful word the disbelievers utter. Nothing escapes His knowledge. The Prophet does not need to defend his own honor or correct every false accusation himself; Allah, who knows all, will take care of the matter. This frees the believer from the exhausting burden of fighting every battle personally. The command to respond with what is best is not a sign of weakness; it is a sign of strength and trust in divine justice. It also has a strategic purpose: kindness in the face of hatred can soften hearts, break the cycle of enmity, and win over those who might otherwise remain enemies. The verse thus provides both a practical strategy for daʿwah and a spiritual discipline for the soul.

For every believer facing hostility, this verse is a guiding light: do not mirror evil, but transcend it with excellence, and trust that the All-Knowing will handle the rest.

=97=

The Prophet is taught to add another supplication to his spiritual armor: “And say, ‘My Lord! I seek refuge in You from the temptations of the devils.’” The word “temptations” translates hamazât, which literally refers to the prickings, proddings, or whisperings of the devils. These are the subtle, persistent suggestions that Satan and his minions cast into the hearts of human beings—whispers of doubt, incitements to anger, desires for revenge, and all manner of evil thoughts. The Prophet, despite his lofty status, was not immune to these external whisperings. Satan tries to influence even the most righteous, seeking to exploit moments of fatigue, frustration, or sadness. By commanding the Prophet to seek refuge from these temptations, Allah teaches him and all believers that the struggle against Satan is continuous and can only be won with divine help.

The phrase “I seek refuge in You” (aʿûdhu bika) is a powerful declaration of dependence. The believer acknowledges that he cannot, by his own strength, repel the attacks of the unseen enemy. Only Allah, the Lord and Master, can provide the protection needed. This supplication should be made frequently, not just once, because the whisperings are relentless.

The verse also identifies the source of many of the evil impulses that lead to sin. When a believer feels a sudden urge to speak harshly, to retaliate unjustly, to entertain sinful thoughts, or to doubt the truth, they should recognize that these are the hamazât of the devils and immediately turn to Allah for refuge. The command is placed right after the instruction to respond to evil with what is best, which makes perfect sense. The greatest obstacle to responding with excellence is the devil’s whisper that urges one to retaliate, to insult back, to nurse a grudge. Seeking refuge from the devils is therefore the spiritual means by which one can obey the previous command. The verse integrates the ethical command with its spiritual prerequisite.

=98=

The supplication intensifies: “And I seek refuge in You, my Lord, that they even come near me.” This is a step beyond seeking protection from the temptations themselves. The Prophet is taught to ask that the devils be kept away entirely, not even allowed to approach him with their whisperings.

The phrase “that they come near me” (an yaḥḍurûn) means that he seeks refuge from their very presence, from any proximity that might allow them to exert influence. This reflects the highest level of spiritual vigilance. It is not enough to resist temptation when it arrives; the believer asks Allah to block the enemy at the gate, so that the battle does not even begin. This teaches that prevention is better than cure in the spiritual realm as well.

The repetition of “my Lord” (Rabbi) emphasizes the personal and intimate nature of this plea. The Prophet is not reciting a formula; he is calling upon his nurturing Lord with a heartfelt request for total protection. The verse acknowledges that the devils are real, active, and intent on leading human beings astray. Their nearness is dangerous because prolonged exposure to their whisperings can wear down a person’s resolve and corrupt their heart. By asking that they not even come near, the believer is recognizing their own vulnerability and seeking a level of divine shielding that goes beyond ordinary human capacity. This verse, together with the previous one, forms a powerful daily invocation. The Prophet, peace be upon him, often recited these words, and they are part of the morning and evening remembrances prescribed in the Sunnah.

For every believer, these verses are a gift: they provide the exact words to use when feeling besieged by negative thoughts, doubts, or evil urges. They also cultivate a mindset of complete reliance on Allah for spiritual protection, reminding the believer that the unseen war with Satan is real, and victory belongs to those who constantly seek refuge in their Lord.

=99=

The sûrah now turns to a scene of profound desperation: “When death approaches any of them, they cry, ‘My Lord! Let me go back.’” The verse describes the moment when a disbeliever, who has spent a lifetime denying the resurrection and mocking the messengers, finally faces the reality of death. At that terrifying moment, the veil of heedlessness is lifted. The angels of death appear, and the soul begins its departure. Suddenly, all the arguments, all the pride, all the material wealth, and all the false gods vanish into nothingness, and the dying person sees a glimpse of what awaits them. In this state, they cry out to the very Lord they had denied or associated partners with, calling Him “My Lord” for the first time with desperate sincerity. Their plea is “Let me go back”—return me to the worldly life for a short while. This cry is motivated by the sudden, terrifying recognition that everything the messengers said was true, and that they are now facing a dreadful punishment. They want a second chance, a little more time, to do what they should have done all along. This verse echoes similar descriptions throughout the Qur’ân, where the disbelievers at the point of death or on the Day of Judgment beg to be sent back so they can do righteous deeds. The plea is not a sign of genuine, transformative repentance; it is a reaction of panic and fear, a desire to escape the immediate consequence rather than a true turning of the heart toward Allah. The timing reveals its insincerity. When they had health, time, and opportunity, they turned away in disgust. Now that the door is closing, they suddenly want to return. The verse paints a chilling picture of wasted life and the horror of regret that comes too late. It serves as a powerful warning to every living person: the time to cry “My Lord” is now, in the days of health and opportunity, not on the deathbed when the plea will be rejected.

The dying disbeliever clarifies what they want to return for: “so I may do good in what I left behind.” They finally understand the value of the good deeds they neglected—the prayers they did not pray, the charity they did not give, the wrongs they did not right, the faith they did not embrace. They want to go back not to enjoy the world again, but specifically to fill the void of good deeds they now realize is their only currency for the Hereafter.

The word “what I left behind” (fîmâ taraktu) implies the life, the wealth, the time, and the opportunities that they squandered. The response from Allah is immediate and crushing: “Never!” (kallâ). This single word is a decisive, irreversible rejection. There is no going back. The test is over. The plea is dismissed with absolute finality.

The verse then explains why: “It is only a useless appeal they make.” It is a word (kalimah) they are uttering, an empty cry born of fear, not a genuine change of heart. If they were sent back, the Qur’ân elsewhere states, they would simply return to their old ways. Their nature is not transformed; they are merely terrified. Then the verse adds a description of their state after death: “And there is a barrier behind them until the Day they are resurrected.” The “barrier” (barzakh) is the intermediate realm between death and resurrection, a state of existence in the grave where the soul remains until the trumpet is blown. “Behind them” means they have left the world and are now in this barrier, and there is no passage back through it. The barzakh is a sealed door; once crossed, return is impossible. This verse is one of the most sobering in the sûrah, tearing away the illusion that one can postpone repentance indefinitely. It calls every human being to urgently consider their state before the moment of death arrives, when cries and regrets will no longer be heard. The only time to do good in what we leave behind is before we leave it behind.
