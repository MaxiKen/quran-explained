=61=

After describing the believers whose hearts tremble even while doing good, the sûrah now shows that this fear does not paralyze them; rather, it propels them forward. “It is they who race to do good deeds, always taking the lead.

” The word “race” translates yusâriʿûn, which implies swift, eager, and competitive haste. The believers do not drag their feet when an opportunity for righteousness appears. They are the first to respond, the first to give, the first to pray, the first to seek forgiveness. Their fear of the return to their Lord does not make them retreat into despair; it ignites a burning desire to be ahead, to reach safety before the door closes.

This is a profound portrait of spiritual health: a heart suspended between fear and hope produces urgency. The phrase “always taking the lead” (sâbiqûn) deepens the image. They are not merely participants in the race of goodness; they are the front-runners, the ones who outstrip others in their devotion. The believers compete not for worldly prizes but for the pleasure of Allah and the gardens of Paradise. This competitiveness in righteousness is encouraged elsewhere in the Qur’ân: “So race to all that is good.” The early Muslims understood this verse well. They would hurry to the mosque at the first call to prayer, hasten to feed the hungry, and vie with one another in acts of charity even when they themselves were in need. Their fear of judgment day made every passing moment precious, not to be wasted.

The verse also serves as a silent rebuke to those who delay their repentance, thinking there is plenty of time. The true believer knows that the term of life is unknown, so they seize the present moment. This quality also reflects a deep understanding that Paradise is not cheaply won; it requires effort, initiative, and a sense of urgency that overcomes laziness and procrastination. The believers combine the inward trembling awe with outward swiftness in action, creating a balanced spiritual personality. The verse thus draws a direct line from the fear of the previous verse to the rapid good deeds of this one, showing that genuine awe of Allah manifests as tireless, joyful racing toward everything that pleases Him.

=62=

Lest the high standard set in the previous verses cause anyone to despair, the sûrah immediately follows with a reassurance of divine justice and mercy: “We never require of any soul more than what it can afford.” This is one of the fundamental principles of Islamic theology. Allah, in His infinite wisdom and fairness, does not burden a person beyond their capacity. Every obligation, every test, and every trial is measured precisely to match what the individual can bear. This verse echoes the famous supplication at the end of Sûrat Al-Baqarah: “Our Lord, do not impose on us that which we have not the strength to bear.” It assures the believers that the striving, the prayer, the guarding of chastity, the fasting, and the racing to good deeds are all within the realm of human possibility. No one is asked to be an angel, only to be the best human they can be, with the help of Allah.

The second part of the verse shifts to the record of deeds: “And with Us is a record which speaks the truth. None will be wronged.” The “record” refers to the book of deeds that every person will receive on the Day of Judgment. This record is not a subjective estimate; it “speaks the truth,” meaning it contains an absolutely accurate and complete account of every action, word, and intention. Nothing is omitted, exaggerated, or minimized.

The phrase “None will be wronged” reinforces the perfect justice of the divine court. No soul will be punished for a sin it did not commit, and no good deed will be overlooked. This verse brings immense comfort to the sincere believer who worries that their efforts fall short. It tells them that Allah knows their limitations and will judge them accordingly. It also serves as a warning to those who think they can escape accountability. The record is comprehensive, and its testimony is irrefutable. In the flow of the sûrah, this verse acts as a pivotal statement that balances the high ideals of the believers’ character with the merciful and just nature of the Lawgiver. It encourages striving without causing despair, and it promises absolute fairness in the final judgment, leaving no room for the complaint that the demands were too great.

=63=

In stark contrast to the believers who race to good deeds with trembling hearts, the verse now describes the state of the disbelievers: “But the hearts of those who disbelieve are oblivious to all of this.” The word “oblivious” translates ghaflah, a deep, encompassing heedlessness. Their hearts are submerged in ignorance and distraction, unable to perceive the realities that the believers hold so vividly—the return to the Lord, the record of deeds, the judgment, and the need for urgent action. This obliviousness is not innocent; it is a chosen state, the result of repeatedly turning away from the truth. The Qur’ân describes their hearts as having veils and locks because of their persistent rejection. They are not simply uninformed; they have made themselves unreceptive.

The verse then adds a critical detail: “and they have other evil deeds, opposite to this, in which they are engrossed.” The word “other” (ukhar) indicates that beyond their core disbelief, they are engaged in a whole range of sinful actions that are the natural fruit of their heedlessness. These deeds are described as being “opposite to this”—diametrically opposed to the racing to good deeds that characterizes the believers. Instead of prayer, they have idolatry; instead of charity, they have greed and exploitation; instead of chastity, they have immorality; instead of truthfulness, they have deceit.

The word “engrossed” (ʿâmilûn) means they are actively, habitually engaged in these deeds. Their lives revolve around them. They are not occasional sinners who struggle and repent; they are workers dedicated to their evil as if it were their profession. This verse provides a penetrating diagnosis of the disbelieving heart. It is not empty; it is filled, but filled with the wrong things. The oblivion to the truth is accompanied by an energetic pursuit of falsehood. For the believers, this verse serves as a warning to guard the heart against ghaflah, and as an explanation for why the disbelievers act as they do. Their actions are not random; they flow from a heart that has lost sight of its ultimate destination.

=64=

The sûrah now pulls back the curtain on the moment when the heedless enjoyment comes to an end: “But as soon as We seize their elite with torment, they start to cry for help.” The word “seize” (akhadhnâ) indicates a sudden, forceful grip that leaves no room for escape. The target of this seizure is specifically “their elite” (mutrafîhim)—the wealthy, pampered leaders who live in luxury and lead others into denial. They are singled out because their arrogance and influence are the greatest, and because their comfortable lives made them the most resistant to the message. When the punishment touches them, whether in this world through war, famine, or disaster, or in the Hereafter, all their pride vanishes instantly.

The phrase “they start to cry for help” (yaj’arûn) vividly depicts a loud, desperate wailing, like the bellowing of an animal in pain. The sophisticated, eloquent chiefs who once mocked the messenger and debated with arrogance are reduced to inarticulate cries. This verse echoes the pattern seen in the stories of earlier nations: Pharaoh, the chiefs of ʿÂd, and the leaders of Noah’s people all maintained their arrogance until the punishment arrived, and then they cried out when it was too late.

The word “but as soon as” emphasizes the suddenness of the reversal. There is no gradual transition; one moment they are secure in their luxury, and the next they are seized.

The verse also teaches that the punishment often begins with the elite because they bear the greatest responsibility for misleading the masses. Their torment is not only for their own disbelief but also for the people they led astray. For the Meccan believers who were being oppressed by the rich Quraysh chiefs, this verse was a direct promise of justice. The very people who seemed untouchable would one day be seized, and their cries would not save them. The verse thus combines a warning for the oppressors with a consolation for the oppressed, reminding everyone that divine justice does not overlook the powerful; it starts with them.

=65=

The response to their desperate cries is crushing and final: “They will be told, ‘Do not cry for help today. Surely you will never be saved from Us.’” The command “Do not cry for help” is a sharp dismissal of their wailing. In the world, they might have called upon their gods, their allies, or their wealth for aid, and perhaps they found temporary relief. But on the Day of Judgment, or at the moment of divine punishment, all such avenues are closed.

The word “today” anchors them in a new and terrifying reality, the day of reckoning when the time for mercy has passed and the time for justice has arrived. The phrase “Surely you will never be saved from Us” (innakum minnâ lâ tunṣarûn) is an absolute negation of any possibility of rescue. Allah Himself is the source of the punishment, and none can protect against Him. The statement is not a threat; it is a verdict. This verse mirrors the experience of the earlier destroyed nations who cried out when the punishment came, only to be told, “Taste what you used to deny.” The severity of the response is proportional to the severity of their crimes: they rejected the clear signs, mocked the messengers, oppressed the weak, and lived in arrogant heedlessness. Now their cries are not met with compassion but with a cold reminder that the day of compassion has been replaced by the day of recompense.

The verse also exposes a profound truth about the nature of delayed punishment. When people are given respite and do not repent, they are simply accumulating more reason for the punishment to be severe. Their eventual cries are futile because they had a lifetime of opportunities to cry to Allah in sincere repentance, but they chose not to. The “today” of punishment is not a day for second chances; it is the day for receiving what one has earned. For the believers, this verse reinforces the urgency of turning to Allah now, in the time of mercy, before the day comes when cries will not be heard. It also provides a sobering answer to those who question why the wicked seem to prosper; their appointed day will come, and on that day, no one will help them.

=66=

The reason for this harsh treatment is now explained to them directly: “Indeed, My revelations were recited to you, but you used to back away in disgust.” The verse places the blame squarely on their own behavior. Allah’s “revelations” (âyâtî) were not hidden or inaccessible; they were recited publicly, clearly, and repeatedly by the Prophet, peace be upon him.

The word “recited” (tutlâ) emphasizes the oral transmission that gave them every chance to hear, reflect, and respond. Their reaction, however, was not one of attentive listening but of physical and psychological aversion.

The phrase “back away in disgust” translates tankiṣûn, which conveys the image of someone recoiling, stepping backward, and turning their heels in contempt. This is more than mere disagreement; it is an emotional revulsion that prevents even a fair hearing. They did not want to be near the recitation, and when they heard it, they physically withdrew. This behavior was well-known among the Meccan pagans. Some of them would say to one another, “Do not listen to this Qur’ân, and make noise during its recitation so that you might overcome.” They would plug their ears, raise their voices, and leave the gathering rather than allow the words of Allah to enter their hearts. This verse exposes the willfulness of their rejection. It was not that they lacked evidence; it was that they actively fled from it.

The mention of “My revelations” adds a personal dimension: the words they were backing away from were not the opinions of a man but the direct speech of their Lord. Their aversion was therefore not merely to a preacher but to the very Creator who now addresses them from a position of judgment. The verse serves as a timeless warning to all who have access to the Qur’ân but treat it with indifference or disdain. To hear the word of Allah and turn away in disgust is to store up a terrible reckoning, and on that day, the memory of the recitation will return not as a comfort but as a witness against them.

=67=

The verse continues the indictment by describing two specific behaviors of the Meccan disbelievers: “boasting of the Sacred House, and babbling nonsense about the Quran by night.” The “Sacred House” is the Kaʿbah, the ancient sanctuary built by Abraham and Ishmael, which Allah had made a place of safety and pilgrimage. The Quraysh took great pride in their role as custodians of this House. They considered themselves its rightful guardians and looked down upon other tribes because of it.

However, their boasting was hollow. They had filled the Sacred House with idols, and their guardianship was a matter of worldly prestige rather than sincere devotion to the Lord of the House. The verse exposes their arrogance: they boasted of a sanctuary dedicated to the One God while worshiping hundreds of gods within it.

The second part of the verse, “and babbling nonsense about the Quran by night,” describes the secret gatherings they held to discredit the revelation. During the pilgrimage season, when delegations from all over Arabia came to Mecca, the Quraysh chiefs feared that the Qur’ân’s eloquence might win hearts. So they would meet at night to strategize and spread contradictory propaganda: some would say the Prophet was a sorcerer, others that he was a poet, still others that he was insane. They would “babble” (tahjurûn), which carries the sense of speaking deliriously or telling absurd tales, deliberately filling the air with confusion to drown out the clarity of the message. This night-time conspiracy showed their desperation; the truth had to be fought with whispers and rumors because it could not be refuted with arguments. The verse thus links the two evils together: by day they boasted arrogantly about the Sacred House, and by night they plotted against the very revelation that called them back to the pure religion of that House. For the believers, this description was a vivid snapshot of the environment they lived in, and a reassurance that Allah was fully aware of the secret schemes against His word.

The verse reminds every generation that the truth does not need dark rooms and whispered slander to survive; falsehood does.

=68=

The sûrah now poses a series of rhetorical questions that probe the real reasons behind the Quraysh’s rejection. The first question is, “Is it because they have never contemplated the Word of Allah?

” The question is a challenge and a diagnosis. The Word, the Qur’ân, was available to them. Its verses were recited, its arguments were clear, and its language was accessible. If they had truly contemplated it with an open mind, they would have recognized its divine origin. Their failure to do so is not the fault of the Word but of their own refusal to engage with it seriously. Contemplation (tadabbur) requires stillness, humility, and a sincere search for truth; the Quraysh lacked all three.

The second question is, “Or because there has come to them something that did not come to their forefathers?” This addresses their attachment to ancestral tradition. The Arabs prided themselves on following the ways of their fathers. The message of pure monotheism, the promise of resurrection, and the moral demands of the Qur’ân were unfamiliar and disruptive to the inherited pagan culture. So they rejected it simply because it was new to them, without evaluating its truth.

This is a classic barrier to faith: the assumption that what is old is automatically correct, and what is new is automatically false. The verse challenges this assumption by showing its irrationality. If their forefathers were astray, then following them would only perpetuate error. The two questions together expose the double failure of the disbelievers: they neither engaged with the revelation intellectually nor were they willing to break free from blind tradition.

For the believer, this verse is a call to constant tadabbur of the Qur’ân. A faith that is inherited but never contemplated can become as hollow as the paganism of the Quraysh. The cure for doubt and the source of conviction is deep, regular reflection on the Word of Allah.

=69=

The next question in the series probes a different, more personal cause of rejection: “Or because they failed to recognize their Messenger, and so they denied him?” This question points to a profound irony. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, had lived among the Quraysh for forty years before the revelation began. He was known by the title al-Amîn, the trustworthy. His honesty, integrity, and noble character were beyond dispute. No one had ever caught him in a lie or an act of treachery. He was not a stranger or an unknown foreigner; he was one of them, from a respected lineage. So why, when he came with the truth, did they fail to recognize him? The verse implies that their failure was willful. They recognized his character perfectly well in worldly matters; they entrusted him with their valuables and sought his judgment in disputes. But when he claimed prophethood, they suddenly “failed to recognize” him. The truth is that they recognized him all too well, and that was precisely the problem. His impeccable character made his message all the more compelling and left them with no legitimate grounds for rejection. So they resorted to ignoring what they knew about him and fabricating new labels—sorcerer, poet, madman—to cover their denial.

This verse also has a wider application. People often reject truth because it comes through a person they have personal biases against—someone of a different class, race, or social standing. The Quraysh did not want to follow a man from among themselves because it hurt their pride. The verse challenges the listener to separate the message from the messenger’s perceived social rank and to evaluate the truth on its own merits. For the believers, this verse was a vindication of the Prophet’s character. It reminded them that they had not followed a stranger or an unknown; they had followed the most trustworthy man they had ever known, and his truthfulness in their daily lives was a guarantee of his truthfulness in conveying revelation.

=70=

The next question brings up the accusation that the disbelievers openly leveled: “Or because they say, ‘He is insane?’” The Quraysh, unable to refute the Qur’ân, resorted to calling the Prophet majnûn—possessed, mad, or mentally unstable. This was a convenient label because it allowed them to dismiss everything he said without having to engage with it. The verse immediately shatters this excuse: “In fact, he has come to them with the truth.

” The word “truth” (ḥaqq) is the exact opposite of the delusions of a madman. The message he brought is coherent, rational, morally upright, and confirmed by the signs in the universe and in the human soul. A madman does not produce a book of sublime eloquence, profound wisdom, and accurate historical and scientific details. A madman does not lead a movement that transforms a whole society from corruption to righteousness. The accusation of insanity was thus a transparent falsehood, a smokescreen for something deeper. The verse uncovers that deeper reason: “but most of them are resentful of the truth.

” The word “resentful” (kârihûn) indicates a strong emotional aversion. They did not merely disbelieve intellectually; they disliked the truth, they were uncomfortable with it, they felt it threatened their way of life, their status, and their desires. The truth demanded that they abandon their idols, give up their unjust economic practices, control their lusts, and accept accountability for their deeds. This was a bitter pill for people whose entire identity was built on the opposite. So their rejection was not born of a sincere intellectual objection but of a deep-seated emotional resistance. The verse thus peels away the layers of rationalization and gets to the heart of kufr: it is often not a problem of evidence but a problem of will. For the believers, this analysis was reassuring. It explained why the Quraysh could hear the most beautiful arguments and remain unmoved. Their hearts were not neutral; they were actively hostile to what the truth required of them. This understanding protects the caller to truth from frustration, reminding them that the rejection they face is often rooted in the listener’s attachments, not in the weakness of the message.

=71=

This verse presents a devastating logical argument against the disbelievers’ demand that revelation should conform to their whims. “Had the truth followed their desires, the heavens, the earth, and all those in them would have certainly been corrupted.” The Quraysh wanted a religion that validated their idolatry, their social hierarchy, their exploitation, and their neglect of the poor. If the truth—the divine order of reality—were to align with such selfish and conflicting desires, the entire universe would collapse into chaos. The heavens and the earth operate according to fixed, wise laws that do not cater to human caprice. Gravity does not ask permission; the sun does not rise according to someone’s preference. In the same way, the moral and spiritual truths revealed by Allah must be objective and unchanging, not subject to the fleeting desires of one tribe or one generation. If every people’s desires were followed, there would be no unified truth, only a clash of wills leading to universal corruption. This argument elevates the mind of the listener to see that divine revelation is a mercy precisely because it does not follow human desires; it provides a stable, perfect standard that corrects those desires.

The verse then turns the tables: “In fact, We have brought them the means to their glory, but they turn away from it.” The word “glory” (dhikr) can also mean remembrance, honor, and renown. The Qur’ân is the very source of true dignity and elevation for the Arabs and for all humanity. By following it, they would rise from the ignorance and tribalism of the pre-Islamic era to become leaders of a just and enlightened civilization. History proved this true: within a generation, those who embraced the Qur’ân moved from being a marginalized, backward people to the teachers of the world.

Yet the Quraysh turned away from the very thing that could have given them lasting honor. They preferred the cheap, temporary pride of their idols and traditions to the eternal glory offered by Allah. The verse thus highlights the tragic irony of their rejection: they dismissed their own gift, their own rescue.

=72=

The next question removes another possible excuse for rejection: “Or is it because you O Prophet are asking them for tribute?” The word “tribute” (kharjan) refers to a payment or financial compensation. Sometimes people reject a messenger because they suspect he is after their money. But the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, asked for no wages, no salary, and no material reward from them. The Qur’ân repeatedly emphasizes that the messengers say, “I ask you not for any reward; my reward is only with the Lord of the worlds.” The Meccans knew this. The Prophet did not live a life of luxury; he and his followers were often in poverty. So the question is a rhetorical device to show that there was absolutely no financial motive behind the message. This point is crucial because it removes the accusation of personal gain. The Prophet was not a charlatan seeking to enrich himself; he was a man who sacrificed everything for the truth.

The verse then offers a gentle encouragement to the Prophet: “But the reward of your Lord is best, for He is the Best Provider.” The Prophet is reminded not to be grieved by their rejection or their false accusations. He is not dependent on their tribute or their approval. Allah, who is the Best Provider (Khayr al-Râziqîn), has reserved for him a reward far greater than any worldly wealth. This also serves as a lesson for every caller to truth: do not let your motivation be tied to the acceptance or material support of people. Seek your reward from Allah, and you will never be disappointed.

The verse also indirectly rebukes the Quraysh for their stinginess and suspicion. They were so attached to their wealth that they even projected that attachment onto the Prophet. But the reality was the opposite: he was offering them eternal treasure without asking for a single coin in return, and they were turning it away because their hearts were locked in the love of this world. The verse invites the listener to reflect on the selflessness of the Messenger and the generosity of the One who sent him.

=73=

After removing all the false excuses, the sûrah now states the positive reality: “And surely you are calling them to the Straight Path.” The address is to the Prophet, peace be upon him, confirming the truth and nobility of his mission. The “Straight Path” (Ṣirâṭ al-Mustaqîm) is the comprehensive term in the Qur’ân for the way of life that leads to Allah’s pleasure and Paradise. It is the path of pure monotheism, righteous conduct, and spiritual purity. This verse stands as a definitive declaration that the message of Islam is not one among many equally valid paths; it is the Straight Path, the clear, direct road without crookedness.

The word “surely” (innaka) adds emphasis, reassuring the Prophet against the accusations of misguidance or insanity. He is not leading people astray; he is guiding them to the very purpose for which they were created. For the early Muslims, this was a powerful affirmation. They were often told by the Quraysh that they had abandoned the path of their fathers and fallen into error. This verse assured them that they were, in fact, on the only true path, and that the rejection of the Quraysh was the rejection of divine guidance itself. The Straight Path is a metaphor that every human being can understand. A straight road is the easiest, safest, and fastest way to reach a destination. Crooked paths lead to confusion, danger, and getting lost. The Prophet’s call was not a call to difficulty for its own sake; it was a call to the simplest, most direct route to eternal happiness.

The verse also acts as a pivot in the sûrah’s argument. After dismantling all the reasons for rejection, it presents the pure truth: the Prophet is offering the Straight Path. This leaves the disbelievers with no excuse; their rejection is not based on any fault in the message, but purely on their own stubbornness, as the next verse makes clear.

=74=

The verse completes the contrast: “but those who disbelieve in the Hereafter are certainly deviating from that Path.” The Straight Path is right before them, clear and accessible, yet they are swerving away from it. The verse identifies the root cause of their deviation: disbelief in the Hereafter.

This is a profound insight. If a person does not believe in a life after death, in judgment, and in reward and punishment, they have no compelling reason to adhere to moral discipline, to restrain their desires, or to sacrifice immediate pleasures for long-term good. The Hereafter is the anchor that gives meaning and urgency to the Straight Path. Without it, the path seems restrictive and pointless.

The word “deviating” (nâkibûn) means turning aside, swerving, or veering off the road. They are not standing still; they are actively moving away from guidance. The more they reject the Hereafter, the further they wander into the desert of heedlessness. This verse connects back to the earlier description of the believers who tremble in awe and race to good deeds precisely because they are certain of the return to their Lord. The disbelievers lack that certainty, so they have no engine to drive them toward righteousness and no brake to stop them from evil.

The verse also answers a practical question: why, when the Qur’ân’s arguments are so clear, do some people still not accept? The missing piece is often the heart’s refusal to accept accountability. The Hereafter is not rejected for intellectual reasons alone; it is rejected because it imposes moral responsibility. If there is no judgment, one can live as one pleases. The verse thus lays bare the moral psychology of disbelief. For the believers, this understanding breeds patience. They see that the disbelievers are not merely disagreeing on a theological point; they are running from the consequences of facing their own deeds. The verse calls all people to examine their own attachment to the Hereafter. Is their faith in the afterlife strong enough to keep them on the Straight Path, or are they gradually deviating?

=75=

The sûrah now reveals the depth of the disbelievers’ stubbornness through a hypothetical scenario: “Even if We had mercy on them and removed their affliction, they would still persist in their transgression, wandering blindly.” The “affliction” (ḍurr) here could refer to the famine, drought, or the specific hardships that the Meccans faced as a warning from Allah. The verse asserts that even if Allah showed them special mercy and lifted their difficulties, they would not change. Their hearts are so hardened that temporary relief would not lead to gratitude or reflection; they would simply return to their old ways with renewed vigor.

The phrase “persist in their transgression” (lajjû fî ṭughyânihim) uses the word lajjû, which means to stubbornly insist and refuse to budge. Their transgression (ṭughyân) is a willful overstepping of boundaries.

The image is of someone who, when the pressure is relieved, plunges back into rebellion with even more abandon, like a cork held underwater that shoots up the moment the hand is removed. The addition of “wandering blindly” (yaʿmahûn) depicts them as lost, confused, and stumbling, unable to see the path even when the obstacles are removed. This blindness is spiritual, a direct result of their persistent rejection. This verse serves several purposes. It consoles the Prophet by explaining that even extraordinary signs and relief would not convert these particular disbelievers, so he should not exhaust himself in grief over them. It also refutes the idea that the disbelievers only needed more evidence or better circumstances to believe. The problem was internal, not external. Mercy and ease, when given to a heart that is fundamentally rebellious, only increase it in arrogance.

This principle is echoed elsewhere: “When they forgot what they had been reminded of, We opened to them the gates of everything, until when they rejoiced in what they had been given, We seized them suddenly.” The verse thus warns that relief from hardship is not always a sign of divine approval; it can be a test that reveals the true state of the heart.

For believers, it teaches not to envy the respite given to the wrongdoers, because their outward ease may be a prelude to greater ruin.

=76=

The verse now moves from the hypothetical to the historical: “And We have already seized them with torment, but they never humbled themselves to their Lord, nor did they submissively appeal to Him.” The “torment” here refers to actual punishments that the Meccans experienced, such as the severe famine and drought that afflicted them, or the defeat they suffered at the Battle of Badr. These events were meant to wake them up, to break their pride, and to drive them back to Allah in humility. But the verse records their tragic response: they never humbled themselves (mâ takhshaʿû). Khushûʿ is the very quality of humility in prayer that describes the successful believers at the opening of the sûrah. The disbelievers lacked it completely, even when faced with clear signs of divine displeasure. Instead of turning inward and examining their deeds, they remained stiff and proud.

The second clause, “nor did they submissively appeal to Him” (mâ yataḍarraʿûn), deepens the indictment. Taḍarruʿ is the act of humbly supplicating, making oneself low and needy before Allah. They did not even call upon Him sincerely in their distress, despite the fact that human beings naturally cry out to God when in desperate situations. Their pride was so all-consuming that it blocked the most basic instinct of a creature in need of its Creator. This verse is a chilling portrait of the hardened heart. Even the whip of affliction cannot soften it. The purpose of calamities, from the Qur’ânic perspective, is not merely to cause pain but to produce spiritual awakening. When that purpose fails, the calamity becomes a witness against the person. For the believers, this verse is a reminder to check their own response to hardship. Do trials make them turn more earnestly to Allah, or do they respond with complaint and further hardening?

The verse also reinforces the sûrah’s earlier message that the disbelievers are not victims of ignorance; they have been given chances, but they have repeatedly refused to humble themselves.

=77=

After describing their failure to humble themselves under lesser afflictions, the verse predicts the outcome of a greater, final punishment: “But as soon as We open for them a gate of severe punishment, they will be utterly desperate.” The word “gate” (bâb) suggests a new dimension of suffering, something far more intense and inescapable than the previous torments. This could refer to the punishment of the Hereafter, which will open before them like a massive door that cannot be shut, or to a decisive worldly punishment like the conquest of Mecca or the death that overtakes them.

The phrase “severe punishment” (ʿadhâb shadîd) emphasizes that this is not a light warning; it is the full force of divine justice. When that gate opens, their previous arrogance, their refusal to humble themselves, and their mocking of the warnings will all collapse. The verse says “they will be utterly desperate” (idhâ hum fîhi mublisûn).

The word mublis comes from iblâs, meaning a state of utter despair, silence, and despondency. It is the condition of someone who has lost all hope, who sees no way out, and who is struck dumb by the enormity of their situation.

This is the very opposite of their former boasting and noisy rejection. At the opening of the sûrah, they were described as rejoicing in their sects and babbling nonsense by night. Now, at the opening of the gate of severe punishment, all that noise is extinguished. They are reduced to a state of silent, crushing despair. This verse serves as a powerful warning not to mistake the delay of punishment for its cancellation. The gate is shut now, but it will open, and when it does, it will be too late for the proud laughter to turn into anything but mute horror. For the believers, it is a reminder of the seriousness of the Hereafter and a motivation to live in such a way that when the gates of Paradise open for them, their state will be one of joyful anticipation, not desperate despair.

=78=

The sûrah now returns to the signs of Allah’s power and the ingratitude of the disbelievers. “He is the One Who created for you hearing, sight, and intellect.

Yet you hardly give any thanks.” This verse lists three of the most fundamental human faculties. Hearing (samʿ) is mentioned first, perhaps because it is the primary channel through which revelation is received. The Quraysh heard the Qur’ân with their ears, yet they plugged them and turned away. Sight (abṣâr) enables them to see the signs in the universe, the ruins of past nations, and the truth of creation, yet they remained blind in their hearts.

The term “intellect” (af’idah) refers to the heart or the core of understanding, the seat of comprehension and emotion. These three faculties together make a human being capable of receiving guidance, processing it, and responding to it with love and obedience.

The verse emphasizes that these are not self-developed; they are created by Allah. The phrase “for you” personalizes the gift, making each listener aware that their own hearing, sight, and heart are direct endowments from their Lord. And yet, the verse concludes, “you hardly give any thanks” (qalîlan mâ tashkurûn).

The phrase “hardly” (qalîlan) indicates that what little gratitude they do show—perhaps acknowledging Allah in moments of distress—is negligible compared to the immense blessings they receive. Most of the time, they use these faculties in disobedience, hearing falsehood, looking at what is forbidden, and filling their hearts with pride and heedlessness. The ingratitude is compounded by the fact that they use Allah’s gifts to deny Him. This verse echoes the opening of the sûrah, which praised the believers for their qualities. Here, the disbelievers are implicitly contrasted: the believers use their hearing, sight, and hearts to humble themselves in prayer, avoid idle talk, and tremble in awe; the disbelievers use the same faculties for denial and mockery. The verse calls every person to pause and reflect on the sheer wonder of being able to hear, see, and understand, and to ask themselves: am I using these gifts to draw closer to the One who gave them, or am I among those who hardly give thanks?

=79=

After mentioning the personal faculties, the verse moves to a larger sign: “And He is the One Who has dispersed you all over the earth, and to Him you will all be gathered.” The word “dispersed” (dhara’akum) means to scatter, to spread out, and to multiply. Allah has distributed human beings across the surface of the earth, into different lands, climates, races, and nations. This dispersion is itself a sign of His power and wisdom. Despite all humans originating from a single pair, they have spread to every corner of the globe, adapting to diverse environments, developing different languages and cultures, yet all sharing the same essential nature. The Quraysh, who prided themselves on their lineage and their central location in Mecca, are reminded that their presence there is not their own doing; it is part of a divine plan of dispersion.

The second half of the verse delivers the punchline: “and to Him you will all be gathered.” The scattering across the earth is not the end of the story. The same One who dispersed them will just as easily gather them back. No matter how far a person travels, no matter where their bones decay or their ashes scatter, the Creator who placed them on the earth in the first place will reassemble them for the Day of Judgment. This argument is a direct response to the disbelievers’ earlier question: “Once we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected?

” The answer is implied: if Allah could disperse humanity from a single origin to every corner of the earth, bringing them all back is no harder. The verse also carries a spiritual message. The dispersion means that no matter where a believer finds themselves, Allah is with them. And the gathering reminds them that their ultimate home is not the patch of earth they currently occupy; it is with their Lord. For the Meccan believers facing the possibility of exile and separation, this verse offered comfort: the earth is wide, but the final meeting is with Allah, and that meeting is certain.

=80=

The verse continues the catalogue of divine signs: “And He is the One Who gives life and causes death, and to Him belongs the alternation of the day and night. Will you not then understand?” Life and death are the most undeniable experiences of human existence. Every day, a child is born and an elder dies. The power to bring a living being from non-existence and then to extinguish that life is exclusively in the hand of Allah. No human, no idol, no force of nature can truly create life or restore it after death.

This fact is so obvious that it should lead any rational person to the conclusion that the One who holds this power is the only one worthy of worship. The verse then adds “and to Him belongs the alternation of the day and night. ” The rhythmic, flawless, and unceasing cycle of day and night is another sign. Night does not intrude upon the day prematurely, nor does the day overstay its bounds. This alternation provides the conditions for rest and work, for cold and warmth, for the entire ecological balance of the planet.

The phrase “to Him belongs” indicates ownership and control; it is not a mechanical process running on its own but a living sign of the Lord’s ongoing management of the universe. The verse then asks a direct, penetrating question: “Will you not then understand?” (a-fa-lâ taʿqilûn). The faculty of reason (ʿaql) is being appealed to. The signs are clear, the evidence is overwhelming, and the conclusion is inescapable for any mind not clouded by stubbornness. The Quraysh prided themselves on their intelligence and eloquence, yet they failed to use that intelligence to make the most basic logical deduction: the Creator who gives life, causes death, and controls time itself is the only one deserving of worship, and He is fully capable of resurrecting the dead.

The question is a gentle but firm rebuke, a final plea to the intellect before the sûrah moves on to expose the flimsy arguments of those who still refuse to understand.
