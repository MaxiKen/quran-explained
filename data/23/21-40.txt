=21=

After drawing attention to the gardens of dates, grapes, and olives, the sûrah now turns to another blessing that was central to the life of its first listeners: cattle. The verse opens with the affirmation, “And there is certainly a lesson for you in cattle.

” The word “lesson” (ʿibrah) points to a sign that enables one to cross over from the visible to the invisible, from the daily experience to the recognition of the Creator. Camels, cows, sheep, and goats were not just livestock; they were a living, moving classroom in which the Arabs could read the attributes of their Lord.

The next phrase, “from whose bellies We give you milk to drink,” isolates one of the most striking wonders. Milk is a pure, wholesome liquid extracted from between the partly digested food and the blood of the animal, yet it emerges white, sweet, and completely free of impurity. The Qur’ân elsewhere asks people to reflect on this very miracle: how something so pleasant comes forth from within a living being, perfectly suited for human nutrition. The wording emphasizes that it is Allah who “gives you” this milk; it is not the animal’s own doing, but a direct provision from the Creator. Then the verse broadens the reminder: “and in them are many other benefits for you.” Their wool, hair, and hides provide clothing and shelter; their strength enables the carrying of heavy burdens; and their dung serves as fuel. The list of benefits was almost inexhaustible for the pastoral society of Arabia.

The final clause, “and from them you may eat,” reminds that their meat is also a lawful sustenance. All of these blessings together demonstrate the comprehensive mercy of Allah. He did not merely create animals; He designed their bodies to yield what human beings need for food, clothing, transport, and warmth. This verse thus fits into the broader argument of the sûrah: the One who gives such elaborate and considerate provision in this life will certainly not abandon humanity to meaninglessness after death. The cattle, which were a daily part of the Arabian experience, were a tangible proof that the same Creator who raises the dead earth with rain and brings forth milk from within animals is fully able to raise the dead to life for judgment.

=22=

The blessing of cattle is now extended to the theme of transport: “And you are carried upon ˹some of˺ them and upon ships.” The verse first refers to riding upon animals—camels, horses, mules, and donkeys—which were the chief means of land travel in the ancient world.

The phrase “some of them” acknowledges that not all cattle are ridden; some are used for milk, meat, or wool. The ability to mount an animal and travel long distances through harsh deserts would have been a deeply appreciated sign for the Meccan audience, whose trade caravans to Syria and Yemen were made possible by the camel. Immediately after mentioning these animals, the verse adds “and upon ships.

” This is remarkable, because ships are not cattle, yet they are placed beside them in this lesson. Ships are vessels that float upon the sea by the design and mercy of Allah, carrying people and goods. In a Meccan context, where the Quraysh themselves organized two major trading journeys, one by land and one partially by sea, this juxtaposition was directly relevant. It tells the listener that whether you travel on the back of a camel or on the deck of a ship, you are being carried by the power of Allah. The ship, like the animal, is a creature of divine provision; the wind that fills its sails, the buoyancy that keeps it afloat, and the intelligence to build it are all gifts from the Creator.

This verse also connects to the theme of resurrection. Just as a person is carried over the earth and the sea, they will one day be carried from the grave to the plain of standing. The One who subdued the powerful camel and the mighty ocean for human use is certainly able to bring His servants forth on the Day of Judgment. The verse humbles human pride by reminding travelers that they are not self-sufficient; at every moment of a journey, they depend on forces they do not control.

Thus, a simple act of riding becomes a call to gratitude and a pointer to the ultimate journey back to Allah.

=23=

With verse 23, the sûrah begins a series of prophetic narratives that illustrate the response of earlier nations to the call of truth, reinforcing the themes of faith, denial, and final outcome. The story of Noah is the first of these accounts. “Indeed, We sent Noah to his people” establishes the principle that Allah does not leave humanity without guidance. Noah was sent as a messenger to a people who had fallen into idolatry and corruption. The core of his message is identical to the message of every prophet: “O my people! Worship Allah ˹alone˺. You have no god other than Him.

” This is the central declaration of tawḥîd, the uncompromised oneness of God. Noah did not begin by discussing secondary matters; he went straight to the root of all guidance—to direct worship toward the Creator alone and to free the heart from false deities. The words “O my people” convey a tone of loving concern and shared identity. He is not a stranger or a tyrant; he is one of them, deeply worried about their state. The call to worship Allah alone is immediately followed by the question, “Will you not then fear ˹Him˺? ” The fear intended here is taqwâ, a protective consciousness of Allah that guards the soul from His displeasure. It is the fear of a person who knows that their Lord is aware of them and that they will stand before Him. Noah’s message thus combines intellectual truth with an emotional appeal. The people were being asked to recognize the absurdity of worshiping idols made by their own hands and to turn with reverent awe to the One who created them.

In the context of Sûrat Al-Mu’minûn, this opening of the narrative parallels the situation of the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, in Mecca. He, too, was a man from among his people, calling them to the same pure tawḥîd, and facing denial from the chiefs who benefited from the old order. The story of Noah was a source of comfort and a warning. It taught the believers that their struggle was not new, and that the outcome would follow the pattern established throughout history.

=24=

The response of Noah’s people is recorded with precision: “But the disbelieving chiefs of his people said ˹to the masses˺, ‘This is only a human like you, who wants to be superior to you.’” The leaders, the mala’, were the wealthy and influential elite who felt their status threatened by a message that placed all human beings on an equal footing before the Lord. Their first tactic was to reduce Noah to an ordinary person with worldly ambitions. By saying “he wants to be superior to you,” they planted the suspicion that Noah’s call to worship one God was merely a cover for his desire for power and leadership.

This is a classic argument of tyrants: to accuse the truthful of hidden motives. Then they moved to a theological objection: “Had Allah willed, He could have easily sent down angels ˹instead˺.” In their shallow reasoning, a true messenger should be an angel, something spectacular and otherworldly, not a man who eats and walks in the markets. This same objection would be hurled at the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. The Qur’ân elsewhere answers that if angels walked the earth, an angelic messenger would have been sent, but the messengers are chosen from among men so that they can be examples, and so that the test of faith remains meaningful. The chiefs then reinforced their rejection with tradition: “We have never heard of this in ˹the history of˺ our forefathers.” For them, the mere fact that their ancestors did not proclaim such a message was proof enough that it was false. This appeal to ancestral custom was a stubborn barrier to truth. The verse exposes a recurring pattern of disbelief: the elite fear loss of influence, so they manipulate the masses by mocking the messenger, demanding supernatural signs, and appealing to blind loyalty to inherited ways. In the Meccan context, this verse would have resonated deeply with the early Muslims, as they witnessed almost identical arguments from the Quraysh chiefs. The story thus becomes a mirror, allowing the believers to see that the opposition they face is not due to any fault in their message but is a repeated human reaction against the call to abandon false gods and submit to the One Creator.

=25=

The chiefs’ attack escalates from questioning Noah’s motives to assaulting his very sanity: “He is simply insane, so bear with him for a while.” They could not refute the truth of what he said, so they resorted to labeling him as majnûn, a man possessed or afflicted with madness. This was a convenient label because it allowed people to dismiss his warnings without having to think about them seriously. If he was insane, then no intelligent person needed to consider his arguments; they could simply feel pity or annoyance and move on. The advice “so bear with him for a while” reveals their strategy. They did not believe they could silence him immediately, perhaps because he had some supporters or because outright violence would cause a backlash. So they counseled patience—not the patience of seeking truth, but the patience of waiting for a problem to resolve itself. They hoped that Noah would eventually tire, or that public interest would fade, or that something would happen to him.

This is the passive-aggressive approach of those who are confident that time will extinguish the flame of truth. However, the verse subtly signals that their calculation was fatally wrong.

The word “simply” in the translation reflects the dismissive tone they used. They reduced a profound prophetic message to the ravings of a madman.

Yet history proved that Noah was the sanest among them, the only one who saw reality clearly, while they were rushing toward destruction in a flood of heedlessness. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, and his companions, this verse was a direct parallel. They too were called insane, poets, and sorcerers. The instruction to “bear with him for a while” echoed the Meccan chiefs’ decision to tolerate the Prophet temporarily, hoping the movement would die out. This verse thus teaches believers that being maligned and accused of madness is part of the prophetic path, and that they should not be shaken by such insults, because the verdict of truth belongs to Allah alone.

=26=

After centuries of patient calling, Noah finally turns to his Lord with a brief and heart-heavy prayer: “My Lord! Help me, because they have denied ˹me˺.” The prayer is remarkably restrained. Noah does not demand immediate destruction; he does not ask for personal revenge. He simply states the fact: they have denied me.

The word “help me” translates the Arabic intiṣâr, which means to grant victory or to judge in one’s favor. Noah is asking Allah to judge between him and his people after his message has been rejected persistently. This prayer came after he had exhausted every method of invitation—public and private, day and night—yet his people only grew more obstinate, plugging their ears and covering their faces. The Qur’ân elsewhere records that Noah said, “My Lord, do not leave upon the earth from among the disbelievers an inhabitant.” But that full expression came later, after revelation informed him that no more of his people would believe. Here, the focus is on the simple, dignified plea for divine intervention. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, this verse offered a model. When the Quraysh denied him, he too was to turn to Allah, not with bitterness or haste, but with humble dependence. This prayer acknowledges that ultimate change comes only from Allah. No amount of human effort can open hearts if Allah does not will it. At the same time, it signals a turning point. When a messenger has done all he can and the people remain in denial, the matter is handed over to the Judge of all.

The verse also reminds the believers that being denied is painful, but it is not a sign of failure. Noah, one of the greatest messengers, was denied by the majority of his people for hundreds of years.

Yet he is honored forever, and those who mocked him are remembered only in curses. The prayer is thus a lesson in turning to Allah when all doors seem shut, trusting that the One who sent the message will certainly protect it and give the final victory to His sincere servants.

=27=

The answer to Noah’s prayer comes not in words of comfort but in detailed instructions for a momentous action: “So We inspired him: ‘Build the Ark under Our watchful Eyes and directions.’” The inspiration was direct divine guidance. Noah was to construct a massive vessel under the protective gaze and teaching of Allah.

The phrase “under Our Eyes” means under divine care, supervision, and preservation. He would not be alone in this enormous task; Allah would watch over him and ensure the work was done correctly. The specific trigger for boarding is then given: “Then when Our command comes and the oven bursts with water.” Commentators have explained “the oven” (al-tannûr) in various ways; some say it was a baker’s oven from which water suddenly gushed as a sign, while others interpret it as the surface of the earth boiling over with springs. The essential meaning is that a clear signal would mark the start of the flood. At that moment, Noah was commanded to “take on board a pair from every species along with your family —except those against whom the decree to drown has already been passed. ” The ark would preserve the believers and the animal species from the coming deluge. The exception about his family is a somber note; not all family ties would save a person. Noah’s own son would later refuse to board and would drown.

The verse adds a firm prohibition: “And do not plead with Me for those who have done wrong, for they will surely be drowned. ” This instruction prepared Noah for the painful moment when his parental love would pull at his heart, yet he was to accept that divine justice must take its course. The details of the ark’s building, the pair of each species, and the exclusion of the condemned wrongdoers all underscore the absolute control of Allah over the events. This verse connects back to the sûrah’s theme of resurrection by showing a historical example of mass destruction and the saving of the righteous few, a pattern that the Meccans were invited to contemplate as a warning.

=28=

The instruction continues with what Noah should say once he and the believers are safely aboard the ark: “Then when you and those with you have settled in the Ark, say, ‘All praise is for Allah, Who saved us from the wrongdoing people.’” This verse teaches the proper response upon deliverance from calamity—immediate gratitude and open praise to Allah. Noah is not to congratulate himself on his skill in building the ark or his foresight in gathering supplies. The praise is to be directed entirely to Allah, who is identified as the sole savior. The description of the drowned people as “the wrongdoing people” emphasizes that their destruction was not an arbitrary act of nature but a direct consequence of their ẓulm, their persistent oppression and rejection of truth. They had wronged themselves by disbelieving, wronged their messenger by mocking him, and wronged the divine order by worshiping false gods.

Thus, their end was entirely just. Noah’s proclamation of praise would serve as both a personal expression of relief and a public declaration of the reason for his survival. The believers on the ark were a small remnant, but they were the carriers of faith and the seed of a new beginning for humanity after the flood.

This verse also contains a profound principle for all believers: when a difficulty is lifted, when a danger passes, the first and most appropriate response is ḥamd—sincere praise to Allah. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, would often say upon finishing a matter, “All praise is due to Allah, by whose favor righteous deeds are completed. ” In the context of the Meccan persecution, this verse was a promise that a day of deliverance would come, and when it did, the believers should not forget the source of their salvation. The ark of Noah prefigures the various means by which Allah saves His faithful servants from the destruction that befalls the obstinate deniers, whether through migration, victory, or a natural end.

=29=

After the praise, Noah is taught a second supplication: “And pray, ‘My Lord! Allow me a blessed landing, for You are the best accommodator.’” The ark had been a place of safety during the flood, but it was not meant to be a permanent home. The time would come to disembark and begin life anew on the cleansed earth. Noah is therefore instructed to ask for a landing that is mubârak, full of blessing, goodness, and divine favor.

The word “landing” (munzal) can refer both to the place where the ark would come to rest and to the act of descending and settling. He prays for a safe and auspicious conclusion to the journey, a landing free from harm and accompanied by ease and prosperity. This supplication shows that even a prophet of towering faith does not rely on his own merit; he turns to Allah for every transition, recognizing that all conditions of life are under divine control.

The closing phrase, “for You are the best accommodator,” expresses complete trust. It is Allah who settles people on the earth, provides them with homes, and grants them hospitality in every sense. By calling Him “the best accommodator” (khayr al-munzilîn), Noah acknowledges that no human host can provide the security and blessing that Allah can. This prayer teaches that the end of one trial is the beginning of another phase of dependence on Allah. When the flood was over, Noah would need Allah just as much as he needed Him during the storm. The believers in Mecca, facing the prospect of an uncertain future, were also learning that their journey—whether they remained in Mecca or eventually migrated—required constant turning to Allah for a blessed outcome.

This verse also points forward to the ultimate landing on the Day of Judgment, when every soul will seek a blessed station. The prayer of Noah thus becomes a model for all believers when they complete a journey, move to a new home, or enter a new stage of life.

=30=

The narrative of Noah concludes with a summary statement: “Surely in this are lessons. And We always put people to the test.

” The word “lessons” translates âyât, which also means signs. The entire story of Noah—his calling, the rejection of his people, the building of the ark, the flood, the rescue of the believers, and the landing—is filled with signs for those who reflect. It is a sign of Allah’s power to destroy and to save, a sign of the consequences of persistent denial, a sign of the patience required of messengers, and a sign of the final separation between truth and falsehood.

The verse then lays down a universal principle: “And We always put people to the test.” The testing of human beings is a constant divine practice. Every generation and every individual faces trials that reveal the sincerity of their faith. The people of Noah were tested with a messenger who called them to abandon their idols. The Quraysh were being tested with the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, and the message of the Qur’ân. And every believer after them will be tested with various forms of hardship, temptation, and the challenge of remaining steadfast in a world that often mocks faith.

This statement places the Meccan persecution in a larger context. The difficulties that the early Muslims endured were not a sign of divine abandonment but exactly the opposite—they were the fulfillment of the eternal pattern by which believers are purified and distinguished from those who merely profess faith.

The verse also serves as a warning: the test is not a one-time event but an ongoing reality. Just as Noah’s people failed their test and were destroyed, so too would the Quraysh face the consequences if they persisted in denial.

Yet embedded in the warning is also comfort for the believers: their suffering is recognized, their patience is seen, and their test will soon be followed by a blessed landing, as it was for Noah. Thus the verse seamlessly connects the ancient story to the immediate situation of the listeners.

=31=

After the destruction of Noah’s people, the verse states: “Then We raised another generation after them.” The word “raised” (ansha’nâ) implies bringing into existence and causing to grow. After the flood, humanity began anew from the believers who were on the ark. But as time passed, new generations arose, and with the passage of centuries, the memory of the deluge faded. People returned to their old patterns of arrogance and idolatry. This verse serves as a transition from the story of Noah to the story of the people of Hûd, but it also teaches a profound lesson about human nature. History is not simply a straight line of progress; it is a cycle of heedlessness, prophetic warning, belief or denial, and then divine judgment. The fact that Allah “raised another generation” shows that His plan continues. The destruction of one disbelieving people does not mean the end of the world; rather, a new people are given their own chance, their own messenger, and their own test.

In the context of the sûrah, this verse reassures the believers that the denial of the Quraysh is not a new phenomenon. Many nations before them were destroyed and replaced, and the earth continued.

Thus the Muslims should not lose hope or think that the cause of truth is defeated when one community rejects it; Allah simply brings forth new people who may respond differently. The verse also subtly warns the Meccans: they are a generation that has arisen after previous ones, and if they persist in the path of those earlier destroyed nations, they will share their fate.

The phrase “after them” links this new generation to the obliterated past, reminding the listener that the ruins of those who came before are still visible in the landscape, and the same Lord who replaced them can just as easily replace anyone else.

=32=

To this new generation, Allah sent a messenger from their own ranks: “and sent to them a messenger from among themselves, declaring, ‘Worship Allah alone. You have no god other than Him. Will you not then fear Him?’” Although the name is not mentioned here, the context identifies him as the prophet Hûd, who was sent to the people of ʿÂd. The message is exactly identical to the message of Noah. The verbatim repetition of the call highlights the unchanging core of all prophetic missions. Whether it was Noah, Hûd, or Muhammad, the first and foremost call was to tawḥîd—the pure worship of Allah without partners—and to taqwâ—the fear and consciousness of Allah that leads to obedience. The messenger is described as being “from among themselves,” which underscores a recurring argument of the disbelievers. They could not claim that the messenger was an alien who did not understand their culture or their ways. Hûd was a man of ʿÂd, known for his honesty and wisdom before his prophethood. His call was not an imported ideology but the revival of the original truth that was known to Adam, Noah, and all the prophets.

The question, “Will you not then fear Him?” is an appeal to reason and conscience. After reminding them that there is no god but Allah, the messenger invites them to draw the logical conclusion: that they should fear displeasing this one true God. In the Meccan context, the repetition of this identical call showed the Quraysh that Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, was not bringing a strange new religion but was restoring the same ancient faith that Hûd, Noah, and all the messengers had taught. The verse thus reinforces the continuity of the divine message and places the Meccan opposition in the long line of those who rejected the call to tawḥîd.

=33=

The pattern of rejection repeats with striking similarity to the story of Noah: “But the chiefs of his people—who disbelieved, denied the meeting with Allah in the Hereafter, and were spoiled by the worldly luxuries We had provided for them—said to the masses, ‘This is only a human like you. He eats what you eat, and drinks what you drink.’” The verse gives a penetrating analysis of the elite’s psychology. They “disbelieved” out of stubborn refusal; they “denied the meeting with Allah in the Hereafter” because accountability threatened their unrestrained lifestyle; and they “were spoiled by the worldly luxuries We had provided for them,” meaning that their wealth, comfort, and power had made them arrogant and heedless. Their material abundance became a veil that blocked their spiritual sight. Their objection to the messenger is identical to what the chiefs of Noah said: he is just a human being. They emphasized that “he eats what you eat, and drinks what you drink,” reducing the prophet to the most basic bodily functions. In their logic, a person who needs food and drink cannot possibly be a recipient of divine revelation. They wanted a messenger who was superhuman, an angel, or at least someone who did not share in ordinary human needs. This argument was not born from a sincere search for truth but from an aversion to following someone they considered their equal. If a prophet was just a man, then accepting his message would mean submitting to one of their own, which their pride could not tolerate. The detail about eating and drinking would also resonate with the Meccan elite, who used the exact same criticism against Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, saying, “What is this messenger? He eats food and walks in the markets. ” The verse thus exposes a timeless arrogance: material comfort blinds people, and then they demand a spiritual guide who conforms to their own inflated standards rather than humbly accepting the one whom Allah has chosen.

=34=

The chiefs press their argument further by warning the common people against following Hûd: “And if you ever obey a human like yourselves, then you would certainly be losers.” This is the language of elite manipulation. The chiefs presented themselves as guardians of the people’s interests, as if they were protecting them from a bad deal. They framed obedience to a mortal prophet as a path to certain loss—loss of status, loss of ancestral tradition, loss of the comfortable life they enjoyed.

The word “losers” (khâsirûn) is heavy, carrying the idea of being defrauded or ending up with nothing. The irony, of course, is that obeying the messenger was the only way to true success, and rejecting him led to the absolute loss of destruction in this world and punishment in the next. The chiefs’ logic rested entirely on a materialistic and arrogant worldview. They measured worth by external factors: if the messenger is not richer, more powerful, or visibly different from us, then following him must diminish us. They could not grasp that true nobility lies in faith and piety, not in wealth or physical distinction.

This verse also reveals a tactic that oppressors often use: they appeal to the people’s fear of losing out. They paint the path of truth as a risk not worth taking, while presenting their own corrupt way as the safe and profitable option. For the early Muslims in Mecca, this struck a familiar chord. The Quraysh elders told the tribes that following Muhammad would mean losing their ancestral religion, angering the gods, and forfeiting their trading privileges. The believers were accused of being losers who had abandoned the secure way of their forefathers.

Yet the sûrah has already declared in its opening that the believers are the truly successful ones. The contradiction between the chiefs’ warning of loss and Allah’s promise of success could not be sharper.

=35=

The chiefs now attack the doctrine of resurrection directly: “Does he promise you that once you are dead and reduced to dust and bones, you will be brought forth alive?” They mock the idea that after the body has decayed and scattered into the earth, it could possibly be reassembled and given life again. By phrasing it as a question, they aim to make the belief sound absurd on its face. They reduce the human being to nothing but “dust and bones,” deliberately ignoring the power of the Creator who made them from nothing in the first place. Their sarcasm reflects a deep-seated resistance to the idea of accountability. If death is the end, then they can live as they please without consequence. But if resurrection is real, then their entire lifestyle of luxury and oppression would have to be answered for. So they fight the message at this critical point. The Qur’ân constantly confronts this objection by pointing to the original creation as evidence. The One who created the heavens and the earth, who fashioned the human being from a drop of fluid, is certainly able to bring them back. The dust and bones are not lost to His knowledge; every particle is recorded.

The question of the chiefs is not an honest intellectual inquiry; it is a rhetorical device to discredit the messenger. In the Meccan context, this exact mockery was directed at Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. The pagans would hold up decayed bones and ask, “Who can give life to these bones when they have rotted away?” The Qur’ân answers them in other chapters with the clear statement that the One who originated the creation will repeat it. The verse here records the words of the disbelievers without immediate rebuttal, allowing the weight of the later destruction to serve as the answer. It also demonstrates that the conflict between faith and disbelief has always revolved around the reality of the Hereafter.

=36=

The chiefs intensify their rejection with an emphatic dismissal: “Impossible, simply impossible is what you are promised!” The translation captures the force of the Arabic word hayhâta hayhâta, an exclamation used to express extreme remoteness and utter improbability. By repeating the word, they hammer home their conviction that resurrection is so far-fetched as to be unworthy of serious consideration. They are not merely doubting; they are declaring with absolute confidence that it cannot happen. This emotional outburst reveals the depth of their attachment to their worldly view. They have so completely invested themselves in the present life that the very idea of a future life threatens their entire framework of meaning. Rather than examine the evidence, they shout it down with ridicule.

The phrase “what you are promised” refers to Hûd’s warnings of resurrection and judgment. By calling these promises impossible, they simultaneously call Hûd a liar or a deluded fool. This echoes the earlier accusation that Noah was insane. In both cases, the disbelievers try to use the force of language—mockery, exclamation, labeling—to shut down the message before it can take root in the hearts of the people. The verse captures a moment of supreme arrogance, but the listener knows, from the overall context of the sûrah, that what they call impossible is not only possible but certain. The very heavens and earth they stand upon were created by Allah, and He will just as easily recreate them.

The repetition of “impossible” stands as a monument to human stubbornness, a monument that would soon be washed away by the blast of divine punishment. For the believers, this verse serves as a warning not to be intimidated by the loud denials of those who reject the afterlife, because such denials are rooted in wishful thinking, not in sound reasoning or evidence.

=37=

The chiefs now articulate their full worldview: “There is nothing beyond our worldly life. We die, others are born, and none will be resurrected.

” This is the creed of materialism in its bluntest form. They reduce existence entirely to what can be seen and touched. Life is a cycle of generations: one group dies, another is born, and nothing lies beyond this endless repetition. There is no judgment, no higher purpose, no moral accountability extending beyond death. This belief was not unique to the people of ʿÂd; it has been the philosophy of every hedonistic and arrogant civilization throughout history. It allows people to pursue pleasure, power, and wealth without any concern for eternal consequences. By stating “none will be resurrected,” they directly contradict the core message of all prophets. The verse places this declaration immediately after their dismissal of the resurrection as “impossible,” showing how their denial of the afterlife is the foundation of their entire rejection. If there is no resurrection, then the messenger’s call to worship Allah alone and to fear Him loses its urgency. Ethics become a matter of personal or social convenience rather than divine command. The Qur’ân challenges this worldview by repeatedly pointing to the signs in creation that indicate a wise and purposeful Creator. A Creator who does everything with wisdom does not create human beings with a sense of justice and a longing for ultimate meaning, only to let them vanish into nothingness. The very existence of a moral conscience points to a day of reckoning.

The verse also exposes the despair that underlies the materialism of the disbelievers. On their view, death is the ultimate end, and life is a fleeting phenomenon without lasting significance.

In contrast, the sûrah began with the triumphant success of the believers, who know that their life stretches into an eternal, blissful future. The two worldviews could not be more opposed.

=38=

The chiefs end their speech with a definitive judgment: “He is no more than a man who has fabricated a lie about Allah, and we will never believe in him.” They have moved from calling him an ordinary human, to warning that following him leads to loss, to mocking his message of resurrection, and now they brand him a deliberate liar. The accusation of fabricating a lie against Allah is the most serious charge that can be leveled against a prophet. They do not entertain the possibility that he might be sincere but mistaken; they declare him a fraud. This serves two purposes for them: it justifies their rejection on moral grounds, as if they are standing up for truth against falsehood, and it closes the door to any future reconsideration.

The phrase “and we will never believe in him” is a statement of absolute, final refusal. They have locked their hearts.

This is the language of people who have allowed pride to calcify into permanent disbelief. The verse holds a mirror to the Meccan pagans, who used nearly identical words about the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. They said, “This is nothing but a lie he has fabricated, and other people have helped him with it.” The Qur’ân responds by asking them to produce a chapter like it if they truly believed it was a human fabrication. Here, the narrative simply records the chiefs’ words and then moves to the consequences, letting the destruction serve as the irrefutable answer.

The verse also teaches the believers an important lesson about the nature of disbelief. When people reach the point of saying “we will never believe,” they are not making a rational decision; they are making an emotional pledge to their own ego. The messenger’s task is to deliver the message with patience, and the final judgment belongs to Allah alone.

=39=

Just as Noah did, the messenger Hûd turns to Allah with a simple and powerful plea: “My Lord! Help me, because they have denied me.” The prayer is verbatim the same as Noah’s prayer in verse 26. This repetition is deeply significant. It shows that the response of the prophets to persistent, stubborn denial is not despair, anger, or a violent reaction, but a quiet, dignified turning to the only true source of help. Hûd had done his duty. He had called his people to the worship of Allah alone, he had reasoned with them, and he had borne their insults. When their rejection became final and they declared they would never believe, he placed the matter in the hands of the Judge. The prayer also reveals that the denial was not just a rejection of ideas but a personal wound. The messenger had come with truth and mercy, yet he was called a liar and a fabricator. He asked for help (intisâr) not out of wounded pride but because the honor of Allah’s message was at stake. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, this verse was a direct parallel and a source of comfort. He too faced a people who had denied him and accused him of fabrication. He was taught to respond with patience and to seek refuge in his Lord. The similarity of the prayers also emphasizes the unity of the prophetic experience. The messengers are brothers, and their trials, emotions, and ultimate recourse to Allah follow the same pattern. This verse is a reminder for all believers that when one has done everything possible to convey the truth and the response is mockery and denial, the best and most powerful thing to do is to raise one’s hands and say, “My Lord, help me.” The victory will come, as the next verse shows.

The response to Hûd’s prayer is immediate and decisive: “Allah responded, ‘Soon they will be truly regretful.’” The word “soon” (ʿammâ qalîl) indicates that the punishment is near, and “truly regretful” (la-yuṣbiḥunna nâdimîn) promises a state of intense, belated remorse. The chiefs who arrogantly dismissed the messenger and declared they would never believe will find themselves overwhelmed by a reality they denied. Their regret, however, will come at a time when it can no longer benefit them.

This is a recurring theme in the Qur’ân: when the punishment arrives, the disbelievers cry out for another chance, but their repentance is not accepted. The verse does not immediately describe the form of the punishment; it simply states the psychological consequence—regret.

This is a profound answer to Hûd’s prayer. The prophet did not ask for their destruction out of personal vengeance; he asked for help. Allah’s help takes the form of demonstrating the truth of the message so clearly that the deniers are forced to confront their error, though too late to change their fate. The regret will be all the more painful because they will remember their own words—how they mocked the messenger, called the resurrection impossible, and pledged never to believe. For the Meccan disbelievers listening to this narrative, the verse served as a direct warning: the same Allah who responded to Hûd is responding to Muhammad, peace be upon him. If they persist, they too will soon taste a regret that no amount of worldly riches can soothe. For the believers, this verse offers the reassurance that the mockery they endure will not go unanswered. Divine justice may delay, but it is certain, and when it comes, those who laughed last will weep, and those who were wronged will be vindicated.

The word “truly” emphasizes that their regret will not be a passing sadness but a deep, soul-wrenching remorse that will define their state.
