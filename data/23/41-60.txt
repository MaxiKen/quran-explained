=41=

The divine response to Hûd’s prayer now unfolds with terrifying swiftness. “Then the mighty blast overtook them with justice.

” The word “blast” (ṣayḥah) indicates a thunderous shout or a piercing sound from the sky. According to many commentators, the punishment of the people of ʿÂd was a violently cold, screaming wind that raged for days, but the Qur’ân here uses the term blast to capture the suddenness and devastating power of what came upon them.

The phrase “with justice” (bil-ḥaqq) is crucial. This destruction was not a random act of nature or a fit of divine anger without cause; it was the execution of a perfectly just decree. Every person who perished had been warned, had received the clear message, and had consciously chosen denial and oppression over faith. The justice of Allah does not wrong anyone; it gives them ample opportunity and then holds them accountable for their choices.

The verse continues, “and We reduced them to rubble” (fa-jaʿalnâhum ghuthâ’an). The word ghuthâ’ originally refers to the dried-up scum and debris carried by a flood, the worthless leftovers that are cast aside. A people who had been strong, wealthy, and proud, who had boasted of their great buildings and their luxurious lives, were suddenly turned into nothing more than scattered refuse. This imagery is meant to strike the heart with the reality of divine power. All human might, no matter how imposing, is utterly fragile before the command of Allah.

The verse then closes with a powerful refrain: “So away with the wrongdoing people!” This is not a statement of petty anger but a divine verdict that echoes through the ages. The ẓulm they committed—wronging their own souls by rejecting truth, wronging their messenger by mocking him, and wronging the divine order by worshiping false gods—made them deserving of being cast far from mercy. The expression “away with” (buʿdan) indicates banishment from Allah’s goodness. For the Meccan elite who listened to these verses, the warning could not be clearer. They were walking the exact path of ʿÂd, and if they did not turn back, the same justice would reduce them to rubble and leave them as cautionary tales for later generations.

=42=

After the destruction of ʿÂd, the verse states simply: “Then We raised other generations after them.” History did not stop. The obliteration of one tyrannical people was followed by the rise of others.

The word “raised” (ansha’nâ) again indicates the creative bringing forth of new communities. Some of these were the people of Thamûd, the people of Lot, and the people of Midian, whose stories are told elsewhere in the Qur’ân, though this sûrah will not detail each one separately. The point of this brief verse is to establish the rhythm of history under divine oversight. Allah does not destroy a nation and then abandon the earth; He populates it afresh, giving new nations their own opportunity to believe, obey, and flourish, or to disbelieve and face their own reckoning. This teaches the believers that the rise and fall of civilizations is entirely in the hands of Allah. A nation that today appears invincible may tomorrow be replaced by another. The Muslims in Mecca, few and weak as they were, could take hope from this. They were the seed of a new generation, a generation that would rise on the ruins of the idolatrous order if the Quraysh continued to refuse the message. At the same time, the verse warns the new generations themselves not to be arrogant. They should not look at the ruins of past peoples and think they are immune. The same Lord who raised them can just as easily replace them. There is also a note of mercy in this cycle: by raising new generations, Allah gives humanity more chances to turn to Him. The door of faith is never permanently closed until the final hour. Every new group that emerges is a fresh invitation to know and worship the Creator. The concise phrasing, without naming specific nations, keeps the focus on the universal principle rather than on historical detail. It serves as a bridge between the specific story of Hûd and the general declaration that follows about the unchangeable law of destruction.

=43=

This verse articulates a fundamental law of divine judgment: “No people can advance their doom, nor can they delay it.” Every nation has an appointed term (ajal) fixed by Allah’s knowledge and wisdom. When a people’s transgressions reach the point where judgment becomes inevitable, the punishment descends precisely on schedule. They cannot bring it forward by their provocations, nor can they push it back through their power, wealth, or schemes. This directly counters the thinking of the arrogant chiefs who assumed they were in control of their own destiny. They mocked the prophets and challenged them to bring on the punishment, saying, “When will this promise come to pass, if you are truthful?” They thought that the delay was proof of the message’s falsehood. But the delay was itself part of the divine plan, an extension of mercy to give them time to repent. When that appointed time arrived, no force could hold it back for a single moment. The verse provides immense comfort to the believers who were being persecuted and wondering when Allah’s help would come. It reassures them that the timing of deliverance and the punishment of the oppressors is not random; it is set by a wisdom that sees the bigger picture. The believer’s task is to be patient, to continue calling to truth, and to trust that the Judge of all will act at the right moment—not too early and not too late. At the same time, it serves as a sober warning to those who enjoy a period of safety while living in sin. A person might think, “I have been sinning for years and nothing has happened to me, so I can continue.” This verse answers that the term is simply not yet due, but when it arrives, it cannot be delayed. The principle applies to individuals and communities alike. The Quraysh were not an exception; they too had a term, and every day that passed brought them closer to it. This verse thus anchors the entire discussion of destroyed nations in the unwavering justice and timing of Allah.

=44=

The historical pattern is now summarized in one sweeping verse: “Then We sent Our messengers in succession.” The phrase “in succession” (tatrâ) conveys the image of messengers coming one after another without long gaps, each bearing the same essential call to tawḥîd.

The verse then reveals the tragic human response: “whenever a messenger came to his people, they denied him.” This was not an isolated incident with Noah or Hûd; it was the recurring story of humanity. Despite the variety of messengers, the different times and places, and the diverse proofs they brought, the majority reaction was denial.

The verse then states the consequence with absolute clarity: “So We destroyed them, one after the other, reducing them to cautionary tales.” The phrase “one after the other” mirrors “in succession,” showing that as the messengers followed one another, so did the destructions. Each obliterated nation became aḥâdîth—stories told and retold, lessons for those who came after. Their mighty civilizations, their towers and gardens, were reduced to narratives passed around campfires.

The verse ends with the same refrain heard after the story of ʿÂd: “So away with the people who refuse to believe!” The word “refuse” highlights the element of willful rejection. These people did not lack evidence; they chose disbelief. The refrain acts as a powerful rhetorical boundary, marking the end of a section and hammering home the moral. For the Meccan Arabs, the ruins of some of these nations were visible along their trade routes—the dwellings of Thamûd carved into rock, the remains of ʿÂd in the sands. They passed by these sites and heard the tales, yet they did not take heed. The verse challenges them directly: you know these stories, you see the ruins, yet you continue in the same denial. What makes you think you will escape the pattern? This sweeping historical summary also strengthens the heart of the Prophet, peace be upon him. He is not alone in his rejection; he stands in a long, noble line of messengers, and the ultimate outcome is already decided by the law of Allah.

=45=

From the general pattern of messengers, the sûrah now moves to a detailed example: “Then We sent Moses and his brother Aaron with Our signs and compelling proof.” The story of Moses is central to the Qur’ân, and here it is introduced as part of the same chain of prophetic missions.

The mention of Aaron alongside Moses highlights the mercy of Allah in granting Moses the support of his brother, whom he had specifically requested as an assistant. The “signs” (âyât) refer to the miracles that Moses was given—the staff that turned into a serpent, the shining hand, and the other clear proofs that demonstrated his truthfulness.

The phrase “compelling proof” (sulṭân mubîn) suggests an authority and clarity that left no genuine room for doubt. These were not vague philosophical arguments but tangible, undeniable evidences of divine power. The story of Moses also held special relevance for the Meccans, who had heard of Pharaoh and his tyranny from Jewish and Christian sources. The verse frames Moses and Aaron as two more links in the unbroken chain of messengers, all of whom faced the same arrogant rejection from the powerful elite. The believers in Mecca were meant to see themselves reflected in the children of Israel, who were an oppressed minority under a tyrannical ruler, yet destined for deliverance. The disbelievers of Quraysh were to see their own likeness in Pharaoh and his chiefs, who possessed worldly power and used it to suppress the truth. The stage is thus set for the confrontation between truth and falsehood, which will unfold in the following verses. By introducing Moses and Aaron right after the summary of destroyed nations, the sûrah demonstrates that even the mightiest empire of the ancient world could not stand against the purpose of Allah.

=46=

The target of Moses and Aaron’s mission is now identified: “to Pharaoh and his chiefs, but they behaved arrogantly and were a tyrannical people.” Pharaoh was not merely a king; he claimed divinity, declaring “I am your lord, most high.” His chiefs (mala’) were the inner circle of advisers, priests, and military commanders who supported his regime and benefited from the oppression of the Israelites. The verse says “they behaved arrogantly” (istakbarû), a verb that captures the puffing up of the self beyond its proper limits. Arrogance is the root disease of disbelief. It makes a person feel that bowing to the truth is beneath them.

This is exactly what happened with Pharaoh and his circle. They saw the signs, they recognized the power behind them, but their pride would not allow them to submit to a God they could not see, let alone to two men from among a slave population.

The verse then adds, “and were a tyrannical people.” They were ʿâlîn—high, dominant, oppressive. They had elevated themselves above others through force and injustice. The description of tyranny connects them to the earlier destroyed nations. The chiefs of Noah’s people, the leaders of ʿÂd, and now Pharaoh’s court all share the same profile: wealth, power, arrogance, and a determination to crush any voice that challenges their authority. This pattern is deliberately drawn to help the Meccans recognize their own chiefs in these portraits. The Quraysh elite, who persecuted the early Muslims, behaved arrogantly and were tyrannical in their own context. The verse implies that the outcome of such arrogance, no matter how powerful the tyrant, is always the same: divine judgment. Pharaoh’s Egypt seemed invincible, a superpower of the ancient world, yet it was swept away. This would have given enormous hope to the oppressed believers, assuring them that Allah sees the arrogance of the powerful and will deal with it in His own time.

=47=

The response of Pharaoh and his chiefs is recorded: “They argued, ‘Will we believe in two humans, like ourselves, whose people are slaves to us?’” The argument is dripping with contempt and social prejudice. First, they reduce Moses and Aaron to “two humans, like ourselves.

” This is the identical objection raised by the chiefs of Noah and Hûd. The idea that God would choose a mere human being as a messenger offended their sense of grandeur. They wanted a sign that matched their inflated self-image. If God were to send a messenger, they reasoned, he should at least be an angel or a king of some visible splendor. Second, they point to the social status of the Israelites: “whose people are slaves to us.

” This is an argument from worldly power and racial superiority. In their eyes, the fact that the Israelites were an enslaved and subjugated people was proof that their God could not be the true Lord of all, or that Moses and Aaron could not be genuine messengers. If the God of the Israelites was real and powerful, why would He allow His people to be slaves? They could not grasp the test of faith that operates through hardship, nor did they understand that the humiliation of the Israelites was a temporary phase in a divine plan. This argument reveals the deep link between social oppression and the rejection of truth. The elite dismissed the message because they dismissed the people it first came to uplift. For the early Muslims in Mecca, many of whom were from the lower classes, slaves, and the marginalized, this verse was a direct mirror. The Quraysh chiefs said similar things: “Are these the ones whom Allah has favored among us?” They could not accept that a message of truth could be carried by people who lacked worldly prestige. The verse exposes how economic and social arrogance becomes a barrier to faith. It also shows that the messengers’ humble human origins are actually a sign of Allah’s wisdom, because it makes the test of sincerity real: those who truly seek truth will recognize it regardless of the messenger’s social rank, while those who are arrogant will stumble over it.

=48=

The verse delivers the outcome with grim finality: “So they rejected them both, and so were among those destroyed.” Pharaoh and his chiefs heard the message, witnessed the signs, and yet chose rejection.

The word “both” (bi-himâ) emphasizes that they rejected Moses and Aaron together, despite the two messengers reinforcing each other’s message. The rejection was not a simple lack of belief; it was an active denial accompanied by persecution of the messengers and their people.

The phrase “and so were among those destroyed” (fa-kânû min al-muhlakîn) links Pharaoh’s regime to the long list of destroyed nations mentioned earlier. They joined the company of Noah’s people, ʿÂd, and others. The passive construction underscores that their destruction was not an accident of history but a deliberate divine action. They were “made to be” among the destroyed, by the decree of the One they had defied. The verse does not go into the details of the drowning here, as the focus is on the principle: rejection of the messengers leads to destruction.

However, the listener knows the full story from other passages—how Pharaoh and his army pursued the Israelites and were swallowed by the sea. That climactic scene is compressed here into a single statement, allowing the sûrah to maintain its swift rhythm.

The verse also contains a subtle grammatical emphasis: they rejected them, so they became among the destroyed. The consequence is presented as the direct and inevitable result of their action. It was not that Allah destroyed them arbitrarily; they placed themselves on the path of destruction by their own choice. For the Quraysh, this was another warning. They were rejecting a messenger whose truthfulness was becoming increasingly clear, and if they persisted, they would be inscribed in the same list of the destroyed. The verse serves as both a retrospective on Pharaoh and a prospective warning to every generation that walks in his footsteps.

=49=

After the destruction of Pharaoh, the verse shifts to what was given to the Children of Israel: “And We certainly gave Moses the Scripture, so perhaps his people would be rightly guided.” The “Scripture” is the Tawrâh, the Torah, a comprehensive book of guidance, law, and light.

The word “certainly” (la-qad) adds emphasis to the fact of this gift. The giving of the Scripture marks a new phase in the prophetic history. Earlier messengers like Noah and Hûd brought oral messages, but Moses was given a detailed written law that would govern the life of a nation. This was a great honor, and with it came great responsibility. The purpose is stated clearly: “so perhaps his people would be rightly guided.

” The phrase “perhaps” (laʿallahum) reflects the hope and expectation that the Scripture would lead them to the straight path. It does not imply doubt on Allah’s part, but rather expresses that guidance is offered and the people have the choice to accept it or turn away. The Torah contained all that the Israelites needed to live righteously, to establish justice, and to maintain a deep relationship with their Lord. It included stories of earlier prophets, laws of worship and social conduct, and promises and warnings. For the Meccan context, the mention of the Torah served several purposes. It reminded the Quraysh that the concept of a revealed book was not new; the Jews had their Scripture, and now the Arabs were being honored with the Qur’ân. It also served as a point of connection with the People of the Book, some of whom recognized the truth of Muhammad’s message because of what they found in their own scriptures. For the believers, the verse highlighted the immense value of divine guidance. A people who follow the Scripture are lifted from ignorance to knowledge, from injustice to justice. The Israelites had the opportunity to be a guided community; sadly, as later verses in the Qur’ân detail, many of them fell into disobedience and altered the meaning of the Book.

Yet the principle remains: Allah’s revealed guidance is the supreme means for human beings to find their way.

=50=

From Moses, the sûrah leaps across centuries to Jesus, the son of Mary: “And We made the son of Mary and his mother a sign.” The miraculous birth of Jesus to a virgin mother is described as a unified sign, encompassing both the mother and the child. Mary’s chastity and her conceiving without a father, and Jesus speaking in the cradle and performing miracles, were together a powerful indication of Allah’s creative will.

The verse emphasizes that they are not divine, but signs pointing to the divine. The verse then adds a detail of compassionate care: “and gave them refuge on high ground—a suitable place for rest with flowing water.” Commentators mention that this refers to a raised plateau, often identified as a location in the vicinity of Jerusalem or elsewhere, where Mary and the infant Jesus found shelter. The high ground (rabwah) is described as having stability (qarâr) and flowing water (maʿîn), making it a place of safety and provision.

This detail of physical refuge underscores that even the most honored of Allah’s servants are not exempt from needing His protection and material sustenance. Jesus and his mother were forced to flee, perhaps from the suspicion and hostility of their community, and Allah provided them a secure dwelling with water and food.

The mention of water flowing is significant; in the arid landscape of the Middle East, a place with running water was a mark of exceptional blessing. By pairing the story of Jesus with that of Moses, the sûrah unites the major prophetic traditions. The Quraysh had heard of Moses and Jesus from the Jews and Christians, and this verse confirms their prophethood while correcting the exaggerations of those who deified Jesus. He and his mother are signs, not gods. The believers are reminded that the path of all prophets includes trials, flight, and reliance on Allah’s provision. The refuge on high ground with water also echoes the blessed landing of Noah’s ark and the deliverance of the Israelites, reinforcing the theme that Allah rescues His righteous servants and gives them a place of security.

=51=

The sûrah now addresses the messengers collectively in a passage that draws together the lessons from the prophetic narratives: “O messengers! Eat from what is good and lawful, and act righteously. Indeed, I fully know what you do.

” This is a beautiful and intimate call, as if the Creator is speaking directly to all the noble messengers across time, instructing them in the very basics of the life they are to model for their followers. The command “Eat from what is good and lawful” emphasizes that prophets are human beings who need food, but they are to ensure that what they consume is ṭayyib—pure, wholesome, and beneficial—and obtained through ḥalâl means.

The mention of eating is significant because the earlier disbelievers mocked the messengers precisely for being humans who eat and drink. Here, Allah turns that mockery into a badge of honor: the messengers do eat, but they do so in obedience to divine command, modeling a life where even the most basic acts are done with consciousness of Allah. The command “and act righteously” (iʿmalû ṣâliḥan) covers all deeds. The prophets are not only to preach righteousness; they are to embody it. Their actions, like their food, must be pure and in accordance with divine guidance. The verse closes with the reminder, “Indeed, I fully know what you do.

” This is both a comfort and a warning. The messengers are under the watchful eye of their Lord, who sees their struggles, their sincerity, and their efforts. Nothing they do is lost or unappreciated. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, this verse placed him within the noble brotherhood of the messengers and reminded him that the standard for all prophets is the same: lawful sustenance and righteous action. For the believers, the verse teaches that following the prophetic model means paying attention to how we earn and what we consume, and then channeling that pure provision into good deeds, all while knowing that Allah is fully aware of every detail.

=52=

The address continues with a powerful declaration of the unity of the divine message: “Surely this religion of yours is only one, and I am your Lord, so fear Me alone.” The word “religion” (ummah) here can be understood as the community of faith or the path of submission that all the prophets followed. Despite the differences in laws and rituals that were given to various nations over time, the core of the message has always been exactly the same: worship Allah alone and live in conscious obedience to Him.

The phrase “only one” emphasizes that there is no plurality in the essential truth. Noah’s religion, Abraham’s religion, Moses’ religion, and Jesus’ religion were all Islam in its universal sense—submission to the One God. The fragmentation and division that later appeared among their followers were human corruptions, not part of the original revelation.

The verse then adds the logical foundation of this unity: “and I am your Lord.” Because the Lord of all the messengers is one, their religion is also one. There cannot be multiple gods with multiple paths leading to different destinations. The one Creator has sent one consistent message through all His messengers. The conclusion follows naturally: “so fear Me alone.” Taqwâ, the reverent fear and consciousness of Allah, is the proper response to recognizing His oneness and His Lordship. This verse was a direct challenge to the Meccan polytheists, who claimed to believe in Allah as the supreme Creator but associated other deities with Him in worship. It also served to unite the believers, reminding them that they are part of a single, ancient community that stretches back to the dawn of humanity. In a time when the Muslims were a small, persecuted group, this reminder of their connection to the great prophets of the past gave them a sense of identity and dignity that transcended their immediate hardships.

=53=

Despite the clear divine command for unity, the verse reveals the tragic human response: “Yet the people have divided it into different sects, each rejoicing in what they have.” The “people” here refers to the followers of the previous scriptures—the Jews and the Christians—but the principle applies to any community that receives guidance and then splinters it. After the messengers passed away, later generations began to differ over the interpretation of the scriptures. Ego, ignorance, and love of the world led them to form separate groups, each claiming to have the exclusive truth and each taking pride in its own set of beliefs and practices, even when those were far removed from the original revelation.

The phrase “each rejoicing in what they have” paints a vivid picture of the psychology of sectarianism. Each sect is happy and content with its own portion, convinced of its own rightness, and unwilling to return to the pure, undivided source. This rejoicing is not the joy of true guidance but the smug satisfaction of those who have tailored religion to their own desires and then declared it complete. The verse serves as a warning to the Muslim community against falling into the same trap. The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned that his ummah would split into many sects, but the saved group would be those who held fast to what he and his companions were upon.

The emphasis on “each rejoicing in what they have” also highlights a psychological barrier to unity: people become emotionally attached to the particularities of their own group, their own scholars, and their own interpretations, to the point where they cannot humbly submit to the pure truth of the Qur’ân and Sunnah. The verse thus calls believers to rise above partisanship and cling directly to the one religion that Allah ordained, abandoning the comfort of group identity when it conflicts with divine command.

=54=

After describing the division of the people into sects, the verse gives a directive to the Prophet: “So leave them O Prophet in their heedlessness for a while.” The command “leave them” (fa-dharhum) is not a call to abandon the duty of conveying the message, but rather a counsel to stop exhausting himself in grief over those who have chosen to remain in their confusion. The Prophet, peace be upon him, was deeply distressed by the rejection and sectarian stubbornness of the pagans and the People of the Book. He yearned for their guidance. This verse consoles him and places a limit on his emotional investment: there comes a point when the truth has been made clear, the arguments have been presented, and those who insist on staying in their heedlessness must be left to face the consequences.

The word “heedlessness” (ghamrah) suggests being submerged, as if they are drowning in a flood of ignorance and distraction. They are not merely making a calm, rational decision to disagree; they are lost in a state of spiritual intoxication, oblivious to the danger they are in.

The phrase “for a while” (ḥattâ ḥîn) indicates that this period of leaving them is temporary. It is the interval before the punishment arrives or before the Day of Judgment. This temporary respite is both a mercy and a test; it gives them time to wake up and repent, but it also allows their true nature to manifest fully. For the believers, this verse teaches a crucial lesson about emotional boundaries. One can care deeply about people and invite them to the truth, but one cannot force them to see. When the invitation has been sincerely extended and rejected with persistent heedlessness, the believer is to entrust the matter to Allah and not let the rejection consume them. The duty is to convey; the outcome belongs to the Lord.

=55=

This verse and the next form a sharp corrective to a common misunderstanding about worldly prosperity. “Do they think, since We provide them with wealth and children…” The disbelievers of Mecca, like the chiefs of earlier nations, were enjoying material abundance. They had thriving trade, plentiful livestock, and many sons—the two greatest markers of status and security in Arabian society. They interpreted this prosperity as a sign of divine favor, and perhaps even as proof that they were on the right path. After all, if Allah was displeased with them, why would He shower them with blessings?

The verse begins to dismantle this false equation by posing a rhetorical question. The form of the question implies that their assumption is deeply flawed. The provision of wealth and children is a test, not a certificate of approval. Allah grants worldly goods to both the righteous and the wicked as part of the trial of life. The wealthy disbeliever is being tested by his riches: will he be grateful and humble, or will he become arrogant and miserly? The poor believer is being tested by his poverty: will he be patient and content, or will he complain and stray? The outward distribution of worldly goods in this life is not a reliable indicator of a person’s standing with Allah. The verse strips away the illusion of the Quraysh elite, forcing them to reexamine their assumption that their comfortable lives were a sign of divine pleasure. For the believers, many of whom were poor and childless or had lost their children, this verse was a deep comfort. It reassured them that their lack of worldly goods did not mean Allah had abandoned them. On the contrary, their patience under hardship was far more valuable in the divine scale than the wealth of the heedless. The verse sets the stage for the next ayah’s full answer.

=56=

The verse completes the question and delivers a decisive answer: “that We hasten to honour them with all kinds of good? No! They are not aware.” The assumption being refuted is that Allah’s swift provision of wealth and children is a form of immediate honor.

The phrase “hasten to honour them” uses the language of racing to do good, but here the irony is that what appears to be good is actually a precursor to accountability. The emphatic “No!” (kallâ) shuts down the false reasoning. Allah is not giving them these blessings as a reward; He is granting them as part of the istidrâj—the gradual leading of a person toward destruction through the very blessings they enjoy. The more they receive, the more they become immersed in their heedlessness, and the greater their punishment will be when it finally arrives. The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned about this when he said that when you see Allah giving a servant the things of the world despite his sins, it is istidrâj, and then he recited verses from the Qur’ân about this concept.

The verse ends with “They are not aware.” This is the crux of the problem. The disbelievers lack the spiritual insight to see beyond the surface of events. They mistake the outer appearance of prosperity for inner reality. They do not understand the true nature of the world as a place of testing. Their wealth and children, which they see as proof of their correctness, are actually evidence of their impending doom, because they are using those gifts in disobedience and arrogance rather than in gratitude and service.

For the believer, this verse cultivates a profound detachment from the glitter of the world. When a righteous person sees a wrongdoer enjoying luxury, they do not envy them; they recognize that the wrongdoer is being fattened for a terrible accounting. True honor lies not in what one possesses but in the state of one’s heart and one’s relationship with the Creator.

=57=

The sûrah now returns to the description of the true believers, contrasting them with the heedless wealthy who were just mentioned. “Surely those who tremble in awe of their Lord.

” The phrase “tremble in awe” translates the Arabic mushfiqûn, which conveys a state of careful, anxious fear combined with deep reverence. This is not the fear of a slave terrified of a harsh master; it is the reverent awe of a heart that is deeply aware of the majesty of Allah and is constantly vigilant not to displease Him. The believers’ hearts are alive and sensitive. They do not take their faith for granted, nor do they become complacent about their own salvation. Their trembling is a sign of true knowledge; the more one knows of Allah’s greatness and of one’s own shortcomings, the more one fears falling short. This quality stands in stark contrast to the arrogance and heedlessness of the disbelievers who rejoiced in their wealth and sects. The verse places this fear at the top of the list of the believers’ characteristics in this section, just as humble prayer was placed at the top of the list in the opening verses. Fear of the Lord is the foundation of all righteousness. It restrains the limbs from sin, motivates the heart to seek forgiveness, and keeps the believer constantly oriented toward the Hereafter. The early Muslims, living under persecution and uncertainty, were people whose hearts trembled with love and awe. This trembling was not a sign of weak faith but of profound spiritual health. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said, “I am the most knowledgeable of you about Allah, and I am the most fearful of Him among you.” True awe of the Lord does not diminish with greater piety; it increases. This verse thus sets the tone for the qualities that follow, reminding the listener that the believers’ righteousness flows from a heart that never feels safe from the need for Allah’s mercy.

=58=

The second quality in this new list is that they “believe in the revelations of their Lord.” The word “revelations” (âyât) encompasses both the verses of the Qur’ân and the signs of Allah in the universe. The believers are those whose hearts are fully open to accepting these signs as truth. Their belief is not a shallow or inherited label; it is a deep conviction that shapes their worldview and their actions. By attributing the revelations to “their Lord,” the verse personalizes the relationship. The believers do not see the revelations as abstract texts or distant miracles; they see them as messages from their own Rabb, the One who nurtures, sustains, and cares for them. This intimate framing makes the act of belief an act of loyalty and love, not mere intellectual assent. Belief in the revelations also implies acting upon them. True îmân leads to submission, obedience, and the implementation of what has been revealed. The believers do not pick and choose from the verses according to their desires, as some of the earlier communities did. They accept the whole package of divine guidance, even when it goes against their personal inclinations or cultural norms.

In the context of the sûrah, this quality connects back to the stories of the prophets. The believers are those who learn from those stories and internalize the warnings and promises they contain. They are not like the Quraysh who heard the revelations and turned away in disgust.

The verse also ties in with the preceding quality: the fear of the Lord makes the heart receptive to His words. A heart that trembles in awe is a heart that listens and believes. Together, these qualities paint a portrait of faith that is both emotionally alive and intellectually committed.

=59=

The third quality in this passage is the core of Islamic monotheism: “and who associate none with their Lord.” This phrase declares that the believers are absolutely pure in their worship, free from any trace of shirk, the sin of associating partners with Allah. Shirk can take obvious forms, like worshiping idols, and more subtle forms, like showing off in worship, relying on other beings as one relies on Allah, or loving created things with a love that belongs only to the Creator. The true believers are constantly vigilant against these forms of association. They direct all their acts of worship—prayer, supplication, sacrifice, vow, reliance, fear, and hope—exclusively to Allah.

The phrase “with their Lord” again personalizes the relationship, emphasizing that Allah is not a distant, abstract deity but the personal Rabb of each believer, to whom they owe exclusive devotion. This quality is placed right after belief in the revelations because shirk often arises from a corruption of revelation.

The earlier communities received scriptures but then inserted false doctrines, deifying their prophets, saints, or angels. The believers mentioned here accept the revelations in their pure form and guard the purity of their worship accordingly. For the Meccan context, this was the most direct challenge to the Quraysh. They did believe in Allah as a supreme Creator, but they associated other gods with Him, claiming that these were intermediaries. The verse declares that the true believers do no such thing. This quality is also the essential link between all the prophets from Noah onward, who each began their call with the words “Worship Allah; you have no god other than Him.” The believers who tremble in awe, believe in the revelations, and associate none with their Lord are the direct spiritual heirs of those prophets, walking the same path of pure tawḥîd.

The final quality in this description of the believers is perhaps the most striking and psychologically profound: “and who do whatever good they do with their hearts fearful, knowing that they will return to their Lord.” These are people who actively perform acts of righteousness—charity, prayer, fasting, kindness—yet their hearts tremble with fear even as they do them.

This is not the fear of being caught doing something wrong, but the fear that their good deeds might not be accepted. They worry about the sincerity of their intentions, the presence of hidden pride or showing off, and the possibility of shortcomings that would render their deeds unworthy of their Lord.

This is a level of spiritual refinement far above mere outward compliance. The verse alludes to an incident involving ʿÂ’ishah, the Mother of the Believers, who asked the Prophet, peace be upon him, whether this verse referred to people who commit sins. The Prophet replied that it referred to those who pray, fast, and give charity, yet fear that their deeds will not be accepted. This hadith reveals the depth of the believers’ consciousness. They do not feel entitled to Paradise because of their good works; they know that salvation comes only through the mercy of Allah, and that their deeds, no matter how many, are insufficient when measured against the majesty and rights of the Creator.

The phrase “knowing that they will return to their Lord” grounds this fear in the certainty of the afterlife. They live with the constant awareness that they are on a journey back to Allah, and that the final judgment will reveal the true value of their deeds. This combination of doing good and fearing for its acceptance produces a believer who is both active and humble, hardworking and broken-hearted. Such a person will never be arrogant about their piety, nor will they despair of Allah’s mercy. They are suspended between hope and fear, and this is the station of spiritual maturity. The quality also connects back to the trembling awe mentioned at the start of this passage. The circle is complete: awe of the Lord leads to belief, pure worship, and a life of righteousness infused with the humbling fear that the ultimate acceptance lies in the hands of the Most Merciful.
