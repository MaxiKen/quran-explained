=101=

After the barrier of the barzakh and the long wait in the grave, the sûrah catapults the listener into the climactic moment of history: “Then, when the Trumpet will be blown.” The Trumpet (ṣûr) is the horn blown by the angel Isrâfîl, an event so tremendous that the Qur’ân describes it as making every nursing mother forget her infant and every pregnant female miscarry. This blowing signals the end of the present order and the beginning of the resurrection. The verse zeroes in on a single, devastating effect of that Day: “there will be no kinship between them on that Day, nor will they even care to ask about one another. ” The word “kinship” (ansâb) refers to the ties of lineage, blood, and ancestry that were so central to Arab society. The Quraysh boasted of their noble lineage and took immense pride in their tribal bonds. On the Day of Judgment, all of that evaporates in an instant. The terror of the situation is so overwhelming that every soul is consumed with its own fate. A man will not turn to his father, a mother will not search for her child, and a lifelong friend will not spare a thought for his companion. The verse says they will not “ask about one another” (yatasa’alûn), meaning that the normal human instinct to check on loved ones, to seek comfort in connection, will be utterly extinguished by the sheer horror of the reckoning. This does not contradict the fact that some intercession and interaction will occur later; rather, in the initial blast and the surge toward the plain of gathering, everyone will be isolated by their own fear. The verse demolishes the worldly pride the Quraysh took in their clans and reminds every human being that the only bond that will matter on that Day is the bond of faith. All other ties, no matter how cherished, will snap.

This is a sobering call to reorder one’s priorities: the relationships we invest in for the sake of Allah are the only ones that will survive the Trumpet’s blast. The verse also offers a strange comfort to those who were marginalized for their faith, abandoned by family and tribe; on that Day, the very ties that caused them pain will become meaningless, while their connection to the Lord of the Throne will be their only anchor.

=102=

After the gathering comes the judgment, and the sûrah describes the central act of the weighing: “As for those whose scale is heavy with good deeds, it is they who will be successful.” The scale (mawâzîn) is a reality of the Day of Judgment, though its exact nature is known only to Allah. Some scholars understand it as a literal scale with pans, while others see it as a metaphor for the precise divine assessment of deeds. In either case, the meaning is clear: every action, word, and intention will be measured with absolute justice. A heavy scale means that the pan of good deeds outweighs the pan of evil deeds.

This is the moment when all the qualities listed at the opening of the sûrah—the humble prayer, the avoidance of idle talk, the payment of charity, the guarding of chastity, the keeping of trusts, and the diligent observance of prayers—appear as solid weight on the scale. The believers who trembled in awe of their Lord, who believed in His revelations without associating partners with Him, and who gave charity with fearful hearts now see their fear turn into joy.

The word “successful” (mufliḥûn) is exactly the same word used in the very first verse: “Successful indeed are the believers. ” The sûrah has come full circle. What was proclaimed as a promise is now declared as a fact. Those who were mocked for their faith, who were told they were losers for following a mere human, are now the undisputed winners.

This verse also carries a profound lesson about the nature of good deeds: they must be sincere, in accordance with the guidance of the Prophet, and performed with the consciousness of standing before Allah. Not every act that appears good to human beings will weigh heavily on that scale; only those deeds that were done purely for Allah’s sake and with the correct methodology will tip the balance. The verse encourages the believer to live with the scale in mind, constantly asking: what am I sending ahead that will weigh in my favor? The weighty deeds are often the ones that seem small in the world—a sincere prayer in the night, a kind word, a restrained anger, a secret charity—but on that Day, their density will be felt as the scale descends, and the heart will flood with gratitude.

=103=

In stark contrast to the heavy scales of the believers, the verse continues: “But those whose scale is light, they will have doomed themselves, staying in Hell forever.” A light scale means that the good deeds are insufficient to outweigh the bad, or that the bad deeds are so numerous and the good so few that the scale barely registers any positive weight.

This is the result of a life spent in heedlessness, denial, and the pursuit of selfish desires. The phrase “they will have doomed themselves” (fa-ulâ’ika alladhîna khasirû anfusahum) is piercing. They did not lose money, property, or status; they lost their very own souls. The loss is total and irreparable. Every human being is given a precious capital: a soul, a lifespan, faculties of hearing, sight, and intellect. Those with light scales squandered this capital on trivialities and sins. They were offered the most valuable asset in existence—the opportunity to earn eternal Paradise—and they traded it for fleeting pleasures or arrogant refusal.

The verse then adds the duration of their loss: “staying in Hell forever.” The eternity of the punishment is stated without qualification.

This is the final destination for those who die in a state of shirk or outright rejection of the truth after it has been clearly conveyed to them. The severity of this verse is meant to shake the listener awake. It is not a comfortable message, but it is a true one, and the only proper response is urgent repentance and a radical reorientation of life. For the believers, this verse deepens their fear of a bad end. They are the ones who give charity with their hearts fearful, precisely because they dread having a light scale.

The verse also clarifies that the ultimate loss is not poverty, illness, or worldly failure; the ultimate loss is to enter the Fire with a light scale. This perspective completely redefines the meaning of success and failure, pulling the believer away from the world’s false standards and anchoring them in the reality of the Day of Weighing.

=104=

The description of the punishment becomes graphic and horrifying: “The Fire will burn their faces, leaving them deformed.” The face is the most honored part of the human body, the seat of identity and dignity. In this world, people take great care of their faces, seeking beauty and avoiding disfigurement. The disbelievers once turned their faces away from the truth in arrogance and disgust; now the Fire seizes those very faces and scorches them.

The word “deformed” (kalihûn) describes a state in which the skin tightens, the lips shrink back, and the teeth are exposed in a permanent grimace of agony. It is an image of utter humiliation and loss of all dignity. The physical pain is compounded by the destruction of beauty and the imposition of a horrifying appearance. This punishment corresponds to the nature of their crime. They used their faces to express scorn, to laugh at the believers, and to look down upon the truth. Now those faces become the primary site of their torment.

The verse also connects to the earlier mention of their scales being light; the burning of faces is a visible manifestation of the inner bankruptcy they now carry. Some scholars note that the face is mentioned because it is the most sensitive part, and the pain there is most acute, but also because the face represents the person’s entire being. To have one’s face burned and deformed is to have one’s whole self disfigured. For the believers who hear this verse, it instills a healthy fear that motivates them to guard their faces from sin in this world—to lower their gaze from the forbidden, to use their tongues for truth rather than mockery, and to keep their expressions soft with humility rather than twisted with arrogance. The verse is a window into the severity of divine justice, a justice that is perfectly appropriate to the deeds committed. It reminds every listener that the body we cherish will one day testify for or against us, and the face we adorn in the mirror will either shine with the light of Paradise or burn in the Fire.

=105=

In the midst of their torment, a voice of rebuke addresses the inhabitants of the Fire: “It will be said, ‘Were My revelations not recited to you, but you used to deny them?’” This is a rhetorical question that serves as a stinging reminder and a statement of justice. The word “My revelations” (âyâtî) personalizes the offense; these were not just any words, but the direct communications of the Lord of all the worlds.

The question “Were they not recited to you?” implies that the disbelievers had full access to the truth. Messengers came, scriptures were revealed, and the verses were proclaimed in their own language. There was no barrier of understanding or lack of warning. The recitation was clear, repeated, and persistent.

The second part, “but you used to deny them,” highlights their willful rejection. The verb “used to” (kuntum bihâ tukadhdhibûn) indicates a habitual, ongoing pattern. They did not stumble once; they built their entire lives on denial. This verse echoes the earlier description in the sûrah where Allah says, “My revelations were recited to you, but you used to back away in disgust.

” The repetition of this theme underscores its importance. The punishment is not arbitrary; it is the direct consequence of their own choices. On that Day, they will have no argument, no defense. They cannot say they were not warned or that the message was unclear.

The question also serves a psychological purpose in the narrative: it forces the disbelievers to remember every time they heard the truth and rejected it, adding the pain of regret to the physical torment. For the living who hear this verse, it is a powerful call to honor the Qur’ân. To have Allah’s revelations recited and to respond with denial is to store up a moment of unbearable questioning. The verse urges every listener to examine their own response: when the words of Allah are recited, does the heart soften, or does it turn away in the same pattern that led these people to the Fire?

The question is not just for the disbelievers of Mecca; it echoes across time for all who receive the Book and must decide what to do with it.

=106=

The response of the damned is a cry of anguish and a partial confession: “They will cry, ‘Our Lord! Our ill-fate took hold of us, so we became a misguided people.’” The phrase “Our ill-fate” translates shiqwatunâ, which can mean our wretchedness, our misfortune, or our evil deeds that led to our ruin. They attribute their condition to something that overcame them.

This is both an admission and a subtle attempt to deflect full responsibility. On one level, they are acknowledging that they were overcome by their own evil inclinations, by the temptations of Satan, and by the pressures of their society. On another level, by saying “our ill-fate took hold of us,” they are speaking as if an external force seized them, reducing their own agency.

This is a common human tendency: when faced with the consequences of sin, the sinner often says, “I was overcome by my desires,” or “Circumstances were against me.” The Qur’ân exposes this as a weak excuse, because they were given the tools to resist—reason, revelation, and the ability to choose.

The second part, “so we became a misguided people,” is the honest conclusion. They recognize that their path led them away from guidance into the darkness of error. There is a note of sorrow in these words, a sorrow that is utterly useless at that stage. They now see the truth of what the messengers said, but their seeing comes too late. This verse mirrors the earlier descriptions of the disbelievers at the moment of death, begging to go back. Now they are beyond even that request; they simply state their condition as a lament. For the believers, this verse is a warning against the mentality that blames fate, circumstances, or society for one’s own sins. The believer takes full responsibility for their choices, repents sincerely, and strives to change. The misguided people in the Fire recognized their misguidance, but recognition without the opportunity for amendment is the essence of despair. The living have the chance to say, “Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves, so forgive us,” and have that plea accepted.

=107=

The cry continues with a desperate plea: “Our Lord! Take us out of this Fire. Then if we ever return to denial, we will truly be wrongdoers.

” This is the same essential request they made at the moment of death, now repeated from within the flames. They beg for extraction, for just one more chance to live differently. The sincerity of their promise might seem compelling: they swear that if they are returned to the world, they will never again fall into denial, and if they do, they will be fully deserving of the title “wrongdoers.

” The word “truly” (innâ idhan la-ẓâlimûn) is an emphasis meant to convince. But Allah knows the reality of their souls. The Qur’ân elsewhere states that even if they were sent back, they would simply return to what they were forbidden from; they are liars in their promises.

This is because their repentance is not born of a true change of heart but of a desire to escape pain. Genuine repentance requires a recognition of the ugliness of the sin itself and a love for the obedience, not merely a fear of the punishment. The disbelievers in Hell have not changed their nature; they simply want relief. The verse illustrates the profound difference between the repentance that is accepted in this world—which is done freely, for the sake of Allah, before death—and the forced “repentance” of the Hereafter, which holds no value. The scene also underscores the mercy and justice of Allah. He gave them an entire lifetime to choose. They were not rushed; they were not coerced. They had decades of opportunities, and they rejected them all. Now that the reality is undeniable, they want the test to be extended. But the test is over, and the examination papers have been collected. For the living, this verse is a call to not delay repentance. Every human being knows that if they were to see Hell with their own eyes, they would immediately promise to be righteous. But the test is precisely to believe in the unseen and to act upon that belief before the unseen becomes seen. The urgency of the verse is unmistakable: make your sincere promise to Allah now, while the door of mercy is open.

=108=

The response from Allah is one of the harshest and most final statements in the Qur’ân: “Allah will respond, ‘Be despised in there! Do not ever plead with Me again!’” The word “be despised” (ikhsa’û) is a command used to shoo away an animal, carrying the strongest connotation of humiliation and contempt. It is the language of utter rejection. They are told to remain in the Fire in a state of degradation, their pleas falling on closed ears.

The second part, “Do not ever plead with Me again,” slams the door of intercession and supplication shut. Their cries, their promises, their admissions—all are met with a wall of silence.

This is the divine anger that the believers were warned about. It is the fulfillment of the threat that those who mock the revelations and forget the remembrance of their Lord will be forgotten in turn. The severity of this response is proportional to the severity of their crime: they spent a lifetime refusing to bow, refusing to ask, refusing to humble themselves. Now when they finally call out “Our Lord,” they are told that the time for that call has passed. The verse echoes the earlier command in the sûrah, “Do not cry for help today. Surely you will never be saved from Us. ” The repetition of this theme throughout the sûrah reinforces that the Day of Judgment is not a day for negotiation; it is a day for reaping what was sown.

For the believer, this verse is a terrifying reminder of the value of calling upon Allah in this life. Every duʿâ’, every tear of repentance, every “O my Lord” whispered in the night is a precious opportunity that will not be available after death.

The verse also teaches that there is a kind of pleading that becomes an insult to divine justice when it comes from a heart that never truly sought Allah while it had the chance. It is a call to take the present moment seriously and to never assume that there will always be time to turn back.

=109=

The tone shifts dramatically as Allah now contrasts the damned with a group of His beloved servants: “Indeed, there was a group of My servants who used to pray, ‘Our Lord! We have believed, so forgive us and have mercy on us, for You are the best of those who show mercy.’” This verse recounts the sincere supplication of the believers whom the disbelievers mocked.

The phrase “My servants” (ʿibâdî) is an expression of honor and closeness. These are not just any people; they are those who have earned the personal ascription to the Lord of all worlds. Their prayer is simple, humble, and deeply trusting. They begin by affirming their faith: “We have believed.

” This is not a boast but a statement of fact that they present as the basis for their request. They then ask for two things: forgiveness (maghfirah) and mercy (raḥmah). Forgiveness to cover their sins and shortcomings, and mercy to grant them the good they do not deserve through their deeds alone. They end their supplication by recognizing that Allah is “the best of those who show mercy,” an acknowledgment that His mercy surpasses all others and that they are utterly dependent on it. This supplication reflects the very qualities described earlier in the sûrah: they tremble in awe of their Lord, they believe in His revelations, and they do good deeds with hearts fearful of the return. Their prayer is a continuous, living expression of their faith.

The word “used to pray” (yaqûlûn) indicates a habit, a consistent practice of seeking Allah’s forgiveness and mercy, not a one-time event. This verse serves as a beautiful model for the believer’s daily supplication. It teaches that the proper attitude is to couple one’s faith with a constant plea for forgiveness and mercy, never becoming self-satisfied. The contrast with the disbelievers’ desperate, rejected pleas could not be sharper: one group humbled themselves early and were honored; the other group refused to humble themselves until it was too late and were humiliated.

=110=

The speech now turns directly to the disbelievers in Hell, explaining why they are there while the humble servants are honored: “but you were so busy making fun of them that it made you forget My remembrance. And you used to laugh at them.

” The word “busy” (tattakhidhûnahum) implies that they took the believers as a target for mockery, making it a regular occupation and a source of entertainment. This was the behavior of the Quraysh chiefs who would ridicule Bilâl, ʿAmmâr, and other poor Muslims, considering them foolish for abandoning the religion of their ancestors.

The verse reveals a deep spiritual dynamic: mockery of the believers led directly to forgetfulness of Allah. When a person occupies their tongue and heart with ridiculing the righteous, there is no room left for the remembrance of God. The more they laughed at the believers, the further they drifted from their own Creator. The sin was not just in hurting the believers; it was in the spiritual self-destruction that the mockery caused.

The phrase “you used to laugh at them” reinforces the habitual nature of their scorn. It was not an occasional slip; it was a defining characteristic of their lives. The laughter was a weapon of arrogance, designed to belittle and silence the voice of truth. But on the Day of Judgment, the roles are completely reversed. The believers who were laughed at are now the triumphant ones, resting in the gardens of delight. The disbelievers who laughed are now the ones weeping in the Fire. This verse offers profound comfort to anyone who has ever been mocked for their faith. It assures them that Allah sees every sneer, every joke at their expense, and every moment of humiliation they endured. The laughter of the disbelievers is recorded, and its recompense is the fire that burns their faces.

The verse also warns believers against mocking others, even in small ways, as it hardens the heart and can lead to the very forgetfulness of Allah that doomed these disbelievers. The tongue must be guarded, and the righteous must be honored, not ridiculed.

=111=

The verdict on the believers is now pronounced: “Today I have indeed rewarded them for their perseverance: they are certainly the triumphant.” The word “Today” (al-yawm) emphasizes the immediacy and certainty of the reward on the Day of Judgment. The long wait in the world, the years of persecution, the patience under mockery—all of it is now compensated in a single day.

The word “perseverance” (ṣabr) covers a wide range of trials: patience in obeying Allah, patience in avoiding sin, and patience with the hardships and injustices of life. The believers were mocked, boycotted, and in some cases tortured or killed, yet they held fast to their faith. Their ṣabr was not passive endurance; it was active, hopeful waiting for the promise of Allah. The reward is described with the emphatic “I have indeed rewarded them,” with Allah Himself as the speaker, making the reward supremely honorable. The nature of the reward is not detailed in this verse, but the whole sûrah has been leading to it: Paradise, eternal bliss, and the pleasure of their Lord.

The phrase “they are certainly the triumphant” (innahum hum al-fâ’izûn) is a powerful echo of the very first verse: “Successful indeed are the believers.” The circle is now closed. The promise made in the opening is fulfilled in the end. Those who were considered losers in the world are declared the true winners by the King of all kings.

The word “triumphant” (fâ’izûn) implies not just success but a glorious victory, a winning of the ultimate prize. This verse is a source of immense strength for every believer who struggles. It says that the pain of today is temporary, but the triumph of tomorrow is eternal. The laughter of the disbelievers will be silenced, and the tears of the believers will be wiped away. The condition for this triumph is ṣabr—steady, unwavering patience rooted in faith. The verse thus encourages the believer to anchor their heart in this promise, to see beyond the immediate difficulty, and to know that the “Today” of reward is coming, and on that Day, every sacrifice will feel like a bargain.

=112=

The scene shifts to a dialogue that highlights the insignificance of worldly life compared to eternity: “He will ask them, ‘How many years did you remain on earth?’” The question is directed at the disbelievers, although it could also be asked of all humanity. Allah, who already knows the answer, asks this question to make a point.

The word “remain” (labithtum) implies a stay, a temporary sojourn. The question invites them to recollect their earthly lifespan, to summon the number of years they walked upon the earth. The very act of recalling those years will make them realize how brief and fleeting it all was. In the world, a lifetime of sixty or seventy years can feel long. People plan, build, accumulate, and dream as if they will live forever. But when standing before the Eternal Lord, surrounded by the reality of the Hereafter, those decades will appear as nothing more than a fleeting moment.

The question also serves a judicial purpose: it makes them acknowledge with their own tongues the shortness of their test, thereby stripping away any claim that they were not given enough time or opportunity. They had years—years in which the call to prayer sounded, in which the Qur’ân was recited, in which seasons of mercy passed one after another.

The question “How many years?” will force them to count, and the counting itself will be an act of self-indictment. For the living, this verse is a profound meditation. How many years have we been given? How many have already passed? And what have we sent ahead?

The question that Allah will ask the disbelievers is one that believers should ask themselves now, in the quiet of their own reflection. To count the years is to realize the urgency of the remaining time. The verse pulls the heart out of the illusion of permanence and anchors it in the reality that this life is a short stay, soon to be followed by an endless tomorrow.

=113=

The answer of the disbelievers reveals the complete transformation of their perception: “They will reply, ‘We remained only a day or part of a day. But ask those who kept count.’” The long decades of their lives have shrunk in their memory to the span of a single day or even a fraction of a day.

This is the natural effect of comparing temporal time with eternity. Just as a dream that feels like hours lasts only minutes by the clock, the entire worldly life, when viewed from the perspective of the Hereafter, is less than a passing afternoon.

The phrase “only a day or part of a day” echoes similar expressions used elsewhere in the Qur’ân to describe the disbelievers’ realization on the Day of Judgment. They are stunned, almost unsure, as if the memory is so dim that they cannot be precise. So they add, “But ask those who kept count”—perhaps the angels who recorded their deeds, or those among them with sharper memories. This hesitancy and referral to others underscore their disorientation. The world that was once so solid and all-consuming is now a distant blur. The verse is a powerful corrective to those who think that the pleasures or pains of this world are worth obsessing over. On that Day, the seventy years of a person’s life will feel like a single day of enjoyment or a single day of hardship. How foolish, then, to sell an eternity of bliss or torment for a “day or part of a day” of choices.

For the believer, this answer is a lesson in perspective. When the tests of life feel overwhelming, one should remember that they are only for “a day or part of a day.” When the temptations whisper that the sacrifice is too great, one should remember how short the sacrifice will seem when eternity begins.

The verse teaches the art of living in the light of the end, making decisions not based on the fleeting now but on the everlasting then.

=114=

The response from Allah confirms their realization and drives the point home: “He will say, ‘You only remained for a little while, if only you knew.’” The phrase “a little while” (qalîlan) is deliberately indefinite but emphatic. It does not matter whether it was a day, a part of a day, or even a hundred years; relative to eternity, it is a little while. The addition of “if only you knew” is deeply poignant. It means: if only you had known this truth during your life, you would have acted differently. The knowledge they now possess—the true brevity of the world—is knowledge that comes too late to change their destiny. They know it now with certainty, but that knowledge only adds to their regret. The “if only” (law) is the signature of missed opportunity.

This verse teaches that the world is not inherently evil or worthless; it is the place of planting seeds for the Hereafter. But its value lies only in its being a bridge, not a permanent home. Those who treat it as a final destination, who build their hopes and identities entirely around it, are the ones who will be told on the Day of Judgment that they had only a little while and wasted it. The believers, by contrast, are those who “knew” this truth through faith. They did not have to wait for the Day of Judgment to understand the brevity of life. The Qur’ân taught them, and they lived accordingly. This verse thus distinguishes between two types of knowledge: the knowledge of faith that benefits in this world and leads to action, and the knowledge of experience that comes in the Hereafter and brings only sorrow. The invitation in this verse is to join the first group, to truly know now what the disbelievers will only know when it is too late.

=115=

The final, piercing question is now put to them: “Did you then think that We had created you without purpose, and that you would never be returned to Us?” This verse is the philosophical and theological core of the sûrah’s argument. It exposes the fundamental error that leads to all the sins and punishments described.

The word “without purpose” translates ʿabathan, which means play, amusement, or vain sport. The disbelievers lived as if the creation of the universe and the human being was a meaningless game, an accident with no goal and no accountability. Their denial of the resurrection was essentially a belief that human life ends in nothingness, that the Creator had brought forth a magnificent cosmos and conscious beings only to let them dissolve into dust with no final reckoning. The verse challenges this assumption with the word “then” (a-fa-ḥasibtum), indicating that this is the logical conclusion of their behavior. If they truly believed they would be returned to their Lord, they would have lived righteously. Their heedless lives are proof that deep down they held this false assumption.

The second part, “and that you would never be returned to Us,” directly addresses the denial of the Hereafter that has been a recurring theme throughout the sûrah. The question is rhetorical; it is meant to leave them speechless, because they have no answer. The very idea that the Most Wise Creator would create with purpose and then abandon that purpose is an insult to divine wisdom. The verse is not just for the disbelievers; it is a question for every human being to ponder. Why am I here? What is the purpose of my existence? The Qur’ân answers: to know and worship Allah, and to be returned to Him for judgment. To live without this purpose is to live like an animal that eats and dies, or worse. The verse is a call to meaning, a call to recognize that our lives matter eternally, and that every choice we make is weighted with the significance of that return to the Creator who fashioned us with intention and care.

=116=

After the extended scenes of judgment, the sûrah turns to a majestic declaration of Allah’s glory: “Exalted is Allah, the True King! There is no god worthy of worship except Him, the Lord of the Honourable Throne.

” The word “exalted” (taʿâlâ) means that Allah is far above any imperfection, any partner, any need, and any comparison to His creation. He is “the True King” (al-Malik al-Ḥaqq)—the King whose sovereignty is real, absolute, and eternal, in contrast to the false, temporary kingships of this world. Pharaoh claimed to be a king and a god, the Quraysh chiefs acted as kings over their tribes, and every tyrant in history has tried to set himself up as a lord. But on the Day of Judgment, all false crowns fall, and the only One remaining as King is Allah, the True Sovereign.

The verse then restates the core creed: “There is no god worthy of worship except Him.” This is the message of every prophet, the purpose of creation, and the dividing line between Paradise and Hell. After witnessing the fate of those who associated partners with Allah, the listener is brought back to the pure foundation of faith.

The verse adds, “the Lord of the Honourable Throne” (Rabb al-ʿArsh al-Karîm). The Throne is the greatest of all created things, symbolizing Allah’s supreme dominion and majesty. The description “Honourable” (Karîm) indicates that the Throne is a place of magnificence, generosity, and nobility. The Lord of such a Throne is not a distant, disinterested deity; He is a King whose rule is characterized by honor, mercy, and justice. This verse serves as a powerful conclusion to the arguments of the sûrah. After the horror of Hell and the bliss of Paradise, the listener is directed to glorify the One who controls all of it. The only proper response to the scenes just witnessed is to declare the transcendence of Allah, to renew one’s commitment to His oneness, and to live in awe of the Lord of the Honourable Throne. The verse is both a doxology and a creed, encapsulating the entire message in a single, breath-taking sentence.

=117=

The sûrah nears its end with a clear, summarizing warning: “Whoever invokes, besides Allah, another god—for which they can have no proof—they will surely find their penalty with their Lord. Indeed, the disbelievers will never succeed.

” The word “invokes” (yadʿu) means to call upon, to worship, to pray to, and to obey. The verse covers anyone who directs any form of devotion to any being other than Allah.

The phrase “another god” (ilâhan âkhar) includes idols, angels, prophets, saints, jinn, one’s own ego, money, or any created thing that is set up as an object of worship alongside or in place of Allah. The critical clause is “for which they can have no proof.” Shirk is not based on evidence or reason; it is based on conjecture, blind imitation, and desire. There is no revelation from Allah that commands the worship of idols, no logical proof that they can benefit or harm, and no innate human knowledge that points to them. The polytheists of Mecca had no scripture, no messenger, and no rational argument for their gods. They simply followed their fathers. The verse states that such people “will surely find their penalty with their Lord.

” The phrase “with their Lord” (ʿinda Rabbihim) indicates that the punishment is stored, preserved, and will be delivered without fail. It is not a possibility; it is a certainty.

The verse then concludes with a powerful echo of the sûrah’s opening: “Indeed, the disbelievers will never succeed.” The first verse said, “Successful indeed are the believers.” The last substantive verse says the disbelievers will never succeed. The contrast is complete.

This is not a temporary failure; it is an eternal loss. The word “never” (lâ yufliḥu) closes the door on any hope for those who die in a state of shirk and denial.

For the believer, this verse is a solemn reminder to guard their tawḥîd with the utmost care, to purify their worship of any hidden shirk, and to be grateful that Allah has given them the proof—the Qur’ân—and guided them to the path of the successful. It is also a motivation to invite others to that proof while there is still time, so that they too may be among those who succeed.

=118=

The sûrah ends, as it began, on a note of hope and supplication: “Say, O Prophet, ‘My Lord! Forgive and have mercy, for You are the best of those who show mercy.’” After the long and weighty journey through the signs of creation, the stories of the prophets, the arguments for resurrection, the descriptions of judgment, the terror of Hell, and the bliss of Paradise, the final word is a duʿâ’.

This is profoundly significant. It tells the believer that the proper response to all that has been revealed is not self-righteousness or despair, but humble, hopeful turning to the Lord of mercy. The Prophet, peace be upon him, though infallible in his mission, is commanded to ask for forgiveness and mercy. If the best of creation is told to do this, how much more must ordinary believers make this prayer their constant refrain? The two requests—forgiveness (maghfirah) and mercy (raḥmah)—cover the two great needs of the human soul. Forgiveness erases the sins of the past and protects from the punishment they deserve. Mercy brings the blessings and goodness that are not earned, culminating in entry into Paradise and the vision of Allah.

The phrase “for You are the best of those who show mercy” acknowledges that all mercy in the universe, whether from a mother to her child or from a friend to a companion, is a reflection of Allah’s mercy. He is the source, and His mercy surpasses all others in quality, quantity, and continuity. By ending the sûrah with this supplication, Allah teaches the believers that no matter how much they know, how many chapters they recite, or how deeply they understand the warnings, their ultimate refuge is always the mercy of their Lord. The sûrah that opened with the declaration of the believers’ success now closes with the secret of that success: seeking forgiveness and mercy from the Most Merciful. It is as if the entire sûrah is enclosed within a frame of divine mercy—the success is a mercy, the guidance is a mercy, and the final word is a prayer for more mercy. For every listener, this verse is an invitation to make this supplication their own, to whisper it in the quiet of the night, to repeat it in the bowing and prostration of prayer, and to live with the comforting assurance that the Lord to whom they turn is the best of those who show mercy.
