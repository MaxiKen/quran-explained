=1=

The surah opens with the single Arabic letter “Nūn,” one of the disconnected letters that preface certain Quranic chapters. Their precise meaning is known fully only to Allah, yet scholars have offered insights that illuminate their purpose. These letters serve as a powerful challenge to the Arabs, who prided themselves on their linguistic mastery: this Quran, which confounds your eloquence, is composed of the very same letters you use daily. The letter Nūn is followed immediately by an oath. Allah swears “by the pen and what everyone writes.” The pen is a tool of immense significance, the instrument through which knowledge is recorded, preserved, and transmitted across generations. In a profound hadith, the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, said that the first thing Allah created was the pen, and He commanded it to write the record of all things until the Day of Judgment.

Thus, the pen symbolizes divine decree and the spread of wisdom. The phrase “and what everyone writes” encompasses every script, every book, every scroll—the entire human and angelic record of knowledge. By taking this oath, Allah elevates the act of writing to a sacred plane, an indirect refutation of the pagan accusation that the Prophet was a madman. A madman does not produce coherent, sublime revelation, nor does he champion the pen and literacy. The Prophet, who himself was unlettered, was now the channel for a Book that would be written, memorized, and recited by billions. The oath also underscores the immense responsibility of the Muslim ummah to be a community of knowledge, reading, and writing. This opening lays the foundation for the defense of the Prophet’s character and sanity, which the following verses will explicitly proclaim. The pen, which records deeds, also reminds us that every word we write and every action we take is being inscribed and will be presented on the Day of Judgment.

Thus, the verse merges the defense of the Prophet with a universal call to value the power of the pen as a divine gift and a witness over human conduct.

=2=

Having sworn by the pen, Allah now delivers the central reassurance that the surah intends to establish. The pagans of Mecca, led by figures like Abu Jahl and Al-Walid ibn al-Mughirah, mounted a campaign of character assassination against the Prophet, calling him a madman, a sorcerer, or a poet possessed by jinn. This verse shatters those lies with a direct address: “By the grace of your Lord, you are not insane.

” The phrase “bi-niʿmati rabbika” invokes the immense favour and blessing that Allah has bestowed upon His Messenger. This grace includes the gift of prophethood, the soundness of intellect, the purity of heart, and the supreme character that the following verses praise. The negation of insanity is emphatic and anchored in that divine grace. It is as if Allah says: the very fact that you have been chosen and surrounded by My grace is the clearest proof that you are not afflicted with madness. A madman is deluded and erratic, but the Prophet’s message was consistent, coherent, and transformative. His personal life, his dealings with family and foe, his steadfastness under persecution, all testified to a mind and soul of the highest order.

The verse also teaches an important principle: when the righteous are slandered, it is Allah who ultimately defends them. The Prophet did not need to hire public relations; the Creator of the universe bore witness to his sanity.

Furthermore, the use of “your Lord” (rabbika) is deeply intimate, reminding the Prophet of the nurturing, caring relationship he has with his Sustainer. In moments of distress, when the insults were heavy, this revelation came as a soothing balm, affirming his worth and silencing his enemies in the most authoritative way possible. For all believers, this verse provides a model of trusting Allah to vindicate us against false accusations, while also reminding us that any spiritual blessing we possess is purely by the grace of our Lord, not our own merit. The stage is now set to describe the immense reward and character of the one they dared to insult.

=3=

After absolving the Prophet of the false charge of madness, Allah immediately comforts him with a promise of immense recompense. The phrase “never-ending reward” translates “ajran ghayra mamnūn,” which carries two beautiful shades of meaning. First, it means a reward that will never be cut off or diminished; it is eternal, continuous, and inexhaustible. Unlike worldly wages that run out, the reward given to the Prophet for his patience, his delivery of the message, and his suffering will flow without end in the Hereafter. Second, “ghayra mamnūn” also means without reproach or reminder of favour. In human interactions, a person might give a gift and then constantly remind the recipient of it, causing embarrassment. But Allah’s reward is given with pure generosity, free from any such belittlement. The Prophet will receive it in perfect honour, without any hint of indebtedness or humiliation. This verse reassured the Prophet that every insult he endured, every sleepless night, every stone thrown at him, was being recorded and would be compensated with a reward so vast that its magnitude could not be conceived. For the believers, this verse establishes a fundamental psychology: the pain of this world is temporary, but the reward for enduring it with faith is without end. It reframes the difficulties of daʿwah and the sting of slander as transactions with Allah that yield eternal profit. The early Muslims in Mecca, who were themselves called crazy and subjected to torture, heard this verse and took heart that their struggles were not in vain.

The mention of a “never-ending reward” also serves as a contrast to the fleeting wealth and status of the Meccan pagans, which they flaunted as a sign of Allah’s favour. The surah will later show how those worldly blessings become a curse for those who are arrogant.

Thus, verse 3 redirects the focus from the temporary noise of the world to the everlasting silence of eternal bliss, grounding the Prophet and his followers in a hope that transcends immediate circumstances.

=4=

This is one of the most magnificent testimonies in the Quran about the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. The word “khuluq” refers to innate disposition, moral character, and habitual conduct. It is the very essence of a person’s behaviour. Allah declares that this character is “ʿaẓīm”—tremendous, great, outstanding. When A’ishah, may Allah be pleased with her, was asked about the Prophet’s character, she famously replied, “His character was the Quran.” He embodied every virtue the Quran commanded: honesty, patience, forgiveness, humility, generosity, bravery, and mercy. His character was not an act he put on; it was the natural outflow of his soul, shaped by divine revelation. The pagans themselves, before his prophethood, called him Al-Amin, the trustworthy.

Yet after the revelation, they resorted to calling him a madman. This verse refutes them by pointing to the very character they had witnessed for forty years. A man of outstanding character is the antithesis of a madman. The greatness of his character is the proof of his prophethood.

This verse also serves as a timeless model for all believers. To follow the Prophet is to strive for this outstanding character, to embody the Quran in one’s daily dealings. The scholars of Islam have derived volumes of ethical teachings from the life of the Prophet, all rooted in this divine compliment.

The phrase “truly” (inna) emphasizes the certainty of this fact. There is no doubt, no exaggeration. The entire universe is being told that this man, accused of madness, sits upon a pedestal of moral excellence that is unmatched. For the believers being persecuted, this was a reminder of the sublime worth of the man they followed, reinforcing their commitment. For all times, this verse remains the ultimate standard of human nobility. A person’s worth is not in lineage, wealth, or power, but in khuluq. And the highest possible khuluq is the one that pleases Allah and is modeled by His final Messenger. This verse bridges the personal comfort given to the Prophet with the universal standard set for humanity.

=5=

The surah now turns to the future, announcing a coming unveiling of truth. The address “you” refers to the Prophet, while “the pagans” are those who accused him of madness.

The word “soon” (sa-tubṣiru) carries a promise that is both near in expectation and certain in occurrence. The pagans thought they had the upper hand in the present, mocking the Prophet and his small band of followers. This verse tells them that a time is coming when the veil of deception will be lifted, and the reality of who is truly afflicted with madness will become absolutely clear. This “seeing” is not merely physical sight but a deep, experiential understanding. It could refer to the day of Badr, where the chiefs of disbelief were slain, or to the conquest of Mecca, where the Prophet entered victorious, or most comprehensively, to the Day of Judgment, where the ultimate truth will be displayed before all creation. The vagueness of the timing is deliberate; it keeps the threat immediate and continuous. For the believers, this verse was a powerful injection of hope. They were told that their current humiliation was temporary, that a day of vindication was coming when they would see the truth of the Prophet’s message and the falsehood of the pagans with their own eyes. It also serves as a psychological blow to the arrogant disbelievers, who prided themselves on their sight yet were blind to the truth. They would soon “see” in a way they never wanted to.

This verse also teaches the principle of divine patience: Allah may give respite to the wrongdoers, but the moment of clarity will inevitably arrive, and when it does, the positions of the oppressor and the oppressed will be reversed. The believer is urged to remain patient and wait for this divine “soon,” which is closer than one might think.

=6=

This verse completes the statement begun in the previous one. The pagans and the Prophet are challenged: soon it will be manifest “which of you is mad.

” The Arabic construction “bi-ayyikumu al-maftūn” literally means “in which of you is the affliction of madness.” The question is rhetorical and the answer is predetermined. The pagans had called the Prophet a “maftūn,” one afflicted by madness or a trial from Satan. But in reality, their own denial of the clear signs, their worship of stones, their burying of daughters alive, their greed, and their hostility to the one who called them to purity—these were the true symptoms of spiritual madness. Their intellects were clouded by arrogance and worldly attachment, making them incapable of seeing the truth. This verse is a powerful example of Quranic rhetoric: it takes the very insult hurled at the Prophet and turns it back on the accusers, but with the weight of divine judgment behind it. The “seeing” mentioned in the previous verse will reveal that the Prophet is the sanest of all humans, the most guided, the most upright, while his accusers are the ones lost in the madness of disbelief. Some scholars also point out that the punishment they will witness—the swords of Badr, the terror of the grave, the Fire of Hell—will make them appear as madmen in their panic and regret.

For the believer, this verse is a reassurance that truth is not determined by majority vote or by the loudness of the mockers. The sane one in the sight of Allah is the one who believes, regardless of how the world labels him. This directly comforts those who are called extremists, backward, or irrational for holding onto their faith. The ultimate court of appeal is the Day when the Lord of the worlds will announce who was truly afflicted with madness. The verse is a profound closure to the initial defense of the Prophet, leaving the judgment to the only Judge whose verdict matters.

=7=

This verse provides the theological foundation for the previous challenge. The knowledge of who is truly astray and who is guided is not a matter of human opinion, propaganda, or social status; it is exclusively with Allah.

The phrase “Your Lord alone knows best” emphasizes that divine knowledge is perfect, absolute, and comprehensive. He knows the inner secrets, the hidden intentions, the subtle biases, and the ultimate outcomes that human beings can never fully grasp. The pagans of Mecca judged by outward appearances: they saw their wealth and power as signs of being guided, and the Prophet’s lack of worldly stature as a sign of being astray. This verse demolishes that false standard. Allah is the One who defines the “Way,” the straight path of Islam, and He alone knows the exact position of every soul along that path. This also contains a subtle warning to the believers: do not become arrogant or judgmental, for even you cannot know the final state of others; that knowledge belongs to Allah. The verse serves as a comfort to the Prophet, who was pained by the misguidance of his people. He is being told that his duty is to convey the message; the judgment of who is astray and who is guided rests with his Lord, who is fully aware of everything.

This verse also refutes the common misconception that worldly success indicates divine favour. Many who are astray enjoy temporary prosperity, while many who are guided face hardship. The real criterion is the state of the heart and the final destination in the Hereafter. The words “knows best” (huwa aʿlamu) remind us of the limitation of human knowledge and the necessity of humility. The believer accepts that Allah’s wisdom may classify things differently from society’s classifications. This verse is a powerful anchor for any Muslim facing the accusation of being on the wrong path; we refer the matter to Allah, the All-Knowing, and continue steadfastly on His Way, trusting that He knows the truth.

=8=

Having established the Prophet’s lofty station and the ultimate judgment, Allah now issues a direct command of firmness. The phrase “do not give in” translates “fa-lā tuṭiʿi,” which means do not obey, do not yield, do not comply. The “deniers” (al-mukadhdhibīn) are those who persistently reject the truth despite clear proofs.

This command is a protection for the Prophet against the immense pressure he was facing. The Quraysh had tried various tactics: bribery, threats, negotiations, and emotional manipulation. They offered him wealth, kingship, and the most beautiful women if he would just tone down his message or stop criticizing their idols. Some even suggested a compromise: they would worship his God for a year if he worshipped their gods for a year. This verse is the divine firewall against all such proposals. The message of absolute monotheism is non-negotiable. The Prophet is to remain utterly unwavering, no matter how powerful or persuasive the deniers appear. For the believers, this verse lays down the principle that there can be no compromise in matters of fundamental truth. While Islam encourages kindness, diplomacy, and wisdom in daʿwah, the core of faith cannot be diluted to please the disbelievers. The Prophet’s life is a practical example of this command. Despite facing the most severe persecution, he never once wavered in proclaiming the oneness of Allah. His uncle Abu Talib, out of tribal concern, asked him to stop, and he replied, “By Allah, if they put the sun in my right hand and the moon in my left to leave this matter, I would not leave it until Allah makes it prevail or I perish.” This verse is the written form of that resolve. It teaches that a believer’s ultimate loyalty is to Allah’s command, not to human approval. In every age, the deniers may ask believers to compromise their ethics, their modesty, their integrity, or their worship; this command echoes across time: do not give in to them.

=9=

This verse reveals the hidden psychology and strategy of the deniers. The word “waddū” expresses a deep desire, a strong wish. They earnestly hoped that the Prophet would soften his stance, that he would “compromise”—the Arabic word “tudhin” comes from a root meaning to be smooth, to flatter, to be lenient to the point of bending the truth. They wanted him to speak well of their idols, to accommodate their traditions, to meet them halfway. In return, they would then “yield” or “compromise” back. This was a classic negotiation tactic: let us all give a little, and we can coexist peacefully. But what they were asking for was not a peaceful coexistence; it was a dilution of tawhid. The verse exposes that their demand for compromise was not a genuine search for truth but a ploy to invalidate the Prophet’s message. If he had compromised on even a single point of revelation, his entire claim of divine inspiration would have collapsed. This verse is a timeless warning against the spirit of syncretism that seeks to blend truth with falsehood for the sake of social harmony. The Prophet was never harsh in his manners, but he was uncompromising in his principles. The Muslims of Mecca were under pressure to just “accept” some of the pagan practices to ease the tension, but this verse forbade it. The beauty of the Quranic argument is that it unmasks the desire behind the enemy’s request. They were not looking for a bridge; they were looking for a surrender disguised as a bridge. For the modern believer, this applies to situations where one is pressured to accept un-Islamic values—whether in business, education, or social norms—under the guise of being moderate or progressive. The verse instructs us to recognize the agenda behind such calls and to maintain the purity of faith while continuing to interact with the world in a just and ethical manner. The Prophet’s mission was to bring people to the truth, not to be brought to falsehood by them.

=10=

After the general command not to obey the deniers, the surah zooms in on a particular archetype of evil that the Prophet must resist. The description is so detailed that many commentators identify it with a specific Meccan leader, Al-Walid ibn al-Mughirah, though the characteristics apply to all who share such vices. The command “do not obey” is repeated for emphasis.

The first trait is “despicable, vain oath-taker. ” “Ḥallāf” means one who swears excessively, making false oaths to cover up his lies, to impress others, or to win arguments. The constant swearing indicates a lack of reverence for Allah’s name, using it as a tool for deceit. “Mahīn” means contemptible, lowly, and vile—despite any outward show of honour, his inner reality is abject. This combination paints a picture of a person who has no moral anchor, whose word is meaningless, and whose soul is debased. The Prophet is commanded to recognize such a person’s worthlessness and not to let his oaths or his threats influence the mission. In the Meccan context, the chiefs would swear by their gods and by Allah that they were sincere, that they meant well for the Prophet, while plotting against him. This verse is a timeless lesson in evaluating the character of those we listen to and obey. A person who trivializes oaths and is known for lying is not to be trusted, no matter how powerful or eloquent.

For the believer, it underscores the sanctity of oaths and the importance of maintaining a character that is the opposite of this: truthful and dignified. The verse also serves as a warning not to be impressed by those who swear grandly to prove their point; true honour comes from honesty, not from loud, empty affirmations. The Prophet was protected by this revelation from falling into the trap of listening to such a corrupt individual.

=11=

The description of the vile character continues with two deeply destructive social vices. “Hammāz” is the habitual slanderer, one who finds fault and belittles others by gesture, word, or insinuation. This person is constantly criticizing, mocking, and attacking the reputation of the righteous, particularly the Prophet. He uses sarcasm and backbiting to undermine the believers and to sow discord. “Mashshā’in bi-namīm” is the gossip-monger who walks around spreading tales and malicious talk between people to create enmity. He hears something in one gathering and rushes to relay it, with added embellishments, to another, fracturing relationships. This pair of traits is especially toxic in a community. The slanderer attacks individuals directly, while the gossip-monger destroys the bonds of trust between them. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said, “A slanderer will not enter Paradise.” He also taught that the most beloved to Allah are those with good character, and the most hated are those who spread gossip and divide friends. In the case of Al-Walid and others, they would mock the Prophet in their private councils, call him a madman behind his back, and then come to him with a false face. The Quran is exposing their real face. For the believers, this verse is a stern warning to guard their tongues. A community that tolerates slander and gossip will inevitably crumble. The fact that these traits are listed alongside the oath-taker shows that such a person’s entire public and private life is corrupted. They have no truth in their oaths, no goodness in their speech, and no desire for unity. The believer is instructed not to obey such people and, by extension, to never become such a person.

The emphasis on “walking about” (mashshā’) in gossip indicates a proactive, energetic effort to spread corruption, showing that this is not a passive slip but a lifestyle of evil. The preservation of social harmony and personal honour is a core objective of Islamic ethics.

=12=

The catalogue of vices expands to include stinginess and active aggression. “Mannāʿin lil-khayr” is the one who habitually withholds good.

The term “khayr” encompasses all forms of goodness: wealth that should be spent in charity, acts of kindness, advice, help, and even the testimony of truth. This person not only fails to do good but actively prevents it. He is a barrier to every noble initiative. When the Prophet called for feeding the poor and freeing the slaves, the Meccan chiefs blocked these acts, hoarding their wealth and discouraging others from giving. “Muʿtadin” is the transgressor, one who crosses all boundaries of justice, morality, and law. His oppression is not occasional; it is his nature. He violates the rights of others, whether through physical harm, economic exploitation, or verbal abuse. “Athīm” is the one deeply immersed in sin, particularly sins that harm others. This word indicates that his inner state is saturated with evil. Together, these three traits depict a man who is stingy with his wealth, aggressive with his power, and corrupt in his soul. He is a complete package of social harm. The Prophet is told to recognize such a person and not to be swayed by any position or wealth he might possess.

This description also served as a mirror for the Meccan elite; they prided themselves on their hospitality and their wealth, but the Quran reveals that their refusal to spend in the way of Allah and their violent opposition to the truth made them the real withholders of good. For the believer, this verse warns against the love of money that leads to stinginess, against the arrogance that leads to transgression, and against the normalization of sin. The path of faith is the path of open-handedness, justice, and purity. The specific order of these traits shows a progression: the person first withholds good, then transgresses, and finally sinks into complete evildoing. It is a downward spiral that must be cut off at the root.

=13=

The culmination of this dark portrait uses two particularly severe terms. “‘Utullin” is translated as brute, but it carries connotations of a harsh, cruel, ill-mannered, and greedy person. In Arabic, it refers to someone who is coarse in appearance and character, a thick-necked bully who devours food and shows no gentleness. The Prophet, peace be upon him, in a hadith, indicated that the “ʿutull” is one who is violent and harsh even with his own family, a person devoid of the softness of faith. Then comes the final, devastating description: “zanīm.” This word has multiple layers of meaning. Most classical commentators understand it as a person of illegitimate birth—one whose lineage is not pure, born out of wedlock, and later attached to a tribe to which he does not truly belong. The specific individual indicated, Al-Walid ibn al-Mughirah, was reportedly such a person, who was adopted into the Quraysh clan at a later age.

The word also means an intrusive person, a hanger-on who does not belong, someone whose presence is a blemish on a noble lineage. This divine exposure of a hidden personal shame was a powerful sign of the Quran’s truth, as it revealed a secret that the man himself tried to conceal.

The mention of “on top of all that” (baʿda dhālika) indicates that after all those vices, he is burdened with this ultimate disgrace of doubtful lineage. It strips him of any social prestige he might have claimed. For the believers, this verse is not a license to investigate people’s lineages or to insult their parentage; rather, it demonstrates that Allah knows the hidden realities and can expose them when needed to defend His Prophet. It is a warning that nobility is not by birth but by faith and character. A person of high status but illegitimate origin may use his position to fight the truth, and Allah will reveal his true worth.

This verse also teaches the grave sin of slandering lineage in general, but here it is a specific divine verdict that serves as a miracle. The comprehensive description of this archetype serves as a timeless profile of the kind of influencer whose words and leadership must be rejected by the people of faith.

=14=

This verse exposes the root cause of the arrogant behavior described earlier. The despicable character, despite his vile traits, has been granted abundant wealth and many children by Allah. Instead of recognizing these as a test and a trust from his Lord, he sees them as a proof of his superiority and correctness.

The phrase “because he has been blessed” (an kāna dhā mālin wa-banīn) indicates that his reaction to Allah’s blessings is perverse: he uses them as a justification for his arrogance. The pagans of Mecca operated on a materialistic worldview: the one with more wealth and sons was favoured by the gods. The Quran reverses this logic. Wealth and children are a test, not a badge of divine approval. Al-Walid ibn al-Mughirah, to whom these verses particularly point, was one of the wealthiest men in Mecca, with ten sons, and he was known as the “unparalleled one. ” He used his riches to fund the opposition against the Prophet.

The verse teaches that worldly prosperity can be a great deception. A person may be drowning in blessings while being utterly bankrupt in the eyes of Allah. The blessings are given as a respite and a trial; they increase his responsibility and will become a witness against him on the Day of Judgment.

For the believer, this verse corrects any envy of the wealthy disbeliever. Their glitter is temporary, and their accountability is tremendous. It also serves as a warning to those who have been blessed with wealth and children not to fall into the same trap of pride. The true believer increases in humility with every blessing, knowing that it is a gift that can be withdrawn, and a test that can be failed. The surah thus deconstructs the false foundation of Meccan elitism, preparing for the following verse where this very man dismisses the Quran because of his worldly standing. The believer’s worth is not measured by the balance in the bank but by the balance of deeds in the scale.

=15=

The true nature of this corrupt character now manifests in his reaction to the divine word. When the clear, powerful, and transformative verses of the Quran are recited to him, he waves them away with a contemptuous label: “Ancient fables!” (asāṭīru al-awwalīn). This phrase implies that the Quran is nothing but old stories, myths, and legends written down from the tales of previous generations, not a genuine revelation from God. This accusation was a cornerstone of the Meccan propaganda. They knew the stories of ʿĀd and Thamūd, of Noah and Moses, were striking a chord, so they tried to discredit them by calling them fairy tales.

The use of “whenever” indicates that this was a persistent, knee-jerk reaction. He did not pause to reflect; he immediately dismissed it to protect his pride and his position. This reveals the psychology of the arrogant sinner: the truth threatens his status, so he must quickly label it as worthless to avoid confronting its implications. His wealth and children, mentioned in the previous verse, become the blinders that prevent him from seeing the light. He thinks, “How could a revelation be sent to that orphan, while I, the wealthy chief, am left out?” So he belittles the message. For the Prophet and the believers, this was heartbreaking to witness. But this verse exposed the baselessness of the objection. The Quran’s eloquence, its profound wisdom, and its accurate accounts of history and nature clearly distinguish it from human tales. The pagans themselves were illiterate; they had no way of knowing these stories except through the Prophet, who was also unlettered. This verse is a timeless mirror for those who, armed with modern wealth and status, mock religion as outdated mythology without giving it a fair and open-minded examination. The response of the believer is to recognize that such dismissals stem from arrogance, not from reason, and to continue reciting the revelations with patience, trusting that the truth will eventually break through the wall of pride.

=16=

This verse delivers a severe, specific threat to the arrogant slanderer. The expression “mark his snout” translates “sa-nasimuhu ʿalā al-khurṭūm.

” The word “khurṭūm” usually refers to the nose of an elephant or a pig, or the prominent nose of a human. By using this term, Allah strips the proud chieftain of his dignity, comparing his most prominent facial feature to that of a beast. The “mark” or “brand” is a sign of disgrace that will be visible and permanent. Most classical commentators state that this was fulfilled at the Battle of Badr, where Al-Walid ibn al-Mughirah’s son, or the man himself in some reports, was wounded on the nose, leaving a scar that served as a perpetual shame. In a broader sense, it refers to the mark of humiliation in this world and the branding of the sinners in the Hereafter, where they will be known by their marks. The verse is a direct response to his insolence: he used his mouth and nose to mock the Prophet and sniff at the truth, and now that very organ will be publicly marked.

This is an example of the Quran’s majestic justice, using a word that perfectly fits the crime. The mention of “soon” indicates the imminence of the punishment, a promise that the believers would witness. For the believers enduring mockery, this verse was a thunderbolt of reassurance. The one who arrogantly called the revelation fables and looked down on the Prophet would himself be brought low in a humiliating way. It taught them that no one gets away with insulting Allah and His Messenger; the retaliation may be delayed, but it is certain.

The verse also serves as a warning against the sin of pride in one’s appearance and lineage. The face that was so full of arrogance will be the very site of his degradation. The imagery is powerful and unforgettable, etching into the hearts of the listeners the certainty of divine retribution against mockery. It also reinforces that Allah defends His Prophet through means that no human court can replicate.

=17=

Having warned the specific denier, the surah now broadens the warning by drawing a parallel between the Meccan pagans and a community mentioned in the ancient lore—the owners of the garden. Allah says, “We have tested them,” meaning the Meccans are undergoing a similar trial of wealth, just as the earlier people were. The test was in the form of a lush garden, a source of immense provision. The owners were a group of brothers, or a family, who inherited a garden that produced abundant fruit. Instead of being grateful and fulfilling the rights of the poor, they became arrogant and plotted to deny the needy their share. The verse sets the scene: they swore to each other, making a determined resolution to harvest all the fruit early in the morning, before the poor could arrive. This premeditated plan was driven by greed and a fear that if they continued the custom of giving a portion, their own wealth would decrease.

The Arabic “la-yaṣrimunnahā” means to cut the fruit, to harvest it completely, leaving nothing behind. This determination, sealed with an oath, was their act of transgression. The Meccans, similarly, had been blessed with the trade routes, the sanctity of their city, and abundant provision, yet they refused to share with the poor and most importantly, refused to accept the Prophet’s message, behaving arrogantly toward the one who called them to purify their wealth and souls. The “test” here is the test of prosperity, which often proves harder than the test of adversity. In prosperity, one forgets the Giver and becomes possessive. This verse introduces the parable, which will unfold over several verses, to illustrate the swift and devastating consequences of ingratitude and stinginess. It serves as a stark warning that the wealth the Meccans prided themselves on could be taken away overnight if they continued in their arrogance and denial of Allah’s rights.

=18=

This verse pinpoints the spiritual crime at the heart of the garden owners’ plot. They made their firm oath to harvest everything “leaving no thought for Allah’s Will,” which translates the crucial phrase “wa-lā yastathnūn.” This means they failed to say “in shā’ Allāh” (if Allah wills). The omission was not a simple forgetfulness; it was a symptom of their arrogance. They felt so confident in their own planning, their own strength, and the certainty of their harvest that they saw no need to condition their action on the will of the Creator. They behaved as if the garden and its fruits belonged to them absolutely, and as if the future was under their control. The act of saying “in shā’ Allāh” is a profound expression of tawhid. It acknowledges that no human plan can succeed without Allah’s permission, that every future event is contingent on His will. To omit it deliberately is to claim a partnership with Allah in power and knowledge.

The Quran teaches the believers the etiquette of always attributing future actions to Allah’s will, as in Surah Al-Kahf: “And never say of anything, ‘I will do that tomorrow,’ except [when adding] ‘If Allah wills.’” The garden owners’ failure to do so was the outward sign of an inner disease: reliance on material means and forgetfulness of the Provider. The Meccan pagans similarly relied on their idols, their trade, and their tribal strength, giving no thought to Allah’s overriding will. They thought their prosperity would last forever. This verse is a profound lesson for all believers in planning and tawakkul. We must exert our best effort, but we must always tether our hearts and our words to the recognition that the ultimate outcome rests with Allah alone. Forgetting this is the first step toward arrogance and eventual loss. The verse prepares the way for the next, where the punishment descends as a direct consequence of this spiritual negligence. The garden was not just destroyed by a natural disaster; it was destroyed by the very arrogance that dispensed with the need for Allah.

=19=

The punishment was swift and decisive. A “torment from your Lord” (ṭā’ifun min rabbika) is a term that conveys a divine visitation of destruction, a sweeping calamity that comes by night.

The word “ṭā’if” implies something that encircles and encompasses completely. Commentators mention that it could have been a fire from the sky, a storm, or a thunderbolt that utterly devastated the garden. The key phrase is “while they slept,” which highlights two things: the peace and unawareness of the owners as they rested, smug in their plan, and the suddenness of divine retribution. They went to bed dreaming of their harvest, and by morning, their life’s work was reduced to nothing.

This is the nature of divine punishment when it comes; it does not announce itself. It arrives when the sinners feel most secure. The Meccans, similarly, slept peacefully in their valleys, plotting against the Prophet, thinking their power was unassailable. The verse serves to instill a profound sense of fear: no locked gate, no guard, no wall can prevent the punishment of Allah when it is decreed.

The phrase “from your Lord” is personal and intimate; it reminds the Prophet that the same Lord who consoles him is also the Lord of justice who defends him. The believers, who were often kept awake by fear of the pagans, are told that their enemies’ security is an illusion. A single night can change everything.

The verse also demonstrates that the wealth one refuses to share can be taken away in a single moment by the One who truly owns it. The garden owners wanted to keep everything for themselves, and in the end, they lost everything. The punishment was not just a material loss but a sign, a test for their souls. The believers are taught to always feel insecure about their own attachment to worldly goods and to seek protection in gratitude and charity.

The next verse will paint the horrifying scene they woke up to, completing the parable’s powerful lesson.

=20=

The final state of the garden is described in stark, devastating terms. “It was reduced to ashes” translates “fa-aṣbaḥat kaṣ-ṣarīm.

” The word “ṣarīm” has two primary meanings, both applicable. It can mean a dark, blackened ruin, like the ash and burnt remains of a field after a fire, where nothing green remains. It can also mean a garden whose fruit has been entirely cut off, like a night that is “ṣarīm” because it is cut off from daylight, or a barren land. The garden, which the night before was lush and full of ripe produce, was now a desolate wasteland. The contrast between their confident oaths and this reality is the core of the parable’s impact. They had sworn to “cut” the fruit in the morning, but Allah “cut” the entire garden. Their own word turned against them.

This is a recurring Quranic theme: the plots of the wicked boomerang. The vivid imagery would resonate deeply with the Meccans, who relied on their gardens in Taif and the fertile valleys for their livelihood. The verse is a warning that Allah can turn a paradise into a wasteland in the blink of an eye. The owners’ loss was total; there was no partial salvage, no remaining tree to lean on.

This is the outcome of ingratitude and the refusal to give the poor their due. For the believers, it reinforces the principle that charity does not decrease wealth; rather, withholding it invites destruction. The Prophet said, “Charity does not decrease wealth. ” The garden owners feared poverty if they gave, and they met poverty in its most extreme form. The verse serves as a timeless admonition against the love of wealth and the illusion of self-sufficiency. The story will continue with their bewildered reaction, but this verse is the visual climax of the punishment. It shows that the provisions we hoard are ultimately not ours, and the One who granted them can instantly reclaim them. The believer’s heart should always remain attached to the Provider, not the provision.
