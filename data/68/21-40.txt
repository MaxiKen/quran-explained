=21=

The scene shifts to the early morning, just as the owners of the garden had planned. The phrase “by daybreak” indicates the first light of dawn, the very time they had intended to harvest their fruit secretly before the poor could gather. Their calling out to one another reflects their eagerness and the coordinated nature of their plan.

The Arabic “tanādaw” conveys a mutual calling, as if one roused the other, all fully committed to the scheme they had sworn to carry out. This image of them waking up with determination is tragically ironic, because during the night, while they slept, Allah’s punishment had already struck. They rise with purpose, completely unaware that their world has already been undone.

This is a profound illustration of how humans can make meticulous plans while being utterly ignorant of the divine decree that has already been executed. The Qur’an often juxtaposes human plotting with divine overriding power, and here it is stark: they were calling each other to a harvest that no longer existed. For the Meccan pagans, this was a mirror. They plotted against the Prophet, held councils at night, and confidently predicted their victory, yet the divine plan was in motion, and they were calling each other to a mirage. The believer learns that while we plan and strive, we must remain humble, knowing that the final outcome is not in our hands. The morning call also symbolises the awakening of the sinner to the consequences of his deeds, an awakening that often comes too late. The verse builds suspense, drawing the listener into the story, as we anticipate the moment of discovery. It also conveys the energy and materialism of these people: they rise early to maximise profit, but they have left no room for God. Their call is purely worldly. The surah thus teaches a deep lesson about the morning of life: one may rise to pursue wealth, but if the heart is not connected to Allah, one may find only ruin. The subsequent verses will reveal their words and then their shock, completing the parable’s devastating power.

=22=

The content of their early morning call is now revealed. They urge one another, “Go early to your harvest,” literally “to your cultivated land or garden.” The instruction is urgent and exclusive; they want to get there before anyone else.

The phrase “if you want to pick all the fruit” reveals their mindset: their sole objective is to gather every last piece of produce, leaving nothing to chance and, as will be made explicit, nothing for the poor. The Arabic “ṣārimīn” means to cut or harvest completely. There is an intensity and a finality to their words. They see the garden as purely their possession, a source of wealth that they have the right to exploit entirely for themselves. This attitude is the essence of the miserly and arrogant mindset condemned in this surah. It mirrors the earlier description of the “withholder of good” (mannāʿin lil-khayr). The garden was a test, a blessing from Allah, but they treated it as an entitlement. The verse starkly portrays the pre-Islamic and indeed universal human tendency to become possessive and to view charity not as a purification but as a loss. There is no mention of thanking Allah, no remembrance of His bounty, no intention to share. Their speech is entirely secular and materialistic. For the believers, this is a warning: when approaching one’s livelihood, the inner dialogue should be filled with reliance on Allah and the intention to use the wealth for good, not just to hoard. The Prophet taught that a believer’s morning should begin with the name of Allah. The absence of God in their words is the spiritual vacuum that precedes disaster.

The verse also highlights the psychological state of those who are tested with wealth: they become fixated on maximising gain and controlling outcomes, forgetting that the true Owner can reclaim His property at any moment. The Meccans, similarly, saw their trade caravans and their wealth as theirs, mocking the idea that they should spend in the way of Allah. The parable is direct and piercing, and the next verse intensifies the secret plotting that often accompanies such greed.

=23=

The group now physically departs for the garden, but their conversation changes tone. They “went off whispering to one another.

” The Arabic “yata khāfatūn” means they spoke in hushed, secretive tones, as if afraid of being overheard. This behaviour indicates a guilty conscience. They are not just planning a harvest; they are plotting to execute a stingy, exclusionary plan. The secrecy suggests they know their intention is morally wrong. If they were simply harvesting their own lawful property, there would be no need for whispers. They are conspiring to prevent the poor from taking what was customarily or charitably given to them. Some classical commentators mention that the garden had been a generous source for the needy, perhaps as a legacy from a righteous father. These sons, however, inherited the garden but not the piety, and they resented the tradition of sharing. Their whispers are the antithesis of the open, generous spirit of faith. The scene is reminiscent of the secret councils of the Quraysh, who would meet at night to plot against the Prophet and then whisper their accusations to each other. Allah exposes their hushed conversations, teaching that no secret plotting escapes divine hearing.

For the believer, this verse underscores the importance of transparency and the repulsiveness of secret schemes to withhold good. Charity, especially, is best done openly if it encourages others, though secret charity is also praised when done to avoid show. But here the secrecy is for a malicious purpose: to deprive the vulnerable. The psychological profile painted is of a community corrupted by wealth, where people sneak around to protect their greed. The whispering also isolates them; they cut themselves off from the wider community of the poor, and ultimately, they are cut off from Allah’s mercy.

The surah continues to build the image of their arrogance, leading up to the specific exclusionary command in the next verse.

=24=

The content of their whisper is now explicitly stated. They agree on a strict exclusion policy: “Do not let any poor person enter your garden today.

” The word “miskīn” refers to a destitute person, one who is impoverished and in need. The phrase “your garden” reinforces their sense of absolute ownership, forgetting that the garden is a trust from Allah. They see the poor as intruders rather than as fellow human beings with a right to a share of the provision.

This is the climax of their miserliness. They are determined to harvest every fruit themselves and to bar the needy from even entering the premises.

This command is a violation of the divine command to give the poor their due, and it reflects a society where the wealthy have completely severed the bonds of human compassion. In pre-Islamic Mecca, the poor, orphans, and widows were often exploited and neglected. The Prophet’s message of charity and social justice was a direct challenge to this greed. This verse serves as a timeless condemnation of any economic system or individual attitude that hoards wealth and excludes the weak. The fact that they say “today” indicates that this was perhaps a specific, intensified act of greed; perhaps in the past they allowed some gleaning, but on this day, seeing the abundant harvest, they resolved to keep it all. Their planned day of maximum profit became the day of maximum loss.

The verse teaches that a society that marginalises its poor is on the brink of divine wrath. For the individual believer, it serves as a warning to always open one’s doors and wallet to the needy, with kindness, never with contempt. The exclusion of the poor from the garden is the symbolic reason for their punishment, as Allah takes the side of the oppressed and the needy. The surah powerfully demonstrates that the true destitution is not the lack of wealth but the lack of mercy in the heart. This whispered command sealed their fate, and the next verses reveal the shattering reality of their morning.

=25=

The verse describes their determined, early morning march to the garden. The word “ghadaw” indicates going out early, at dawn, which shows their eagerness. But the key phrase is “ʿalā ḥardin qādirīn.

” The word “ḥard” has been interpreted in multiple ways, all converging on a negative state. It can mean they were “totally fixated” on their purpose, meaning they had a single-minded, intense resolve to carry out their greedy plan, leaving no room for second thought or compassion. Another interpretation is that “ḥard” means to prevent or to be severe, so they were determined to prevent the poor from receiving anything, and they felt fully capable of doing so.

The phrase “qādirīn” indicates that they considered themselves powerful, in control, and able to accomplish their goal. They had the manpower, the tools, and the will. This combination—stern determination and a sense of power—is the peak of human arrogance. They felt no need for Allah’s will, as shown earlier, and they were absolutely sure of their ability. The verse captures the final moment of false confidence before the fall. The Qur’an uses this psychological portrait to show that when humans feel most powerful, they are actually most vulnerable to divine intervention. The Meccans, too, marched forth with their armies and their plans, feeling invincible, only to be defeated at Badr and elsewhere. The lesson for the believer is to never rely on one’s own “qādīr” capacity, for true power belongs only to Allah. The resolve to do good is praiseworthy, but resolve to commit injustice, coupled with a sense of self-sufficiency, is a recipe for disaster. The verse sets the stage perfectly for the shocking discovery in the next verse. The contrast between their determined, able march and the desolation they will find is stark. It is a reminder that all human strength is a mirage if it is not aligned with the divine will. The surah is teaching that the test of wealth is to humble oneself, not to swell with a sense of control and power over the resources Allah has granted.

=26=

The moment of confrontation has arrived. They reach the garden, but their eyes do not recognise it. The lush, fruit-laden paradise they had left the night before is gone, replaced by a blackened, barren ruin. Their immediate reaction is one of total disorientation. They cry out, “We must have lost our way!

” The Arabic “innā la-ḍāllūn” means “we are astray” in terms of direction. They assume they have taken a wrong path and arrived at someone else’s destroyed land, not their own garden. This initial thought shows how impossible it was for them to conceive that their garden, which seemed so permanent and secure, could have been destroyed overnight. Their minds are unable to process the reality, so the only logical explanation is a navigational error.

This is a profoundly human reaction: when faced with a catastrophe that shatters our worldview, we often first deny it or misinterpret it. The verse captures the disbelief and confusion of the arrogant when divine punishment strikes.

The word “ḍāllūn” also has an ironic layer; in the spiritual sense, they were indeed astray, but they had never admitted it. Now, their physical misguidance mirrors their spiritual misguidance. The Meccans, upon seeing the truth of the Prophet’s message and the eventual victory of the believers, would similarly be bewildered and unable to comprehend how their power evaporated.

For the believer, this verse inspires awe at the suddenness with which Allah can alter circumstances. It teaches that nothing in this world is truly ours; what we think we possess can be taken in a moment, and we might not even recognise the place we once thought we owned. The cry “We must have lost our way” is the first crack in their wall of arrogance, and the next verse will force them to face the unbearable truth.

=27=

The initial confusion gives way to a dawning, horrifying realization. After inspecting the area and perhaps recognising the boundaries of the land, they correct themselves.

The phrase “In fact” translates “bal,” which is a word used to retract a previous statement and assert a new, often opposite, fact. They are not astray in terms of location; they are in the right place. The new, terrible truth is: “We have been deprived.

” The Arabic “maḥrūmūn” comes from ḥaram, meaning to forbid or deprive. They have been utterly cut off from the provision they had expected. The garden’s fruit, which they had sworn to harvest, has been forbidden to them by a power greater than their own.

The word also carries the sense of being deprived of a blessing due to one’s own actions. This is the moment of full awareness. Their plot to deprive the poor has backfired, and they themselves are now the deprived ones. The recognition is not accompanied by immediate repentance but rather by a sense of shock and loss. They see the physical manifestation of divine wrath, but initially, they only grieve their material loss. This mirrors the state of many people who, when struck by calamity, focus on the loss of wealth or status, rather than on the spiritual cause.

However, this acknowledgment is a necessary step. The Meccans, too, when faced with defeat and famine, would cry out about their misfortune before some of them turned to faith. The verse is a powerful reminder of the reality of divine retribution in this life.

The word “maḥrūmūn” is also a mirror: they wanted to make the poor maḥrūm, and now they are the maḥrūm. The exact punishment fits the crime.

For the believer, this verse is a warning to never scheme to deprive anyone of their rights, for the Depriver of all is Allah, and He can turn the tables in an instant. It also teaches that true deprivation is not the loss of a crop, but the loss of faith and Allah’s pleasure, which they had already suffered.

=28=

Amid the shock and despair, a voice of reason emerges. One member of the group, described as “the most sensible of them” (awsaṭuhum), which can mean the most moderate, the best, or the most intelligent, steps forward. He reminds them of his earlier advice. The phrasing “Did I not urge you” shows that he had counselled them against their arrogant omission, but they had ignored him. He had urged them to say “in shā’ Allāh,” to condition their plan on the will of Allah. The fact that he is now the one speaking indicates that there was some good left in the group, or at least that a minority voice of wisdom was present but overruled. The lesson is profound: even among those who are ultimately punished, the presence of one who advises good is a mercy, but the majority’s stubbornness leads to the destruction of all, though the sensible one’s individual accountability differs.

The verse also reveals that the root cause of the calamity was not merely the intention to exclude the poor, but the deeper spiritual disease of self-sufficiency, symbolised by the failure to say “Allah willing. ” Saying “in shā’ Allāh” is not a magical incantation; it is an expression of dependence on and trust in Allah. The sensible man understood that all human plans are subject to the divine will, and to ignore that is to invite disaster. For the Meccan pagans, this was a call to recognise that their plots against the Prophet would fail unless Allah willed them, and since they were opposing His messenger, He would never will their success. For the believers, this verse underscores the critical importance of always remembering Allah in one’s plans, not just verbally but with the heart. The sensible man’s rebuke is a moment of moral clarity in the story, a reminder that truth is often spoken by those who are ignored by the powerful. His words will now lead to the collective confession in the following verse.

=29=

The wise man’s rebuke finally breaks through their arrogance. Their response is a collective exclamation of “Glory be to our Lord!”—“Subḥāna rabbinā.

” This is an immediate declaration of Allah’s transcendence and perfection. They acknowledge that He is far above any injustice or flaw, and that the fault lies entirely with themselves.

This is the first proper spiritual utterance in the story. They had previously not mentioned Allah except in their oath, but now they glorify Him.

The second part, “We have truly been wrongdoers” (innā kunnā ẓālimīn), is a full admission of guilt. The word “ẓulm” means oppression and wrongdoing, particularly the sin of placing something out of its proper place. They oppressed the poor by withholding their right, they oppressed themselves by their arrogance, and they committed the ultimate ẓulm of failing to recognise Allah’s absolute sovereignty. This confession is significant. It shows that the calamity achieved its spiritual purpose: it shattered their false pride and brought them to repentance. The story contrasts sharply with the earlier description of the arrogant pagan chief who, despite receiving revelation, persisted in calling it “ancient fables. ” Those who refuse to repent are destroyed eternally, while those who repent after a worldly trial may yet earn Allah’s mercy. Some scholars suggest this confession may have been a genuine turning back to Allah, or at least an acknowledgment of their specific sin. For the believers, this verse teaches that the appropriate response to any calamity is to immediately turn to Allah with glorification and self-reproach, rather than complaining or blaming others. The Meccans who would later face the punishment of Badr or the conquest of Mecca had the opportunity to make this same confession, but many did not. The verse is a timeless call to repentance: even after the worst of plans, the door of saying “Subḥāna rabbinā innā kunnā ẓālimīn” remains open, and that admission can transform the trial into a path back to the Lord.

=30=

Even in the midst of their confession, human weakness surfaces. After acknowledging their collective wrongdoing, they “turned on each other, throwing blame.

” The Arabic “aqbala baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍin yatalāwamūn” paints a scene of mutual recrimination. They start pointing fingers, each one blaming the others for the idea or for not heeding the sensible one’s advice.

This is a classic reaction of a group that has suffered a self-inflicted disaster: unity dissolves, and everyone tries to shift the responsibility. The phrase indicates that while they had all been complicit, they now sought to distance themselves from the guilt. This behaviour contrasts with true repentance, which requires each individual to bear his own burden and not merely deflect onto others.

However, the mutual blame also shows that they fully recognise the severity of their mistake, even if their initial reaction is flawed. The Qur’an captures human psychology with precision. The Meccans, too, would later blame their leaders on the Day of Judgment, but mutual blame in Hell is no remedy. In the present context, this verse serves as a warning that sin often has a social dimension, and those who conspire together in evil will inevitably turn on each other when the consequences hit. True brotherhood is found in righteousness, not in shared transgression. The believers are taught to avoid the blame game and instead focus on personal accountability. The story will soon show that they moved past the blaming to a more sincere hope in Allah’s mercy, but this verse captures an important step in the psychology of regret. It also demonstrates that the unity of the wicked is fragile and shatters under pressure, whereas the unity of the believers, like the community of the Prophet, only grows stronger under trial, because it is bound by faith, not by worldly interest.

=31=

The mutual blaming gives way to a unified, deeper lament. The exclamation “Yā waylanā”—“Woe to us!”—is an intense expression of grief and self-condemnation. They are mourning their own destruction. The confession “We have certainly been transgressors” (innā kunnā ṭāghīn) uses the word “ṭughyān,” which means to exceed all bounds, to become arrogant and rebellious beyond measure.

This is a stronger word than “ẓālimīn” (wrongdoers). It indicates that they now see the full magnitude of their crime: it was not just a simple act of stinginess but a rebellion against the divine order, a transgression that merited annihilation. Their wealth and their sense of power had led them to “ṭughyān,” exactly the disease that afflicted the Meccan chiefs. The Prophet had been sent to warn them against their ṭughyān, but they had accused him of madness. Here, the garden owners, stripped of everything, can finally see clearly.

This verse shows that calamity, while bitter, can be a powerful medicine that breaks the ego and allows the soul to recognise its true state. For the listener, it prompts a reflection: must we wait for a disaster to realise our own transgressions? The believers are encouraged to constantly engage in self-reflection and to declare their own shortcomings before they are forcibly made to do so.

The use of “ṭāghīn” also ties back to the earlier descriptions of the pagans. The surah is building a parallel: the garden owners’ transgression was in wealth, the Meccans’ transgression is in denial of the message. Both are rooted in the same arrogance of self-sufficiency. The lament “Woe to us” is a cry that will echo from the depths of Hell, but for the garden owners, it came in this world, possibly as a means of purification.

The next verse reveals the fruit of this brokenness: a turn toward hope in their Lord.

=32=

The story concludes with a beautiful transformation. Having recognised their transgression, they do not fall into despair. Instead, they express a sincere hope in Allah’s mercy.

The phrase “ʿasā rabbunā”—“It may be that our Lord”—captures the hopeful uncertainty that characterises the believer’s attitude towards Allah’s mercy. They do not presume entitlement, but they hope. They ask that He replace their destroyed garden with “a better garden than this.

” The word “khayran” means better in quality, in blessing, and in permanence. They have learned that true goodness lies not in the garden itself but in the Garden that is accompanied by Allah’s pleasure.

The phrase “innā ilā rabbinā rāghibūn” means “we are indeed turning to our Lord with hope and desire.” The word “rāghibūn” conveys a strong yearning, a turning away from all else and directing one’s full attention and love towards Allah.

This is the culmination of their trial: from arrogant self-sufficiency, through shock, mutual blame, and confession, to a state of pure raghbah (longing) for the Lord. The story offers a profound illustration of how worldly loss can be the doorway to spiritual gain. For the Meccans, it was a clear message: the Prophet is calling you to give up your false gods and your arrogant attachment to wealth, and to turn with hope (raghbah) to the One Lord. If you do, He will replace your worldly losses with something far better in this life and the next. If you persist, the loss will be total and eternal, as the next verse warns. The believer learns from this that no matter how great the calamity, if it leads to a sincere turning to Allah, the outcome is khayr. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said, “How wonderful is the affair of the believer, for all his affairs are good. If prosperity comes to him, he is grateful, and that is good for him; if adversity befalls him, he is patient, and that is good for him.” The garden owners’ turnaround, if genuine, is a perfect example of adversity leading to good.

=33=

The parable concludes with a direct divine commentary. “That is the punishment” refers to the destruction of the garden and the worldly loss they suffered. The verse explains that this is the pattern of divine chastisement in this life for those who transgress.

However, it then delivers a far more sobering truth: “But the punishment of the Hereafter is certainly far worse. ” The Arabic “la-ʿadhābu al-ākhirati akbar” means the torment of the next life is greater in intensity, duration, and terror. The loss of a garden, no matter how devastating, is temporary. The loss of Paradise and the suffering in Hell are eternal. The verse is a direct warning to the Meccans who were listening. Allah had already shown them small punishments—drought, the defeat of their trade caravans, the deaths of their leaders—but these were mere samples. If they did not repent, the full punishment awaited them in the Hereafter.

The phrase “if only they knew” (law kānū yaʿlamūn) indicates that their lack of true knowledge is the root of their heedlessness. If they truly comprehended the reality of the afterlife, they would not cling to their idolatry and arrogance. For the believers, this verse serves as a powerful deterrent. It reminds them that the trials of this world, painful as they are, are mercy compared to the punishment of the next. It also reinforces the principle that Allah’s justice is not fully served in this life; the ultimate tribunal is the Day of Judgment. The story of the garden owners, then, is a miniature of the greater drama of human life. Some repent after the worldly warning, and some do not. The verse urges the listener to be among those who take the worldly warning to heart and avoid the far worse punishment. It also connects back to the beginning of the surah, where the Prophet was assured of a never-ending reward. The righteous, like him, will have the Gardens of Bliss, while the wicked, if unrepentant, face the endless fire.

=34=

After the warning of the terrible punishment, the surah immediately presents the contrasting reward, following the Quranic pattern of pairing threat with promise. “The righteous” are the muttaqūn, those who have taqwā—a constant awareness of Allah that leads to obedience and avoidance of sin. Their destination is “Gardens of Bliss” (Jannāti an-Naʿīm), gardens filled with every conceivable pleasure and delight, where there is no fear, no fatigue, and no deprivation. The addition of the phrase “with their Lord” (ʿinda rabbihim) elevates the description to its highest level. The ultimate bliss is not just the gardens themselves, but the nearness to the Lord, the honour of being in His presence, and the vision of His noble countenance.

This is the “better garden” that the repentant garden owners hoped for. For the believers in Mecca who had lost their worldly homes, wealth, and families due to persecution, this verse was a divine guarantee of an incomparably superior compensation. It also answers the question that the surah will soon explicitly ask: how can the submissive and the wicked be treated equally? The mere existence of the Gardens of Bliss for the righteous is the evidence of divine justice. The verse uses the emphatic “inna” to affirm the certainty of this promise. For the listener, it is a call to strive for the character of the righteous: those who, unlike the garden owners, share their wealth, say “in shā’ Allāh,” and glorify their Lord.

The mention of “their Lord” personalises the relationship; these righteous ones are those who believe in the unseen and have awe of their Lord, as described in Surah Al-Mulk. This verse is a beacon of hope, pulling the believer’s heart away from the glitter of this world to the eternal Gardens of Bliss that are firmly reserved, by Allah’s promise, for those who endure and remain steadfast.

=35=

The surah now delivers a powerful rhetorical question that appeals to the innate human sense of justice. The word “Muslimīn” refers to those who have submitted themselves wholly to Allah. The “wicked” are the “mujrimīn,” the criminals who persist in disbelief and sin.

The question asks: is it conceivable, is it befitting of Allah’s perfect justice and wisdom, that He would treat these two groups equally? Would He give the obedient the same fate as the rebellious? The obvious answer is no. This rhetorical device is used to demolish the false claims of the pagans who said that even if there were a resurrection, they would be given good things in the Hereafter just as they had been given in this world. Some of them believed that their worldly wealth and status were proof of Allah’s favour, which would continue in the next life. The verse completely refutes this. Allah’s judgment is based on faith and deeds, not on material possessions. It also serves as a profound comfort to the Muslims who were poor and oppressed, like Bilal and Ammar, while their tormentors enjoyed luxury. The verse assures them that the cosmic scales of justice are perfectly balanced; their suffering is not ignored, and the tyrants’ comfort is not rewarded.

This is a central Quranic theme: the moral order of the universe will be fully vindicated on the Day of Judgment. For the modern believer, this verse reinforces the conviction that no matter how much evil seems to flourish unpunished, a day of absolute justice is coming. It also calls each person to examine their own state: am I among the Muslims, those who truly submit, or am I resembling the mujrimīn?

The question “Should We then treat...” is phrased as a divine statement of principle, using the royal “We,” indicating that this is a law decreed by the Almighty. It is illogical and inconsistent with Allah’s attributes of wisdom and justice that the outcome of these two groups would be the same. The subsequent verses will further expose the absurdity of the pagans’ claims to special favour.

=36=

The questioning intensifies, now directly addressing the Meccan pagans with a tone of rebuke and astonishment. “What is the matter with you?” (mā lakum) is an expression of confusion at their irrational stance. How have you reached such a distorted judgment that you equate the believers with the criminals, or that you assume you will be favoured in the Hereafter despite your disbelief?

The phrase “How do you judge?” (kayfa taḥkumūn) challenges the very basis of their reasoning. It points to a flaw in their intellectual and moral compass. They are applying a faulty standard—material wealth—to determine spiritual worth.

This is a classic Quranic critique of the jahili worldview. The verse forces the listener to pause and reassess the criteria by which they evaluate themselves and others. In a broader sense, it is a timeless question posed to every human being: on what basis do you form your judgments about right and wrong, about who is blessed and who is cursed? Is it based on divine revelation and sound reason, or on base desires and superficial appearances? The Meccans judged that the powerful and wealthy were the favoured ones. The Quran says: how utterly flawed is that judgment, because it contradicts the fundamental purpose of life as a test, where worldly blessings can be a means of leading one astray.

For the believer, this verse is a reminder to always align one’s judgment with the Quran and Sunnah, not with societal trends or materialistic measures of success. The rhetorical questions serve to break down the wall of arrogance by exposing the emptiness of their logic.

The following verses will now dismantle their specific claims, revealing that they have no divine warrant for their expectations.

=37=

The surah now begins to deconstruct the pagans’ delusions of entitlement. The question “Or do you have a scripture” is a sarcastic challenge.

The word “kitāb” here means a revealed book, a divine scripture. Allah asks the Meccans: do you possess a heavenly book, sent down from God, in which you study and find your claims validated? The Arabs were largely unlettered and had no scripture of their own, unlike the Jews and Christians. This question exposes the absurdity of their position. They act as if they have a divine guarantee, yet they have never even pretended to have received a revelation that grants them special status.

The emphasis on “in which you read” (tadrusūn) indicates that this hypothetical scripture is not just a dusty relic but one they regularly study and from which they derive their beliefs. This is a pointed critique, as the pagans mocked the recitation of the Quran, yet they themselves had nothing to recite. The verse highlights the Quranic principle that all claims about the unseen and about divine judgement must be backed by authentic revelation, not by conjecture, ancestral customs, or wishful thinking. It establishes the authority of revealed knowledge against the ignorance of unguided human speculation.

For the believer, this verse reinforces the importance of clinging to the Quran as the ultimate criterion. Any belief or practice must have a basis in the wahy. The rhetorical question leaves the pagans speechless, because their only scripture was the poetry and legends they inherited, none of which promised them the outcomes they desired. The series of “or” questions in the following verses will further corner them, forcing them to admit they have no legitimate basis for their arrogance.

=38=

This verse completes the hypothetical scenario introduced in the previous one. If they had such a miraculous scripture, what would be its astonishing content? It would state that “you will have whatever you choose.” This satirically summarises their attitude. They live as if they can simply pick and choose their destiny: they want the pleasures of this life, they want to be resurrected to even more pleasures, and they want to be exempt from accountability. Their desires become their religion.

The phrase “whatever you choose” exposes the self-worship at the heart of their denial. They refuse to submit to Allah’s will because they want their own will to be supreme. The Quran mocks this mentality by framing it as a hypothetical divine decree. If they truly had a book from Allah promising them whatever their hearts desire, then they might have a reason for their confidence. But they have no such book; they have only their empty wishes. The verse is a powerful warning against the sin of making one’s desires into a god, a concept explicitly condemned elsewhere in the Quran: “Have you seen the one who takes his own desire as his god?” (25:43).

For the believer, this is a call to submit every desire to the criterion of revelation. A Muslim does not choose his way of life based on what pleases him, but based on what pleases Allah. The Meccans’ “choose” is the antithesis of “islam,” which means surrender. They choose their gods, choose their ethics, choose their prejudices, and expect Allah to rubber-stamp their choices. The verse declares this hope utterly baseless. The satirical tone is a divine rhetorical weapon, intended to shame the listener into recognising the folly of such an assumption.

The next verse will intensify the satire, leaving no room for their delusions.

=39=

The satire escalates. The question now is: do they have solemn, binding oaths from Allah Himself, guaranteed and sworn by Him, that will remain in force until the Day of Judgment, promising them that their judgments and decisions will be honoured?

The word “aymān” (oaths) is the same word used for the false oaths of the despicable oath-taker earlier in the surah. The irony is sharp: they rely on their own false oaths, but do they have an oath from Allah?

The phrase “binding on Us” (ʿalaynā) is startling; they would have to claim that Allah has obligated Himself to fulfill their personal whims. The clause “until the Day of Judgment” indicates that this supposed covenant would have to be permanent and unbreakable. Such an idea is, of course, preposterous. The pagans never even claimed to have such oaths. The rhetorical questions are designed to show that their hopes are based on nothing but arrogance and wishful thinking. They act as if they have a contract with God, but they are actually in a state of rebellion against Him. This verse is a powerful lesson in the nature of divine sovereignty. Allah is not bound by the decisions of His creatures; He is the Sovereign who decrees as He wills based on His wisdom and justice. Any claim that a person or a group is guaranteed salvation or special treatment independent of their faith and deeds is a lie against Allah. The believers are warned never to assume they have a guaranteed ticket to Paradise simply because of their nominal affiliation; salvation is by Allah’s mercy and through sincere faith and righteous action. The Meccans’ delusion is shared by many across history who think that their religious affiliation, their lineage, or their cultural privilege gives them a pass. This verse demolishes that illusion entirely, leaving them exposed with no contract, no scripture, and no grounds for their assurance.

=40=

The sequence of rhetorical questions culminates in a direct command to the Prophet to challenge them. “Ask them which of them can guarantee all that.

” The word “zaʿīm” means a guarantor, a champion, or a leader who will take responsibility for delivering the promised outcome. The challenge is for them to produce anyone who can stand as a surety for their baseless claims. Who among their idols, their chiefs, or their soothsayers can guarantee that they will be treated like the righteous in the Hereafter? Who can guarantee that their choices will be honoured by Allah?

The answer is none. They have no guarantor, no intercessor, no authority.

This is a public humiliation of their arrogance. The Prophet is told to throw down this challenge, which is part of his prophetic duty to establish the proof against them.

The verse also serves as a transition to the next set of questions about their false gods. They cannot produce a guarantor from among themselves, so they might turn to their associate-gods.

The next verse will address that. For the believers, this verse instills a profound sense of reliance on Allah alone. We have a true Guarantor in Allah, who has guaranteed the preservation of the Quran, the victory of the believers, and the fulfillment of His promises. But the disbelievers have no such guarantee. The challenge also teaches the method of dawah: one should ask probing questions that force the interlocutor to examine the foundations of their own beliefs. The falsehood crumbles under such scrutiny. The verse is the end of this particular argumentative section, and it perfectly sets up the next series of verses, where the surah will now address the associate-gods and then move on to the scenes of the Day of Judgment. The arrogant Meccan is left speechless, his delusions shattered by the weight of simple, logical challenges.
