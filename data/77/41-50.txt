=41=

After the relentless repetition of woe upon the deniers and the horrifying descriptions of Hell, the surah now offers a breathtaking contrast. "Indeed, the righteous will be amid cool shade and springs." The Arabic "inna al-muttaqeena fee dhilalin wa 'uyoon" opens the door to Paradise for the God-fearing.

The word "muttaqeen" refers to those who have taqwa, who lived in constant awareness of Allah, fearing His punishment and seeking His mercy. They are the ones who believed, prayed, gave charity, and avoided what Allah forbade. Their reward is described in terms that are the direct opposite of the deniers' punishment. The deniers were told to proceed into the "shade of smoke" that provided no coolness. The righteous, in contrast, are in "dhilal," pleasant, cool, refreshing shades. This shade is not made of choking smoke but of lush trees, canopies, and eternal comfort. It is the shade of the Gardens, where the sun's heat does not penetrate, and the climate is perfect. The "uyoon" are springs—flowing sources of pure, delightful water. While the deniers were given the boiling, putrid fluids of Hell, the righteous are given crystalline springs that gush forth at their will.

The pairing of shade and water is the essence of refreshment, especially for a desert-dwelling people. It signifies peace, satisfaction, and the quenching of every thirst. The verse uses the word "fee" (amid) to show they are enveloped, surrounded by these blessings. They are not just looking at the shade and springs from a distance; they are immersed in them.

This is the immediate, tangible reality of their reward. The transition from the previous verses is stark and intentional. The same surah that hammered the deniers with "woe" now gently cradles the believers with "indeed." It is a literary and spiritual shift from terror to tranquility, demonstrating the dual nature of the divine message: a warning and a glad tiding. The believer hears this verse and feels his heart settle into hope after being shaken by fear. The taqwa that seemed burdensome in the world has blossomed into eternal comfort. The surah will now elaborate on the fruits that accompany these springs, completing the picture of their hospitality.

=42=

The blessings of Paradise continue to be described in terms of complete fulfillment. "And any fruit they desire." The Arabic "wa fawakiha mimma yashtahoon" means they will have fruits, all kinds of fruits, whatever they crave.

The word "yashtahoon" comes from "shahwa," meaning desire, appetite, longing. In Paradise, every desire for any fruit is immediately satisfied. There is no season, no scarcity, no waiting. The fruit is always ripe, always perfect, and always within reach. The verse highlights the personalized nature of the reward. Each believer will have exactly the fruits he or she loves most.

This is a profound mercy. In the world, even the richest person might crave a fruit that is out of season or unavailable. In Paradise, the mere thought of a fruit brings it into existence.

The verse also implies a removal of all dietary restrictions. The believers can eat whatever they desire without any harmful consequences, no illness, no fullness, only pure pleasure.

The mention of fruit, rather than other foods, is significant because fruit is a symbol of pure, natural delight, a gift that combines beauty, fragrance, and taste. It is the food of leisure and luxury. The deniers in Hell are given choking food and boiling water; the righteous are given fruits of their choice. The contrast is absolute.

The verse also serves as a motivation for the believer to control his desires in this world. The one who restrains his "shahawat" from the forbidden for the sake of Allah will find his lawful desires fulfilled beyond imagination in the Hereafter.

The word "yashtahoon" indicates that the appetite itself is a source of pleasure, and its immediate satisfaction is a double pleasure. The believer is not a passive recipient; his desires actively shape his experience.

The surah now adds the welcoming words that accompany these gifts, words that bring the deepest joy to the soul.

=43=

The divine hospitality reaches its emotional peak with the words that the believers will hear in Paradise. "They will be told, 'Eat and drink happily for what you used to do.'" The Arabic "kulu washrabu hanee'an bima kuntum ta'maloon" is an invitation and a commendation. The command "kulu washrabu" (eat and drink) is the warm welcome of a generous host.

The word "hanee'an" means wholesomely, with complete pleasure, without any fear of harm, without any consequence. It is a state of pure, unadulterated enjoyment. But the most touching part is the reason given: "bima kuntum ta'maloon" (for what you used to do). The believers are not receiving this as a random handout; it is the direct result of their own deeds in the world. Every prayer they prayed, every fast they endured, every charity they gave, every temptation they resisted, every tear they shed in repentance—all of it has been transformed into this food and drink, this shade and these springs.

The phrase "what you used to do" is a beautiful acknowledgment of their striving. It tells them that their efforts were not in vain, that every small act of obedience was recorded and is now being repaid with exquisite interest.

The word "hanee'an" also implies that the food and drink are not just physically pleasing but are accompanied by a profound spiritual satisfaction. They eat and drink with the full approval of their Lord, with no guilt, no anxiety, no fear that the pleasure will end. This verse echoes the similar commendation in Al-Insan: "Your striving has been appreciated. " The believers are honoured by having their own deeds recognized.

The verse teaches the fundamental principle of the Hereafter: it is a world of reaping what you sowed. The deniers are told to "taste" their punishment for what they used to do; the righteous are told to "eat and drink" for what they used to do. The same law of cause and effect governs both outcomes.

For the believer, this verse is a profound source of motivation. It transforms the daily acts of worship from dry obligations into seeds of future bliss. Every time a believer restrains himself from a halal pleasure out of fear of Allah, or engages in an act of obedience, he is storing up a "hanee'an" for the Day when he will hear these words.

The surah now states the general principle behind this reward, linking back to the recurring refrain.

=44=

The surah articulates the divine principle that governs the reward of the righteous: "Surely this is how We reward the good-doers." The Arabic "inna kadhalika najzee al-muhsineen" is a statement of consistent divine practice. "Kadhalika" (thus, in this manner) refers to the lavish rewards just described—the shade, springs, fruits, and the honoured invitation to eat and drink.

This is not a special favor reserved for a few; it is the standard treatment that Allah accords to "al-muhsineen." The word "muhsineen" comes from "ihsan," which is the highest level of faith: to worship Allah as if you see Him. The muhsin is the one who excels in his deeds, performing them with excellence, sincerity, and awareness of Allah's presence. He goes beyond the minimum obligations into the realm of loving devotion. The verse uses "muhsineen" instead of "muslimeen" or "mu'mineen" to highlight that this level of reward is for those who perfected their faith and adorned it with beautiful deeds.

However, by Allah's mercy, even those with lesser degrees of faith will eventually enter Paradise and receive their share of rewards, but the highest degrees of the described bliss are particularly for the muhsineen. The statement "this is how We reward" establishes a law. It tells the believers that Allah's generosity is not arbitrary; it is His way. They can trust in this promise. It also encourages them to strive for ihsan. The verse provides a moment of reflection after the emotional high of the divine invitation. It generalizes the specific, making the promise available to all who choose the path of excellence.

The surah then brings back the refrain, contrasting the fate of the good-doers with the deniers, keeping the two groups in sharp opposition throughout the chapter. The refrain acts as a constant reminder that the beautiful rewards are not for everyone; they are for those who believed and did good, while the deniers are excluded.

=45=

The refrain returns for the eighth time, this time immediately after the description of the reward of the good-doers. "But woe on that Day to the deniers!" The conjunction "but" is implied in the Arabic "waylun yawma'idhin lil-mukadhdhibeen," standing in stark contrast to the preceding verse. Allah rewards the good-doers in this generous manner, but the deniers are met with woe.

The placement of the refrain here is a masterstroke. The reader or listener has just been bathing in the cool shade and springs of Paradise, tasting the delicious fruits and hearing the welcoming voice. Then, suddenly, the "wayl" crashes down like a hammer, reminding that this bliss is not universal. The deniers are excluded. The contrast heightens both the desirability of Paradise and the dreadfulness of Hell. The deniers are those who belied the "Day of Decision," who rejected the prophets, who mocked the warnings. They refused to be among the good-doers, so they cannot share in the reward. The woe is their portion.

The verse also serves as a check on spiritual complacency. A believer might hear the descriptions of Paradise and feel a surge of hope, which is good, but the "woe" immediately reminds him that entry is conditional upon faith and deeds. It is a balanced spiritual psychology. The refrain continues to function as the drumbeat of the surah, marking the rhythm of justice.

The surah now shifts back to the deniers, delivering a final, chilling admonition in the form of a sarcastic, temporary permission.

=46=

The surah now addresses the deniers directly with a statement dripping with irony and warning. "Eat and enjoy yourselves for a little while, for you are truly wicked." The Arabic "kulu wa tamatta'u qaleelan innakum mujrimoon" mirrors the words spoken to the righteous in Paradise, but with a terrifying difference. The righteous were told "Eat and drink happily" as a reward for their deeds. The wicked are told "Eat and enjoy yourselves" as a temporary respite before the punishment.

The word "qaleelan" (for a little while) is the key. Their enjoyment in this world is brief, fleeting, and will soon be cut off by death. Then comes the condemnation: "innakum mujrimoon" (indeed, you are criminals).

The word "mujrimoon" is stronger than mere disbelievers; it labels them as guilty criminals, those who have committed the heinous crime of denying their Creator. This verse is a profound example of the Quranic technique of "istidraj," the divine plan of giving the wicked respite and worldly pleasures so that they increase in sin and become completely deserving of the punishment. The command "eat and enjoy yourselves" is not a genuine permission; it is a statement of the reality of their current state, coupled with a threat. It is as if Allah is saying, "Go ahead, enjoy your few days in the world. Eat your fill, laugh, and be merry. But know that you are criminals, and your time is short." The verse serves as a wake-up call. The deniers are so absorbed in their worldly pleasures that they have forgotten their criminal state. The verse shatters their complacency. The "enjoyment" is a bait that leads to the trap.

The surah now follows this with the inevitable refrain, as if to say: this brief enjoyment is the prelude to the eternal woe.

=47=

The refrain follows the ironic permission, marking the ninth occurrence: "Woe on that Day to the deniers!" The "wayl" is the reality behind the fleeting enjoyment. The deniers are told to eat and enjoy for a little while, but the end is woe. The verse creates a powerful juxtaposition: present pleasure versus future pain. The deniers, who choose the brief enjoyment of the world over the eternal bliss of Paradise, are making a disastrous trade. The woe is the price they will pay for their choice.

The repetition of the refrain here emphasizes that the worldly respite is not a sign of Allah's approval but a prelude to His wrath. The deniers might misinterpret their worldly success as evidence that they are on the right path, as the Quraysh did with their wealth and trade. The Quran corrects this misunderstanding. The "wayl" is what truly awaits them, and it is near. The surah has now used the refrain nine times, building a crescendo that will reach its climax in the final verses. The listener is conditioned to expect the refrain after each block, and each time it lands with increased weight.

The surah now gives one final reason for the woe: their refusal to bow to Allah.

=48=

The surah identifies a specific, concrete act of disobedience that encapsulates the deniers' entire attitude. "When it is said to them, 'Bow down before Allah,' they do not bow." The Arabic "wa idha qeela lahumu irka'u la yarka'oon" refers to the command to perform ruku' (bowing) in prayer, which represents the entire act of salah and, by extension, submission to Allah. The deniers are called to worship, to humble themselves physically before the Creator, but they refuse. This verse could refer to the Quraysh's refusal to pray when the Prophet called them to Islam. Many of them said they would not bow down to a God they could not see. Some were too proud to put their foreheads on the ground. Their refusal to bow is a symbol of their arrogance and their rejection of servitude.

The verse also has an eschatological dimension. On the Day of Judgment, when the great horror is revealed and Allah commands the people to prostrate, the believers will fall down in prostration, while the backs of the hypocrites and disbelievers will become stiff like boards, unable to bow.

This is mentioned in Surah Al-Qalam. The refusal to bow is a damning indictment. It shows that their problem is not intellectual confusion; it is arrogance and disobedience. The simple act of bending the back is a line they refuse to cross because it would mean admitting they are not gods, they are not independent, they are servants of a Higher Power. This verse highlights the centrality of prayer in Islam. The refusal to pray is a defining characteristic of the deniers, as seen in Al-Muddaththir and Al-Qiyamah. The verse is a powerful call to the believer to cherish and guard the prayer, to never be among those who, when called to bow, refuse.

The surah now pronounces the final refrain of woe, the tenth and last in the surah, as the ultimate verdict on this refusal.

=49=

The refrain appears for the tenth and final time: "Woe on that Day to the deniers!" This is the last nail in the coffin. After exposing their specific sin—the refusal to bow—the woe is sealed. The number ten in Arabic rhetoric often signifies completeness and finality. The tenfold "wayl" is a complete, all-encompassing curse upon the deniers. The refrain has been the backbone of the surah, and its final occurrence here leaves the listener with a profound sense of the certainty and severity of the judgment. The deniers are defined by their denial, and their punishment is defined by woe.

The repetition has hammered this association so deeply that the very word "deniers" now echoes with the sound of "wayl. " The verse is a final warning. The door of repentance is still open in the world, but once the Day comes, the woe is irreversible. The surah has used the refrain to create a powerful emotional and spiritual impact, moving the listener through cycles of evidence, warning, and judgment. Now, with the tenth woe pronounced, the case against the deniers is complete.

The surah ends with a verse that shifts from warning to a profound rhetorical question, leaving the listener in a state of urgent reflection. The question challenges the very foundation of the deniers' rejection and opens the door for the next revelation, the next opportunity to believe.

=50=

The surah concludes with a penetrating, unforgettable question: "So what message after this Quran would they believe in?" The Arabic "fa-bi-ayyi hadeethin ba'dahu yu'minoon" is the final challenge to the deniers. The particle "fa" (so) links the question to everything that has preceded: the oaths, the evidence from history, the signs in creation, the descriptions of the Day, the horrors of Hell, the bliss of Paradise, and the tenfold woe. After all of this, if they still do not believe in this "hadeeth" (message, discourse, revelation), then what else will they believe in?

The word "hadeeth" here refers to the Quran, the greatest, most truthful, most eloquent, most convincing message ever delivered. If the Quran does not move them, no other message will. If they reject the word of Allah, what word of who will they accept?

The question exposes the irrationality of their position. It is not that the Quran lacks evidence; it is that they have shut their hearts.

The question also carries an implicit prophecy: they will not believe in any other message because the Quran is the pinnacle of divine revelation. There is no "hadeeth" after it that can guide if it does not.

The question is both a lament and a condemnation. The surah that began with powerful oaths by the forces of nature and angels delivering the reminder ends with the declaration that this reminder is the final, ultimate word. No further arguments, no additional signs, no new prophets will come. If they reject this, they are hopeless. The verse is a call to urgency. The listener who has not yet believed is asked to look at the Quran, to recognize its truth, and to seize the opportunity before the Day when no speech will be permitted. The surah's ending leaves the matter hanging in the air, a challenge for every human being until the Day of Judgment. It is a fitting conclusion to a surah that has relentlessly pursued the theme of denial and the certainty of the Day of Decision.
