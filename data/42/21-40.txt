=21=

After explaining the law of intention and harvest in the previous verse, the surah now refutes a core claim of the polytheists. “Or do they have associate-gods who have ordained for them some polytheistic beliefs, which Allah has not authorized?” This rhetorical question exposes the utter lack of authority behind their idolatrous practices. The Quraysh followed religious customs that were not revealed by Allah; they had no scripture, no prophet among them at that time, and their so-called gods had no power to legislate. They simply inherited these beliefs from their forefathers. So the question mocks the very idea that their associate-gods could prescribe a religion. The notion of divine authorization is central: only Allah has the right to ordain what is lawful and unlawful in worship and conduct. When people invent beliefs and claim they are part of religion, they are usurping a role that belongs to Allah alone.

The verse then repeats a phrase similar to one earlier: “Had it not been for a prior decree on Judgment, the matter would have certainly been settled between them at once.” The “prior decree” refers to Allah’s wisdom in setting a fixed term for the world and delaying the final judgment until the Day of Resurrection. If not for that decree, He could have punished these fabricators immediately, removing them from the earth. But His forbearance gives them time to reflect and repent.

However, the verse ends with a severe warning: “And surely the wrongdoers will suffer a painful punishment.” The delay should not be mistaken for impunity. The punishment is reserved and will be inflicted in full measure when the appointed time arrives. This verse strengthens the heart of the believer by showing that the falsehood of the idolaters has no divine backing whatsoever, and it also serves as a caution against introducing unauthorized innovations into faith.

=22=

The verse now gives a glimpse of the Day of Judgment, contrasting the states of the wrongdoers and the believers. “You will see the wrongdoers fearful of the punishment for what they committed” is an address to the Prophet, and through him to every believer, as if witnessing the scene directly. The fear the wrongdoers experience is not a passing anxiety but an overwhelming terror caused by the certainty of the punishment they had denied. Their own deeds will confront them, and they will realize that no escape is possible.

The phrase “but it will be inevitable for them” indicates that the punishment cannot be averted; it is the necessary consequence of their persistent wrongdoing. The contrast is immediate and sharp: “whereas those who believe and do good will be in the lush Gardens of Paradise.

” The word “lush” conveys the vibrant, green, and refreshing nature of Paradise, a place of perfect comfort and beauty. They will not merely be admitted but will be settled there, residents of an eternal home. The description “they will have whatever they desire from their Lord” shows that the blessings of Paradise are not limited to a fixed list; rather, the inhabitants’ very wishes will be fulfilled. The ultimate source of these blessings is “their Lord,” emphasizing the personal relationship and generosity of Allah.

The verse concludes, “That is truly the greatest bounty.” The greatest bounty is not just the physical pleasures but the comprehensive honor, security, and nearness to Allah that Paradise represents. This verse balances the warning with a powerful incentive, encouraging believers to remain steadfast by visualizing the immense gap between the two outcomes.

=23=

The reward of Paradise described in the previous verse is now called “the good news which Allah gives to His servants who believe and do good.” This is the glad tiding that the believers carry in their hearts, a source of strength in the face of hardship. Then the Prophet is instructed to address his people with a disarming statement: “I do not ask you Meccans for a reward for this message.” He is not a hired preacher seeking wealth or status; his call is purely for Allah’s sake. The exception that follows, “only honour for our kinship,” has been understood by many scholars in the context of the Prophet’s family ties with the Quraysh. He is basically saying, “If you do not want to believe for Allah’s sake, then at least maintain the bond of kinship between us and do not harm me.

” This is a plea rooted in Arab culture where blood relations were highly respected. It removed any pretext that the Prophet was driven by greed; his only worldly request was for the basic decency of kinship.

The verse then shifts to a general principle: “Whoever earns a good deed, We will increase it in goodness for them.” The divine system multiplies rewards far beyond the original deed, showing Allah’s immense generosity.

The verse ends with two beautiful names: “Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Appreciative.” He forgives the slips and shortcomings of His servants, and He is “Shakur”—Most Appreciative—meaning He recognizes and richly rewards even the smallest acts of obedience. This name is particularly comforting; it means Allah does not overlook a single sincere effort, and He expresses His appreciation through multiplied reward. So the verse teaches the Prophet to carry his message with purity of intention, and it assures the believers that their good deeds are seen, valued, and magnified.

=24=

Here Allah confronts another accusation hurled by the disbelievers. They claimed that the Prophet had invented the Quran and falsely attributed it to Allah. The verse poses the question: “Or do they say, ‘He has fabricated a lie about Allah!’?” This was a painful charge against a man known for his truthfulness, but the Quran exposes its absurdity. The response is layered: “If you had, Allah would have sealed your heart, if He willed.

” This is a conditional statement that nullifies their claim. It means that if the Prophet had dared to forge a lie against Allah, Allah would have certainly intervened and sealed his heart, eliminating his ability to continue. The fact that the Prophet’s heart remained open, that the revelation continued for over two decades, and that his sincerity never wavered is itself evidence that he was not a fabricator.

The verse then asserts a universal law: “And Allah wipes out falsehood and establishes the truth by His Words.” Falsehood, no matter how formidable it appears, is inherently weak and temporary. Allah’s Words, meaning His revelation and His decrees, gradually erase falsehood and firmly plant the truth in its place. This was a promise to the Prophet that the lies and propaganda against him would ultimately vanish, while the Quran would remain as a lasting truth.

The verse concludes, “He certainly knows best what is hidden in the heart.” Allah is fully aware of the Prophet’s pure intentions, and He also knows the envy and concealed animosity within the hearts of the accusers. This knowledge is the ultimate basis for trust; the believers can rest assured that the All-Knowing will deal justly with both the sincere and the insincere.

=25=

Following the mention of the hidden contents of hearts, the verse immediately opens the door of mercy. “He is the One Who accepts repentance from His servants.

” The word “repentance” here translates “tawbah,” which means turning back to Allah in regret, abandoning the sin, and resolving not to return to it. Allah’s acceptance of this repentance is an act of pure grace, because it is He who inspires the servant to repent in the first place, and then He graciously accepts it.

The phrase “and pardons their sins” emphasizes that He does not merely accept the repentance but actively wipes away the sin, sometimes even transforming it into a good deed. This verse serves as a powerful invitation to the very people who accused the Prophet of fabrication. Even those who spread such a monstrous lie are not beyond hope; if they turn sincerely to Allah, He is ready to accept their repentance and forgive them.

The verse adds, “And He knows whatever you do. ” This is both a comfort and a caution. It comforts the penitent because they know that their hidden remorse and genuine change of heart are fully known to Allah, even if people are unaware. It cautions the unrepentant that their secret deeds and unspoken intentions are also completely exposed to Him. This verse flows naturally from the previous one: while Allah knows the hidden accusations, He is also the most ready to forgive those who abandon falsehood and turn to the truth. No sin is too great for His mercy as long as the servant’s heart sincerely returns.

=26=

Continuing the theme of divine responsiveness, this verse highlights a special relationship. “He responds to those who believe and do good” means that Allah answers their prayers, removes their difficulties, and grants them from His bounty. The condition is “believe and do good,” combining faith with righteous action. Their response from Allah is not merely in the form of material gifts but also spiritual guidance, tranquility, and help in times of need.

The phrase “and increases their reward out of His grace” is particularly beautiful. Even after they have performed their good deeds, Allah adds more reward as a pure gift, far beyond the calculated equivalent.

This is the “increase” that was mentioned in verse 20 regarding the harvest of the Hereafter. Then the verse presents the opposite: “As for the disbelievers, they will suffer a severe punishment. ” This contrast is striking. While the believers experience continuous divine generosity, the disbelievers are moving towards an unrelenting punishment. The severity of the punishment is directly tied to their rejection of truth and their failure to turn to the One who is ever-ready to respond and forgive. This verse is a microcosm of the Quranic method of pairing encouragement with warning. It reminds believers to maintain their faith and good works, knowing that their Lord is listening and will increase them beyond measure, while it jolts the heedless into realizing that ignoring this responsive Lord leads to a severe loss.

=27=

This verse answers a common human question: why doesn’t Allah grant immense wealth and ease to everyone, especially the righteous? The answer reveals profound divine wisdom. “Had Allah given abundant provisions to all His servants, they would have certainly transgressed throughout the land.” The nature of many human beings is such that excessive wealth and unrestricted prosperity often lead to arrogance, corruption, and oppression. History and observation confirm that those who are given unchecked abundance frequently rebel against their Lord and exploit others.

Thus, Allah’s decision to apportion provision selectively is actually a mercy to humanity, preventing the world from becoming filled with even greater injustice. The phrase “But He sends down whatever He wills in perfect measure” indicates that provision is distributed according to an exact, wise measurement. Some are given wealth as a test, some are restricted as a test, and every circumstance is perfectly calibrated for the spiritual good of the individual and the collective.

The verse concludes, “He is truly All-Aware, All-Seeing of His servants.” Because He knows the inner states, the capacities, and the potential reactions of each soul, His allocation is never random or unfair. He sees who will be grateful and who will be driven to transgression.

For the believer experiencing financial hardship, this verse brings deep contentment: the limitation is not a sign of neglect but of divine protection, keeping them from a test of abundance they might not be able to bear. It also fosters empathy for the poor and curbs the arrogance of the rich, reminding everyone that the distribution is in the hands of the All-Aware.

=28=

Following the discussion of provision, this verse turns to the most vivid symbol of divine mercy: rain. “He is the One Who sends down rain after people have given up hope.” The scenario is universally relatable: a prolonged drought pushes people to the brink of despair; they look at the sky and see no sign of relief, and their hearts become barren like the land. At that very moment of hopelessness, Allah sends down the life-giving water. This demonstrates that His mercy often arrives when all apparent means have failed, so that people recognize the source. The rain is described as “spreading out His mercy,” because the water revives the dead earth, brings forth crops, quenches thirst, and sustains life. This physical mercy is a tangible sign of spiritual mercy; just as Allah revives the barren land, He revives hearts that have become hardened and distant.

The verse ends with two majestic names: “He is the Guardian, the Praiseworthy. ” As the Guardian, He protects, provides, and cares for His creation continuously. The rain is just one act of His ongoing guardianship. As the Praiseworthy, He is inherently deserving of all praise, whether people acknowledge it or not.

The sequence in this passage is remarkable: the restriction of provision is followed by the sudden descent of rain, teaching believers that while Allah may withhold some things for a time, His ultimate mercy will manifest in the most perfect moment, and He is worthy of praise in every condition.

=29=

This verse expands the lens from rain to the entire cosmos. “And among His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth” calls humanity to reflect on the very existence of the universe. The sheer scale, order, and intricacy of the heavens and the earth are signs pointing to an all-powerful Creator.

The verse then adds a fascinating detail: “and all living beings He dispersed throughout both. ” While living beings on earth are clearly visible, the mention of living beings in the heavens has been understood by many commentators as referring to the angels and possibly other forms of creation unknown to us, scattered through the vast celestial realms. This opens the mind to the immensity of Allah’s creative power; life is not a random earthly phenomenon but a deliberate act of divine dispersal.

The verse then shifts from the origin to the end: “And He is Most Capable of bringing all together whenever He wills.” This refers to the gathering on the Day of Judgment. The One who originally dispersed living beings across the cosmos can just as easily reassemble them for accountability. The logic is irrefutable: if He created them from nothing and scattered them, bringing them together again is a simple matter for His power. For the skeptic who finds the resurrection improbable, this verse provides a compelling argument rooted in the observable signs of creation. It connects the beginning, the middle, and the end of existence into a seamless narrative of divine power and wisdom.

=30=

Here the focus turns to the hardships and calamities that strike human beings. “Whatever affliction befalls you is because of what your own hands have committed” establishes a direct link between many of the troubles people face and their own sins.

This is a general principle: the disobedience and transgressions of human beings bring about negative consequences in their lives. However, this is not an absolute rule for every individual case; it refers to the broad pattern of divine justice in the world. The verse does not say that every single illness or loss is a direct punishment for a specific sin, as that would contradict the possibility of trials that elevate the ranks of the righteous. Rather, it points to the human tendency to bring ruin upon itself through collective and individual wrongdoing. Immediately after this sobering statement, the verse softens the blow with profound mercy: “And He pardons much.” If Allah were to hold people accountable for every sin with immediate affliction, no one would be left on the face of the earth. But He overlooks a great deal of human error out of His forbearance and mercy. This dual message cultivates a balanced state in the believer’s heart: when afflicted, one reflects on their own deeds and turns to repentance, rather than blaming fate or others. At the same time, one does not fall into despair, knowing that Allah’s pardon far exceeds His immediate chastisement. It is a delicate balance between introspection and hope.

=31=

Following the reminder that affliction is often the result of human actions, this verse clarifies the limits of human power. “You can never escape Him on earth” means that no matter where a person goes, they remain under Allah’s authority and grasp. There is no place of refuge, no hidden corner where divine knowledge and power cannot reach. Even the most powerful tyrants cannot flee from Allah’s decree. The idea of escaping here is tied to attempts to avoid the consequences of one’s sins. Some people might think that by denying accountability or by running away, they can evade divine justice. The verse shatters this illusion. Then it adds, “nor do you have any protector or helper besides Allah. ” When affliction strikes or when judgment arrives, there is absolutely no ally, intercessor, or savior who can shield a person from Allah’s will without His permission. This echoes the earlier verses that condemned taking protectors besides Him. The only true protector is Allah, and if He intends to hold someone to account, none can help.

However, the verse also contains a hidden comfort for the believers: since they have no protector besides Allah, and they have taken Allah as their Protector, their protection is secured by the Most Powerful. For the wrongdoers, it is a frightening loss; for the believers, it is a reaffirmation that their reliance on Allah is placed in the only source of real help. This verse thus acts as a final warning and a call to renew one’s trust in the Almighty alone.

=32=

After the intense warning, the surah returns to signs in the natural world that inspire reflection. “And among His signs are the ships like mountains sailing in the sea.

” The image is of massive vessels, large enough to appear like floating mountains, gliding across the vast ocean. In the time of the Prophet, sea travel was perilous, and only Allah’s power allowed heavy, laden ships to stay afloat and move without sinking. The ship is a sign because it combines many elements: the wood that was once a tree on land, the craftsmanship of humans, the wind that propels it, and the water that carries it. All these elements are under Allah’s command.

The mention of “like mountains” emphasizes their size and solidity; it is astonishing that something so heavy can be held up by something as fluid as water. This is a subtle pointer to the reality that the laws of nature are not autonomous; they are sustained moment by moment by the Creator. If He so willed, the water could refuse to bear the weight. The ship is also a symbol of human journey through life. We are all passengers on the ship of existence, and our safe arrival depends entirely on the Captain of the universe. Recognizing these signs deepens one’s faith in Allah’s control and fosters gratitude. The verse prepares the ground for the following verses, which describe what Allah can do with the ships—either calming the wind or causing a wreck—to illustrate human dependence.

=33=

The previous verse mentioned the ships as signs; this verse now explains the control Allah exercises over their movement. “If He wills, He can calm the wind, leaving the ships motionless on the water.” Ships rely on wind for their sails, and in an instant, the wind can cease completely. The vessel, which moments before was cutting through the waves with strength, becomes stranded in the middle of the ocean, utterly still. No human effort can generate the wind needed to move it. This total suspension of power is a direct demonstration of Allah’s authority over all means.

The verse then comments, “Surely in this are signs for whoever is steadfast, grateful.” The two qualities—steadfastness and gratitude—are the keys to interpreting the signs correctly. A person who is steadfast in reflecting on Allah’s signs and patient in the face of life’s stillness or storms will see profound lessons. The grateful person recognizes that the smooth sailing, the favorable wind, and the safe journey are all gifts from the Most High. When the wind stops, the steadfast soul does not panic but understands that the One who stopped the wind can just as easily send it again. This verse contains a powerful spiritual parallel: human life sometimes feels like a stranded ship—everything is still, progress halts, and one feels stuck. The sign teaches that these times are under Allah’s will, and they require patient perseverance and gratitude for past movement, with firm hope in the next gust of divine mercy.

=34=

This verse presents the other possibility: not just stillness but destruction. “Or He can wreck the ships for what the people have committed.

” The phrase indicates that Allah may destroy the vessels as a consequence of the sins of those on board. The wrecking is not arbitrary but tied to the principle mentioned earlier, that affliction often results from human wrongdoing. A shipwreck is a catastrophic event in which all human strength becomes meaningless, and this serves as a powerful reminder of divine justice.

Yet immediately, the verse inserts a merciful qualification: “though He forgives much.” This interjection is crucial. It emphasizes that despite the sins being committed continuously by humanity, Allah does not send destruction upon every sinner in every moment. He overlooks and pardons a great deal. Most ships sail and return safely, not because the people on them are sinless, but because Allah’s attribute of forgiveness is dominant. The rhythm of this passage is remarkable: after showing the possibility of being stranded, then of being wrecked, the mercy of Allah is highlighted so that the heart does not sink into terror. Instead, it oscillates between fear of accountability and hope in forgiveness. This verse, along with the next, also serves as a response to the Quraysh who traveled by sea for trade. Their safe journeys were not due to their idols or their own navigational skill, but solely due to Allah’s forbearance. The verse invites them to reflect and turn back before that forbearance is lifted.

=35=

The verse explains the ultimate purpose behind the display of power over the ships. “So those who dispute about Our signs may know that they have no refuge.” When people witness or hear about ships being wrecked by divine will, it becomes glaringly obvious that when Allah’s decree descends, there is absolutely no escape. The disputers are those who argue against the truth of the Quran, who deny the resurrection, or who mock the warnings. Their intellectual debates and objections crumble in the face of a single natural disaster. The sea, which they considered a predictable environment governed by natural laws, suddenly turns into an instrument of divine punishment. At that moment, they realize that all their imagined protectors, their wealth, and their arguments cannot offer them a safe haven.

The phrase “no refuge” resonates deeply; a refuge is a safe place to which one flees. The verse declares that such a place does not exist outside of Allah’s mercy. This serves as the culmination of the sea imagery: the ship is a microcosm of human life. Just as a ship at sea has no escape from the surrounding water and the sky, the human being in this world has no escape from Allah’s jurisdiction. The only true refuge is to seek Allah’s protection through faith and righteous action before the moment of crisis arrives. The lesson is clear: cease disputing and instead submit to the One who holds absolute sway over the elements.

=36=

The surah now transitions to a direct evaluation of worldly life. “Whatever pleasure you have been given is no more than a fleeting enjoyment of this worldly life.

” The word “pleasure” encompasses all the material comforts, luxuries, and delights that people strive for. The verse does not deny their existence or even their permissibility, but it defines their true nature: they are “fleeting. ” They are temporary, passing, and ultimately insignificant when compared to eternity.

This is the Quranic perspective that prevents the heart from becoming overly attached to possessions or status. The contrast is immediate: “But what is with Allah is far better and more lasting.” What is with Allah refers to the rewards of Paradise, His pleasure, and the spiritual contentment that comes with faith. “Far better” in quality and purity, and “more lasting” because it is eternal and untainted by any fear of loss. The condition for obtaining this is “for those who believe and put their trust in their Lord.” Faith and trust are the two wings that lift the believer beyond the grip of worldly desire. Trust in Allah means relying on His wisdom and provision, so that the heart is not constantly anxious about worldly gain. This verse serves as a powerful remedy for envy and materialism. When a believer sees others enjoying more worldly pleasures, they recall that these are merely fleeting shadows, and they turn their focus to acquiring what is far better and more lasting with their Lord.

=37=

The description of those for whom the lasting reward is prepared continues. “Who avoid major sins and shameful deeds” identifies a core quality of the believers: they stay away from the great destructive sins, such as associating partners with Allah, murder, and usury, as well as open immoralities. This avoidance is not passive but a conscious decision to steer clear of what displeases Allah.

The word “shameful deeds” includes acts whose ugliness is self-evident, such as adultery or indecency. Then the verse mentions a quality that relates to interpersonal conduct: “and forgive when angered.

” This is a remarkable trait because anger is a powerful emotion that demands retaliation. However, the true believer, despite feeling the surge of anger, chooses to swallow it and forgive. This does not mean they become doormats; it means they do not let anger control their response or lead them to injustice. Forgiving at the moment of anger requires immense self-restraint and a strong connection with Allah. It is in that heated moment that the sincerity of one’s character is truly tested. The verse links the avoidance of major sins with forgiveness because both require moral courage. A person who can control their passions—whether the lust for forbidden things or the impulse for revenge—is truly prepared for the lasting reward. This quality was embodied by the Prophet, who, even when he had the power to take revenge on his former persecutors after the conquest of Mecca, chose to forgive them, saying, “No blame will there be upon you today.” The verse thus paints a portrait of a believer who is pure in private life and magnanimous in social life.

=38=

The chain of virtues continues, now highlighting the active, positive dimensions of faith. “Who respond to their Lord” means they hear the call to faith and obedience, and they answer it promptly, without hesitation. This response is a comprehensive submission to divine commands. “Establish prayer” is the most visible manifestation of that response. Prayer is the daily renewal of the bond with Allah, and its establishment implies performing it regularly, with proper concentration and humility. Then the verse mentions a quality that gives this surah its name: “conduct their affairs by mutual consultation.” The believers do not make decisions unilaterally or despotically; they consult with one another in matters of common concern.

This principle, known as shura, is a fundamental feature of Islamic social and political life. It promotes collective wisdom, prevents tyranny, and increases buy-in and unity. The matters for consultation typically include community affairs, governance, and any issue where multiple perspectives add value.

The verse then adds, “and donate from what We have provided for them.” This is the financial dimension of worship. The believers recognize that their wealth is ultimately from Allah, so they spend a portion of it in charity, supporting the needy and the causes of the faith.

The sequence is beautiful: they respond to Allah, they connect with Him through prayer, they organize their collective life through consultation, and they serve the community through spending. These four traits together form a balanced and complete picture of a mature believer, one who is spiritually connected, socially responsible, and generous.

=39=

This verse adds a final trait that balances the forgiveness mentioned earlier. “And who enforce justice when wronged.” The believers are not passive in the face of oppression or aggression. They have the right to defend themselves and to seek justice. This shows that Islam does not demand unconditional forgiveness in every situation; there are times when justice must be upheld and the wrongdoer must face consequences. The verse does not promote revenge out of personal anger, but rather the enforcement of justice within divinely set limits. Forgiveness was praised earlier, but that was primarily in personal disputes where forgiving might lead to a greater good.

However, when a wrong involves the rights of others, or when forgiveness would lead to further injustice and transgression, the believer stands firm for justice. This balanced approach prevents the community from descending into cycles of unchecked oppression. The believer responds to being wronged not with chaotic rage, but with a measured, lawful enforcement of rights. The wording also implies that this enforcement is a collective responsibility; the believers, as a community, support one another in ensuring justice. This trait, together with the previous ones, completes the profile of a righteous believer: they are pure, forgiving in personal matters, responsive to Allah, established in prayer, consultative, generous, and firm in justice.

This is the comprehensive character that earns the lasting reward described earlier.

=40=

This verse sets the framework for retaliation and forgiveness. “The reward of an evil deed is its equivalent” establishes the principle of just reciprocity in matters of harm and punishment. In cases where harm is inflicted, the injured party has the right to exact a response that is equal to the harm done, but no more.

This is the basis of qisas (just retaliation) in Islamic law, which ensures that punishment does not escalate into further injustice. However, the verse immediately offers a higher, more virtuous option: “But whoever pardons and seeks reconciliation, then their reward is with Allah.” Pardoning means letting go of the right to retaliate, and seeking reconciliation means actively working to mend the relationship and remove ill feelings.

The phrase “their reward is with Allah” is intentionally open-ended; it does not specify the exact reward, indicating that it is immense, far beyond the satisfaction of revenge. Allah himself takes charge of compensating the one who forgives. This motivates believers to rise above the natural impulse for revenge and to aim for a spiritual reward that is guaranteed by the Lord of all worlds.

The verse ends with a clarification: “He certainly does not like the wrongdoers.” This assures that the call to forgiveness does not mean Allah is pleased with the aggressor. The wrongdoer is still disliked by Allah, and they will face His judgment. The instruction to forgive is for the benefit of the one who forgives and for the health of society; it does not absolve the wrongdoer of their ultimate accountability. This final note maintains the balance between mercy and justice, leaving both the victim and the oppressor with a clear understanding of their positions before Allah.
