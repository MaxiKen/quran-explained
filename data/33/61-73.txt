=61=

This verse continues the severe warning against the hypocrites, the sick-hearted, and the rumour-mongers mentioned in the previous verse. It opens with a succinct declaration of their moral state: “They deserve to be condemned.

” The Arabic la‘nah denotes expulsion from Allah’s mercy, a curse that clings to them because of their persistent treachery. This condemnation is not an arbitrary decree but the natural outcome of their chosen path of spreading corruption and undermining the community from within.

The verse then intensifies the threat by outlining what will happen if they do not cease their activities: “If they were to persist, they would get themselves seized and killed relentlessly wherever they are found.” The phrase “wherever they are found” indicates that once the order for their expulsion or punishment is given, there will be no safe haven for them. They will be taken (uqifu) meaning they will be captured and prevented from fleeing, and then “killed relentlessly” (quttilu taqtila).

The use of the intensive form of the verb conveys a thorough, decisive execution. This is not a command for vigilante action but a prophecy and a legal ruling that would be implemented by the legitimate authority—the Prophet and, by extension, the Islamic state—against those who persist in high treason and sedition. The severity reflects the extreme danger these elements posed to the nascent Muslim community, which was in a state of continuous external threat. Internal sabotage could lead to the annihilation of the entire community, hence the uncompromising response.

However, it is crucial to note that this fate was conditional on their persistence. The door of repentance remained open; if they desisted, they could avoid this outcome. The verse, therefore, serves both as a powerful deterrent and as a legal foundation for dealing with sedition, always balancing justice with the opportunity for reform.

=62=

To emphasize that the threat in the previous verse is not an empty one or an anomaly, this verse anchors it in the unchanging pattern of divine dealing with hypocrisy. “That was Allah’s way with those hypocrites who have gone before.” The sunnah of Allah refers to His established and consistent method of dealing with nations and individuals based on their behaviour. Throughout history, when hypocrites and seditious elements infiltrated the communities of previous prophets and persisted in their corruption, Allah eventually gave the command to the prophet of that time to deal with them firmly, often through expulsion or punishment. This verse reassures the Prophet Muhammad that the treatment prescribed for the hypocrites of Medina is not a new invention but a continuation of the divine law that applied to earlier nations. The hypocrites of the past met the same fate of condemnation and destruction. Then the verse states a fundamental principle: “And you will find no change in Allah’s way.” The laws of Allah that govern the rise and fall of communities, the victory of truth over falsehood when the conditions are met, and the punishment of persistent internal treason do not alter to suit any individual’s convenience. The constancy of divine law provides moral stability; believers can trust that if they fulfill their covenant, Allah’s help will come, and that if traitors are left to wreak havoc, eventually the divine law will catch up with them. This also serves as a warning: the hypocrites of Medina should not think that they will escape the fate of their predecessors. Allah’s way does not show favoritism; it is absolute and predictable. For the believers, this verse imparts a deep sense of trust in the moral order of the universe, knowing that persistent evil, no matter how hidden, will ultimately be uprooted by the very laws set by the Creator, either in this world or the next.

=63=

The surah now shifts from the immediate threat of the hypocrites to the broader cosmic context of accountability. The disbelievers and hypocrites frequently mocked the Prophet by asking about the timing of the Day of Judgment, attempting to cast doubt on his message. “People ask you O Prophet about the Hour” refers to these recurring questions, often posed with sarcasm. The Prophet is commanded to respond with a clear, definitive answer: “Say, ‘That knowledge is only with Allah.’” The exact time of the Hour is one of the five keys of the unseen that Allah has kept exclusively for Himself. No prophet, no angel, no human being has access to this knowledge. This response cuts off all speculative arguments and redirects the focus from idle curiosity to urgent preparation. The Prophet is not to speculate or offer signs that humans could use to calculate a date. The absolute reservation of this knowledge to Allah alone emphasizes human dependence and the futility of those who demand it as a condition for belief. Then the verse adds a powerful psychological turn: “You never know, perhaps the Hour is near.” While the exact timing is hidden, its nearness is a real possibility. It could come upon humanity suddenly, without further warning.

This statement shatters any illusion of a distant, abstract future. It brings the reality of the Hour into the present moment, urging immediate repentance and righteous action.

The word “perhaps” (la‘alla) in Arabic does not indicate doubt on Allah’s part but introduces a sense of imminent possibility for the human audience. The verse thus brilliantly combines two truths: the absolute unknowability of the exact date, which humbles human arrogance, and the real nearness of the Hour, which ignites urgency.

In the context of the surah, after exposing the hypocrites and their fate, this reminder of the Hour underscores that the ultimate judgment is not in this world but in the next, and that everyone will be gathered to face their Lord.

=64=

The discourse now shifts from the specific hypocrites to the broader category of disbelievers, linking their fate to the certainty of divine condemnation and punishment. “Surely Allah condemns the disbelievers.” The la‘nah, or curse, of Allah upon the disbelievers means they have been expelled from His mercy, deprived of His guidance, and destined for humiliation. This condemnation is not merely a verbal pronouncement but a metaphysical reality that envelops their existence, cutting them off from the spiritual light. Then the verse specifies the concrete outcome of this condemnation: “and has prepared for them a blazing Fire.” The fire is described as sa‘ir, fiercely blazing, indicating an intensity of heat and torment that is already created and waiting.

The use of the past tense “has prepared” underscores the certainty of Hell’s existence; it is not a future possibility but a present reality, its chambers ready for their designated inhabitants. This serves as a stark contrast to the “great bounty” and “honourable provision” prepared for the believers. The Quran consistently presents these two preparations side by side, allowing the human being to visualize both destinations and make a conscious choice. The blazing Fire is not an arbitrary punishment but the just recompense for the crime of disbelief, which is the ultimate ingratitude and rebellion against the Creator who gave them life, provision, and countless signs. The verse stands as a powerful warning, leaving no ambiguity about the outcome of those who reject the message after it has been clearly conveyed. The immediacy of the prepared Fire should shake the conscience of anyone who hears this verse, prompting reflection on which destination they are heading toward.

=65=

This verse emphasizes two aspects of the punishment that make it utterly terrifying: its eternity and its absolute hopelessness. “To stay there for ever and ever” translates the Arabic khalidina fiha abada, which uses the strongest possible terms to denote permanence without end.

This is the punishment for those who die in a state of disbelief and for the hypocrites whose hypocrisy was their true inner state. The eternity of Hell for disbelievers is a core doctrine, highlighting that the rejection of the truth when presented with clear evidence is a crime of infinite gravity because it is a rejection of the Infinite. The soul in Hell will experience no diminution of the punishment over time, no hope of eventual release, and no annihilation of consciousness.

The verse then adds, “never will they find any protector or helper.” In this world, people often rely on their wealth, their allies, their social status, or their false gods to protect and help them. On the Day of Judgment, all such supports will vanish. The disbelievers will be utterly alone, stripped of every illusion of power. No intercessor will speak for them without Allah’s permission, and none will be permitted for them. The despair of that situation is complete; they will realize that the warnings they dismissed were true, and there is no way back. This verse, along with the preceding one, serves to create a profound sense of urgency and fear that is intended to shake the believer into holding fast to faith, and to warn the disbeliever before it is too late. The psychology of “never” and “no protector” targets the deepest human need for hope and security, showing that these can only be truly found in turning to Allah in this life, before the door of opportunity closes forever.

=66=

This verse paints a specific, graphic scene from the Day of Judgment that illustrates the nature of the punishment and the regret of its inhabitants. “On the Day their faces are constantly flipped in the Fire.” The description “flipped” indicates that their faces will be turned over and over, roasted on all sides, so that no part of their face escapes the burning. The face is the most honoured part of the human body, the locus of identity and dignity; its exposure to the Fire in this degrading manner symbolizes the complete humiliation and reversal of status for those who were arrogant in the world. The punishment is not static but dynamic, continuously renewed. In the midst of this torment, they will cry out with a deep sense of regret: “Oh! If only we had obeyed Allah and obeyed the Messenger!

” The word “ya laytana” expresses a painful, desperate wish to return to the past, but it is too late. Their cry reveals the core of their crime: they failed in the fundamental duty of obedience to Allah and His Messenger. In the world, they were called to this obedience and they refused, mocked, or delayed. Now, when the reality is undeniable, obedience is no longer possible, and all that remains is regret. The coupling of obedience to Allah with obedience to the Messenger is significant; it confirms that obeying the Prophet is inseparable from obeying Allah. This cry of the damned serves as a powerful admonition to the living. It is a preview of the ultimate regret, intended to provoke immediate self-reflection: Am I truly obeying Allah and His Messenger in my daily life? The verse gives voice to the agony of missed opportunity, making it a potent tool for da‘wah and self-rectification. It also ties back to the surah’s repeated emphasis on obeying the Prophet, showing that the entire social and moral guidance given in this surah is not optional advice but the path that distinguishes salvation from eternal regret.

=67=

The lament of the damned continues, and here they shift from expressing general regret to identifying the specific cause of their misguidance. They address Allah directly: “Our Lord! We obeyed our leaders and elite.” The words “leaders” (sadatan) and “elite” (kubara’) refer to those in positions of authority, influence, and prestige—political rulers, religious scholars who deviated, tribal chieftains, wealthy aristocrats, and cultural trendsetters. In their earthly life, these people chose to follow these figures blindly instead of exercising their own God-given intellect to verify the truth of the message. They surrendered their personal responsibility to the herd mentality, assuming that the elite must know better. Now they confess this misplaced obedience, not as an excuse, but as a fact that they now realize was their ruin. “But they led us astray from the Right Way. ” The leaders did not guide them to safety; instead, they diverted them from the straight path onto paths of misguidance, innovation, and denial. This verse contains a profound warning against blind following (taqlid) in matters of faith. Islam calls people to use their reason and to evaluate the message itself, not to follow their ancestors or social leaders uncritically. On the Day of Judgment, the followers cannot shift their blame entirely onto the leaders; they still bear the responsibility for their choice to obey creatures rather than the Creator. The leaders will bear the sin of their misguidance, and the followers will bear the sin of their blind compliance. This verse urges every believer to anchor their obedience directly in the clear sources of Quran and Sunnah, to be wary of charismatic but deviant figures, and to constantly check their allegiances. It also reveals the bitter irony of Hell: those whom they proudly followed in the world are now the very chains around their necks.

=68=

This verse continues the speech of the damned, but their tone shifts from confession to a cry for justice against their corrupt leaders. “Our Lord! Give them double our punishment.” Having realized that their leaders not only went astray themselves but also actively misled their followers, the damned ask that the leaders receive a multiplied torment. This request is a reflection of divine justice, as the leaders committed two crimes: their own misguidance and the misguidance of those who followed them. The Quran elsewhere confirms that those who lead others astray will bear their own burdens and some of the burdens of those they misled, without diminishing the punishment of the followers. The request for “double” is not an exaggeration but a recognition of the compounded sin. Then they add, “and condemn them tremendously. ” The word la‘nan kabira means a great, immense curse, asking Allah to expel them from His mercy in the most profound way. The fact that the followers will turn against their leaders with such vehemence shatters the false bonds of loyalty that existed in the world. The friendships based on sin and defiance of Allah will turn into bitter enmity on that Day. This scene serves as a terrifying warning to anyone in a position of influence: imams, teachers, social media influencers, parents, and community leaders. If they use their position to lead people away from the straight path, they will face not only their own punishment but also the multiplied wrath and the curses of those whom they deceived. It also serves as a warning to followers to choose their leaders wisely, for a leader who leads to Hell will be a curse upon them both in this life and the next. The verse is a vivid dramatization of the ultimate breakdown of false loyalties, reinforcing that the only true allegiance is that which is built on the obedience to Allah and His Messenger.

=69=

The surah now turns to a direct exhortation to the believers, using the example of Prophet Moses to teach a lesson about refraining from baseless accusations and offensive speech towards the righteous. “O believers! Do not be like those who slandered Moses, but Allah cleared him of what they said.” This refers to an incident in the life of Moses where he was subjected to false accusations. According to authentic hadith, Moses was a very modest man who would not show his skin, and some of the Israelites, out of malice, claimed he had a skin disease or defect. One day, while he was bathing alone, a stone took his clothes and fled; Moses ran after it, and the Israelites saw his body and realized it was perfect, thus Allah cleared him of their slander. The lesson is that believers must not engage in the slander of prophets or righteous people.

In the context of this surah, which deals heavily with the slander against the Prophet Muhammad and his family, the verse is a clear reminder: the hypocrites and disbelievers who spread rumours about Aisha or mocked the Prophet’s marriage are following the pattern of the sinful Israelites who maligned Moses. Just as Allah personally cleared Moses, He will clear the Prophet and his household.

The verse adds, “And he was honourable in the sight of Allah,” meaning Moses had a special position of wajahah, honour and esteem, with his Lord. His status was not diminished by the slander of the people, and Allah’s swift clearing of his name demonstrated his honour. The believers are thus called to emulate the respect that Allah showed His prophet and to never be the source of slander, especially against the people of faith.

This is a direct admonition to those who might have participated in or listened to the slander against the Prophet’s wife, and a timeless instruction on guarding the tongue and honouring the righteous.

=70=

This concise, powerful verse encapsulates the ethical core of the believer’s speech and behaviour after the preceding lessons. “O believers! Be mindful of Allah” is the call to taqwa, the comprehensive consciousness of Allah that governs every thought and act. When taqwa is firmly established in the heart, it naturally filters the speech.

The second command follows: “and say what is right.” The Arabic qawlan sadida means speech that is straight, correct, truthful, firm, and hitting the mark. It encompasses several qualities: it is factually true, free from lies and distortion; it is just, avoiding bias and harm; it is appropriate to the occasion, neither too harsh nor too soft; and it is effective in achieving its righteous purpose.

This command was revealed after the incident of the slander (ifk) against Aisha, in which many people had spoken based on rumours, suspicion, and hearsay. They had failed in both taqwa and in saying what is right. The verse is a profound corrective to the culture of gossip, slander, and unverified speech that can destroy reputations and divide communities. It commands that before speaking, a believer must check two things: is my heart in a state of mindfulness of Allah, and are these words exactly right? If the words are not true, or not just, or not beneficial, then taqwa demands silence. The Prophet said that whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day should say good or remain silent. This verse thus lays the foundation for a society where speech is responsible, dignity is protected, and the atmosphere is one of trust and integrity. Every believer, especially those in positions of influence, must constantly apply this filter to their words, remembering that every utterance is recorded and will be accounted for.

=71=

This verse reveals the beautiful consequences of embodying taqwa and speaking what is right, as commanded in the previous verse. “He will bless your deeds for you” translates yuslih lakum a‘malakum, which means Allah will rectify, improve, and make righteous your actions. When a person is mindful of Allah and guards their tongue, Allah responds by putting their entire life in order. Their deeds, which might otherwise be tainted by hidden sins of the tongue such as backbiting or slander, become purified and accepted. The blessings can also mean that Allah makes their affairs easy, grants success in their endeavours, and inspires them to do more good. Next, “and forgive your sins.” The guarding of the tongue and the fear of Allah lead directly to divine forgiveness.

This is a tremendous promise because the sins of the tongue are among the most frequent and destructive. The one who controls their speech and aligns it with the truth will find that Allah overlooks their other shortcomings.

The verse then expands the reward to encompass the entire scope of the believer’s life: “And whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger, has truly achieved a great triumph.” Obedience to Allah and His Messenger is the comprehensive term that includes everything from inner taqwa to correct speech to all other acts of worship and social conduct. The fawz ‘azim is the supreme success, which includes safety from Hellfire, entry into Paradise, and the attainment of Allah’s pleasure.

The word fawz literally means the winning of the prize, the ultimate victory. In the context of the surah, this promise serves as a powerful motivation to adhere to all the instructions given: from the rules of hijab, to the loyalty in battle, to the respect for the Prophet’s household, to the control of the tongue. The one who does all this has attained everything worth attaining. It is a verse of immense hope, showing that the path of obedience, though it may require struggle and self-restraint, leads to an outcome that is the very definition of triumph.

=72=

This verse, one of the most profound in the Quran, presents a cosmic parable about the nature of human responsibility. “Indeed, We offered the trust to the heavens and the earth and the mountains.” The “trust” (al-amanah) has been interpreted by the companions and scholars in various integrated ways, including the obligations of the divine law, the faculty of intellect and free will, and the capacity to choose obedience or disobedience. It is the burden of moral responsibility, the ability to consciously submit to Allah or to rebel. This trust was offered to the mighty creations: the heavens, the earth, and the mountains. These are vast, powerful, and stable, yet they all “declined to bear it, being fearful of it.” They recognized the immense weight and the severe consequences of failing in this trust, so out of awe and fear of Allah, they refused. They chose the safety of compulsory submission (islam takwini), where they obey Allah by their very nature without the risk of disobedience. Then the verse states, “But humanity assumed it.” Man, in his unique constitution, accepted this trust, taking on the burden of free will and moral obligation. The reason for this acceptance is then given: “for they are truly wrongful to themselves and ignorant of the consequences.

” This is not a praise of humanity’s courage but a lament over our inherent tendency towards injustice (zulm) and ignorance (jahl). Man did not fully appreciate the magnitude of what he was undertaking. He was, in his primordial state, too hasty and unaware of his own weakness.

Yet, by accepting the trust, humanity became the arena for the highest potential: the attainment of wilaya (sainthood) and the knowledge of Allah, but also the lowest depths: the betrayal of the trust leading to eternal loss. The verse explains the human condition: we are a mixture of nobility and wretchedness, capable of bearing the divine trust because of our unique faculties, yet constantly at risk of failing because of our inherent flaws. This parable frames the entire moral struggle of life.

=73=

This verse concludes the surah by explaining the grand outcome of humanity’s assumption of the trust described in the previous verse. The assumption of the trust set the stage for a universal sorting of souls based on their free choices. “So that Allah will punish hypocrite men and women and polytheistic men and women.” The consequence of betraying the trust is punishment. The verse mentions hypocrites first, which is fitting given the heavy focus on their exposure throughout Surah Al-Ahzab, and then polytheists, who openly associate partners with Allah. Both groups failed in the fundamental requirement of the trust: the hypocrites by outwardly accepting but inwardly rejecting, and the polytheists by openly refusing the call to pure monotheism. Their punishment is a direct result of their misuse of the free will that the trust entailed. Then the verse provides the glorious contrast: “and Allah will turn in mercy to believing men and women. ” The word yatuba, which is from the root of tawbah, indicates that Allah will accept their repentance and guide them to turn back to Him, showering them with His mercy. The believing men and women are those who, despite their human ignorance and tendency to injustice, held fast to the trust, fulfilled its obligations, and constantly sought Allah’s forgiveness for their shortcomings.

The surah then concludes with a reminder of Allah’s attributes that give hope: “For Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” This conclusion echoes the very beginning of the surah, where the Prophet was commanded to be mindful of Allah, and ties together all the legal rulings, the battle narratives, the domestic guidance, and the warnings.

The final message is one of immense mercy: the trust was heavy, humanity was weak, and mistakes were made, but for those who believe and turn to Allah, His forgiveness and mercy encompass them. The surah ends by highlighting the ultimate success of the believers, contrasting it with the loss of the hypocrites and polytheists, and leaving the listener with a profound sense of hope, responsibility, and the need to constantly return to the Most Merciful.
