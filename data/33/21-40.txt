=21=

After exposing the cowardice, greed, and hollow faith of the hypocrites, the Quran turns the believers’ gaze to the perfect model: the Prophet Muhammad himself. “Indeed, in the Messenger of Allah you have an excellent example” translates the Arabic term uswah hasanah, a pattern of conduct that is beautiful in its entirety and worthy of imitation. This declaration is especially powerful given the preceding descriptions of eyes growing wild and hearts jumping into throats. The Prophet was on the same battlefield, facing the same enemy, enduring the same cold, hunger, and fear.

Yet his behaviour was entirely different. During the digging of the trench he laboured alongside his companions, tying stones on his stomach to suppress hunger. He led the prayers, remained calm, and continued to make du’a for victory, never once uttering a word of despair or doubt. His example was one of absolute trust in Allah, tireless effort, and merciful leadership.

The verse then specifies who can truly benefit from this example: “for whoever has hope in Allah and the Last Day, and remembers Allah often.” Hope in Allah and the Last Day gives the believer a horizon beyond the present crisis. The one who expects to stand before Allah and desires His reward will see the Prophet’s conduct not as a story but as a roadmap for their own life. And the mention of “remembers Allah often” connects the outward imitation to its inner source. The Prophet’s steadfastness was fueled by constant dhikr; his heart was anchored in Allah, and that is what made his example so firm. The hypocrites, who had no real hope in the Hereafter and whose remembrance of Allah was scant, could never emulate him. The verse is a timeless invitation. Every believer facing personal or communal trials can find in the Prophet’s life the perfect balance of human vulnerability and prophetic perfection. He got angry, he felt pain, he experienced loss, yet his response was always governed by revelation and the remembrance of his Lord.

This is why the scholars say that his seerah is the living tafsir of the Quran, and why this verse is a cornerstone for the concept of following the Sunnah.

=22=

This verse shows the believers’ radically different reaction to the very sight that caused the hypocrites to tremble and flee. “When the believers saw the enemy alliance, they said, ‘This is what Allah and His Messenger had promised us.’” The confederate army was a terrifying sight, but the believers recognised it as the fulfillment of a prior promise. Earlier revelations, such as the verse that says “Do people think they will be left alone on saying, ‘We believe,’ and they will not be tested?” or the Prophet’s specific warnings that the Arabs would unite against them, had prepared them mentally. They understood that trial was a sunnah of Allah, a necessary crucible for the sincere. So when the enemy masses appeared on the horizon, they did not see a catastrophe; they saw a divine promise coming true. This reinterpretation of a frightening event is a profound fruit of faith. Instead of breaking their spirits, the sight of the alliance confirmed the truthfulness of the revelation and the Messenger.

Consequently, “this only increased them in faith and submission. ” Iman is not a static quantity; it grows with obedience and decreases with sin. The believers’ faith deepened precisely because they were witnessing the fulfillment of Allah’s word. Their submission (islam) also increased, meaning their outward surrender and compliance became more complete. There was no hesitation, no second-guessing the command to dig the trench or to stand firm.

This verse teaches believers how to frame difficulties: they are not accidents but parts of a divine plan, and recognising them as such transforms fear into spiritual growth. The hypocrites saw the same army and called it a delusion; the believers saw it and called it a confirmation. The difference was the lens of iman.

The verse also serves as a psychological tool: by training ourselves to view trials as promised tests, we can experience the same increase in faith that the companions did, turning every hardship into an opportunity to draw closer to Allah.

=23=

Having described the collective response of the believers, the Quran now singles out the highest rank among them: those who were utterly true to their covenant with Allah. “Among the believers are men who have proven true to what they pledged to Allah.

” The word “rijal” here refers not just to males but to individuals of spiritual maturity and heroic steadfastness. These believers had made a covenant with Allah at various pledges, such as the Second Pledge of Aqabah or the pledge to fight and not flee. They proved true by their actions, never wavering.

The verse then divides them into two honourable categories. “Some of them have fulfilled their pledge with their lives” refers to those who have already been martyred in previous battles like Badr and Uhud. They paid the ultimate price, and their covenant is complete. “Others are waiting their turn” refers to those still alive, yearning for martyrdom and ready to meet it without a trace of hesitation. They are described as being in a state of active, patient waiting, not passive or fearful. The famous companion Anas ibn an-Nadr, who missed Badr but fought valiantly at Uhud saying he could smell the fragrance of Paradise, is a perfect example of this category.

The final clause, “They have never changed their commitment in the least,” emphasises total fidelity. There was no alteration, no secret reservation, no gradual cooling of resolve.

The Arabic form negates even the slightest hint of change. This is the pinnacle of human loyalty to the divine. The verse immortalises these companions and sets a standard for every generation: true manhood and womanhood in Islam are measured by fidelity to one’s covenant with Allah, a covenant renewed daily in prayer, and ultimately proven when life itself is on the line.

=24=

This verse explains the divine wisdom behind the entire sequence of testing at the Battle of the Trench. “It all happened so Allah may reward the faithful for their faithfulness.” The trial was not arbitrary; it was designed to bring forth the quality of sidq (truthfulness) in the believers and then reward them for it. The reward is not merely for having faith in theory, but for demonstrating that faith under extreme pressure. The suffering, the cold, the fear, and the long vigil all became the raw material for eternal reward. The believers’ faithfulness was manifested in their words, their lack of retreat, and their trust in Allah’s promise.

The second purpose concerns the other group: “and punish the hypocrites if He wills or turn to them in mercy.” The verse does not doom the hypocrites to an automatic, irrevocable punishment. It leaves the door open for Allah’s mercy.

The phrase “if He wills” indicates that even the hypocrites, if they genuinely repent and mend their ways, can be forgiven. The exposure of their hypocrisy during the siege could lead some of them to feel remorse and change. Allah’s mercy is not closed to anyone who turns back.

The verse then concludes with two powerful names: “Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” Even after describing the most despicable acts of cowardice and treachery, the Quran reminds us that Allah’s forgiveness is immense and His mercy envelops all things. This prevents the believers from falling into despair about their own sins and from becoming arrogantly judgmental about others. The balance is exquisite: the faithful are rewarded for their truthfulness, and the hypocritical are warned yet given a chance. The whole historical episode was thus a theater of divine justice and mercy, serving as a blueprint for how Allah deals with human beings in every age.

=25=

After detailing the internal spiritual dynamics, the verse returns to the external outcome of the siege. “And Allah drove back the disbelievers in their rage, totally empty-handed.” The confederates had marched on Medina filled with anger and ambition, expecting to annihilate the Muslim community. They returned in a state of intense fury because they had failed to achieve any of their objectives, yet their hands were completely empty. No booty, no conquest, no humiliation of the believers—just the bitter cold wind and the terror cast into their hearts.

The phrase “in their rage” captures their psychological defeat: not only were they materially unsuccessful, but their pride was shattered, and their anger had no outlet except to burn within themselves. “And Allah spared the believers from fighting.” This was a special favour. The believers had dug the trench and prepared for a defensive battle, but a direct, large-scale engagement did not materialize because the unseen forces and the wind sufficed to scatter the enemy. Allah’s direct intervention meant that the believers did not have to shed their blood in a pitched battle that could have been devastating given their numerical disadvantage. The verse attributes this entirely to Allah’s power: “For Allah is All-Powerful, Almighty.” He possesses absolute might, and no force can stand against Him. The believers’ role was to do their human best—digging the trench, enduring the cold, standing guard—and then Allah took over the results. This teaches that ultimate victory is from Allah, not from human numbers or strength. The event also fulfills a psychological purpose: after the fear and the shaking, to see the enemy vanish without a direct fight was a massive boost to the believers’ confidence and a sign of divine support that would resonate in their hearts for years to come.

=26=

This verse recounts the fate of the Jewish tribe of Banu Qurayza, who broke their treaty with the Muslims during the Battle of the Trench and sided with the confederates. “And He brought down those from the People of the Book who supported the enemy alliance from their own strongholds.” The Banu Qurayza had formidable fortresses southeast of Medina. They felt secure behind their walls and had plotted with the Quraysh to attack the Muslims from the rear. After the confederates fled, the Prophet was commanded by Angel Jibril to march against them immediately. Allah brought them down from their strongholds, meaning their physical defences could not protect them from divine decree. The siege of their fortresses lasted about twenty-five days. “And cast horror into their hearts” indicates that Allah placed an overwhelming terror within them before the final assault, making their resistance crumble from within, just as He had done to the confederates. This psychological warfare was a direct divine intervention. “You believers killed some, and took others captive.” When they eventually surrendered, they agreed to the judgment of Sa‘d ibn Mu‘adh, an Ansari leader whom they had once trusted. He ruled according to their own Torah, which decreed that the fighting men be executed and the women and children taken captive. The verse states this plainly to show that the outcome was a direct consequence of their treachery and a fulfillment of divine justice. The incident serves as a stern warning about the consequences of breaking solemn covenants, particularly during times of war. At the same time, it demonstrates that Allah defends the believers and that treachery, even from those who hold scripture, does not go unpunished. The believers were not the aggressors; they were the victims of a planned betrayal, and this outcome re-established security in Medina.

=27=

This verse highlights the material outcome of the divine victory over the Banu Qurayza and its wider implications. “He has also caused you to take over their lands, homes, and wealth.” After the judgment was carried out, the Muslims inherited the territory, houses, properties, and assets of the Banu Qurayza. This was a significant economic gain for a community that had largely been made up of impoverished Muhajirun and self-sacrificing Ansar. But the verse does not stop there; it adds “as well as lands you have not yet set foot on.

” This is a prophecy of future conquests. The Muslims were promised that they would inherit not just the immediate spoils but vast territories they had never even visited. This prediction was spectacularly fulfilled with the later conquests of Khaybar, then the lands of Persia, Rome, and beyond. The promise given at a time when the Muslims were a small, besieged community in Medina served to expand their vision and fill them with confidence. It connected the immediate local victory to a grand divine plan.

The verse ends with “And Allah is Most Capable of everything,” reaffirming that such sweeping promises are not difficult for the One whose power has no limits. The believers were taught to see every victory, whether small or large, as a sign of Allah’s absolute capability and as a trust from Him. The wealth and lands were not ends in themselves but means to establish the religion and spread justice. This verse, therefore, serves as a lesson on how to view worldly success: it comes from Allah, it is often beyond what one imagines, and it is a test of gratitude and stewardship. The believers who had shivered in the trench and seen their eyes grow wild were now the recipients of divine generosity, a pattern that repeats whenever a community remains steadfast and true.

=28=

The surah now shifts from the external battles to the internal affairs of the Prophet’s household, addressing a sensitive moment with profound wisdom. “O Prophet! Say to your wives, ‘If you desire the life of this world and its luxury, then come, I will give you a suitable compensation for divorce and let you go graciously.’” The background is well-known: the Prophet’s wives, after the conquest of Khaybar and the influx of wealth, had asked for an increase in their modest maintenance. Some had even wished for a little more comfort. The Prophet, distressed by the pull of worldly desires within his household, withdrew from them for a month. Then this verse was revealed, offering them a clear choice. The wording is exceptionally dignified. He does not rebuke them harshly but instead presents the world and its adornment as an option, along with a generous exit.

The phrase “suitable compensation” refers to the mut‘ah, a parting gift given to a divorced wife, and “let you go graciously” indicates a release with honour and without rancour. There is no coercion, no threat. The door to a comfortable worldly life is open, but it means the end of their unique status as Mothers of the Believers. This approach honours their human inclinations while setting the bar of the prophetic household. The choice is laid out with absolute clarity, and it places the responsibility squarely on their shoulders.

The verse teaches an important principle about marriage and leadership: when conflicts of values arise, a noble separation with kindness is superior to a relationship filled with resentment or compromise in religious standards. The Prophet was teaching his wives, and through them all believers, that the standard in his home was not mere subsistence but complete orientation towards Allah and the Hereafter.

=29=

This is the second half of the choice presented to the wives of the Prophet. “But if you desire Allah and His Messenger and the everlasting Home of the Hereafter…” The structure is beautiful: the first option was the life of this world and its luxury, a temporary and fleeting enjoyment. This option is the exact opposite: desiring Allah, meaning His pleasure, closeness, and the fulfillment of His commands; desiring His Messenger, meaning his companionship in this life and his special nearness in the next; and desiring the Home of the Hereafter, which is the everlasting abode. The three are linked; you cannot truly desire Allah without desiring His Messenger and without longing for the eternal life where He will be seen and His rewards enjoyed.

The verse continues: “then Surely Allah has prepared a great reward for those of you who do good.” The reward is not merely for choosing correctly in words but for embodying that choice through muhsinat, women who excel in goodness. The great reward is beyond imagination, far surpassing the fleeting luxury of this world. This verse is a testament to the free will and spiritual dignity of the Prophet’s wives. All of them, led by Aisha and Umm Salamah, chose Allah and His Messenger without a moment’s hesitation. The story of their choice is one of the most moving episodes in the seerah. They understood that their position was not one of privilege in a material sense but of immense spiritual responsibility and honour. For all believers, this verse encapsulates the fundamental human choice: the dunya or the akhirah. It teaches that the two demands are often in tension and that choosing the Hereafter may require sacrificing worldly comforts, but the reward Allah has prepared is infinitely greater.

The verse also sets the stage for the special legal and ethical guidelines that follow for the Mothers of the Believers.

=30=

Having established the principle of choosing the Hereafter, the Quran now outlines the weighty consequences that come with the unique status of the Prophet’s wives. The direct address “O wives of the Prophet!” underlines their special position. “If any of you were to commit a blatant misconduct, the punishment would be doubled for her.

” The Arabic phrase fahishah mubayyinah refers to a clear, grievous sin—something obviously and unequivocally wrong. Because these women are the wives of the Messenger, their proximity to revelation and their status as role models for the entire Muslim community make any misdeed far more serious. The higher the rank, the greater the responsibility, and the more severe the accountability. The doubling of punishment is an application of divine justice, not harshness. It is because they have been given unparalleled spiritual advantages: living with the Prophet, hearing the Quran directly from him, witnessing his conduct day and night. With such immense blessings comes immense accountability.

The verse adds “And that is easy for Allah” to affirm that carrying out this doubled punishment is in no way difficult for the Almighty. The statement also serves as a deterrence: the ease with which Allah can punish should make anyone think twice before transgressing. This verse, taken together with the next, forms a balanced pair. The severity of the warning is not meant to crush hope but to protect them from slipping, and to establish the principle that honour and responsibility are inseparable. It also serves as a general principle for all believers: those who have greater knowledge and occupy positions of religious leadership will be held to a stricter account. The Mothers of the Believers are thus both honoured and cautioned, their lives a living lesson for the ummah.

=31=

This verse forms the beautiful counterpart to the previous warning. “And whoever of you devoutly obeys Allah and His Messenger and does good” describes a state of qunut, which implies continuous, humble obedience, not sporadic compliance. It is an obedience born of love and sustained by sincerity. To this is added “does good,” meaning they perfect their actions, excelling in worship, character, and service. For such women, the promise is stunning: “We will grant her double the reward.” Just as the punishment for a sin would be doubled due to their elevated position, so too the reward for their obedience and good deeds is multiplied.

This is the manifestation of Allah’s immense generosity. Every prayer, every act of patience with the Prophet’s simple life, every moment of teaching the community—all these are weighted with double the merit compared to others.

The verse continues: “and We have prepared for her an honourable provision.” This rizq karim is a provision that is not only abundant but also noble, pure, and accompanied by honour.

In the Hereafter, it refers to the highest levels of Paradise, a provision that befits their status as wives of the Beloved of Allah in this world. The pairing of doubled punishment and doubled reward within two consecutive verses is a masterful display of divine balance. It encourages the wives to see their difficult situation—material simplicity, restrictions on movement, constant public scrutiny—not as a burden but as an opportunity for exponential spiritual growth.

The verse also teaches the community that the wives of the Prophet are not to be viewed as ordinary individuals but as spiritual giants whose every action carries immense weight, and that the community owes them respect and love proportionate to their station.

=32=

This verse begins a new set of instructions specifically tailored to the unique status just declared. “O wives of the Prophet! You are not like any other women.” This foundational statement is not one of inherent superiority in their essence as human beings, but a declaration of their unique role, responsibility, and rank in the sight of Allah. Because they are not like other women, the standards of conduct expected of them are correspondingly higher. “If you are mindful of Allah,” meaning if you truly have taqwa and desire to maintain that elevated status, “then do not be overly effeminate in speech with men.” The prohibition is against khudu‘ bil-qawl, a manner of speaking that is deliberately soft, sweet, or seductive in tone, designed to attract or allure. The concern is not about normal, necessary conversation, but about a stylistic choice that could stir improper feelings in someone whose heart is diseased with lust or hypocrisy. The verse explains the reason: “or those with sickness in their hearts may be tempted.” There were hypocrites in Medina who harboured impure intentions, and an overly soft manner from the Prophet’s wives could give them an opening for evil thoughts. The command is preventive, safeguarding both the women and the community’s moral climate. The alternative is given: “but speak in a moderate tone. ” Qawl ma‘rufan is speech that is upright, dignified, clear, and appropriate to the situation. It conveys the message without any unnecessary softening or emotional colouring that could be misconstrued. This instruction is not about silencing women or depriving them of normal interaction; rather, it is a lesson in intentional dignity and self-respect, especially for those in positions of public visibility. The principle extends to all believing women, though the standard for the Mothers of the Believers is the strictest. It protects the sanctity of their role as teachers and role models.

=33=

This verse continues the instructions to the Prophet’s wives with commands that shape their daily conduct and inner state. “Settle in your homes” (wa qarna fi buyutikunna) is a command to make the home the primary sphere of their activity and presence. It does not mean they never left their houses—they did, for worship, necessary business, and to visit family—but the home was to be their base, their place of dignity and stability. The underlying wisdom is protection and the preservation of their unique status. “And do not display yourselves as women did in the days of pre-Islamic ignorance.” Tabarruj refers to a conspicuous display of beauty, walking out adorned and perfumed in a manner that attracts attention.

The first Jahiliyyah was marked by such public flaunting. This prohibition safeguards modesty and prevents the commodification of women’s appearance.

The verse then balances the restriction with positive spiritual commands: “Establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and obey Allah and His Messenger.” The external conduct of staying home and avoiding display must be accompanied by a vibrant inner life of worship and social responsibility. Prayer and zakah are mentioned together, linking devotion to Allah with care for the needy. Then comes one of the most celebrated passages in the Quran: “Allah only intends to keep the causes of evil away from you and purify you completely, O members of the Prophet’s family! ” The address “Ahl al-Bayt” includes the wives as the primary context, though the famous hadith of the cloak extends it specifically to Ali, Fatimah, Hasan, and Husayn as the core household. The divine will expressed is a continuous purification from all spiritual impurities, sin, and moral blemish. This verse is a declaration of the spiritual nobility of the prophetic household and a reminder to them—and to all believers—that the commands of Allah are not arbitrary but are rooted in His loving desire to purify and honour.

=34=

This verse gives the spiritual nourishment that sustains the elevated life described in the previous verses. “Always remember what is recited in your homes of Allah’s revelations and prophetic wisdom.” The homes of the Prophet’s wives were the very places where the Quran was often revealed, where Jibril descended, and where the Prophet taught, prayed, and explained the faith. These homes were not ordinary houses; they were centers of revelation and wisdom. The command to “remember” here includes reciting, studying, pondering, and putting into practice the verses of the Quran and the hikmah—which many commentators interpret as the Sunnah, the Prophet’s words and actions that explain the Book. By keeping the Quran and the Prophetic wisdom alive in their homes through constant remembrance, the wives would fortify themselves against the temptations of the world and remain conscious of their special role.

The verse then concludes with two magnificent names of Allah: “Surely Allah is Most Subtle, All-Aware.” Al-Latif, the Most Subtle, indicates that Allah’s knowledge and kindness penetrate the finest details; He understands the delicate nuances of every situation. Al-Khabir, the All-Aware, means He is fully cognizant of all inner and outer realities. These names comfort the wives: Allah is aware of their struggles, their devotion, their whispered recitations at night, and their efforts to uphold the standards set for them. He is not a distant overseer but the Subtle, the Aware, who appreciates every sincere act and who gently guides hearts. For all believers, this verse underscores the importance of making our homes centers of Quranic recitation and the study of the Prophet’s life, filling them with the light that chases away ignorance and misguidance.

=35=

This magnificent verse is a profound declaration of spiritual equality and the comprehensive nature of Islamic virtue. It was reportedly revealed when some women asked the Prophet why the Quran seemed to address men predominantly. The response is a cascade of paired qualities, explicitly naming both genders for each virtue. “Surely for Muslim men and women” starts with the foundational act of submission, the outward embrace of Islam. “Believing men and women” then moves to the inner dimension of iman, the deep-seated faith in the heart. “Devout men and women” refers to qanitin, those who are constantly obedient with humility. “Truthful men and women” are those whose words, actions, and intentions are aligned in truthfulness. “Patient men and women” endure hardships, temptations, and the struggles of life with beautiful patience. “Humble men and women” are khashi‘in, possessing a heart full of reverent awe before Allah. “Charitable men and women” spend their wealth and efforts freely in the path of Allah. “Fasting men and women” observe fasts with sincerity, mastering their desires. “Men and women who guard their chastity” protect their private parts from all that is unlawful. “And men and women who remember Allah often” engage in constant dhikr, making the remembrance of Allah the background music of their lives. This list encompasses the ideal Muslim character, balanced between acts of worship, moral virtues, and social responsibilities.

The verse then promises: “for all of them Allah has prepared forgiveness and a great reward.” The forgiveness covers past sins, and the great reward is Paradise, described as vast and magnificent. The deliberate repetition of “men and women” with each attribute destroys any notion that piety or spiritual rank is gender-based. Before Allah, the standard is taqwa, and both men and women have an equal capacity to attain these qualities and an equal share in the resulting reward. It is a charter of gender equity in the spiritual path.

=36=

This verse establishes a fundamental principle of iman that is directly tied to the events about to be narrated. “It is not for a believing man or woman—when Allah and His Messenger decree a matter—to have any other choice in that matter.” Once a matter has been decided by divine revelation or by a clear ruling from the Prophet, the believer’s personal preference, cultural custom, or emotional inclination must yield. The phrasing “it is not for a believing man or woman” indicates that iman itself precludes the option of autonomous choice in the face of divine decree. A believer’s core identity is one of submission, and that submission is meaningless if it is contingent on agreeing with the decree.

The second part of the verse emphasizes the gravity of the alternative: “Indeed, whoever disobeys Allah and His Messenger has clearly gone far astray.” Disobedience is not a minor misstep but a clear and far deviation from the straight path.

The word “dalal” (astray) implies losing one’s way in the desert, ending up in destruction. This verse was the direct preamble to the story of Zaid and Zainab that follows. The social custom of Arabia considered a man marrying the ex-wife of an adopted son as an abominable act. Allah was about to abrogate that custom, and the Prophet himself felt the weight of public opinion. The verse lays down the uncompromising rule: no matter how deeply ingrained a social norm is, once Allah decrees otherwise, the believer has no choice but to obey.

This principle extends to every aspect of life—marriage, business, governance, personal desires. The verse safeguards the religion from becoming subject to human whims and cultural fashions, anchoring it firmly in revelation. It is a timeless call to check our hearts whenever we feel resistance to a clear divine command.

=37=

This verse narrates a deeply personal and theologically significant episode. The “one for whom Allah has done a favour” refers to Zaid ibn Harithah. Allah’s favour upon him was guidance to Islam, and the Prophet’s favour was freeing him from slavery and adopting him before the abolition of adoption’s legal effects. Zaid was married to Zainab bint Jahsh, a woman of noble Qurayshi lineage. The marriage encountered severe discord due to social incompatibility, and Zaid complained repeatedly to the Prophet, who was divinely informed that Zaid would eventually divorce Zainab and that the Prophet himself would marry her.

Yet the Prophet told Zaid, “Keep your wife and fear Allah.” He was “concealing within yourself what Allah was going to reveal” out of a natural human concern for what people would say—accusing him of marrying his adopted son’s former wife. The verse gently but firmly corrects this: “And so you were considering the people, whereas Allah was more worthy of your consideration. ” Even the Prophet’s delicate concern for public perception had to be set aside for the sake of implementing a divine legal reform. Then the verse declares the outcome: “So when Zaid totally lost interest in keeping his wife, We gave her to you in marriage.” The marriage was solemnized in heaven; it was not a personal whim but an explicit divine command to abolish the pagan taboo against marrying the ex-spouse of an adopted son. The explicit purpose is stated: “so that there would be no blame on the believers for marrying the ex-wives of their adopted sons after their divorce. ” The verse ends, “And Allah’s command is totally binding.” This was a watershed moment that liberated marriage from the false equivalence between adoptive and biological kinship, a reform that required the Prophet himself to be the example, however heavy it was.

=38=

This verse continues to comfort the Prophet and silence any criticism regarding the marriage. “There is no blame on the Prophet for doing what Allah has ordained for him.

” The Arabic haraj means difficulty, sin, or moral embarrassment. The verse declares him completely free of any moral culpability because the marriage was not his personal choice but an obligation from Allah. This shields the Prophet from the slander of the hypocrites who whispered that he had married his son’s wife.

The verse then broadens the perspective: “That has been the way of Allah with those prophets who had gone before.” The sunnah of Allah with previous prophets included commands that were unique to their missions, which might have appeared unusual or difficult for ordinary people. For instance, Prophet Ibrahim was commanded to sacrifice his son; Prophet Yusuf was given authority over the land of Egypt through a path of slavery and imprisonment; Prophet Musa’s life was filled with decrees that defied normal human logic. The Prophet Muhammad’s marriage to Zainab was part of this divine pattern.

The verse then seals this with “And Allah’s command has been firmly decreed.” The qadr maqdur conveys a decree that is precisely measured, unchangeable, and must come to pass. There is a profound reassurance here for the Prophet: the matter was settled in the eternal knowledge and will of Allah, so no amount of human criticism can alter its rightness.

For believers, this verse teaches that sometimes Allah’s commands will challenge deeply held cultural norms, and the faithful response is to remember that obedience is a liberation from the tyranny of human opinion. The Prophet’s personal difficulty in carrying out this command humanizes him and shows his profound sense of honour, while his ultimate obedience demonstrates the perfection of his servitude.

=39=

This verse elaborates on the “way of Allah with those prophets” mentioned in the previous verse, describing the essential characteristics of the men who carry the divine message. “Those who deliver the messages of Allah” are the prophets whose first and primary function is to convey exactly what Allah commands without addition, subtraction, or concealment. The Prophet Muhammad did precisely that regarding the marriage to Zainab: he delivered the message in action, even though it was personally difficult. “And consider Him” translates yakhshawnahu, which means to fear Allah with a fear born of profound reverence and knowledge, not a fear of a tyrant but awe of the Majestic. Their fear of Allah overrides every other fear, including the fear of people’s tongues.

The phrase “and none but Allah” emphasizes the exclusivity of this reverent fear. They do not fear any besides Allah, neither human critics nor powerful opponents. The life of every genuine prophet demonstrates this singular devotion.

The verse then concludes, “And sufficient is Allah as a vigilant Reckoner.” Hasib means one who keeps account, who will call to account. Allah’s reckoning is sufficient; the prophets do not need to worry about the judgment of people because Allah’s judgment is the only one that matters. He is fully aware of their intentions, their struggles, and their perfect obedience. For the believers, this verse provides a model of leadership and mission: one who speaks the truth of Allah must cultivate a heart that fears only Allah, delivers the message fearlessly, and leaves the outcome to the One who reckons all things with perfect justice and knowledge. It is a call to integrity and courage in religious work.

=40=

This verse delivers two interlocking declarations of enormous consequence. “Muhammad is not the father of any of your men.” His male children, such as Qasim, Ibrahim, and Tayyib, all died in infancy. None of his sons lived to manhood.

Thus he is not the biological father of any adult male among the believers. This statement directly undercuts any objection to his marriage to Zainab on the grounds that she had been married to Zaid, his adopted son. Since Zaid was not his biological son, there was no violation of the prohibition against marrying a son’s ex-wife. The verse makes the biological reality definitive. Then the verse pivots to the Prophet’s true, universal identity: “but is the Messenger of Allah and the seal of the prophets.” Khatam an-nabiyyin means he is the last of the prophets, the one with whom the long line of prophethood is sealed and completed. No prophet will come after him, and his message is the final, universal, and preserved guidance for humanity until the Day of Judgment.

This is a core article of Islamic faith. The combination is striking: his biological fatherhood ended with his deceased sons, but his spiritual fatherhood to the ummah is eternal through his prophethood. His legacy is not carried by a physical male heir but by the final revelation and his enduring example.

The verse ends, “And Allah has perfect knowledge of all things.” This reminds the believers that this arrangement—the absence of surviving male children, the finality of prophethood—was all within Allah’s infinite knowledge and wisdom. The believers are called to trust that Allah’s choices for His final messenger were the most perfect, and to accept that Muhammad’s status is not diminished by the lack of adult sons but rather magnified by his being the seal.
