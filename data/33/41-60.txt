=41=

This verse opens a new section that calls the believers to a life of constant devotion after the weighty revelations about the Prophet’s household and the finality of his prophethood. The address “O believers” links the instruction directly to iman, indicating that abundant remembrance of Allah is a fruit of true faith and a necessity for its preservation. “Always remember Allah often” translates the command dhikran kathira, an abundant, frequent, and heartfelt remembrance that goes far beyond occasional utterances.

In the context of the Battle of the Trench, this command is deeply significant. The believers had just passed through a trial that shook them violently, and they witnessed the hypocrites crumble because their hearts were empty of Allah’s remembrance. The antidote to fear, hypocrisy, and the temptations of worldly luxury that the Prophet’s wives were warned against is precisely this: filling the heart and tongue with the remembrance of Allah. Dhikr includes the recitation of Quran, the phrases of glorification, supplication, and any thought or word that brings Allah to mind. Scholars have explained that kathira means it should be done in all states—standing, sitting, lying down—and that the tongue should be moist with it. The Prophet himself exemplified this; Aisha reported that he used to remember Allah at all times. When the heart is occupied with Allah, there is little room for the whisperings of Satan, the love of the world, or the fear of people.

The verse also connects to the earlier description of the excellent example of the Prophet: “whoever has hope in Allah and the Last Day, and remembers Allah often.” Thus, the command to remember Allah often is not an isolated devotional tip but the engine that drives the entire spiritual life. It produces the inner mindfulness that enables one to obey the difficult commands, to withstand sieges, to give up worldly desires, and to follow the Prophet’s pattern without hesitation. It is the constant renewal of the covenant with Allah, keeping the believer anchored in the reality that every matter is in His hands.

=42=

This verse specifies a particularly powerful form of remembrance and anchors it in time. “And glorify Him morning and evening.” Tasbih, glorification, is to declare Allah free from any imperfection, to exalt Him above all that is not befitting His majesty. The specification of morning and evening provides a daily rhythm of devotion. The morning glorification, covering the time from dawn to sunrise, and the evening glorification, from late afternoon until night, frames the day with the purity of Allah’s transcendence. The human being enters the day with the declaration that Allah is free from the flaws that will be encountered in the world—the lies of hypocrites, the plots of enemies, the temptations of desire—and ends the day with the same declaration, cleansing the heart from the dust that accumulated.

This is not to the exclusion of remembrance at other times, but it establishes the minimum of a spiritual discipline. The specific mention of morning and evening is linked by many commentators to the prescribed prayers: the Fajr and Asr prayers, or the extended times of Subh and the evening adhkar. The Prophet taught many specific phrases for these times, such as “Subhan Allah wa bihamdihi” one hundred times, and the well-known morning and evening supplications that seek protection, express gratitude, and renew faith. These two times also correspond to the shifts of the angels, as recorded in authentic hadith, so that the servants’ glorification coincides with the gathering of the heavenly beings who carry their deeds upward. The spiritual benefit of this practice is immense; it cultivates a state of constant awareness that the day begins with Allah and ends with Allah, and that everything in between is under His watch. For the believers who had just been reminded of the Battle of the Trench, the command to glorify morning and evening was a prescription for maintaining the spiritual high that kept them firm during the siege, ensuring that the passage of time did not erode the lessons learned.

=43=

This verse reveals the divine source and purpose of the spiritual life commanded in the previous two verses. “He is the One Who showers His blessings upon you” uses the Arabic yusalli, the same root as salah, conveying the sense of Allah’s immense mercy, praise, and blessings descending upon the believers.

This is an extraordinary honour: the Lord of the worlds turns towards His servants with special grace. Then the verse adds an even more breathtaking reality: “and His angels pray for you. ” The angels, those pure and sinless beings who never disobey Allah, are engaged in continuous prayer for the believers. They ask for their forgiveness, their protection, and their elevation in ranks.

This is a cosmic support system that surrounds the faithful. The purpose of these divine and angelic blessings is then stated: “so that He may bring you out of darkness and into light. ” The darknesses (zulumat) include the darkness of ignorance, doubt, hypocrisy, sin, fear, and all forms of misguidance. The light (nur) is the light of faith, knowledge, peace, guidance, and ultimately the light of Paradise. The movement is from multiple layers of darkness into a singular, clear light. This process is ongoing throughout a believer’s life. Every act of dhikr, every obedience, every trial endured with patience is a step further out of darkness. The verse closes with “For He is ever Merciful to the believers.” The specific mercy to believers (rahim) is the mercy that delivers the best outcomes for those who respond to His guidance. It is a mercy that actively works to save them, purify them, and bring them into His light. This verse is a profound source of hope and motivation. It tells the believer that the struggle to remember Allah and live righteously is not a lonely uphill battle but one that is supported by the very Lord of the universe and His angelic hosts, all conspiring in mercy to guide the soul to eternal light.

=44=

After describing the process of being brought from darkness into light, this verse offers a glimpse of the culmination: the meeting with Allah. “Their greeting on the Day they meet Him will be, ‘Peace!’” The Day they meet Him is the Day of Judgment, when the believers will have the ultimate honour of the encounter with their Lord, an experience of joy and nearness that surpasses all other rewards. The greeting they receive is “Salam,” peace. This word, coming from Allah, is not a mere salutation but a declaration of safety, security, and complete well-being. It means they are safe from punishment, safe from fear, safe from any future harm. It is the peace of the eternal abode. This greeting will also be what they exchange among themselves in Paradise, but here the emphasis is on the greeting from Allah Himself, or the greeting that pervades that moment.

The verse also echoes the earlier description of Allah’s blessings and the angels’ prayers; now the believers are welcomed into the ultimate peace. “And He has prepared for them an honourable reward.” The reward is described as karim, honourable and generous, implying that it is not merely adequate but beyond what they could imagine, filled with dignity. This honourable reward includes the vision of Allah, which is the greatest bliss. The contrast with the hypocrites and disbelievers, who were promised humiliation and punishment, could not be sharper. The believers who stood firm in the trench, who chose Allah and the Hereafter, who remembered Allah often, will find their waiting ended with a peace that washes away every memory of earthly hardship. This verse serves as a powerful motivation, encouraging the believers to persevere through the restrictions, the battles, and the inner struggles, because the final meeting with Allah will be a moment of pure peace, crowned by the most generous and noble of rewards.

=45=

After the promises to the believers, the Quran again turns to address the Prophet directly, detailing his noble functions. “O Prophet! We have sent you as a witness, and a deliverer of good news, and a warner.” The title “Prophet” itself is a mark of honour.

The first role mentioned is “a witness” (shahid). The Prophet is a witness over his ummah in this life and on the Day of Judgment. He will testify to Allah that he conveyed the message, and he will testify concerning the deeds and faith of his people. His life, his character, and his judgments are a living witness to the truth.

The second role is “a deliverer of good news” (mubashshir). He brings the glad tidings of Allah’s mercy, forgiveness, and Paradise to those who believe and do righteous deeds. His message is fundamentally optimistic for those who turn to Allah; it is filled with hope and the promise of infinite joy.

The third role is “a warner” (nadhir). He warns of the consequences of disbelief and sin, the punishment in the grave and in Hellfire, so that people may be shaken from their heedlessness.

The pairing of good news and warning is a hallmark of the prophetic method; it balances hope and fear, ensuring that the heart neither becomes complacent nor despairs. These three functions encompass the entirety of the Prophet’s mission. Every verse he recited, every sermon he delivered, every letter he sent to kings was an expression of witnessing, giving glad tidings, or warning. The order is significant: he is first a witness for the truth itself, and from that foundation he offers the two-fold human response of hope and fear. For the believers, this verse reminds them that the Prophet is not merely a conveyor of information but a witness over them, which calls for a profound sense of accountability. His presence, and now his Sunnah, stands as the criterion against which all claims of faith are measured.

=46=

This verse adds two more profound dimensions to the Prophet’s mission. “And a caller to the Way of Allah by His command.

” The Arabic word da‘i means one who calls, invites, summons. The Prophet’s entire life was a call to Allah’s path—the path of submission, peace, and eternal salvation.

The phrase “by His command” indicates that this call is not a personal initiative but is carried out under direct divine authorization and in exact accordance with what Allah has ordered. The call is to the straight path, to the worship of the One God, to the purification of the soul, and to the establishment of justice. This calling is persistent, compassionate, and universal; the Prophet is commanded to invite with wisdom and beautiful preaching.

The second description is “and a beacon of light” (sirajan munira). A siraj is a lamp that gives light, often described as a brilliant, illuminating lamp. The addition of munira, meaning luminous or shedding light, emphasizes that the Prophet is not merely reflecting light from elsewhere but is himself a source of illumination for humanity. The light he brings is the Quran, the Sunnah, and the living example of his character. In a world darkened by shirk, ignorance, and oppression, he is the lamp that dispels that darkness. This metaphor connects beautifully with the earlier verse about Allah bringing believers out of darkness into light. The Prophet is the instrument of that light. The combination of caller and lamp means that the Prophet actively invites people to the light and is himself the very light by which they find their way. For the believers, this description deepens their love and reliance on the Prophet. He is not a distant figure but a radiant source of guidance, and his call continues through his message until the end of time. Every Muslim who calls others to Allah is inheriting a tiny fragment of this prophetic function, inviting towards the same light.

=47=

After detailing the Prophet’s roles, he is commanded to deliver a specific message of glad tidings. “Give good news to the believers that they will have a great bounty from Allah.

” The word fadl means bounty, grace, favour, something given abundantly without any corresponding obligation on the part of the recipient. The adjective “great” (kabir) magnifies the scale of this favour beyond normal imagination. This bounty includes all the rewards of Paradise—the gardens, the rivers, the pure spouses, the vision of Allah, and the eternal pleasure of the Lord—as well as the honour, victory, and guidance bestowed in this world. The verse is a direct injection of hope and optimism. The believers in Medina had endured years of hardship, poverty, and war. They were about to receive further instructions on marriage, modesty, and social conduct that would demand sacrifice of ego and desire. To sustain them, Allah instructs the Prophet to constantly reassure them of the immense favour awaiting them. This bounty is not because they earned it through their deeds alone, but because of Allah’s immense grace.

However, it is conditional on being true believers. The hypocrites, who lacked sincere iman, are excluded from this announcement. The psychological effect of this verse on the companions was profound; they knew that whatever they gave up for the sake of Allah, whatever restriction they accepted, whatever battle they fought, the divine bounty that awaited them was incomparably greater. For contemporary believers, the verse serves the same purpose. The life of faith is often a series of sacrifices, but the glad tidings of Allah’s great bounty should fill the heart with joy that overpowers any temporary difficulty. It shifts the focus from the pain of the present to the magnificence of the future, a hallmark of prophetic encouragement.

=48=

This verse echoes the very first command of the surah but adds a crucial element for the Prophet’s psychological state. “Do not yield to the disbelievers and the hypocrites” is the repeated core instruction: no compromise in the truth, no giving in to their pressure to modify the religion or to relax its moral demands. But then the verse adds, “Overlook their annoyances.” This means to ignore their hurts, their insults, their plots, and their provocations with a noble turning away, not allowing them to occupy mental space or to cause emotional disturbance.

The word adha covers all forms of verbal and psychological harm. The Prophet faced slander, mockery, covert schemes, and open hostility. Instead of being consumed by responding to every insult, he is told to overlook them. This does not mean there is no occasion for measured response or legal justice, but that his personal reaction should be one of majestic disregard. Then, “and put your trust in Allah” follows, completing the triad: do not yield, overlook harm, and trust in Allah. Trust in Allah is the active reliance that allows the overlooking to happen without inner turmoil. When one truly believes that Allah is the ultimate protector and judge, the stings of enemies become manageable.

The verse ends, “For Allah is sufficient as a Trustee of Affairs,” the same phrase that closed verse 3, tying the surah together with a unifying theme. The Prophet is being reminded that he does not have to manage every aspect of the conflict himself; Allah is managing the affair.

This is a powerful lesson in emotional resilience for all believers: the combination of unwavering principles, personal magnanimity, and deep trust in Allah forms a fortress that no enemy can penetrate. The Prophet’s life after this command showed its application: he forgave his most bitter enemies when Mecca was conquered, exemplifying the overlooking of personal injury while never yielding an inch on the truth.

=49=

The surah returns to legal rulings pertaining to marriage and divorce, continuing the reform of social norms. “O believers! If you marry believing women and then divorce them before you touch them…” The word “touch” is a respectful allusion to sexual intimacy.

This verse addresses the situation where a marriage contract has been concluded but the marriage has not been consummated. In such a case, “they will have no waiting period for you to count.” The waiting period (iddah) is normally three menstrual cycles for a divorced woman, designed to determine if she is pregnant and to provide a period for potential reconciliation. But when there has been no intimacy, there is no possibility of pregnancy, so an iddah is unnecessary. This ruling is a mercy and a practicality, freeing the woman from an unnecessary wait and allowing her to move on with her life immediately.

However, the verse immediately balances this legal ease with an ethical obligation: “so give them a suitable compensation, and let them go graciously.” The compensation (mata‘) is a parting gift, which the scholars have detailed as an appropriate sum or provision depending on the husband’s financial capacity. It is not the full dowry if one was specified but a portion of it, or a separate gift, as a gesture of goodwill and to compensate for the emotional distress and the social impact of a divorce.

The phrase “let them go graciously” (sarrahu hunna sarahan jamila) means a release that is beautiful, without abuse, public shaming, or withholding of rights. The emphasis is on the manner of the separation; Islam aims to infuse even the dissolution of a marriage with dignity and kindness. This verse, placed in the midst of directives about the Prophet’s household, reminds the ordinary believers that their marital affairs must also be conducted with the highest ethics. It prevents the misuse of the ruling of no waiting period to treat the woman callously and mandates a graceful, generous farewell.

=50=

This verse details the categories of women lawfully marriageable to the Prophet, some of which were exclusive to him. It begins with “your wives to whom you have paid their full dowries,” referring to his existing wives whom he had married and given their mahr. Next, “those bondwomen in your possession, whom Allah has granted you” includes captives of war who came under his ownership; marriage to a bondwoman after due process was a means of elevating her status.

The verse then lists a category of relatives: “the daughters of your paternal uncles and aunts, and the daughters of your maternal uncles and aunts, who have emigrated like you. ” These are cousins from both sides who had performed Hijrah, emphasizing that faith and migration created a special bond. Notably, the women of Quraysh who were not emigrants were not lawful to him because the disbelieving family ties did not constitute the same moral community. Then a unique provision: “a believing woman who offers herself to the Prophet without dowry if he is interested in marrying her.

” This is the woman who gifts herself in marriage, asking no worldly compensation, seeking only the honour of being the Prophet’s wife. This was permitted exclusively for the Prophet, as the verse emphasizes, “this is exclusively for you, not for the rest of the believers.” For ordinary believers, marriage requires a dowry and a formal contract; a woman cannot simply give herself. The Prophet, however, received this special dispensation, and he availed himself of it in some reports, though the specifics are discussed by scholars.

The verse then reassures: “We know well what rulings We have ordained for the believers in relation to their wives and those bondwomen in their possession. ” Allah is not imposing arbitrary laws; the general law for the community is already set and known to Him. The exclusive permissions for the Prophet are part of divine wisdom. “As such, there would be no blame on you” lifts any potential discomfort the Prophet might feel about special rules.

The verse ends with “And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful,” indicating that any past practices that needed to be aligned with this new clarification are covered by Allah’s forgiveness and mercy.

=51=

This verse addresses the Prophet’s relationship with his wives and provides a ruling that eased his domestic life. “It is up to you O Prophet to delay or receive whoever you please of your wives.

” The Arabic word turji means to postpone or set aside the turn of a wife, and tu’wi means to take back or receive. The Prophet had multiple wives, and Islamic law mandates equal division of nights and maintenance among them.

However, this verse gave the Prophet a special dispensation: he was not bound by the strict obligation of equal allocation if he chose to rearrange the schedule based on circumstances or to set aside a wife’s turn without divorcing her, while still maintaining their marital bond. “There is no blame on you if you call back any of those you have set aside” means he could later restore such a wife to the normal rotation without any sin or reproach. This flexibility was a mercy to the Prophet, whose duties of prophethood often made a rigid schedule extremely difficult. The verse explains the underlying wisdom: “That is more likely that they will be content, not grieved, and satisfied with what you offer them all. ” This arrangement, where the wives understood that the Prophet’s time was governed by revelation and necessity, would actually bring them greater peace of heart. It would prevent jealousy and grief because they knew that any delay was not a sign of disfavour but a divinely sanctioned flexibility, and they would be pleased with whatever amount of his company they received. “Allah fully knows what is in your hearts” assures the wives that Allah is aware of their love, their sacrifices, and their occasional human emotions. “And Allah is All-Knowing, Most Forbearing” serves as a gentle reminder that Allah is patient with their feelings, not punishing them for natural human sentiments, while also knowing their inner states perfectly. This verse, far from being a license for neglect, was a practical solution for a household under unique pressure, a regulation aimed at the contentment of all.

=52=

After granting the Prophet certain expansions in marriage, this verse imposes a significant restriction. “It is not lawful for you O Prophet to marry more women after this.” This effectively sealed the number of his wives; he was not to add any new wife through marriage, whether by contract or by the gift of a woman herself as previously permitted. The “after this” refers to this revealed ruling, which finalized his household.

The verse also forbids replacing wives: “nor can you replace any of your present wives with another, even if her beauty may attract you.” He could not divorce one wife to marry another, a practice that would cause deep emotional harm and was beneath the dignity of the Mothers of the Believers. Even if another woman’s beauty captivated him, the prohibition stood, emphasizing that the Prophet’s household was not based on fleeting desire but on divine selection and permanence. The wives he had at that moment were the ones Allah had chosen for him to remain with him for the rest of his life. The one exception is “except those bondwomen in your possession.” The permission to take bondwomen as concubines remained, as that was not a marriage that expanded the number of free wives or altered the status of the existing wives.

The verse ends, “And Allah is ever Watchful over all things.” Raqib means the One who watches with attentive care and complete awareness. Nothing escapes His observation.

This reminder is both a comfort and a caution: Allah watches over the Prophet’s compliance, and He also watches over the welfare of the wives, ensuring that no change occurs outside His wise decree. For the wives, this verse was a source of profound honour and security; they were the final, irreplaceable mothers of the believers, and their position was eternally sealed by revelation. It also demonstrates that the Prophet’s multiple marriages were not a matter of uncontrolled desire, as orientalists have claimed, but a divinely orchestrated program that had a defined conclusion.

=53=

This lengthy verse establishes the rules of etiquette for visiting the Prophet’s homes, revealed after the wedding feast of Zainab bint Jahsh, where some guests overstayed and engaged in prolonged conversation, causing discomfort to the Prophet. The verse begins: “O believers! Do not enter the homes of the Prophet without permission.” The foundation is that the prophetic household has a sanctity that requires explicit consent. The specific context of a meal invitation follows: “and if invited for a meal, do not come too early and linger until the meal is ready.” Some companions, in their eagerness, would arrive early and wait, which imposed on the household’s privacy. “But if you are invited, then enter on time” sets the proper protocol. After eating, the instruction is firm: “Once you have eaten, then go on your way, and do not stay for casual talk. ” The Arabic hadith indicates conversation after the meal, which often turned into lengthy sessions.

The verse then reveals the Prophet’s noble character: “Such behaviour is truly annoying to the Prophet, yet he is too shy to ask you to leave.” His hayaa (modesty and shyness) prevented him from directly telling guests to depart, so he would endure the inconvenience. “But Allah is never shy of the truth” means Allah will declare what is necessary for the Prophet’s comfort without hesitation. Then the verse introduces the rule of hijab: “And when you believers ask his wives for something, ask them from behind a barrier.” This screen was a physical partition. Communication was to be from behind it, preserving the dignity and privacy of the Mothers of the Believers. The reason is “This is purer for your hearts and theirs. ” It prevents any improper thoughts or glances, keeping both parties in a state of spiritual purity.

The verse then prohibits a major act: “And it is not right for you to annoy the Messenger of Allah, nor ever marry his wives after him. This would certainly be a major offence in the sight of Allah.” Marrying the Prophet’s widows is forever forbidden, as they are the mothers of the believers. This entire verse is a masterclass in social decorum, sensitivity to leadership, and the protection of private life.

=54=

This short verse functions as a powerful divine seal on the detailed instructions just given about entering homes, lingering after meals, and speaking to the Prophet’s wives from behind a barrier. “Whether you reveal something or conceal it” addresses both the outward behaviour and the hidden intentions of the believers. A person might follow the external rules of not overstaying and of speaking from behind a screen, yet inwardly harbour curiosity, annoyance, or a lack of respect. Others might comply outwardly but secretly think that these rules are too strict or unnecessary.

The verse reminds that Allah’s knowledge encompasses both the visible and the hidden. The hypocrites, in particular, might have outwardly obeyed while inwardly mocking the regulations, but nothing escapes Allah. “Surely Allah has perfect knowledge of all things.

” The word ‘alim is in the superlative form, emphasizing the completeness and perfection of His knowledge. This knowledge covers the past, present, and future, the apparent and the concealed, the words and the unspoken thoughts. This divine attribute serves multiple purposes in this context. It comforts the Prophet and his household, assuring them that Allah is fully aware of the discomfort they endured from the insensitive guests, and that He will deal with every intention. It acts as a deterrent for the believers, making them realize that the etiquette is not merely social convention but a matter of divine observation, and that their inner attitude of reverence is just as important as their outward compliance. It also reinforces the purpose of the barrier: purity of hearts. Hearts are the domain that Allah watches most closely.

Ultimately, the verse engrains the principle that the foundation of Islamic social ethics is the consciousness that Allah sees all; this awareness transforms manners from performance into worship.

=55=

After establishing the rule of the barrier for unrelated men, this verse clarifies the categories of people before whom the Prophet’s wives may appear without the full hijab screen, meaning they can be seen in a state of normal household dress. The list includes “their fathers,” the closest male guardians, “their sons,” which includes both biological sons and the sons of the Prophet from previous wives, “their brothers,” whether full or half-brothers, “their brothers’ sons” (nephews), “their sisters’ sons” (nephews), “their fellow Muslim women,” and “those bondspeople in their possession.” These are the close relatives and dependents who normally live in close proximity and for whom the strictest screening would be an impractical hardship.

The mention of “fellow Muslim women” clarifies that the screening is not required among women, though modesty remains. The term “those bondspeople in their possession” includes both male and female slaves who were part of the household, reflecting the social structure of the time; the scholars have discussed the extent to which male slaves were included, with the majority holding that it referred to those who were permanent household attendants with no sexual access and for whom the normal rules of hijab would be unduly restrictive.

The verse then directs the wives to the inner dimension: “And be mindful of Allah O wives of the Prophet! Surely Allah is a Witness over all things.” Even in the permitted presence of relatives, taqwa is paramount. The relaxation of the physical screen does not mean relaxation of modesty, dignity, and the consciousness that Allah is watching.

This reminder is crucial because social ease can sometimes lead to lowering of standards. The verse beautifully balances legal clarity with spiritual exhortation, teaching that the ultimate safeguard is not the physical barrier but the fear of Allah in the heart.

The closing statement, “Surely Allah is a Witness over all things,” reinforces that every glance, every word, and every interaction is observed by the Divine, ensuring that the Mothers of the Believers maintain the highest standard in all circumstances.

=56=

This is one of the most celebrated verses in the Quran, commanding the believers to send blessings and peace upon the Prophet. “Indeed, Allah showers His blessings upon the Prophet, and His angels pray for him.

” The Arabic yusallun denotes Allah’s salah as His mercy, honour, praise, and elevation of the Prophet’s mention, while the angels’ salah is their supplication for him, seeking Allah’s forgiveness and the raising of his ranks. This declaration elevates the Prophet’s status to an unimaginable height: the Lord of the Worlds Himself is constantly bestowing blessings upon him, and the heavenly host of angels is continuously engaged in praying for him.

This is an eternal, ongoing reality in the celestial realm. Then the verse turns to the believers: “O believers! Invoke Allah’s blessings upon him, and salute him with worthy greetings of peace.” The command “sallu ‘alayhi” means to ask Allah to bestow His blessings and mercy upon the Prophet, and “sallimu taslima” means to greet him with the salam, the prayer for his peace and security.

This verse establishes the obligatory and recommended practice of sending salawat. The companions asked the Prophet how to do it, and he taught them various formulations, the most famous being the Salawat Ibrahimiyya recited in the tashahhud of every prayer. The connection of this verse to the preceding context is profound. After the rules of visiting and the barrier, the believers are reminded that their relationship with the Prophet is not just one of formal etiquette but of love, honour, and continuous spiritual connection. The sending of salawat is a manifestation of that love and gratitude. It also counters any tendency that the strict rules of hijab might create a distance between the believers and the Prophet; the spiritual connection through salawat remains open and intensely encouraged. Every time a believer says “Allahumma salli ‘ala Muhammad,” they are participating in the divine and angelic honouring of the Prophet, and their own status is raised in return. The verse is a timeless invitation to fill one’s tongue and heart with this blessed invocation.

=57=

This verse issues a severe warning, forming a sharp contrast with the honour and blessings described for the believers who send salawat. “Surely those who offend Allah and His Messenger…” Offending Allah occurs through shirk, blasphemy, and disobedience to His commands. Offending the Messenger includes insulting him, slandering his wives, ignoring his Sunnah, and causing him any form of hurt. The verse declares a double consequence: “are condemned by Allah in this world and the Hereafter. ” The curse (la‘nah) means being expelled from Allah’s mercy, cut off from all that is good. In this world, it may manifest as disgrace, defeat, a hard heart, and a miserable life devoid of true peace.

In the Hereafter, it is eternal exclusion from Paradise. Then the verse adds, “And He has prepared for them a humiliating punishment. ” The word muhin emphasizes that the punishment is not only painful but also deeply degrading, stripping away every pretense of pride and honour they falsely claimed. This verse, in the context of the surah, is directly aimed at the hypocrites and disbelievers who had been slandering the Prophet, spreading rumours about his wives, or mocking the laws of Allah. Their offences were not minor disagreements but attacks on the very foundation of the faith. The severity of the warning underscores a fundamental principle: the honour of the Prophet is not a personal matter but is directly tied to the honour of the divine message. To attack the Messenger is to attack the One who sent him. The verse serves as a timeless deterrent, reminding all believers to guard their tongues and hearts from anything that could constitute even a slight offence to the Prophet, and to recognize that the consequences of such offences are devastating and eternal.

=58=

This verse extends the protection from offence specifically to the ordinary believers, having just spoken of offending Allah and His Messenger. “As for those who abuse believing men and women unjustifiably…” Abuse (adha) covers slander, backbiting, mockery, physical harm, and any form of wrongful hurt.

The word “unjustifiably” is crucial because it indicates that there are certain contexts in which a believer might be subject to legal punishment or criticism for a proven crime, but this verse addresses the baseless, malicious infliction of harm. The perpetrators “will definitely bear the guilt of slander and blatant sin. ” Buhtan is slander, a false accusation of enormous gravity, and ithm mubin is a sin that is clear and undeniable. The combination indicates that the abuser has committed a compounded crime: the specific harm against the victim and the open transgression against Allah’s law.

The placement of this verse immediately after the threat to those who offend the Prophet links the honour of the believers to the honour of the prophetic household. The community is being taught that just as the sanctity of the Prophet and his wives is divinely protected, so too the dignity of every believing man and woman is sacred. Slandering a believer, particularly the innocent and the chaste, is a direct challenge to the moral order Allah has established.

The verse also serves as a warning to those who were spreading rumours or abusing their tongues in Medina. It declares that such actions are not minor slips but buhtan, a calumny that carries its own heavy punishment. For all believers, this verse establishes the principle that the honour of a fellow Muslim is inviolable. The Prophet said that the blood, property, and honour of a Muslim are sacred. This Quranic foundation shapes Islamic ethics on speech and interpersonal conduct, making the protection of reputation a paramount moral duty, the violation of which is a manifest sin.

=59=

This verse, known as the verse of the jilbab, extends the principle of modest covering to all believing women, after the special rules for the Prophet’s wives. “O Prophet! Ask your wives, daughters, and believing women to draw their cloaks over their bodies.

” The word jalabib (plural of jilbab) refers to an outer garment, a loose covering that is draped over the body and covers the head and chest. The command is to “draw” (yudnina) this garment, meaning to let it down and wrap it such that it conceals the body’s shape and ornaments. This instruction was partly occasioned by the harassment that some hypocrites and immoral men in Medina were directing at women whom they claimed to mistake for slave-women or women of ill repute. The verse explains the purpose: “In this way it is more likely that they will be recognized as virtuous and not be harassed.” The jilbab serves as a mark of identity, distinguishing free, believing women from those who might be targeted. It signals to potential harassers that these are chaste, protected women, thereby deterring annoyance.

The phrase “recognized as virtuous” does not mean that a woman’s virtue is solely determined by a garment, but that in the social context of that time, this outward sign communicated a claim to modesty that decent people would respect. The verse concludes, “And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” This conclusion is profoundly comforting. It acknowledges that there may have been lapses in the past, women who did not cover properly out of ignorance or habit, but Allah’s forgiveness and mercy are open to them as they adopt this new standard. It prevents the ruling from being a source of despair for those who fell short before the command was known.

The verse establishes a general principle of female modesty that has been interpreted and applied in various cultural contexts, all aiming at the same goal: the protection of women’s dignity, the preservation of public morality, and the fostering of a society where people are judged by character rather than appearance.

The surah returns to the threat against the internal enemies of the Muslim community, specifically mentioning three categories of troublemakers. “If the hypocrites, and those with sickness in their hearts, and rumour-mongers in Medina do not desist…” The hypocrites are those who outwardly claimed faith but plotted against it. Those with sickness in their hearts are people with weak faith, lustful intentions, or a propensity to doubt and spread corruption. The rumour-mongers (murjifun) are those who spread false news, create panic, and destabilize the community by circulating alarming reports. All three groups were active in Medina, especially during the Battle of the Trench, and their harmful activities continued. The verse delivers an ultimatum: if they do not stop, “We will certainly incite you O Prophet against them.” Allah will command the Prophet to take decisive action. The specific consequence: “and then they will not be your neighbours there any longer. ” They will be expelled from Medina, unable to remain in the vicinity of the Prophet and the believers. This threat was later fulfilled; many hypocrites either repented, fled, or were eventually banished, and the Jewish tribes that had broken treaties were expelled. The verse demonstrates that while patience and overlooking were commanded for personal annoyances, the security and integrity of the community could not be endlessly compromised. There is a limit to tolerance for those actively working to undermine the social fabric. The threat also served as a test: it gave the hypocrites a chance to abandon their behaviour, and some scholars note that after this verse, some of them did cease their activities. The verse highlights the realism of the Quran in dealing with social ills: it combines spiritual guidance, moral exhortation, and, when necessary, the firm hand of authority to protect the believing community from internal sabotage.
