=41=

The fourth principle drawn from the scriptures of Moses and Abraham brings the sequence on accountability to its climax. After establishing that each soul bears only its own burden, that a person has only what they strive for, and that their striving will be seen, the verse now declares that they will be fully rewarded for it.

The Arabic expression "yujzahu al-jaza' al-awfa" conveys the idea of a recompense that is complete, undiminished, and most ample. Nothing will be deducted. No effort, no matter how small, will be overlooked or underpaid. On the Day of Judgment, the scales of divine justice will be set with absolute precision, and every soul will receive exactly what it is due, and for the righteous, even more, because Allah in His generosity multiplies the reward of good deeds manifold.

The word "awfa" is a superlative, indicating that the reward is not merely sufficient but is the fullest possible payment. This is the culmination of the moral logic of the scriptures: a world in which humans are morally accountable, where deeds are recorded and visible, must logically conclude with a day of complete recompense. Without this final reward, the entire moral structure of the universe would be incomplete. The righteous who suffered and strove would have their efforts go unanswered, and the wicked who oppressed and transgressed would escape without consequence. The full reward is the guarantee that the universe is fundamentally just, ruled by a Lord who is Al-Adl, the Just, and Ash-Shakur, the Appreciative.

For the believer, this verse is a wellspring of hope and motivation. It means that the patience exercised in the face of persecution, the quiet acts of charity that no one saw, the struggle against one's own desires, the tears shed in repentance, all of these are not lost in the void. They are being stored, accounted for, and will be returned in a form so beautiful and so complete that the believer will wish they had done even more.

The verse also carries a sobering reminder for those who do evil. Their recompense will also be complete. There will be no escape, no loophole, and no partial punishment. The divine justice is as perfect in its reward for good as it is in its punishment for evil.

This principle, inscribed in the earliest of scriptures, now stands as a pillar of the Quranic message, calling every soul to take its own striving seriously.

=42=

The fifth and final principle quoted from the scriptures of Moses and Abraham brings the entire sequence to its ultimate conclusion. Everything, every soul, every deed, every creation, is on a journey back to the Lord. The ultimate return, the final destination, the end of all paths, is to Allah alone.

The Arabic "al-muntaha" is the same word used in the phrase "Sidrat al-Muntaha," the Lote Tree of the Extreme Limit. Here, it refers to the ultimate end point of all existence, the final stop where all journeys terminate. There is no going past this point, no alternative destination, no other court of appeal. Every human being, regardless of their beliefs or their worldly status, is inexorably moving toward this single, universal return.

This principle anchors the entire moral teaching of the scriptures. If the return is to the Lord, then life has a direction and a purpose. It is not a random, circular motion of birth and death with no meaning. It is a linear journey from the Creator back to the Creator, a journey during which one is tested and given the opportunity to earn the best of returns.

The verse also refutes any notion that there are multiple gods each with their own domains, or that some souls might return to a different deity. The return is to your Lord, the one Lord who created, sustained, and guided. For the pagan Quraysh, who acknowledged Allah as the supreme Creator but also worshipped lesser deities, this verse was a powerful reassertion of tawhid. If the ultimate return is to Allah alone, then worship, devotion, and hope should be directed to Him alone. The idols have no role in the final destination. They cannot receive souls, judge them, or determine their fate. The principle also serves as a profound existential anchor for the believer. In moments of confusion, loss, or despair, the believer remembers that the journey ends with the Lord. This gives perspective to worldly suffering and joy alike. Both are temporary waystations on the path to the final return. The believer lives with the end in mind, and that end is a meeting with the Most Merciful, a homecoming to the One who has been watching over the journey all along.

=43=

Having concluded the quotation from the earlier scriptures, the surah now launches into a powerful series of declarations about Allah’s absolute power and sole dominion over every aspect of existence. The first of these declarations is that He is the One who brings about joy and sadness.

The Arabic words "adhaka" (He makes laugh) and "abka" (He makes weep) are used. These are simple, universal human experiences. Every human being laughs, and every human being weeps. Laughter is the expression of joy, relief, and delight. Weeping is the expression of sorrow, pain, and grief. Both are entirely in the hand of Allah. This does not mean that Allah directly causes every specific laugh or tear without any intermediate causes, but rather that He is the ultimate source and controller of the conditions that lead to these emotions. The person who laughs is laughing because of a situation that Allah, in His wisdom, brought about. The person who weeps is weeping because of a circumstance that Allah decreed. This attribution of laughter and weeping to Allah alone is a powerful antidote to the pagan mindset that attributed fortune and misfortune to various deities, stars, or spirits. The Quraysh would often credit their idols or fate for their joys and blame unseen forces for their sorrows. The verse brings both back under the sole sovereignty of Allah. It also has a deep spiritual and psychological impact.

For the believer, understanding that both laughter and tears come from Allah creates a state of equanimity. In joy, one is grateful to Allah and does not become arrogantly self-congratulatory. In sorrow, one is patient with Allah’s decree and does not fall into despair or resentment. The Prophet Muhammad taught that amazing is the affair of the believer, for all of it is good. If prosperity comes, he is grateful, and that is good for him. If adversity comes, he is patient, and that is good for him. This verse encapsulates that teaching at its root. The One who gives laughter is the same One who ordains tears. Both are from Him, and both are part of the human journey toward the ultimate return. The believer accepts both with a heart surrendered to the Lord of both laughter and tears.

=44=

The second declaration in this series of divine attributes moves from the emotional experiences of laughter and tears to the most fundamental realities of existence: life and death. Allah is the One who gives life and causes death.

This is a foundational belief in Islam, repeated throughout the Quran. Life is not a random accident of nature, nor is it a property inherent in matter. It is a gift bestowed by the Creator. Every living being, from the smallest microbe to the largest galaxy, exists because Allah gave it life. Similarly, death is not the mere cessation of biological functions. It is an active decree from Allah, a taking back of the life that He granted. The angel of death and his assistants are the executors of this divine command.

The pairing of life and death in this verse, following laughter and tears, creates a comprehensive picture of the human experience under divine control. The things that matter most to human beings, their joys, their sorrows, their very existence and its termination, are all in the hand of Allah. For the pagan Arabs, death was often viewed with a sense of finality and despair. They lived for this world, and the idea of resurrection seemed absurd to them. By emphasising that Allah is the One who causes death, the verse implicitly prepares the mind for the next declaration: He is also the One who will bring about re-creation. The One who gave life the first time and then caused death is perfectly capable of giving life again.

The phrase also serves as a reminder of human powerlessness. No matter how wealthy, strong, or intelligent a person may be, they cannot give life to a single soul, and they cannot prevent their own death when its appointed time arrives. The Quraysh leaders who arrogantly opposed the Prophet were utterly unable to control these two fundamental events. The verse thus humbles the arrogant and comforts the oppressed. The oppressor’s power does not extend to life and death; the victim’s weakness does not determine their ultimate fate. The Giver of life and the Causer of death is the true Sovereign, and to Him all will return.

=45=

The series of divine declarations now turns to the creation of the sexes. Allah states that He created the pairs, the male and the female. This brief statement is packed with profound significance. The existence of two complementary sexes is not a random evolutionary development; it is a deliberate act of creation by the All-Wise.

The pairing of male and female is a fundamental principle that pervades all of life, and as the Quran mentions elsewhere, even things that humans might not perceive. The mention of this specific creation here serves multiple purposes in the surah's argument. First, it continues the theme of Allah's exclusive creative power. The pagans acknowledged Allah as the Creator, and this verse reminds them of a creation that they witness every day, the birth of sons and daughters. Second, it subtly refutes their false beliefs about gender and divinity. Earlier in the surah, the pagans were rebuked for preferring sons for themselves while attributing daughters to Allah in the form of angels. Now, the verse reminds them that the very concept of male and female is a creation of Allah. He is the one who originated these categories. How then can they assign to Him a category that is part of His creation? The Creator is not subject to the characteristics of His creatures. Third, the creation of pairs is a sign that points to the Hereafter. Just as the male and female are created from a single essence and then made into complementary pairs for the continuation of life in this world, Allah will recreate humanity in the Hereafter.

The pairing is a sign of the design and purpose inherent in creation. Nothing is created without a mate, and the ultimate mate of the righteous soul is the bliss of Paradise. The verse is a call to reflect on the wonder of gender, the love and mercy that Allah places between the sexes, and the fact that this beautiful system is the work of the One who will also bring about the resurrection. The believer looks at the creation of male and female and sees a sign of the Creator's wisdom and a promise of His ability to recreate.

=46=

This verse specifies the humble origin of the human being, despite the grandeur of the creation of pairs. The male and the female, with all their complexity, their intellect, their emotions, and their physical capabilities, originate from a tiny, seemingly insignificant fluid.

The Arabic "nutfah" refers to a drop of sperm, a minute quantity of liquid that is emitted during the marital act. This image is deliberately unglamorous. It strips away human arrogance. The Quraysh leaders who strutted with pride, the kings who thought themselves divine, the philosophers who denied the resurrection, all began their existence as a drop of despised fluid. The Quran repeatedly draws attention to this origin to humble the human being. If this is your beginning, what is the basis for your arrogance?

The verse also serves as a powerful argument for the resurrection. The One who created a full, complex human being from a single drop of sperm is surely capable of recreating that human being after death.

In fact, the initial creation from a drop is, to human perception, more astonishing than a re-creation from bones and dust. The drop has no bones, no organs, no brain, no consciousness.

Yet from it emerges a being that can see, hear, think, and challenge the Creator. If Allah can program all of that biological and spiritual complexity into a drop of fluid, why would He be unable to repeat the process? The pagans denied the resurrection, and this verse is one of the many Quranic arguments that refute their denial by pointing to the observable miracle of birth. Every child born is a walking proof of the Creator's power to bring life from what is lifeless. The sperm-drop, when emitted, is not even visible to the naked eye in its individual form.

Yet it contains the blueprint for a unique human soul. The verse invites the listener to move from the evidence of the first creation to the certainty of the second, a logical move that only a heedless heart can refuse to make.

=47=

This verse is the explicit conclusion of the argument implied in the previous verses. Having described the initial creation from a sperm-drop, the Quran now declares that the re-creation, the resurrection, is an obligation upon Allah.

The Arabic phrase "inna 'alayna" means that it is upon Us, it is incumbent upon Us, it is a binding commitment that Allah has taken upon Himself. This is a remarkable expression. Allah, the absolutely sovereign and independent, is not obligated by anything external to Himself.

Yet, He has chosen, out of His own wisdom, justice, and mercy, to bind Himself to the promise of resurrection. This is a guarantee of the highest order. The One who never breaks His word has declared that the re-creation is upon Him.

The word "nash'ah al-ukhra" refers to the second genesis, the other bringing-forth, the resurrection that will occur on the Day of Judgment. It is the recreation of the human being after death, the reassembling of the bones, the restoring of the flesh, and the returning of the soul. For the human mind, this re-creation seems impossible. Once a body has decomposed and its particles have scattered, how can it be brought back? The Quran answers that the re-creation is not a problem for the One who created from a drop.

In fact, many scholars have noted that the second creation is, in one sense, easier than the first, although nothing is difficult for Allah. The first creation was from nothing, or from a simple fluid.

The second creation is a reassembly of materials that already existed. The verse is a direct rebuttal to the repeated question of the pagans: "Who will revive these bones after they have decayed?

" The answer is: the One who created them in the first place. And He has taken it upon Himself as a promise. This verse should instil in the believer a profound sense of certainty and hope. The resurrection is not a vague possibility. It is a divine commitment. The believer can live their life knowing that every sacrifice, every injustice suffered, and every good deed done is headed toward a moment of ultimate recompense. The re-creation is the door to the final justice and eternal bliss.

=48=

The series of declarations continues, returning to the practical realities of human life. Allah is the One who enriches and who impoverishes.

The Arabic "aghna" means to make self-sufficient, to grant wealth and abundance, to free from need. "Aqna" means to make content with little, or to cause poverty and dependence, to give just enough to get by. Together, they cover the entire spectrum of financial and material states. Every human being experiences either wealth or poverty, or fluctuates between the two. The verse ascribes both conditions directly to Allah. This was a direct challenge to the Quraysh, who prided themselves on their wealth and attributed it to their own shrewdness, their idols' favour, or the fortunes of trade. The verse tells them that their wealth is not self-generated. It is a gift from Allah, and He can take it away as easily as He gave it. Similarly, poverty is not a sign of divine disfavour or a cause for shame. It is a state decreed by Allah for reasons of wisdom and testing.

This teaching has profound social and spiritual implications. For the wealthy, it is a call to humility and gratitude. The wealth is from Allah, so it should be spent in ways that please Him. Arrogance and hoarding are absurd responses to a gift. For the poor, it is a call to patience and dignity. Their poverty is not a stain on their worth. The Lord who gives abundantly to some and sparingly to others is the same Lord, and He knows what is best for each soul.

The verse also destroys the materialistic worldview that measures human value by net worth. If Allah is the One who enriches and impoverishes, then a person's true worth is not determined by their bank balance but by their taqwa, their God-consciousness. The believer neither envies the rich nor despises the poor. Both states are tests from the same Lord. The verse is a powerful reminder of the transience of worldly wealth and the absolute sovereignty of Allah over the economic affairs of humanity. It encourages the believer to seek provision from Allah, to work hard, but to place ultimate trust not in the bank account but in the Provider who can give and withhold as He wills.

=49=

This verse singles out a specific celestial object for mention: Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky. In Arabic, it is known as "ash-Shi'ra." The pagan Arabs, particularly the tribe of Himyar or Khuza'ah, revered Sirius and worshipped it. They believed that this brilliant star influenced their fortunes, brought rain, and held power over their lives. Some offered sacrifices to it. The verse comes as a direct refutation. Allah alone is the Lord of Sirius. The star that you worship is itself a creature, owned and controlled by Allah. It has no power, no divinity, and no authority. It rises and sets by the command of its Lord. By asserting His lordship over Sirius, Allah is declaring that even the most magnificent object the pagans deified is nothing but a subject in His kingdom. This verse is a masterstroke of argumentation. The Quraysh were deeply impressed by the stars and planets. They used them for navigation, regulated their calendar by them, and attributed supernatural powers to them. Instead of denying the beauty or the utility of Sirius, the Quran reclaims it. Sirius is real, but it is not a god. It is a sign of the true God. The verse demonstrates that Islam does not require the denial of the natural world's wonders but their correct attribution to the Creator. The believer looks at Sirius and says, "Glory be to the One who created that magnificent star." The pagan looks at Sirius and worships the star itself. The difference is the difference between guidance and misguidance.

The mention of Sirius also serves as a specific example of the general principle that Allah is the Lord of all that they worship besides Him. By naming the most prominent star deity, the verse implicitly refutes the worship of all celestial bodies, and indeed all creation. The Lord of Sirius is the Lord of the heavens, the earth, and everything in between. This verse, like the earlier ones in this passage, builds the case for tawhid by demonstrating Allah's absolute ownership and control over every facet of existence, from the microscopic sperm-drop to the macrocosmic star. There is no room left for any partner.

=50=

The surah now shifts from the signs of Allah's power in creation to the signs of His power in destruction. The historical examples of destroyed nations serve as warnings.

The first example is 'Ad, described as the first people of 'Ad, or 'Ad the ancient. 'Ad were the people of the prophet Hud. They were a mighty civilization, renowned for their physical stature, their architectural prowess with lofty pillars, and their power. They lived after the flood of Noah. But they became arrogant, worshipped idols, and rejected Hud's call to monotheism. So Allah destroyed them with a devastating, barren wind that raged for seven nights and eight days, as described in other surahs. The verse states bluntly that He destroyed them. The destruction is attributed directly to Allah, emphasising that it was not a natural disaster in the impersonal sense but a deliberate act of divine punishment. The 'Ad were not killed by a random weather event; they were annihilated by the Lord of Sirius, the Lord of life and death, as a consequence of their transgression.

The mention of the first 'Ad distinguishes them from any later tribe that might have adopted the name or been confused with them. It also indicates that their punishment was a historical event, not a myth. The Quraysh knew of 'Ad through oral tradition and the visible ruins of their civilization. By recalling 'Ad, the surah is warning the Meccans that the same Lord who destroyed those mighty people can and will destroy them if they persist in their disbelief. The power of 'Ad did not save them. Their tall buildings, their strong bodies, their advanced society, all were reduced to rubble. The Quraysh, who prided themselves on their own strength and prosperity, are being told that they are not more powerful than 'Ad, and if 'Ad could not withstand the divine punishment, neither can they. The verse is a sobering reminder that the rise and fall of civilizations is not random. It is in the hand of the Lord who enriches and impoverishes, who gives life and causes death.

=51=

The second example of destruction follows immediately: Thamud. Thamud were the people of the prophet Salih. They succeeded 'Ad in the land, inheriting their civilization and building their own impressive structures, carving homes out of the mountains. They were given a clear sign, the miraculous she-camel that emerged from the rock, but they arrogantly defied Salih, hamstrung the camel, and challenged the punishment to come. So Allah destroyed them with a thunderous blast, an earthquake, or a shriek from the sky.

The verse adds the chilling phrase "sparing no one." The Arabic "fa ma abqa" means that the destruction was total. Not a single one of the disbelievers was left alive. The punishment did not miss anyone. It was comprehensive and absolute.

This detail is crucial because it underlines the precision and thoroughness of divine justice. The previous surah, At-Tur, mentioned that the deniers would face a Day when their scheming would be of no benefit and they would not be helped. The destruction of Thamud is a historical preview of that Day. When the divine command came, there was no escape. No one was spared because of their wealth, their status, or their cunning. The tribe was completely erased. This stands in stark contrast to the salvation of Salih and the believers, who were saved by Allah's mercy. The verse thus reinforces the message that when the punishment of the Lord comes, none can avert it. For the Quraysh, Thamud was an even more familiar tale, as their ruins were located in the Arabian Peninsula, in the region of Al-Hijr, which the Meccan caravans passed by on their way to Syria. They could see the empty, carved dwellings with their own eyes. The verse is saying: you pass by the ruins of Thamud, you see their empty homes, and you know what happened to them.

Yet you continue in the same arrogance? The total annihilation of Thamud is a warning that the delay of punishment is not a sign of safety. When it arrives, it may leave nothing behind. The believer should reflect on these ruins and renew their commitment to obedience before a day comes that spares no one.

=52=

The historical survey moves further back in time to the people of Noah. They were destroyed even before 'Ad and Thamud. The flood of Noah is the original archetype of divine punishment for a rebellious humanity. The verse characterises them as truly far worse in wrongdoing and transgression.

The Arabic "azlam" and "atgha" are superlatives, meaning they were more unjust and more excessively rebellious than the later nations. This is saying something, considering how wicked 'Ad and Thamud were. But the people of Noah had the unique distinction of rejecting a prophet who called them for nine hundred and fifty years. Their persistence in disbelief over such an immense span of time, their mockery of Noah as he built the ark, and their utter refusal to heed any warning placed them in a category of their own in terms of transgression.

The mention of Noah's people serves multiple purposes. First, it completes the historical cycle of destruction, showing that no era is immune.

The first great human civilisation after Adam was destroyed for its sins. Second, it demonstrates that the length of respite is not a sign of divine approval. Noah's people were given centuries, far more time than 'Ad or Thamud, but their ultimate fate was the same drowning. Third, the description of their being "far worse" implies that the Quraysh, though deeply transgressing, were not yet as bad as the people of Noah.

This is a subtle indication that the door of repentance is still open for them. If even Noah's people were given such a long chance, the Quraysh should seize the opportunity they have while the Prophet is among them.

The verse also highlights the consistency of the divine law. The Lord who destroyed the later nations is the same Lord who destroyed the earlier one. The pattern is unbroken. The surah is building a case that the power of Allah is not limited to creation; it is equally manifest in the punishment of the wicked.

For the believer, this history is not just information; it is a warning and a lesson that strengthens the heart's fear and hope.

=53=

The fourth example of divine destruction is the most physically dramatic: the overturning of the cities of Lot's people. The Arabic "al-mu'tafikah" refers to the cities that were flipped, turned upside down. This refers to Sodom and Gomorrah, the cities where the prophet Lot was sent. Their sin was unique in its depravity, and their punishment was uniquely matched to their crime. They were destroyed by a rain of stones of baked clay, and the entire region was physically upended. The archangel Gabriel raised the cities on his wing and hurled them down, turning the land inside out. The Dead Sea area, with its strange geological features, is believed to be the site of this catastrophe. By attributing this overturning directly to Himself, Allah is stating that the event was not a natural earthquake but a targeted act of divine judgment. The verb "hawat" means He hurled them down or turned them over. The destruction of Lot's people is frequently mentioned in the Quran as a sign for those who reflect. The Quraysh passed by these ruins on their trade journeys as well. The overturned cities were a visible, undeniable testimony to the fate of a people who defied their prophet and indulged in sins that violated the natural order.

The placement of this example after 'Ad, Thamud, and Noah creates a comprehensive list of different forms of punishment: wind, blast, flood, and now geological upheaval. The variety of the punishments demonstrates that Allah's arsenal is not limited. He can strike from the sky, from the earth, from the sea, or from within the earth itself. No environment is safe from His reach. The verse is a powerful reminder that the moral laws are as inviolable as the physical laws. Just as the cities were turned upside down physically, their moral order had been inverted, with evil becoming normal and good being shunned. The physical inversion was the outward manifestation of their spiritual inversion. The believer is reminded that societies that invert the natural order ultimately face an inversion of their own security.

=54=

This verse is a final exclamatory comment on the destruction of Lot's people. The Arabic "ghashshaha ma ghashsha" is a rhetorical device that creates a sense of awe and terror by leaving the exact description vague. "It covered them, that which covered them." What was it that covered them? It was a rain of stones, an overwhelming punishment, a cover of destruction that enveloped them completely and from which there was no escape. The vagueness adds to the horror. The listener's imagination fills in the magnitude of the calamity. The verb "ghashsha" means to cover, to envelop, to overspread. The punishment was not a glancing blow; it was a total submersion.

The use of this rhetorical style, where a word is repeated in a different form to magnify the meaning, is a feature of eloquent Arabic. It is as if the speaker is so overwhelmed by the magnitude of the event that precise description fails. The verse conveys the sense of a catastrophe so immense that words cannot do it justice. This comment functions as a pause, a moment for the listener to absorb the sheer terror of what happened to those cities. It is a warning to the Quraysh that the punishment they mockingly ask to be hastened is not a trivial matter. It is an overwhelming horror.

The verse also echoes the description of the Lote Tree being overwhelmed with heavenly splendours earlier in the surah. There, the covering was of light and beauty. Here, the covering is of darkness and destruction. The same Lord who covers the tree with light covers the sinful cities with ruin. The contrast could not be starker.

For the believer, this verse evokes a healthy fear of Allah's punishment and a deep gratitude for the mercy that has protected them from such a fate. It also inspires reflection on the transience of worldly power and the suddenness with which divine judgment can descend. The people of Lot woke up that morning going about their daily lives, and by the end of the day, they were covered by an overwhelming destruction. The believer should never feel secure from the plan of Allah, but should always live in a state of humble watchfulness.

=55=

After the lengthy recitation of Allah's signs in creation and His acts of punishment in history, the surah turns directly to the human being with a challenging question. "Now, which of your Lord's favours will you dispute?" The Arabic word "ala'" refers to the favours, blessings, and signs of the Lord. This question implies that everything that has been mentioned, the creation of the heavens and the earth, the bringing of laughter and tears, life and death, the creation of pairs from a sperm-drop, the promise of re-creation, the enrichment and impoverishment, the lordship over Sirius, and the historical examples of destroyed nations, are all among the favours and signs of the Lord. They are favours because they guide to the truth, warn against destruction, and call to eternal bliss. They are signs because they point unmistakably to the power, wisdom, and justice of Allah.

The question challenges the denier to identify which of these countless signs and favours they can reasonably dispute. Can they dispute the reality of life and death? Can they dispute the miracle of their own creation from a sperm-drop? Can they dispute the historical ruins of 'Ad and Thamud that they can see with their own eyes?

The question is rhetorical because the implied answer is that there is nothing to dispute. The signs are clear, the favours are undeniable. The only reason to dispute them is arrogance and obstinacy, which the surah has already diagnosed. This verse serves as a powerful conclusion to the argumentative section of the surah. It places the full weight of the evidence before the listener and asks them to be honest. The favours of the Lord include the revelation itself, the Quran, and the prophethood of Muhammad. To dispute any of these favours is to dispute the truth itself. The believer, upon reading this verse, should respond with gratitude and affirmation, acknowledging that the favours of the Lord are beyond count and beyond dispute. The denier is left speechless, with no rational ground to stand on, only the stubbornness of the heart. The verse marks a transition point, after which the surah will issue its final warnings and calls to action.

=56=

Having presented the historical examples of destroyed nations, each of whom had a warner sent to them, the surah now explicitly states that the Prophet Muhammad is a warner just like the warners of old. The Arabic "nadhir" is a warner, someone who alerts his people to an impending danger and calls them to safety. The verse connects Muhammad directly to the long chain of prophets mentioned earlier: Noah, Hud, Salih, Lot, and by extension Moses and Abraham. He is not an anomaly. He is not a madman or a poet or a fortune-teller. He is a warner, fulfilling the same function as his noble predecessors.

This statement serves multiple purposes. First, it normalises the Prophet's mission. The Quraysh should not be surprised that a warner has come to them.

This is how Allah has always dealt with humanity. Every nation that was destroyed had first been sent a warner. The sending of Muhammad is a mercy, not a strange occurrence. Second, it authenticates his message. He is bringing the same fundamental warning that every prophet brought: worship Allah alone, do good, and prepare for the Day of Judgment. The consistency of the message across millennia is proof of its divine origin. Third, it is a threat.

The earlier warners were rejected, and then their people were destroyed. The Quraysh are now following the exact same pattern. They have received their warner. Their response so far has been rejection and mockery. The implication is clear: if they do not change their ways, they will follow the fate of the destroyed nations. The verse is both a comfort to the Prophet and a warning to his people. To the Prophet, it says: you are not alone; you are part of a blessed brotherhood of warners. To the people, it says: the clock is ticking, and the warner is among you. The opportunity to heed the warning is now. Once the punishment arrives, the warning will be of no benefit. The verse thus encapsulates the entire message of the surah in a single, powerful sentence: the Prophet is a warner like the warners who came before, so take heed before it is too late.

=57=

The warning becomes even more urgent with this verse. The Hour, the Day of Judgment, has drawn near.

The Arabic "azifati al-azifah" is another example of the emphatic rhetorical style seen earlier. "The approaching has approached." The use of the same root in different forms intensifies the meaning. The Hour is not a distant, abstract event. It is imminent, advancing, drawing ever closer. From the perspective of the Quran, the Day of Judgment is always near, because the lifespan of humanity compared to the eternal life of the Hereafter is like a single hour.

Moreover, the coming of the Prophet Muhammad is itself a sign of the approaching Hour, as he is the final messenger. His advent signals that the history of divine revelation is reaching its climax and that the final act of the human drama is about to begin. The Quraysh used to mock the idea of the Hour, asking when it would come, as if its delay was proof of its falsehood. This verse answers that it is not delayed in any meaningful sense. It is drawing near, and every passing day brings it closer. The certainty of its approach is stated with absolute emphasis.

For the believer, this verse is a call to constant readiness. The nearness of the Hour means that one's own death, which is one's personal hour, is also near. No one knows how much time they have left. The wise person prepares for the journey now, not later. For the denier, the verse is a stark warning that the thing they treat as a joke is actually rushing toward them. The approaching Hour will change everything, making all their worldly pursuits and mockery utterly irrelevant. The verse is one of many in the Quran that seek to shake the human being out of the complacency of daily life. The commercial transactions, the family gatherings, the political ambitions, all will be interrupted by the sudden arrival of the Hour. The only ones who will benefit are those who heeded the warning of the warner when there was still time. The verse is short, but its impact on a receptive heart is profound, creating a sense of urgency that transforms how one lives each day.

=58=

This verse continues on the theme of the Hour by stating a crucial truth: no one except Allah can reveal it, remove its cover, or cause it to come forth. The Arabic "kashifah" comes from kashf, meaning to uncover, to disclose, to expose. The Hour is currently hidden, concealed behind the veils of the unseen. Its exact timing is known only to Allah. No angel, no prophet, no saint has the power to bring it about or to reveal its date.

This is a direct response to the pagans who would mockingly ask, "When is this promise, if you are truthful?" The verse tells them that the question itself is misplaced. The timing is not something the Prophet knows or can be compelled to reveal. The Hour belongs exclusively to the domain of divine knowledge and divine power. Only Allah can disclose it. This exclusivity serves several purposes. It reaffirms the absolute sovereignty of Allah over the future. It prevents the believers from becoming obsessed with predicting the exact date, a practice that leads to error and distraction from the more important task of preparation. It also preserves the element of suddenness and surprise that is integral to the Hour's nature. If the date were known, people could plan their repentance for the last moment, which would defeat the purpose of the test. The sudden disclosure of the Hour is a key element of its impact. The verse is a profound lesson in humility and the limits of human knowledge. The Prophet, despite his intimate connection to the divine, was not given knowledge of the Hour's timing. When the angel Gabriel asked him about the Hour, he replied that the one questioned knows no more than the questioner. This teaches the believers that some things are simply not for us to know. Our task is not to speculate about the date but to live each day as if it might be our last, in a state of preparedness. The concealment of the Hour is a mercy in itself, for it keeps the believer vigilant. The verse, in its brevity, reorients the mind from curiosity about timing to the certainty and suddenness of the event itself.

=59=

The surah now addresses the reaction of the deniers to the Quran itself. The verse asks a rhetorical question: "Do you find this revelation astonishing?

" The Arabic "hadith" refers to the discourse, the narrative, the revelation that is being recited. The Quraysh heard the Quran, with its warnings of resurrection, its stories of destroyed nations, its vivid descriptions of Paradise and Hell, and its eloquent, powerful language. Instead of being moved to faith, they found it astonishing in a negative sense. They marvelled at it as something strange, unbelievable, and outlandish. They said it was magic, poetry, or the ravings of a madman.

The question exposes their reaction as inappropriate and foolish. What is truly astonishing is not the Quran but their refusal to accept it. The Quran's message is not strange; it is the same message that all the prophets brought. Its descriptions of the Hereafter are not fanciful; they are the truth from the One who created the heavens and the earth. Its moral teachings are not extreme; they are the natural law of human flourishing. So why do they find it astonishing?

The answer lies not in the Quran but in their own hardened hearts. A person whose heart is covered by heedlessness finds the truth strange, while falsehood seems normal. The verse is a mirror held up to the deniers, asking them to reflect on their own reaction. The believer, in contrast, finds the revelation deeply familiar, a reminder to the soul of the covenant it made with its Lord before birth. The Quran is the most natural and fitting message for the human heart.

The verse also serves as a transition to the final, devastating description of the deniers' behaviour in the next verse. Their astonishment is not one of respectful wonder; it is a dismissive astonishment that quickly turns into mockery. The surah is building toward its concluding command, which will be a call to prostrate and worship. Before that call, the surah diagnoses the sickness so that the cure can be sought.

The question "Do you find this revelation astonishing?" hangs in the air, unanswered by the deniers because their only honest answer would condemn them.

=60=

This verse describes the actual behaviour of the deniers in response to the revelation. They laugh at it, and they do not weep. The contrast between laughter and weeping is deliberate and echoes the earlier verse that stated Allah is the One who brings about laughter and tears. Here, the deniers are using the faculty of laughter in the wrong way. They are not laughing from joy or from a sense of healthy humour. They are laughing mockingly at the truth, scoffing at the verses of Allah. The Quran that should make hearts tremble with awe instead becomes the object of their ridicule.

This is a profound inversion of the proper human response to the divine word. The appropriate reaction to the warnings of the Quran is to weep, to shed tears of fear and remorse, to be moved by the gravity of the message. The deniers, however, are dry-eyed and laughing. The verse does not just say they laugh; it says they do not weep. The absence of weeping is highlighted. This absence is the sign of a heart that has become stone-like. The Prophet and the early believers would weep when the Quran was recited. Their hearts were soft, and the words of their Lord penetrated deeply. The deniers' lack of tears is a symptom of their spiritual death. This verse is a powerful diagnostic tool for every believer. When we listen to the Quran, what is our reaction? Do we feel a desire to laugh it off, to make light of its demands, to find it overly strict or fantastical? Or do we feel the prick of tears, the tightening of the heart, the sense of awe that leads to reverence and obedience? The laughing of the deniers is a defence mechanism. By turning the warning into a joke, they protect themselves from having to take it seriously. If it is just a joke, then there is no need to change their lives. The verse exposes this psychological game for what it is: a tragic waste of the opportunity to be saved. The laughter of the deniers will be short-lived. The surah is about to describe their heedlessness and then command the believers to a completely different posture: prostration and worship.
