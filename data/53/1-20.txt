=1=

The surah opens with a solemn oath sworn by the stars as they fade and disappear. This is one of the most evocative oaths in the Quran, connecting the ending of the previous surah, At-Tur, which concluded with the command to glorify Allah at the fading of the stars. Here, Allah swears by that very moment, the time just before dawn when the stars that had illuminated the night sky begin to dim and vanish. The oath draws attention to a daily cosmic phenomenon that is both beautiful and deeply symbolic. The fading of the stars represents the end of darkness and the approach of light. It is a moment of transition, a sign that the reign of the night is over and the sun is about to rise. In the spiritual realm, this mirrors the coming of the Prophet Muhammad, whose message of truth arrived after a long night of ignorance and idolatry. The stars that fade can also symbolise the passing away of false gods, false ideas, and the powers of darkness when the light of divine revelation shines. An oath in the Quran always serves to emphasise the importance and certainty of what follows. By swearing by the fading stars, Allah is preparing the listener for a declaration of immense weight, which in this surah includes the truthfulness of the Prophet's vision, the reality of the Night Journey, and the absolute falsehood of idol worship. The stars were objects of wonder and even worship in pre-Islamic Arabia. Some tribes revered the stars and believed they influenced human destiny. By swearing by the stars and immediately pointing to their fading, the Quran subtly dethrones these objects of worship. The stars are not eternal deities; they are created things that fade and disappear by the command of their Creator. The oath also evokes a sense of melancholy beauty, the quiet hour before dawn when the world is still and the heavens declare the glory of God. It invites the listener to reflect on the order of the cosmos and the One who set it in motion. The surah will soon recount the Prophet's ascent through the heavens, and this opening oath by the fading stars sets the celestial stage for that extraordinary journey, anchoring the entire discourse in the majesty of the created universe.

=2=

After the oath by the fading stars, the surah delivers its central opening declaration. The Prophet is described as "your fellow man," a translation of the Arabic "sahibukum," which means your companion, your friend, the one who has lived among you.

This is a powerful rhetorical choice. The Quraysh knew Muhammad intimately. He had spent forty years among them before prophethood. They knew his character, his truthfulness, his trustworthiness. They called him Al-Amin, the trustworthy. The Quran does not present him as a stranger making outlandish claims but as their own companion, someone whose integrity they have witnessed with their own eyes.

The verse then negates two accusations that were commonly levelled against him. The first is "dalal," meaning misguidance or error, going down the wrong path without knowledge.

The second is "ghawayah," meaning to be astray due to being overcome by desires or to be seduced into folly. The deniers would sometimes say that Muhammad had lost his way, that he had abandoned the religion of his forefathers in error. At other times, they would insinuate that he was driven by some personal delusion or demonic inspiration. This verse refutes both. The Prophet is not lost, and he is not deceived. He is on the straightest path, guided by the clearest light.

The verse also implies that the people making these accusations know, in their hearts, that they are false. Their own companion, whom they trusted for decades, cannot suddenly have become a misguided fool. The problem is not with him; it is with their unwillingness to accept his message. For the believers, this verse is a reassurance that their Prophet is exactly who they believe him to be: a man of sound mind, pure heart, and perfect guidance. For the deniers, it is a challenge to their conscience. If they are honest with themselves, they must admit that their companion is neither misguided nor astray. The verse paves the way for the explanation that follows: his guidance comes not from himself but from a divine source.

=3=

After establishing that the Prophet is neither misguided nor astray, the verse now explains the source of his guidance. He does not speak from his own whims, desires, or personal inclinations.

The Arabic word "hawa" refers to the inclinations of the self, the subjective desires and opinions that arise from human nature. This is a crucial point. The Prophet is a human being, and like all humans, he has personal likes and dislikes. But the message he delivers, the Quran, is not a product of his personal preferences. It is not his opinion, his philosophy, or his invention. He does not speak in religious matters based on what he thinks is right or what appeals to him. Every word of the revelation is from a higher source, transmitted with complete fidelity. This verse refutes any claim that the Quran is Muhammad's own composition, shaped by his psychology, his environment, or his ambitions. It also establishes the principle that in matters of religion, the Prophet's own words, his Sunnah, are also guided by revelation, as other Quranic verses confirm. The Prophet does not legislate from his own desire.

This is a cornerstone of Islamic belief regarding the authority of the Prophet. He is not a freelance thinker offering his personal insights. He is a messenger delivering a precise and preserved message.

The verse also serves as a timeless lesson for anyone who speaks about religion. Religious truth is not a matter of personal opinion or feeling. It must be rooted in divine revelation as conveyed by the authentic sources. When people today promote their own ideas about God, morality, or the afterlife based on their personal feelings or cultural trends, they are speaking from hawa. The Prophet, the model for all believers, is absolutely free from this. His words are a trustworthy conduit of divine will. The listener is thus prepared to receive what follows with the confidence that it is not Muhammad's speculation but a revelation from the Lord of the worlds.

=4=

This verse provides the positive counterpart to the previous negation. He does not speak from whim, because what he speaks is nothing less than revelation sent down.

The Arabic word "wahy" means a swift, secret communication, specifically the divine inspiration that Allah bestows upon His chosen prophets. The phrase "sent down" reinforces the concept of the Quran's heavenly origin. It is not something that originated in the Prophet's mind or in his earthly environment. It descended from above, from the Preserved Tablet, through the agency of the angel Gabriel, to the heart of the Prophet.

This is the core Islamic belief about the Quran: it is the uncreated word of Allah, revealed in clear Arabic to the final messenger. The word "only" in the translation captures the restrictive particle "in" in Arabic, which emphasises exclusivity. The Prophet's speech in the context of the message is exclusively revelation. There is no mixture of divine and human in the Quran itself. Every verse, every word, is from Allah. This verse is a direct refutation of all the alternative theories that the Quraysh proposed: that the Quran was poetry, magic, soothsaying, or fabrication. It is none of those things. It is pure, unadulterated wahy.

The concept of revelation is central to all Abrahamic faiths, and Islam presents itself as the culmination of that tradition. God does not leave humanity to grope in the dark. He sends revelation, a clear and authoritative guidance.

The verse also has profound implications for the believer's relationship with the Quran. When one recites or hears the Quran, one is not encountering the words of a man but the words of the Creator. This awareness should transform the act of reading and listening into an act of worship, filled with awe, humility, and intense attention. The verse sets the stage for the detailed description of the means by which this revelation was conveyed, which will be elaborated in the following verses about the angel Gabriel.

=5=

The surah now begins to describe the conduit of the revelation, the angel Gabriel. The Prophet has been taught this Quran not directly by Allah without intermediary, but through a specific angelic being described as possessing mighty power.

The Arabic "shadid al-quwa" paints a picture of immense, formidable strength. Gabriel is not a gentle, ethereal presence of vague spirituality. He is a being of terrifying power, capable of executing the commands of Allah throughout the cosmos. In other verses, Gabriel is described as having six hundred wings that cover the horizon. His power is a reflection of the power of the One who sent him.

The use of the word "taught" is significant. It emphasises the transmission of knowledge from the angel to the Prophet. Gabriel is the teacher, and Muhammad is the student. The Quran was not a book handed over as a physical object. It was a process of instruction, recitation, and memorisation that occurred over twenty-three years. Gabriel would come, recite the verses, and the Prophet would learn them, internalise them, and then convey them to the people.

This teaching relationship underscores the trustworthiness of the transmission. The Prophet did not merely receive an inspiration and then formulate it in his own words. He was taught the precise wording by the angel of mighty power. For the Quraysh, who were familiar with Jewish and Christian ideas about angels, this description established the identity and authority of the source. This was not a lowly jinn or a human teacher. It was the chief of the angels, the one who had come to Moses, to Mary, and to all the prophets. The verse thus builds a bridge between the Quran and previous revelations, asserting that the same mighty angel who taught the Prophet now taught the prophets before him. The description of Gabriel's power also prepares the listener for the description of his magnificent appearance that follows, as the surah recounts the Prophet's direct encounters with this mighty being.

=6=

The description of Gabriel continues with another attribute: he possesses great perfection. The Arabic "dhu mirrah" can be understood in several complementary ways. It refers to soundness of mind and judgment, to a perfect and beautiful appearance, or to immense strength and vigour. Some commentators have combined these meanings to describe Gabriel as an angel of flawless form, complete intelligence, and unmatched capability. He is the pinnacle of angelic creation, a being without any defect or weakness. After describing his attributes, the verse then refers to a specific historical event: Gabriel once rose to his true form.

This is a reference to the very first encounter between Gabriel and the Prophet Muhammad, which occurred in the cave of Hira. When Gabriel first appeared to the Prophet, he came in a form that the Prophet could perceive and interact with. But later, Gabriel revealed himself in his true, original angelic form, the form in which Allah created him. According to authentic hadiths, the Prophet saw Gabriel in his true form twice.

The first time was in the valley near Mecca after the initial revelation, when Gabriel appeared on the horizon covering the entire sky with his six hundred wings. The second time was during the Night Journey, the Mi'raj, at the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary. The verse is preparing to recount these awe-inspiring visions. The rising to his true form indicates that Gabriel usually appears in a form other than his own, such as the form of a man, when interacting with humans. But on these special occasions, he revealed his full, overwhelming angelic reality to the Prophet, demonstrating the truth of his identity and the majesty of the message he carried. This rising to the true form is a sign of the unique honour bestowed upon the Prophet Muhammad, who was permitted to witness the angel in a state that no other human had seen.

=7=

This verse describes the location and manner of Gabriel's appearance. He rose to his true form while positioned on the highest point above the horizon.

The image is of the angel filling the sky, standing at the uppermost edge of the visible world. The Prophet was in the valley, and he looked up to see the angel dominating the horizon, a figure so immense and radiant that he occupied the entire span of the sky from east to west. This was not a subtle or ambiguous vision. It was an overwhelming, unmistakable manifestation of angelic presence. The height emphasises the majesty and transcendence of the revelation. The message is not coming from the earth, from the depths of a cave or the mind of a man. It is descending from the highest reaches of the cosmos. The fact that Gabriel appeared on the highest horizon means that the Prophet saw him in a position of elevation and grandeur, a sight that would leave no doubt in his mind about the heavenly origin of what he was receiving.

This verse also serves to refute any claim that the Prophet's experience was a subjective hallucination or a dream. It happened while he was awake, in a specific location, and it involved a visible, external reality that his eyes beheld. The horizon is the boundary between the earth and the sky, and Gabriel's appearance there symbolises his role as the link between the divine realm and the human world. He stands at the intersection, receiving the command from above and delivering it to the Prophet below. The Quraysh could not claim that Muhammad was deceived by a spirit lurking in a cave. The angel appeared openly on the highest horizon, a public sign, though only the chosen Prophet was granted the spiritual eyes to fully perceive its meaning. The verse builds the anticipation for the description of the intimate encounter that follows.

=8=

After describing Gabriel's appearance on the highest horizon, the verse narrates the movement of the angel toward the Prophet. Gabriel approached, descending from his elevated position and drawing near to the Prophet. This approach was not a distant vision that remained far away. It was a movement toward intimate proximity. The verse emphasises the closeness in the next statement, but here the focus is on the action of approaching itself. This signifies Gabriel's condescension and honouring of the Prophet. The mighty angel of great perfection, who could have remained in his distant majesty, chose to come close to the human messenger, bridging the gap between the celestial and the terrestrial. This approach also conveys the sense of reassurance.

The first encounter in the cave had been terrifying. The angel had seized the Prophet and pressed him until he feared for his life. But this subsequent vision, where Gabriel reveals his true form, is different. The angel approaches with a majesty that still inspires awe, but it is an awe that brings the Prophet into a deeper intimacy with the divine message. The approach is the prelude to the most direct form of communication that is possible between an angel and a human prophet.

In the broader context of the surah, which will go on to describe the Night Journey, this approach may also refer to Gabriel's role as the Prophet's guide and companion during the ascension through the heavens. Gabriel came close to take the Prophet on the journey of a lifetime, to be his escort into the divine presence. The verse, read in conjunction with the next, creates an atmosphere of hushed reverence. The reader can almost feel the angel drawing near, the space between the creator's messenger and the human messenger shrinking until only an intimate distance remains. It is a moment of profound connection, a visual representation of the mercy of Allah who sends His mighty angel not to destroy but to teach, guide, and elevate.

=9=

This verse specifies just how close Gabriel came to the Prophet. The distance was only two arms-lengths, or even less.

The Arabic mentions the measure of two bows, "qaba qawsayn," which some have interpreted literally as the length of two bows placed end to end, a customary measurement among the Arabs. The addition of "or even less" indicates that the closeness was so intense that it cannot be precisely quantified. It was a proximity beyond ordinary human experience, a nearness that symbolises the utmost intimacy between the messenger and the angel who brought the revelation. This extraordinary closeness was a special honour granted to the Prophet Muhammad. No other prophet is known to have experienced such intimate proximity to the chief of the angels. It demonstrated the unique status of the final messenger and the importance of the message he was receiving. The closeness also served as a preparation and a strengthening for the immense spiritual journey that lay ahead. Gabriel was not merely delivering a letter from a distance; he was personally, intimately, directly conveying the divine communication, and the Prophet was receiving it in a state of complete wakefulness and clarity. Some scholars have discussed whether the one who approached and came close was Gabriel approaching the Prophet, or whether this verse and the surrounding ones refer to Allah Himself drawing near to the Prophet during the Mi'raj. The majority view, however, is that the verses describe Gabriel, as the context of the surah clearly speaks of the angel who taught the Prophet. Regardless, the intimacy described is staggering. For the Quraysh who mocked the Prophet, this verse was a slap in the face. They thought they were dealing with a deluded man, but they were dealing with a man who had been honoured with a proximity to the divine realm that they could not even conceive of. The verse elevates the Prophet's status and demands respect for the message he brought, for it came through an intimacy of transmission that is unparalleled in human history.

=10=

After establishing the identity, power, and close proximity of the angelic messenger, the verse now states the content of that intimate encounter. Allah revealed to His servant what He revealed. The verse is deliberately ambiguous and majestic in its phrasing. It does not specify exactly what was revealed, leaving it in a state of grand generality that indicates the immensity and the sacredness of the communication.

The word "awha" is the same root for revelation used earlier, emphasising that this was a direct act of divine communication. The recipient is described as "His servant," a title of utmost honour in the Quran. The highest rank a human can attain is to be a true servant of Allah. The Prophet Muhammad is the supreme servant, and it is in this capacity of perfect submission that he received the supreme revelation.

The repetition of "what He revealed" in the same verse creates a sense of awe and mystery. It is as if the exact details of that moment are too sacred, too immense for human language to capture.

However, from other Quranic verses and authentic hadiths, we learn that during the Mi'raj, the Night Journey, the Prophet was given the command of the five daily prayers, the closing verses of Surah Al-Baqarah, and other gifts and instructions. This verse, according to many scholars, refers to that direct divine communication that occurred beyond the Lote Tree, a communication that bypassed even Gabriel.

The phrase "what He revealed" encompasses all of that, and more that we do not know. The verse also serves as a refutation of any claim that the Prophet was making up the religion as he went along. The source of his knowledge is not speculation or education. It is a direct divine revelation delivered in a moment of unimaginable closeness. This mystery and majesty invite the believer to approach the Quran with a profound sense of reverence, knowing that behind every verse lies this moment of intimate, awe-inspiring revelation to the chosen servant.

=11=

After describing the external event of the angel's approach and the divine revelation, the verse turns to the internal state of the Prophet. His heart did not doubt what he saw.

The Arabic word "fu'ad" refers specifically to the heart as the centre of perception and emotion, a deeper faculty than mere intellect. The Prophet's heart, that innermost core of his being, did not deny, falsify, or question the reality of the vision. He saw Gabriel in his true form. He saw the signs of his Lord. And his heart accepted it all with complete certainty, without a trace of scepticism, fear, or confusion.

This is a testimony to the Prophet's spiritual perfection. A lesser person might have been overwhelmed, might have doubted their own sanity, might have tried to rationalise the experience away as a hallucination or a dream. But the Prophet's heart was so pure, so attuned to the truth, that it recognised the vision for what it was and embraced it fully. This verse is also a response to anyone who might later question the Prophet's account of his visions. The Quraysh would mock him for claiming to have journeyed to Jerusalem and ascended through the heavens in a single night. They would say that such a thing is impossible and that he must have been dreaming or deluded. This verse preemptively refutes that. The Prophet's heart did not doubt. His certainty was absolute. The vision was not a product of wishful thinking or an overactive imagination. It was an objective reality that his heart, guided by Allah, accepted without hesitation. For the believers, this verse is a lesson in the importance of having a heart that is trained to recognise and accept divine truths. The heart that is purified by faith and devotion does not waver when confronted with the unseen. It finds its rest in the certainty that Allah's revelations are true. The Prophet's heart is the model for all hearts. When revelation comes, the believing heart says, "We hear and we obey," without doubt or hesitation.

=12=

Having established the reality and certainty of the Prophet's vision, the Quran now turns to the deniers with a sharp rhetorical question. How can they possibly dispute with him regarding what he saw?

The question is designed to expose the absurdity of their position. The Prophet witnessed these events directly. He saw Gabriel on the horizon. He experienced the journey. He received the revelation. His heart is certain about what he saw. The deniers, on the other hand, were not present. They have no alternative account of what happened. They have no evidence to contradict his testimony. Their dispute is not based on knowledge or observation but on arrogance and the desire to reject.

The question implies that their dispute is utterly baseless. When a person of known integrity reports something they witnessed, the rational response is to accept their testimony unless there is strong evidence to the contrary. The Quraysh had forty years of evidence that Muhammad was truthful. Now he was reporting spiritual experiences of the unseen, and instead of believing him, they called him a liar. On what grounds? Their dispute was groundless. This verse is also a defence of the Night Journey. When the Prophet returned and told the Quraysh about his journey to Jerusalem and his ascension, they mocked him and saw it as an opportunity to discredit his entire message. Some Muslims even wavered in their faith. But the Quran here stands as a firm rebuttal. Do not dispute what you did not witness. The witness is truthful, and his heart is certain. For the believers, this verse teaches an important principle: the unseen matters of faith are accepted based on the testimony of the truthful Prophet, not based on our own limited experience or imagination. We were not there to see Gabriel, but we trust the one who did see him. The verse is a call to humble acceptance of the Prophet's reports and a warning against the arrogance that makes a person think they can dispute something simply because they have not personally experienced it.

=13=

The surah continues to describe the Prophet's encounters with Gabriel, now mentioning a second distinct vision. He certainly saw the angel descend a second time. This second vision is widely understood by the scholars to refer to the encounter that took place during the Night Journey, the Mi'raj, at the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary in the seventh heaven.

The first vision, described earlier, occurred on the earth when Gabriel appeared on the highest horizon. This second vision occurred in the highest reaches of the heavens.

The repetition of the vision underscores its importance and confirms its reality beyond any doubt. The Prophet was blessed to see the angel in his true, magnificent form not once but twice. Each vision was at a crucial moment in the Prophet's mission.

The first was during the early period of revelation, confirming the truth of the initial call. The second was during the Mi'raj, which occurred around the time of great hardship, after the Year of Sorrow, when the Prophet had lost his beloved wife Khadijah and his uncle Abu Talib, and had faced rejection in Ta'if.

The second vision was a divine consolation, an honour that lifted the Prophet's spirits and confirmed his supreme status. The word "certainly" emphasises the certainty of this event, preempting any doubt or denial.

The use of "nazala," meaning to descend, indicates that Gabriel came down from an even higher station to where the Prophet was, or that the Prophet saw him as a distinct, descending figure. This second vision is intrinsically linked to the Lote Tree, which the next verses will describe in awe-inspiring terms.

For the believer, this verse reinforces the truth of the Mi'raj, an event that is a cornerstone of Islamic faith, demonstrating that the Prophet's knowledge of the heavens was not hearsay but direct observation. He saw the angel, and he saw the celestial realms, and what he reported is the absolute truth.

=14=

This verse identifies the location of the second vision. The Prophet saw Gabriel at the Lote Tree of the most extreme limit.

The Arabic name is "Sidrat al-Muntaha." This is a magnificent tree located at the boundary of the seventh heaven, beyond which no created being, not even Gabriel, can pass. It marks the furthest limit of creation, the point where the material and the divine realms meet. The lote tree, or sidr, was known to the Arabs as a tree of shade and beauty, but this celestial lote tree is beyond anything in earthly imagination. The hadith describes it as a tree of immense size and splendour, with leaves like elephant ears and fruits like large water jars. At its base flow the rivers of Paradise.

The name "al-Muntaha" means the ultimate end, the final boundary. It is the point where the knowledge of all creatures stops, where the decrees of Allah descend, and where the souls of the righteous ascend. That the Prophet was brought to this tree is an indication of his unparalleled status. Even Gabriel, the mighty angel of revelation, stopped at this tree and said that if he went any further, he would be consumed. But the Prophet was permitted to advance beyond it into the direct divine presence. This location thus symbolises the unique honour granted to the final messenger. The vision at the Lote Tree was a confirmation of the Prophet's truthfulness and a preview of the Hereafter. For the Quraysh who mocked the Night Journey, this description was a profound challenge. The Prophet was speaking of things no human could know except through divine revelation. The Lote Tree of the Extreme Limit is a sign of Allah's majesty, a reminder that creation has a boundary and that beyond that boundary is the unseen realm of the divine, which the Prophet was privileged to glimpse. The believer's heart is filled with longing and awe at the mention of this tree, a place of such beauty and sanctity that it is described as being near the Garden of Eternal Residence.

=15=

The description of the celestial landscape continues with a crucial detail. Near the Lote Tree of the Extreme Limit is the Garden of Eternal Residence, Jannat al-Ma'wa.

This is Paradise, the final abode of the righteous. The location of Paradise near the Lote Tree places it at the highest level of creation, beyond the seven heavens, in the vicinity of the divine throne itself.

The name "al-Ma'wa" means the place of shelter, the refuge, the home to which one returns. This name evokes a sense of comfort, safety, and belonging. For the believers who were facing persecution, homelessness, and the threat of death in Mecca, the mention of the Garden of Refuge was a powerful source of hope. The Prophet was shown this Garden during his Night Journey. He saw it with his own eyes. This means that his descriptions of Paradise are not speculative; they are eyewitness accounts. The proximity of the Garden to the Lote Tree also connects the vision of Gabriel to the promise of eternal reward. The angel who brought the revelation is seen at the boundary of the place where the faithful will dwell forever. This visual link between the message and its ultimate reward is profoundly comforting. The Quran here is telling the believers: the messenger who brings you this book has seen the paradise that awaits you. His call is not empty words; it is a description of a reality he has glimpsed. The Garden of Refuge is the destination of the souls of the martyrs and the righteous. According to some hadiths, it is a specific garden within Paradise where the souls of the believers reside in the form of green birds until the Day of Resurrection. Whether understood as the whole of Paradise or a part of it, the mention of Jannat al-Ma'wa adds a dimension of merciful promise to the awe-inspiring vision. The deniers might mock, but the Prophet has seen where the believers are headed, and that vision sustains him and his followers through every trial.

=16=

This verse describes the state of the Lote Tree at the moment of the Prophet's vision. It was overwhelmed, covered, or enveloped in heavenly splendours.

The Arabic verb "yaghsha" means to cover completely, to envelop, to overspread. What covered the tree is described as something indescribably beautiful and majestic. The commentators mention that the tree was covered with a radiance, a light, a multitude of angels, or colours of unimaginable beauty. Some reports say it was covered with golden butterflies or with a light so brilliant that it defies description. The overall impression is one of overwhelming divine glory manifesting upon this boundary tree. The tree itself was not the object of worship or attention; rather, it was the canvas upon which Allah's light and majesty were displayed. This vision served to humble the Prophet and to fill him with awe before his Lord. The splendours that covered the tree were a sign of Allah's presence and power, a theophany that marked the spot as sacred and set apart. For the Quraysh who worshipped idols of stone and wood, this description was a stark contrast. Their idols sat in darkness in the Kaaba, lifeless and devoid of any splendour. The Prophet had seen the real splendour, the light of the Lord covering the celestial tree. Why would he ever trade that vision for their empty superstitions?

The verse also carries a mystical significance that has inspired Sufi commentators for centuries. The Lote Tree overwhelmed with splendours represents the station where the seeker is so overcome by the manifestations of divine beauty that the self dissolves, and only Allah remains.

Yet, the Quran is clear that this was an objective external event witnessed by the Prophet, not merely an internal mystical state. The emphasis is on the reality of the vision and the magnitude of the divine glory that was displayed. The tree, already magnificent, became a locus of such stunning beauty that words fail to capture it. The believer is invited to let the imagination reach toward that which cannot be fully imagined, and in doing so, to appreciate the greatness of the God who created such a sight.

=17=

This verse testifies to the perfect steadiness and discipline of the Prophet during his vision. His sight did not wander, meaning his eyes did not shift to the left or right, did not stray from the object of the vision, and did not get distracted by other sights. Nor did it overreach, meaning it did not transgress the proper bounds, did not try to see more than what he was permitted to see, and did not seek to penetrate the veils of the unseen beyond what Allah willed to show him.

The Arabic "ma zagha" and "ma tagha" convey a state of perfect focus, balance, and spiritual courtesy. The Prophet was not overwhelmed by the splendours to the point of losing control of his gaze. He remained composed, attentive, and reverent.

This is a remarkable testimony to the Prophet's spiritual perfection. A lesser human being, faced with such overwhelming divine manifestations, might have been dazzled, might have looked away in fear, or might have stared inappropriately in a state of greedy curiosity. The Prophet did none of these things. His sight was disciplined, his heart was collected, and his entire being was in a state of perfect adab, or etiquette, before his Lord.

This is the model for the believer in all acts of worship and in all moments of spiritual experience. The gaze should be controlled. The heart should not wander. One should not seek to overreach into matters that are beyond one's station. The Prophet was shown what he was shown, and he received it with exactly the right measure of attention and restraint.

The verse also refutes any insinuation that the Prophet was hallucinating or that his eyes played tricks on him. His vision was clear, focused, and accurate. He saw exactly what Allah willed him to see, and his report is completely reliable. This verse is a powerful reminder that true spiritual experience is not about losing control or being swept away by ecstasy. It is about maintaining the clarity and discipline that allow one to be a faithful witness to the truth.

=18=

This verse serves as the culmination of the entire passage describing the Prophet's visions. It declares with emphasis that the Prophet certainly saw some of the greatest signs of his Lord.

The Arabic "laqad ra'a" reinforces the certainty. The signs, "ayat," refer to the miraculous phenomena, the angelic forms, the celestial landscapes, the Lote Tree, the Garden of Refuge, and whatever else Allah showed him during the Night Journey and the visions.

The use of "greatest" indicates that these were not ordinary signs but the most magnificent and significant of all the signs that any human has ever witnessed. The Prophet's experience was unparalleled. He saw the angel Gabriel in his true form. He saw the Lote Tree. He saw Paradise and Hell. He was brought into the direct presence of his Lord. These are the "kubra," the greatest signs.

The verse clarifies that the Prophet did not see Allah Himself with his physical eyes, as the majority of scholars affirm, based on other Quranic verses and hadiths where the Prophet stated that he saw light and a veil. What he saw were the signs that indicated Allah's majesty, the created manifestations of His power and glory. The distinction is important because no vision can encompass Allah.

Yet the vision of His greatest signs was an honour beyond measure. This verse is the final answer to the deniers. They ask what the Prophet saw and mock his claims. The Quran answers: he saw the greatest signs of his Lord. These signs are not fables. They are real, witnessed by a truthful heart and steady eyes. For the believers, this verse inspires a profound sense of awe and gratitude. The Prophet saw these things for us, so that our faith could be built on the testimony of the most reliable witness. The greatest signs he saw are a promise of the greatness that awaits the believers in the Hereafter, and a confirmation that the path he calls us to is the path that leads to the vision of divine beauty and eternal bliss.

=19=

With the majestic description of the Prophet's vision complete, the surah makes a dramatic turn. The rhetorical question "Have you considered?" challenges the Quraysh to look with honest reflection at their own objects of worship. The tone shifts from the cosmic and sublime to the earthly and absurd. The idols mentioned are Al-Lat, Al-Uzza, and in the next verse, Manat. These were the three most important goddesses worshipped by the pagan Arabs. Al-Lat was a white stone idol located in Ta'if, housed in a shrine and venerated primarily by the Thaqif tribe. Some traditions say the idol was shaped like a woman.

The name Al-Lat is etymologically linked to the word for "the goddess," and some believe it derived from a corruption of "Allah," indicating that the pagans considered her a female counterpart to God. Al-Uzza was a tree or a rock shrine located near Mecca, on the road to Ta'if, and was the most revered idol of the Quraysh. Its name means "the mighty one." The Quraysh, especially the tribe of Ghatfan, would worship Al-Uzza with sacrifices and seek her blessings. The Prophet later sent Khalid ibn Al-Walid to destroy this idol. By asking the Quraysh to consider these idols immediately after the description of the Prophet's vision of the greatest signs of the Lord, the Quran creates a devastating contrast. On one hand, there is the Lote Tree, the angels, the heavenly splendours, and the direct revelation from the Lord of the worlds. On the other hand, there are stones, trees, and man-made shrines that can neither see, nor hear, nor benefit, nor harm. The rhetorical question invites the pagans to compare. What is more worthy of worship? What the Prophet saw in the heavens, or the idols they inherited from their forefathers? The verse exposes the absurdity of paganism by simply placing it next to the truth. The idols are named specifically, which is a direct assault on the central pillars of the Qurayshi religion. The Quran does not speak in vague terms; it names the false gods and challenges their divinity openly, demonstrating that the truth has no fear of falsehood.

The verse completes the list of the three chief idols by naming Manat, the third goddess of the pre-Islamic Arab pantheon. Manat was an idol located near the Red Sea coast, on the road between Mecca and Medina, in a place called Qudayd. She was worshipped by the tribes of Aws and Khazraj, who later became the Ansar of Medina, as well as by other tribes. Her name is related to the concept of fate, destiny, or death, and she was considered a goddess who controlled the fortunes of human beings. Pilgrims to Mecca would often visit her shrine to shave their heads and complete their rites. The phrasing "the third one" emphasises the arbitrary nature of their pantheon. They had three main goddesses, ranked and named, as if they were part of a manufactured hierarchy.

The question "as well?" links Manat to the previous two and implies that they are all equally false, equally powerless, and equally unworthy of worship. The historical context of these verses includes a notorious incident known as the "Satanic Verses," a fabricated story that some unreliable sources mentioned, claiming that the Prophet once praised these idols. Authentic Islamic scholarship, examining the chains of narration, has thoroughly refuted this story. It is inconsistent with the Prophet's entire life and mission, and the Quran itself condemns these idols in the strongest terms. What actually happened is that the Quraysh heard the Prophet reciting Surah An-Najm, and when he reached the verses about Al-Lat, Al-Uzza, and Manat, they listened intently. Then when the surah continued to condemn these idols and call them mere names, they were enraged. This verse is a direct and unambiguous rejection of the foundational deities of the pagan system. By naming them publicly in the Quran, Allah ensures that there is no ambiguity about their falsehood. The verse challenges the Quraysh to think: the Prophet has seen the greatest signs of the Lord, and you are still clinging to stones and names you yourselves invented? The absurdity is made glaringly obvious. The surah is about to deliver a series of devastating refutations of idolatry, building on this powerful opening challenge.
