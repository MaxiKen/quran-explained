The description of the deniers’ reaction continues with another piercing observation. They are not merely laughing at the revelation; they are doing so while persisting in heedlessness.

The Arabic word translated as “heedlessness” is “samidun,” which has a rich range of meanings. It can refer to singing, playing, and amusement, indicating that the deniers filled their time with entertainment and distraction to avoid the Quran. It can also mean lifting the head in arrogance, turning away with pride, refusing to bow or humble oneself. Some scholars say it refers to a kind of aimless, distracted play, like someone humming a tune to drown out a serious conversation. All of these meanings converge on the picture of a person who is completely absorbed in their own world of triviality and self-importance, utterly oblivious to the weight of what is being recited. The verse is a critique not just of an action but of an entire posture of the heart. The deniers were “samidun” when the Quran was recited. They were not giving it any serious thought. They were not pausing to reflect. Their minds were elsewhere, wrapped up in their business deals, their tribal rivalries, their songs, and their poetry. They kept themselves busy with anything and everything except the truth. This heedlessness is the very opposite of the focus and reverence that the Prophet exhibited during his vision, when his sight never wandered and never overreached. While the Prophet was fully present and attentive to the greatest signs, the deniers were distracted, playing around, and refusing to engage. The verse exposes the tragic choice of a soul that prefers the temporary amusement of heedlessness to the eternal reality of the divine word. This state is self-reinforcing. The more one indulges in heedlessness, the harder it becomes to break free from it. The laughter and the singing become a wall that blocks out the sound of the warning. The Quran calls this “samidun” as a diagnosis: the root problem is not intellectual but spiritual. It is a disease of the will, a determination to remain in a state of playful ignorance rather than face the demands of faith. The believers, in contrast, are those who listen to the Quran with attentive hearts and eyes that weep.

The question embedded in this verse, and the one before it, is a wake-up call to anyone who has become too comfortable in the diversions of life to take the message seriously.

The surah reaches its powerful and climactic conclusion with a direct command. After all the oaths, the descriptions of the Prophet’s visions, the refutation of idolatry, the cataloguing of Allah’s signs, and the chilling warnings of destruction, the only fitting response is given: prostrate to Allah and worship Him alone.

The Arabic “fa usjudu lillahi wa’budu” is a command of tremendous force. The particle “fa” indicates an immediate and inevitable conclusion. In light of everything that has been said, the only logical and proper course of action is to fall in prostration and to engage in devoted worship. Prostration is the ultimate symbol of submission. It is the physical act where the highest and most honoured part of the human body, the forehead, is placed upon the lowest point, the ground, in total humility before the Creator. This act directly counters the arrogance of the deniers who were “samidun,” lifting their heads in pride. The surah tells them to bring those proud heads down to the earth in submission to the Lord of Sirius, the Lord of life and death. Prostration is also the bodily manifestation of tawhid. It is the act that the angels perform continuously, the act that the trees and the stars do in their own way, and the act that the believer offers willingly to the One who alone deserves it. The command to worship Him alone anchors the prostration in the exclusive devotion that the entire surah has been arguing for. Worship is not just a ritual; it is the comprehensive orientation of a life toward God, encompassing prayer, obedience, love, and service. This verse is one of the places in the Quran where a prostration of recitation, sajdah tilawah, is legislated. When the Prophet recited this verse, he prostrated, and the believers prostrated with him. Even some of the pagans present were so overcome that they fell into prostration, except for one proud man who took a handful of dirt and touched it to his forehead, saying that this was enough for him. The verse divides humanity into two groups: those who hear the truth and prostrate, and those who persist in heedlessness.

The surah ends on this note of urgent, heartfelt invitation. It is as if all the arguments have been laid out, and now the listener is given a simple, direct choice: remain standing in arrogance, or fall down in worship.

The final word of the surah is “worship,” making it clear that the purpose of all the signs and all the warnings is to bring human beings into a state of loving, humble servitude to their Lord. With this command, the surah closes, having taken the listener on a journey from the fading stars to the prostration of the heart.
