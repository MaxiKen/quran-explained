=21=

After naming the three goddesses and challenging the Quraysh to consider what they are worshipping, the Quran now exposes a glaring contradiction in their belief system. The pagan Arabs had a deep-seated cultural preference for sons. They considered daughters a source of shame and embarrassment, so much so that some of them would bury their infant daughters alive. This was a society that valued sons for their strength, their ability to carry on the family name, and their economic contribution. Daughters were seen as a burden.

Yet, despite this intense aversion to having daughters themselves, they had the audacity to attribute daughters to Allah. They claimed that the angels, whom they worshipped as deities alongside Allah, were female, and that they were the daughters of God. The verse asks a devastatingly simple question: do you prefer to have sons for yourselves, while you assign daughters to Him? The implication is clear. If daughters are so undesirable that you bury them in the sand, why do you insult the Creator by ascribing them to Him? Do you think that what is shameful for you is fitting for the Lord of the worlds?

This is not just a logical inconsistency; it is a profound disrespect. The verse uses their own cultural standards against them. It forces them to confront their hypocrisy. They cannot claim that daughters are good enough for God when they themselves despise having them. The rhetorical question is unanswerable. It leaves the pagan exposed as someone who is not only worshipping false gods but is also dishonouring the true God in the most insulting manner imaginable. This argument is a masterpiece of rational refutation. It does not require deep theology; it simply appeals to the innate sense of fairness and consistency that even a pagan possesses. For the believers, this verse is a reminder of the perfection of Allah. He is far above having children, sons or daughters. He is the Self-Sufficient, and the very concept of divine progeny is a pagan corruption that degrades the majesty of the Creator.

The verse also carries a subtle social message, challenging the devaluation of daughters in Arab society and, by extension, in any society that favours male children.

=22=

The verse delivers the conclusion of the argument with a tone of astonished condemnation. If you claim that Allah has daughters while you prefer sons for yourselves, then this is a division that is unjust, unfair, and utterly skewed.

The Arabic word "dizha" conveys the sense of something that is lopsided, defective, and corrupt. It is as if they have taken a scale and weighted it heavily in their own favour, giving themselves the best portion and leaving Allah with what they themselves despise. The verse exposes the arrogance of the human being who wants the best for himself but is willing to assign the worst, according to his own standards, to God.

This is not the behaviour of a sincere worshipper. A true believer gives to God what is best, what is most beloved. The polytheists, by contrast, were making a division that was patently biased and insulting.

The verse also demolishes any notion that their idol worship was based on a genuine, if misguided, reverence. How can they revere beings they would be ashamed to have in their own families? The inconsistency reveals that their religion was not the product of honest spiritual seeking but of cultural habit and the following of desires.

The word "truly" emphasises that there is no escaping this verdict. No rational person can examine this distribution and call it fair. The verse is short, but it serves as a powerful punctuation mark on the argument against the goddesses. For the believers, it reinforces the principle that all judgments about Allah must be based on His own revelation, not on human projection. We do not attribute to Allah what we ourselves dislike, nor do we presume to decide what is fitting for Him. We receive His description of Himself with humility and accept that He is far above any comparison. The biased distribution of the pagans is a warning against the human tendency to create God in one's own image, assigning to Him the leftovers of our understanding while hoarding the best for ourselves.

=23=

This verse delivers the final, crushing verdict on the idols. They are nothing but names, empty labels without any corresponding reality. The Arabs called them Al-Lat, Al-Uzza, and Manat, and they invented stories and rituals around these names. But there is no essence, no power, no divine attribute behind these words. They are linguistic phantoms, created by human imagination and passed down through generations. The verse explicitly states that this naming is a human invention, a practice that Allah has never authorized. No revelation, no scripture, no prophet ever sanctioned the worship of these stones.

This is a crucial point. Religion is not a field for human creativity. True worship must be based on divine instruction, not on cultural tradition. By stating that Allah has sent down no authority for these idols, the Quran establishes the principle that in matters of faith, the only valid source is revelation. Anything else is an innovation, a bid'ah, that leads to misguidance.

The verse then diagnoses the psychological and social drivers of idolatry. The pagans are following nothing but inherited assumptions and the desires of their souls. They cling to the religion of their forefathers not because it is true but because it is familiar and comfortable. They are slaves to custom and to the inner whims that crave tangible, accessible gods who can be manipulated. This combination of blind tradition and unchecked desire is the root of all false religion. The tragedy is that true guidance has already come to them from their Lord. The Prophet is among them, reciting the clear verses of the Quran, offering the pure monotheism of Abraham, and performing no miracles other than the book itself.

Yet they turn away, preferring the dead weight of ancestral custom and the fleeting satisfaction of their desires over the light of divine guidance. This verse is a timeless diagnosis of the human condition. People today may not worship Al-Lat and Al-Uzza, but they follow inherited ideologies, cultural norms, and personal desires with the same blind devotion, rejecting the clear guidance that calls them to the worship of the one true God.

=24=

The surah now addresses another aspect of the pagans' false beliefs: their reliance on intercessors. The Quraysh claimed that they worshipped the idols because these idols would intercede for them with Allah. They imagined that they could do whatever they pleased in this life, violate the moral law, ignore the prophets, and then on the Day of Judgment, their gods would step in and secure their salvation. This verse demolishes that fantasy with a sharp rhetorical question. Should every person simply have whatever they desire? The implication is clear: the universe is not a wish-fulfilment machine. You cannot live a life of sin and then expect that your chosen intercessors will automatically grant you what you want.

This is wishful thinking of the most naive kind. The pagans were essentially creating a religion of convenience. They wanted the freedom to live as they pleased, combined with a guarantee of safety in the afterlife. They invented gods and assigned them the role of intercessors to suit their own desires. The verse exposes the absurdity of this self-serving theology. If salvation were simply a matter of desiring it and naming an intercessor, then there would be no justice, no accountability, and no need for prophets or revelations. Everyone would follow their own desires and appoint their own saviours. The verse forces the listener to recognise that divine judgment is not subject to human whims. The right to intercede is not something that can be claimed by any created being on its own authority. Intercession belongs to Allah alone, and He grants it to whom He wills, under conditions that He alone sets. The pagan concept of intercession was a corruption of a true principle. Intercession on the Day of Judgment is real, as the Quran affirms, but it is entirely under divine control. The idols have no power to intercede. The verse prepares the ground for the next two verses, which will explain the true nature of intercession and the absolute sovereignty of Allah over this world and the next.

=25=

This verse delivers the fundamental truth that dismantles all false notions of intercession and autonomous deities. To Allah alone belongs the Hereafter and the first life.

The Arabic word "al-akhirah" refers to the next world, the eternal realm, and "al-ula" refers to the first, the present world. The totality of existence, from beginning to end, from the seen to the unseen, is the exclusive property and dominion of Allah.

This is a comprehensive statement of divine sovereignty. If the entire universe belongs to Allah, then no one else has any independent authority whatsoever. The idols possess nothing, control nothing, and can grant nothing. The angels possess nothing; they are merely honoured servants. The prophets possess nothing; they are messengers dependent on their Lord. This absolute ownership means that any intercession, any forgiveness, any reward, and any punishment can only occur by the permission of the One who owns everything. The pagans, by setting up intermediaries, were implicitly assuming that Allah had partners who held shares in His dominion. This verse annihilates that assumption.

The next world and this world are His alone. This truth has profound implications for the believer. If everything belongs to Allah, then we are merely stewards of what He has entrusted to us. Our wealth, our health, our time, and our very existence are not truly ours. They are a trust from the Owner. This awareness breeds humility, gratitude, and a sense of accountability. We cannot do with our lives whatever we desire, because we are living in a dominion that is not our own.

The verse also provides immense comfort. The One who owns both worlds has the power to manage our affairs in both. We do not need to rely on flimsy intermediaries. We can go directly to the Owner, who is also the Most Merciful. The believer's relationship with Allah is not one of distant awe mediated by a hierarchy of lesser deities. It is a direct, personal connection with the Lord of both worlds, who invites us to call upon Him directly. This verse is a foundational pillar of tawhid, the oneness of God in His lordship.

=26=

This verse continues the theme of intercession by turning the attention to the most honoured of created beings: the angels. The pagans worshipped the angels, considering them daughters of Allah and hoping for their intercession. The Quran responds by highlighting the sheer number and nobility of the angels. There are multitudes of them in the heavens, countless beings of light, engaged in perpetual worship.

Yet, despite their number, their nearness to Allah, and their purity, their intercession is of absolutely no benefit unless two conditions are met. First, Allah must give permission for the angel to speak. The angel cannot intercede on its own initiative. Second, Allah must approve of the person for whom the intercession is made. Intercession is not a blanket amnesty for anyone who claims it. It is a precisely granted privilege that occurs within the framework of divine justice and mercy. The angels, with all their glory, are utterly subservient to these conditions. If this is the case for the angels, what then of the dead stones and lifeless idols that the pagans worshipped? They are infinitely lower than the angels, possessing no consciousness, no speech, and no standing before Allah. The argument is a classic Quranic move from the greater to the lesser. If the most honoured creatures in the universe have no independent power of intercession, then the wooden and stone idols certainly have none.

The verse also clarifies the Islamic doctrine of intercession. It is not a pagan-style lobbying where one can choose any patron and be guaranteed success. Intercession is a gift from Allah, granted to certain chosen servants, such as the Prophet Muhammad, and it will only benefit those with whom Allah is pleased. This preserves the balance between hope and fear. The believer hopes for the Prophet's intercession but also strives to be among those whom Allah approves. The sinner cannot rely on intercession as an excuse to persist in sin, because intercession is conditional upon Allah's approval, and Allah does not approve of persistent, unrepentant wrongdoing. The verse thus purifies the concept of intercession from all pagan corruptions and places it firmly within the framework of absolute divine sovereignty.

=27=

The surah now directly addresses the specific accusation against the pagan belief system: their gendering of the angels. The verse states that it is those who do not believe in the Hereafter who label the angels as female.

This is a profound observation. The denial of the Hereafter is linked to this particular false belief. Why is there a connection? When people do not believe in an ultimate judgment and an eternal life where justice will be served, their theology becomes corrupted by worldly prejudices and desires. The pagan Arabs, steeped in their patriarchal culture, projected their own gender biases onto the divine realm. They considered females inferior, so they relegated the angels to a female identity, simultaneously making them divine and yet subordinate. The denial of the Hereafter freed them from accountability, allowing them to concoct whatever beliefs suited their cultural preferences.

In contrast, a believer in the Hereafter is constrained by the truth of revelation and the fear of judgment. Such a person would never dare to assign a gender to the angels without divine authorization. The verse is not merely describing the pagans; it is diagnosing the spiritual disease that produces such false beliefs. It is the absence of eschatological conviction that opens the door to theological chaos. For the Quraysh, the angels were a convenient invention. By calling them daughters of Allah, they could worship them, claim their intercession, and yet maintain their own sense of male superiority. The verse exposes this entire framework as a product of disbelief in the afterlife. It is a powerful reminder that correct belief about Allah and the unseen is inseparable from belief in the Hereafter. If a person truly believed that they would stand before their Lord and be questioned about every belief they held, they would not be so casual about inventing doctrines.

The verse also highlights the Quran's consistent position that the angels are neither male nor female. They are a distinct creation, described as honoured servants, without any specification of gender as humans understand it. Attributing gender to them is a slander against the unseen realm, a slander born of ignorance and heedlessness.

=28=

This verse continues the indictment by pointing out the complete lack of evidence for the pagan belief. The pagans have no knowledge whatsoever to support their claim that angels are female. They did not witness the creation of the angels. They have no scripture that reveals this. They have no rational argument that proves it. Their belief is based entirely on inherited assumptions, on zann, which is conjecture, guesswork, and unverified tradition.

The verse establishes a fundamental epistemological principle: assumptions can never replace the truth. Al-haqq, the truth, is something solid, established, and verified. Zann is fleeting, unstable, and unreliable. In matters of the unseen, especially concerning the nature of God and the angels, nothing short of certain knowledge is acceptable. And the only source of certain knowledge about the unseen is divine revelation. Since the pagans had no revelation authorising their beliefs, they were following nothing but the shadows of their own imagination. This verse is a powerful condemnation of blind following, or taqlid, in matters of faith. The Quran does not accept that a person can simply inherit their religion from their parents and be excused. Every human being has a duty to seek the truth, to question the traditions they were raised with, and to demand evidence for the claims that affect their eternal destiny. The pagans of Mecca were not short of opportunities to learn the truth. The Prophet was among them with the clear book.

Yet they preferred the comfortable path of inherited assumptions over the demanding path of genuine inquiry. The verse also speaks directly to the modern age, where countless people hold religious or philosophical beliefs not because they have examined them critically but because they absorbed them from their culture. Islam calls to a faith that is grounded in knowledge, not in blind imitation. The believer should be able to say, "I believe because the evidence points to the truth of this message," not simply "I believe because I was born into it." The contrast between zann and haqq is a central Quranic theme, and this verse serves as a timeless warning against the dangers of intellectual laziness in the most important matter of all.

=29=

After the extensive refutation of pagan beliefs, the surah now gives a directive to the Prophet. He is told to turn away from those who have shunned the Reminder. The Reminder, or dhikr, is the Quran itself, the divine message that calls people to remember their Creator and their ultimate destiny. To shun it means to turn one's back on it deliberately, to avoid listening to it, to refuse to engage with its message. These people have been given the truth, they have heard the arguments, and they have chosen to reject it. They are not interested in guidance. Their only concern is the fleeting life of this world.

The Arabic "al-hayat al-dunya" refers to the lower, immediate, and transient life. Their horizons are limited to what they can see, touch, and enjoy in the here and now. The Hereafter, with its eternal consequences, holds no weight in their calculations. The command to turn away is not a command to stop calling to Islam altogether. It is a command to cease the exhausting and fruitless effort of trying to convince those who have made a final decision to reject. The Prophet's energy is precious, and it should be invested where there is hope of a response, not wasted on those who have sealed their own hearts.

This is a lesson in spiritual prioritisation. The believer, like the Prophet, must know when to engage and when to withdraw. There are people who thrive on argument and conflict, whose only goal is to waste the believer's time and drain their emotional energy. Turning away from such people is an act of wisdom and self-preservation. It also carries a subtle warning to those being turned away from. When a prophet, the most merciful of humans, turns away from a people, it signals that their case has reached a critical point. They are being left to their own devices, to the fleeting pleasures that they have chosen over eternal bliss. The verse thus balances mercy with justice. The reminder is available for all who seek it, but those who actively shun it will be left to face the consequences of their choice.

=30=

The verse delivers a concise and devastating judgment on the intellectual state of the deniers. "This is the extent of their knowledge." The Arabic "mablagh" means the furthest reach, the ultimate limit. Their knowledge goes no further than the visible, the material, the worldly. They may be experts in trade, in poetry, in genealogy, and in the affairs of this life, but their knowledge stops at the surface of existence. They have no insight into the deeper realities, the purpose of creation, the nature of the soul, or the reality of the Hereafter. Their knowledge is shallow and truncated. The verse is not denying that they possess intelligence or skills. It is stating that their knowledge, in the ultimate sense, is severely limited. It does not reach the truth that matters most.

This is the natural result of shunning the divine reminder. When a person turns away from revelation, the only knowledge left to them is the knowledge of this world, which is like a single drop compared to the ocean of divine knowledge.

The verse then moves from the limitation of their knowledge to the perfect knowledge of the Lord. "Surely your Lord knows best who has strayed from His Way and who is rightly guided. " The Prophet is not the ultimate judge of people's hearts. He may be hurt by their rejection, but he does not know their final fate. Allah alone possesses the complete knowledge of who is truly astray and who is guided.

This statement is a comfort to the Prophet, absolving him of the responsibility for their choices. It is also a warning to the deniers, for they may think they are guided, but Allah knows their true state.

The verse teaches a profound humility. Human judgment is fallible and based on externals. Only Allah sees the inner reality and the final outcome. The believer should neither despair over those who seem hopeless nor feel smugly secure about their own guidance, for the ultimate knowledge rests with Allah. The focus should remain on one's own sincerity and continued effort to remain on the path of guidance as defined by the Lord who knows best.

=31=

This verse reasserts the absolute ownership and sovereignty of Allah, introduced earlier in verse 25, and now links it explicitly to the purpose of judgment. Everything in the heavens and the earth belongs to Allah. This comprehensive ownership is the foundation of His right to judge and His power to reward and punish.

The phrase "so that" indicates a purpose, a consequence of this ownership that is rooted in divine wisdom and justice. The universe was not created in vain, and the dominion of Allah is not a passive possession. It entails an active moral government. The evildoers, those who reject the truth and commit sins, will be rewarded, or rather recompensed, according to what they did.

The word "yajziya" means to pay back, to give the exact due. Their punishment will be a precise and fair reflection of their deeds, with no injustice whatsoever. On the other hand, the good-doers, those who believed and acted righteously, will be rewarded with the finest reward, "al-husna." This word is comprehensive, encompassing Paradise, the vision of Allah, and every beautiful and perfect gift that Allah has prepared for them. The reward of the good-doers is not merely proportional; it is multiplied, beautified, and elevated by divine grace. The contrast between the two groups is stark. The evildoers receive what they earned, a just recompense. The good-doers receive a reward that far exceeds the strict merit of their deeds, a reward from the Most Generous. This verse thus provides the ultimate answer to the problem of evil and injustice in the world. The wicked may prosper for a time, and the righteous may suffer, but the universe has an Owner, and that Owner will settle all accounts with perfect justice and boundless mercy. The verse serves as a powerful motivation for the believer to strive for the finest reward and a dire warning to the wrongdoer that their deeds are not forgotten. Every action, open or secret, is recorded in the dominion of the One who owns everything, and He will bring it to account on a Day when there is no escape.

=32=

This verse continues the description of the good-doers, providing both a standard and a reassurance. They are those who avoid the major sins, the "kaba'ir," and the shameful deeds, the "fawahish." Major sins are those that carry severe warnings in the Quran and Sunnah, such as shirk, murder, and consuming the property of orphans. Shameful deeds include particularly repugnant acts like adultery and lewdness.

However, the verse acknowledges human weakness. The good-doers are not sinless angels. They may stumble into minor sins, the "lamam," which refers to small, inadvertent slips that a person falls into without persistent intention. The inclusion of this phrase is a profound mercy. It prevents the believer from falling into despair over their imperfections. The verse immediately follows with the assurance that "Surely your Lord is infinite in forgiveness.

" The Arabic "wasi' al-maghfirah" means His forgiveness is vast, encompassing, and expansive. It covers the minor sins that the believers inevitably commit, as long as they repent and strive to avoid the major transgressions. Then the verse grounds this forgiveness in the comprehensive knowledge of Allah. He knew well what would become of you when He created you from the earth, referring to the creation of Adam, and while you were still fetuses in the wombs of your mothers.

This is an incredibly intimate description of divine knowledge. Allah has known every human being from the very beginning, from the dust of the earth to the darkness of the womb. He knows our inherent weakness, the temptations we face, and the struggles we endure.

Therefore, His judgment is tempered with a mercy that takes our creation and our nature into account. The command "So do not falsely elevate yourselves" is a direct rebuke to those who claim purity and righteousness for themselves, looking down on others. Only Allah knows who is truly righteous. The believer should remain humble, aware of their own slips, and rely on the vast forgiveness of their Lord rather than boasting of their own virtue.

=33=

The surah now presents a specific case study of a person who embodies the rejection of the truth. The verse asks, "Have you seen the one who turned away?

" This is a rhetorical question drawing attention to a particular individual whose behaviour serves as a warning. The Arabic "ra'ayta" implies not just physical sight but reflection and consideration. Look at this person, ponder his actions, and learn the lesson.

The phrase "turned away" translates "tawalla," meaning to give one's back, to abandon and reject. The context, as understood from many reports, refers to a specific incident involving a man from the Quraysh. The most commonly cited report, though there are variations and discussions about its chain, identifies this person as Al-Walid ibn Al-Mughirah, a prominent leader of the Quraysh, or possibly another figure. This individual was initially inclined towards Islam, or at least seemed close to accepting it, but then he turned away. He abandoned the truth after being on the verge of embracing it. The verse captures the tragic drama of a soul that comes close to the light but then retreats into the darkness.

The question "Have you seen?" is also an expression of astonishment and indignation. It is as if Allah is saying: look at the sheer absurdity and tragedy of this choice. This man had the truth within his reach. The Quran was being recited to him. The Prophet was his own fellow tribesman.

Yet he turned away. The verse sets up the narrative that will unfold, detailing his miserly behaviour and his arrogance. It is a case study in how wealth, pride, and the fear of losing social status can cause a person to reject faith even when their heart recognises its truth. The believer is invited to examine this example and to see the ugliness of such a choice, contrasting it with the steadfastness of the believers who, despite all pressures, did not turn away. The verse thus personalises the abstract discussion of disbelief, making it vivid and concrete.

=34=

This verse reveals the specific behaviour of the man who turned away. He initially paid a little, and then he stopped and became hard-hearted. The commentators offer a detailed narrative behind this. It is reported that Al-Walid ibn Al-Mughirah, after listening to the Quran and being moved by it, was approached by some of the Quraysh leaders. They feared he might accept Islam. He told them that he needed time to think. He listened to the Quran again and acknowledged its extraordinary beauty and power, famously saying that it surpassed all poetry and speech.

However, his fellow pagans pressured him, and he eventually struck a deal. He agreed to pay a certain amount of money to the Quraysh's collective funds, essentially buying back his loyalty to the pagan religion and formally denouncing Muhammad. He paid some of that money, but then he stopped and did not fulfil the full amount, or he stopped because he began to have second thoughts about the whole transaction. Alternatively, some interpretations say that he "paid a little" by initially acknowledging the truth of the Quran with his tongue, but then he "stopped" and withheld the full testimony of faith. In either case, the image is of someone who took a half-step toward the truth, made a small investment, but then held back from the complete commitment.

This is a picture of spiritual miserliness. The man was unwilling to pay the full price of faith, which is the total submission of the heart and the readiness to face social consequences. He wanted to keep a foot in both worlds. The verse condemns this halfway stance. Faith is not a transaction where you can pay a little and keep the rest for yourself. It is an all-or-nothing surrender. The one who "stops" after paying a little reveals that the love of the world and the fear of people are greater in his heart than the love of truth. This verse serves as a warning to those who delay their full commitment to faith, thinking they can negotiate with God or keep their options open. The moment of truth demands a complete turning, not a partial payment.

=35=

Following the description of the man's miserly behaviour, the surah asks a piercing rhetorical question. Does this person, who turned away and withheld the full commitment, possess the knowledge of the unseen?

The Arabic "a'indahu 'ilm al-ghayb" challenges his presumed authority to make such a calculation. His behaviour suggests that he acted as if he had insider knowledge. He weighed the pros and cons, made a payment, and then stopped, as if he knew exactly what the future held and what the outcomes would be. He acted as if he could see the Hereafter and determined that the deal he made, paying a little and stopping, was the optimal choice for his ultimate destiny.

The question exposes the absurdity of this stance. He has no knowledge of the unseen. He does not know when he will die, what will happen after death, or what the Day of Judgment will bring. His decision to turn away was based on pure guesswork and worldly calculation, not on any certainty about the unseen. If he truly had access to the knowledge of the unseen and could see the Hellfire and the Gardens, he would never have made such a foolish trade. The verse implies that his limited, miserly payment was a gamble based on ignorance.

This is a profound lesson for every human being. We often make spiritual decisions based on our very limited perception of the world. We prioritise immediate, visible gains over the unseen, eternal realities because we cannot see the Hereafter. But the wise person trusts the one who does see the unseen, the Creator, and His revelation.

The verse also functions as a refutation of the pagan belief in soothsayers and their claimed access to the unseen. No one except Allah has the knowledge of the unseen. The man's behaviour is that of someone who presumes to know the outcome, but that presumption is baseless. The believer, in contrast, acknowledges the limits of their knowledge and submits to the guidance of the All-Knowing.

=36=

The surah now shifts from challenging the individual's access to the unseen to reminding him and the entire audience of the continuity of the divine message. The question asks: has he not been informed of the contents of the Scripture of Moses? The Torah, the revelation given to Moses, is cited as a witness. This rhetorical question implies that the core truths of Islam are not novelties. They are the same truths that were revealed to Moses long ago. The man and the Quraysh had access to the People of the Book, the Jews and Christians, who could confirm the contents of the earlier scriptures. If he was truly seeking the truth, he could have inquired.

The verse also suggests that the fundamental principles that follow in the next verses are not new inventions of the Prophet Muhammad. They are ancient, established tenets of the Abrahamic faith. This serves multiple purposes. It refutes the accusation that the Prophet fabricated the Quran, because the Quran aligns with the core teachings of the previous revelations. It challenges the Quraysh, who prided themselves on being descendants of Abraham, to recognise that what the Prophet is teaching is actually the religion of their forefather Abraham, purified from idolatrous corruptions. It also consoles the Prophet by showing that his message is part of a grand, divine tradition.

The mention of Moses specifically is significant because Moses was the great lawgiver, and his scripture was widely recognised. The surah is about to quote the essential principles found in the scriptures of Moses and Abraham, principles that refute the pagan notions of intercession and earning salvation through proxy, and instead establish personal accountability. The rhetorical question thus prepares the listener for the profound verses that follow, anchoring the new revelation in the old and asserting the unity of the divine message across the ages.

=37=

The verse adds a second scriptural witness: the scripture of Abraham. Abraham is a central figure for the Quraysh, who considered themselves his descendants and the custodians of his legacy. The Quran frequently invokes Abraham to show that true monotheism, the "millat Ibrahim," is the original faith, and that Muhammad is reviving it, not inventing a new religion. The verse describes Abraham with a powerful phrase: "who perfectly fulfilled his covenant. " The Arabic "waffa" means to fulfil completely, to pay in full, to discharge one's duty with perfection. This stands in stark and deliberate contrast to the man described in verse 34 who "paid a little and then stopped." Abraham is presented as the paragon of total commitment. He fulfilled his covenant with Allah. What was this covenant? It was the covenant of prophethood and servitude, the command to submit completely, to sacrifice, to leave his homeland, to argue against idolatry, and to be the friend of Allah. Abraham did not hold back. He did not make a partial payment. He gave everything. When commanded to sacrifice his son, he prepared to do so without hesitation. When thrown into the fire, he trusted in Allah. His entire life was a fulfilment of the covenant. By contrasting the miserly half-commitment of the denier with the perfect fulfilment of Abraham, the Quran presents two models of human response to the divine call. One is the model of calculation, withholding, and turning away. The other is the model of complete surrender, trust, and unwavering fidelity. The Quraysh claimed Abraham as their forefather, but their behaviour was the antithesis of his. This verse implicitly challenges them: if you truly revere Abraham, then follow his way. His scripture, his "suhuf," contained the very truths that the Quran is now reiterating.

The mention of Abraham's fulfilment of the covenant also serves as a beautiful tribute to the father of prophets and an inspiration for the believers. The path to Allah is not one of lukewarm gestures. It demands a wholehearted fulfilment of the covenant of faith, a complete payment, not a miserly one.

=38=

The surah now begins to quote the core principles that are found in the scriptures of Moses and Abraham. The first principle is that no soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another.

This is a foundational doctrine of divine justice. Every individual is responsible for their own actions. The sin of one person is not transferred to another. The innocence of one person does not automatically atone for the guilt of another.

This principle directly refutes the pagan concept of inherited guilt, the Christian doctrine of original sin as a burden passed from Adam to all humanity requiring a divine sacrifice, and any notion that one can pay for the sins of another. On the Day of Judgment, each soul stands alone with its own record. A father cannot carry the sins of his son, and a son cannot carry the sins of his father.

This is both a warning and a comfort. It is a warning because it means that no intercessor, no ancestor, and no tribe can shield a person from the consequences of their own deeds. The Quraysh could not rely on the supposed righteousness of their forefathers to save them. It is a comfort because it means that no one is born sinful or condemned. Every child is born pure, on the fitrah, with a clean slate.

The concept of personal accountability is central to the moral framework of Islam. It dignifies the human being as a responsible agent and establishes justice as the bedrock of divine judgment. The verse is also a rebuke to those who thought that their idols or their priests could somehow absorb their sins or that they could sin freely and then pay someone else to perform religious rituals on their behalf. In the economics of the Hereafter, such transactions are void. The only currency is one's own faith and deeds.

This principle, enshrined in the earliest scriptures, is now reiterated in the Quran, showing the consistency of the divine message regarding individual moral responsibility.

=39=

The second principle quoted from the earlier scriptures builds directly on the first. Not only is no one burdened with another's sin, but each person will have only what they themselves strived for.

The Arabic "sa'a" means to strive, to endeavour, to exert effort with a goal in mind. The verse establishes a direct and exclusive link between a person's own effort and their reward. You will not be rewarded for the deeds of your ancestors, your spouse, or your community. Your reward in the Hereafter is the fruit of your own planting, your own struggle, your own sacrifice.

This is a profoundly motivating principle. It tells the believer that no effort is wasted. Every sincere act of worship, every struggle against temptation, every act of patience in hardship, every step taken in the path of knowledge and righteousness, is being recorded and will be returned to you in full. There is no cosmic injustice where the hardworking person is forgotten and the lazy person is gifted. The world may sometimes seem to distribute rewards unfairly, but the Hereafter is the realm of perfect equity.

This verse also demolishes any notion of spiritual complacency. A person born into a righteous family cannot coast on their family's piety. A person with a saintly parent must still do their own work.

This emphasis on personal endeavour gave rise to the dynamic, action-oriented spirit of early Islam. The companions understood that Paradise is earned through the sweat of the brow and the tears of the heart, not through lineage or wishful thinking.

The verse also has a beautiful nuance: what one has is what one endeavoured towards. This means that the intention and the effort are what count, even if the outcome is not achieved due to circumstances beyond one's control. The one who sincerely strives for a good deed but is prevented from completing it is rewarded for their striving.

This principle is a well of hope for every believer who faces obstacles. The effort itself, when coupled with sincere faith, is the treasure that will be presented on the Day of Judgment.

=40=

The third principle continues the theme of accountability by stating that the outcome of a person's endeavours will be seen. The Arabic "sa'yahu sawfa yura" means that their striving, their effort, their deeds, will be presented and made visible. On the Day of Judgment, every deed, whether hidden or open, will be brought to light. Nothing will remain concealed. The record of deeds will be opened, and the person will see every action they performed, every word they spoke, and every intention they harboured. This seeing is not merely informational; it is a confrontation. The person will be faced with the undeniable evidence of their own life. The righteous will see their good deeds and rejoice, while the evildoers will see their sins and be filled with shame and terror. The transparency of that Day is absolute. In this world, we can hide our sins from others, and we can even fool ourselves. But on that Day, the veil will be lifted.

This principle is a powerful deterrent against secret sins. The believer is trained to live with the constant awareness that even the most private action is being recorded and will one day be displayed.

Yet, it is also a source of comfort. The good deeds that no one else knew about, the quiet charity, the tears shed in the night, the silent struggles, will all be seen and acknowledged. The believer who felt unrecognised and unappreciated in the world will find that nothing was lost. The seeing of the endeavour is also a prelude to the next principle, which is the full reward. The record is presented so that the reward can be based on clear evidence. The divine court operates with perfect transparency. The accused will see their own record and will have no grounds for objection.

This principle, like the previous ones, is stated as part of the scriptures of Moses and Abraham, emphasising that the Day of Judgment with its detailed accountability has always been a core part of the prophetic message. It is not an invention of the Quran; it is the eternal truth that every prophet came to affirm.
