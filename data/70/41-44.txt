=41=

This verse completes the powerful oath that began in the previous verse. After swearing by the Lord of all the points of sunrise and sunset that He is truly capable, Allah now clarifies what that capability entails: “of replacing them with others better than them.” The “them” refers directly to the disbelieving mockers who rush toward the Prophet in groups and arrogantly expect the Garden of Bliss despite their rejection. Allah is fully able to remove them entirely from existence and bring forth a new creation, a people who are better in faith, in obedience, and in character.

The word “better” is profound. It shows that the value of a people is not in their lineage, wealth, or worldly power, but in their spiritual quality. The Quraysh prided themselves on being the noblest tribe, the custodians of the Kaaba. They thought they were irreplaceable. This verse shatters that illusion. If they persist in mockery and disbelief, Allah can effortlessly replace them with a community that prays, gives the rightful share of their wealth, believes in the Day of Judgment, fears the punishment, guards their chastity, and is true to their trusts. That community, which at that moment was the small band of persecuted believers, was indeed better in the sight of Allah. History itself fulfilled this warning: the Quraysh leadership that mocked the Prophet was largely destroyed or humbled, and the believing community rose to become the carriers of faith.

The phrase “and We cannot be prevented from doing so” adds a final note of absolute sovereignty. No power in the universe can stop Allah from executing His will.

The word “masbuq” implies being outrun or overtaken; nothing can outmaneuver the Divine decree. If He decides to replace a people, no one can stand in His way or delay it.

This is a stern warning that should terrify those who take Allah’s forbearance for granted. At the same time, it is a source of immense comfort for the oppressed believers. The small, mocked minority is being told that Allah can tip the scales completely. Their patience is not in vain. The verse links back to the command of beautiful patience: the Prophet does not need to retaliate, because Allah’s capability is supreme. The replacement of a corrupt people with a better one is a recurring theme in the Quran, as in the story of Noah and other prophets. This verse, then, is both a promise and a threat, rooted in the undeniable power of the Lord of all easts and wests.

=42=

After the strong assertion of Allah’s ability to replace them, the surah delivers a command that carries a terrifying permissiveness: “So let them indulge in falsehood and amuse themselves until they face their Day, which they have been threatened with.” The word “so” connects this directive to the preceding oath of capability. Since Allah is fully able to replace them and since they stubbornly refuse to believe, they are left to their own devices. The command “let them” is not a positive instruction to sin; it is a threat in the form of abandonment. It means: if they choose to remain in their ignorant indulgence and amusement, allow them to do so. Their time is short. They will continue, and the Day of Judgment will arrive suddenly.

The phrase “indulge in falsehood” translates the Arabic “yakhudu,” which means to wade deeply into something, to engage in it heedlessly and immersively. They are absorbed in their false talk, their mockery of the Quran, and their denial of the resurrection. They amuse themselves with worldly pleasures, thinking that life is just a game. The verb “amuse themselves” suggests a frivolous, carefree play that distracts from serious reflection.

This is the same attitude that the “halu‘” nature produces when unchecked. The verse warns that this period of heedlessness has an endpoint: “until they face their Day.” That Day is not just any day; it is specifically “which they have been threatened with.” The threat has been delivered clearly through the Prophet, backed by vivid descriptions of the molten sky, the mountains like wool, and the raging Flame. They mocked it, but now they are told it is exactly what they will encounter. The tone is chilling. When a parent warns a child repeatedly and the child persists in dangerous play, the parent might finally say, “Fine, do as you wish, and you will see the consequences.” That is the sense here, but multiplied by the eternal weight of the Hereafter. For the believers, this verse is a lesson in detachment. They should not be consumed by anger at the mockers; rather, they should realize that the disbelievers are only being given rope. Their laughter will cease, and they will face what they denied.

The verse also implicitly calls the believers to avoid such heedless indulgence. Instead of wading into falsehood and amusement, they are to remain steadfast in prayer and all the noble qualities listed earlier. The contrast between the two groups could not be sharper.

=43=

The surah now vividly describes the moment the disbelievers will face their threatened Day: “the Day they will come forth from the graves swiftly, as if racing to an idol for a blessing.” The Day of Judgment begins with the resurrection, when all humanity will burst out of their graves in response to the Divine call.

The word “swiftly” (sira‘an) indicates a rapid, almost frantic emergence. There will be no slow, groggy awakening; it will be an urgent, uncontrollable rush. Then comes a startling comparison: “as if racing to an idol for a blessing.

” The Arabic word “nusub” refers to the stone altars or idols that the pre-Islamic Arabs used to venerate. They would gather around these idols during their pilgrimage and sacrificial rites, racing toward them and vying for the honour of reaching them first to receive what they believed to be a blessing. The imagery is deeply ironic and layered with meaning. Throughout their lives, these disbelievers hurried toward their false gods, their stone idols, with devotion and zeal.

In the Hereafter, they will hurry toward the scene of judgment with the same energy, but this time it will not be to an idol. They will be racing toward their own reckoning. Some commentators also say that “nusub” could refer to a flag or a standard that an army rallies around. They will run as if toward a banner, driven by a force they cannot resist. The verse exposes the habit of misplaced devotion. The energy they spent on false worship in this world will be mirrored in the helpless urgency on that Day, but now it brings no benefit. They ran to stones that could neither harm nor help them, and now they run to face the punishment they denied. Another layer of the comparison is the sheer speed and single-mindedness. In the pre-Islamic pilgrimage, tribes would race to the idol to gain prestige. Here, all of humanity races toward the gathering place with a similar haste, but with a vastly different destination. The verse thus mocks their earlier behaviour while warning of the inevitable.

For the believer, it is a reminder to direct one’s urgency and devotion toward the true goal: the pleasure of Allah, not the false idols of this world. It also reinforces that the Day of Judgment is not a calm, ordered assembly; it is a terrifying, chaotic emergence that strips away all human pride.

=44=

This final verse of the surah zooms in on the physical and emotional state of the disbelievers on that Day: “with eyes downcast, utterly covered with disgrace.” The Arabic word “khashi‘ah” describes eyes that are humiliated, lowered, and staring downward in abject shame. In this world, the disbelievers’ eyes were full of arrogance and mockery; they would glance at the Prophet and the believers with contempt and pride. On that Day, the direction of their gaze will be reversed. They will not dare to look up. Their eyes will be fixed on the ground, weighed down by the horror of their situation.

The phrase “utterly covered with disgrace” (tarhaquhum dhillah) paints a picture of disgrace clinging to them like a garment, enveloping them completely. The word “dhillah” is intense humiliation, the complete opposite of the honour (ikram) that the faithful will enjoy in the Gardens, as mentioned in verse thirty-five. This disgrace is not just an external punishment; it is an internal state of worthlessness and regret that overwhelms them. They are not just physically punished; their very souls are crushed with shame. The contrast with the believers is stark. The believers were those who guarded their chastity, were honest in their testimony, and feared their Lord’s punishment. They walked on earth with modesty and dignity. Now they are held in honour. The disbelievers, who strutted arrogantly and mocked the truth, are now covered in disgrace.

The final sentence, “That is the Day they have always been warned of,” brings the entire surah full circle. From the very first verse, where a challenger demanded a punishment bound to come, the surah has been describing this Day and warning of it. Every image—the molten sky, the severed family ties, the raging Flame ripping scalps—was a warning. Now the surah closes by reminding them that all those warnings were true. The warning came through the Prophet and through the Quran, but they treated it as a joke. Now they see it with their own downcast eyes, and it is too late. This conclusion serves as both a final, sobering threat to the disbelievers and a profound reassurance to the believers. The believers endured mockery and hardship while clinging to beautiful patience, and they are now vindicated. The surah that began with a mocker’s challenge ends with the absolute fulfilment of the warning he mocked. It is a masterful structure, leaving the listener with an overwhelming sense of the reality and certainty of the Hereafter.
