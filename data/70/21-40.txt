=21=

The previous verse described the human tendency to become intensely distressed when touched by hardship. Now the surah reveals the other side of the same impatient nature: “and withholding when touched with good.” When Allah bestows blessings—wealth, health, status, or comfort—this raw human disposition clings to those gifts and refuses to share them.

The Arabic word “manu‘a” means to hold back greedily, to prevent others from accessing what one possesses, and to become stingy. It is the instinct to gather and hoard, as mentioned earlier in the context of those summoned by the Fire. This withholding is not merely a financial issue; it can also manifest in withholding kindness, knowledge, or even simple gratitude. The person touched with good often forgets that the good came from Allah and imagines that it is his own permanent possession. So he tightens his grip, fearing that any sharing will diminish him.

This is a direct symptom of the “halu‘” mentioned in verse nineteen—an agitated, anxious condition that panics at loss and hoards in gain. The contrast is sharp: when evil touches him, he falls apart; when good touches him, he becomes miserly. Both reactions are rooted in a lack of trust in Allah and a failure to see beyond the immediate moment. The verse exposes a profound psychological truth: the same heart that cannot patiently bear trial also cannot generously handle prosperity. Without spiritual training, both adversity and ease become traps. Adversity leads to despair, and ease leads to arrogance and selfishness. The surah is preparing to introduce the transformative power of faith and worship, which will be described in the following verses. The faithful are those who break free from this cycle. They are patient in hardship and generous in ease. This verse, then, serves as a mirror. It asks the listener: when good touches you, do you open your hands or clench your fists? It also sets the stage for the beautiful exception that begins with “except those who pray,” showing that prayer and the qualities that follow can cure this deep-seated miserliness and make a person a channel of Allah’s blessings rather than a dead end.

=22=

After diagnosing the human condition with its restless impatience and its flip-flopping between despair and greed, Allah opens the door of hope with a single, powerful exception: “except those who pray.” The prayer here, “salah,” is not just the physical ritual. It is the act of turning to Allah, standing before Him, and realigning the soul. The ones who pray are taken out of the default, destructive pattern because they are connected to the source of all stability. The impatient nature described earlier is a condition of the soul left to its own devices, buffeted by events. But when a human being regularly stands in prayer, he or she anchors the heart in the recognition that there is a Lord who gives and withholds, who heals and tests. This relationship completely transforms the reaction to good and evil. When evil touches a praying person, he turns to prayer for patience, rather than disintegrating into panic. When good touches him, he remembers that it is from Allah and that it carries a responsibility, so he gives rather than withholds. The verse singles out prayer as the first and most central quality of the faithful because it is the foundation upon which all other virtues are built. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that the first deed for which a person will be held accountable on the Day of Judgment is the prayer. If it is sound, the rest of the deeds will be sound. This exception reminds us that the human default can be overcome. The “halu‘” is not an unchangeable fate; it is like a wild horse that can be tamed through the discipline of worship.

Moreover, the placement here is subtle. The surah began with the deniers who mocked the Day of Judgment, then described the horrors of that Day, and then diagnosed their spiritual sickness. Now it offers the medicine: prayer. The disbelievers refused to bow to Allah, so they remained trapped in their anxious hoarding and mockery. The believers, by praying, step out of that prison. The prayer is what shifts a person from being a mere creature of impulses to being a servant of the Most High.

=23=

This verse adds a crucial modifier to the quality of prayer: those who are “consistently performing their prayers.” The Arabic word “da’imoon” means perpetual, constant, unceasing. It is not enough to pray once in a while, or during a crisis, or only when motivation strikes. The faithful are those who have made their prayers a permanent, uninterrupted feature of their lives. They do not abandon the prayer when they are busy, tired, or prosperous. The prayer becomes like a steady stream of water that never dries up. The Prophet, peace be upon him, was asked which deeds are most beloved to Allah, and he replied, “The most constant of them, even if they are few.

” This emphasis on consistency teaches us that the transformative power of prayer is not in an occasional burst of intense devotion but in the regular, disciplined rhythm that reshapes the soul over time. The prayer times are spread throughout the day—dawn, noon, afternoon, sunset, night—ensuring that the believer repeatedly returns to Allah. Each return reinforces the cure for impatience and greed. When a person consistently prays, the habit of turning to Allah becomes stronger than the habit of panicking at hardship or clinging to wealth.

The verse also implicitly refutes those who claim to believe but neglect their prayers due to laziness or worldly distractions. Such negligence is a sign that the root disease of “halu‘” has not been cured. By mentioning consistency right after naming prayer, Allah is telling us that the prayer’s benefit depends on its permanence. A broken connection is almost as ineffective as no connection. For the companions and the early Muslims, this verse was a reminder to guard the five daily prayers with their hearts and not just with their limbs. The constant prayer becomes a fortress; every time the world shakes the believer, the next prayer is only a few hours away, offering a chance to regain composure, to shed the accumulated impatience, and to realign with the Lord of the pathways of ascent.

=24=

Moving from the relationship with Allah to the relationship with wealth, the faithful are described as those “who give the rightful share of their wealth.” The Arabic term “haqqun ma‘lum” means a known, determined right. This refers primarily to the obligatory zakat, but it also encompasses any right that the Shariah has fixed upon wealth, such as charity to relatives, expiations, and even voluntary giving that a person has made a regular habit.

The phrase teaches a profound shift in mindset: the poor and the needy have a “right” in the wealth of the well-off. It is not merely a favour or a gift from the rich to the poor; it is a debt that the wealthy owe to the vulnerable by the command of Allah. This understanding directly cures the disease of withholding described in verse twenty-one. A person who is naturally miserly and hoards when good touches him can only break free by recognizing that his wealth is not entirely his own. A portion of it belongs to others, and he is merely a trustee. By giving this rightful share, he detaches his heart from the love of hoarding and purifies his soul.

The placement of this quality right after prayer is significant. The Prophet, peace be upon him, often paired prayer and zakat together in the Quran. Prayer connects the servant to his Lord, and zakat connects the servant to his fellow human beings. Both are essential for a complete faith. The one who prays but withholds the rightful share has not yet cured the “halu‘” disease, because he still clings to good with a closed hand. The early Muslims in Mecca, where this surah was revealed, were often persecuted and poor.

Yet even in that hardship, the principle of giving was instilled. Those who had little were encouraged to share. The verse challenges the believer to look at his wealth and ask: have I given the known right that Allah has placed upon me? The “rightful share” is a known amount, not a vague feeling of generosity, meaning that faith requires concrete, measurable action that overcomes the selfish whims of the impatient soul.

=25=

The verse now specifies the recipients of that rightful share: “to the beggar and the poor.” The Arabic distinguishes between two categories. The “sa’il” is the one who asks, who stretches out his hand and makes his need known. The “mahrum” is the poor person who does not beg, perhaps out of dignity or shyness, yet remains deprived. By mentioning both, the verse teaches the believer to give both to those who openly request help and to those who suffer in silence. The sa’il is easy to identify, but the mahrum requires awareness and effort. The faithful community is expected to actively seek out those hidden poor rather than waiting for them to beg. This care for the mahrum reflects a deep sensitivity; it shows that the cure for hoarding goes beyond merely responding to requests. It involves a proactive concern for the welfare of others. In a society like pre-Islamic Mecca, the poor and the deprived were often neglected and looked down upon. The tribal chiefs and wealthy merchants hoarded their riches and paid no attention to the weak. This surah overturns that value system completely. The true believers are those who have established a recognized right in their wealth for these two groups.

The pairing with the previous verse shows that giving is not optional charity; it is a binding obligation and a right. It also connects to the earlier image of the wicked person who would ransom himself with his entire clan on the Day of Judgment. That person hoarded wealth in the world and refused to give the beggar and the poor their due. Now, in the Hellfire, he offers everything to escape, but it is too late. The faithful, by giving the rightful share in this life, have already released themselves from that destructive love of wealth. They have invested in their own salvation. This instruction, revealed in Mecca, laid the foundation for the later detailed system of zakat in Medina, showing that the principle of caring for the poor was integral to Islam from the very beginning.

=26=

The next quality of the faithful is that they “firmly believe in the Day of Judgment.” The Arabic construction “yusaddiqoona bi yawm id-deen” implies not just a vague acceptance but an active, heart-felt confirmation.

The word “deen” here means recompense—the Day when every soul will be repaid for what it earned. This belief is the anchor that keeps all the other qualities in place. Why does a person pray constantly? Because he believes he will stand before his Lord on that Day. Why does a person give the rightful share of his wealth? Because he knows that his wealth will be accounted for. Why does a person restrain his impatience and fear? Because he is certain that a just judgment is coming.

The earlier part of the surah described the horrors of that Day in vivid detail: the molten sky, the mountains like wool, the severed relationships, and the raging Flame. The faithful do not see these as distant myths; they hold them as absolutely true, and this conviction shapes their actions.

The placement of this quality here also contrasts with the disbelievers who saw the Day as impossible. The faithful are the opposite: they “firmly believe.” This belief is not a blind leap; it is based on the revelation of Allah and the signs within creation and within the soul. It becomes a lens through which every event is interpreted. When touched with evil, the believer remembers the Day of Judgment and says, “This trial is temporary; the ultimate justice is with Allah.” When touched with good, he remembers the Day and says, “This blessing is a test; I will be asked about it. ” Thus, firm belief in the Day of Recompense directly cures the two symptoms of impatience: distress at evil and withholding at good. The surah is building a complete portrait of the transformed human being. Prayer, charity, and belief in the Hereafter form a triangle of faith that stabilizes the restless soul. Without this belief, all other efforts risk collapsing under the weight of worldly pressures.

=27=

The description of the faithful deepens with an inner quality: “and those who fear the punishment of their Lord.” This fear, “khawf,” is not a paralyzing terror that makes one despair of mercy; it is a reverential awe and a constant awareness that Allah’s punishment is a real possibility if one falls into disobedience. The faithful do not feel secure from the consequences of their sins. They live with a balanced consciousness that combines hope in Allah’s mercy and fear of His justice. The verse specifically links the fear to “the punishment of their Lord,” not to an abstract concept. It is personal: their Lord, who is also the Most Compassionate, can punish. This fear protects a person from becoming complacent. A believer might pray, give charity, and profess belief, but still trembles inwardly at the thought of the Day of Judgment, knowing that no deed is perfect and that only Allah’s mercy can ultimately grant entry into Paradise. This quality stands in sharp contrast to the disbelievers who mocked the warning and arrogantly demanded the punishment. They had no fear whatsoever. The faithful, on the other hand, have a sensitive heart that shudders at the mention of Hellfire. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that if you knew what I know, you would laugh little and weep much. That is the fruit of this fear. It is also the antidote to the impatience described earlier. The “halu‘” person panics only at worldly loss. The believer fears something far greater—the displeasure of the Lord and the punishment of the Hereafter—so worldly hardships become relatively small in comparison. This fear fuels the beautiful patience commanded in verse five. It helps the believer endure trials because he is more afraid of failing the test than of the test itself.

The verse also subtly prepares for the next verse’s explanation that no one should feel secure from this punishment.

=28=

This verse explains the reason behind the fear mentioned in the previous one: “knowing that none should feel secure from their Lord’s punishment.” The faithful are fully aware of a fundamental spiritual principle: absolute security from Allah’s plan is a sign of heedlessness. Even the most righteous person cannot say with certainty, “I am safe from the punishment,” because the heart can change, the end of life is unknown, and true judgment belongs only to Allah. This knowledge prevents spiritual arrogance. It is the opposite of the attitude of the disbelievers who saw the Day as impossible and felt no fear.

The verse teaches that fear of Allah’s punishment is not a sign of weak faith; rather, it is a sign of deep understanding. The one who knows Allah’s power and His rights over His servants can never feel completely immune.

This is not a call to despair; rather, it is a call to constant vigilance. The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught that no one enters Paradise by his deeds alone, but only by the mercy of Allah. When the companions asked, “Even you, O Messenger of Allah?” he replied, “Even me, unless Allah envelops me in His mercy.” This demonstrates the perfect balance: the greatest of creation did not feel secure, yet he was the most hopeful in Allah’s mercy. The faithful, then, live between fear and hope. The fear keeps them from sin, and the hope keeps them from giving up.

In the context of the surah, this knowledge is the inner engine that drives the beautiful patience and all the other qualities. If a person felt secure from punishment, he might slack in prayer or withhold the rightful share. But knowing that none is safe, he strives continually.

This verse also ties back to the challenger in the opening. That man felt completely secure, mocking the punishment. The faithful are nothing like him. They take the warning seriously, and that seriousness saves them from the very punishment they fear.

=29=

The surah now turns to another essential dimension of the faithful character: “and those who guard their chastity.” The Arabic phrase “hafizoona li-furujihim” literally means they are guardians over their private parts.

This is a comprehensive expression for sexual purity and modesty. It means they protect themselves from all unlawful sexual relations, from immodest gazing, from lewd speech, and from any act that violates the boundaries set by Allah. Chastity is a hallmark of the true believer, and it directly counteracts the impatient, desire-driven nature described earlier. The “halu‘” person is someone who is a slave to immediate gratification. Whether it is the gratification of complaining when touched by evil or hoarding when touched by good, the root problem is the inability to delay or control impulses. Sexual desire is one of the most powerful human impulses. The faithful are those who do not let this impulse rule them; they channel it within the limits set by Allah. Guarding chastity requires patience, self-discipline, and a deep sense of accountability. This quality connects to the fear of punishment and the belief in the Day of Judgment. One who truly believes he will stand before Allah and who fears His punishment will not risk eternal loss for a fleeting pleasure. In the Meccan context, immorality and indecency were rampant. The surah is setting a new standard of purity for the emerging Muslim community. It is also worth noting that guarding chastity applies equally to men and women; the Arabic term is gender-inclusive in this context, referring to all believers. This verse leads naturally into the next two verses, which define the lawful outlet for sexual desire. By stating “those who guard their chastity” first, Allah praises the principle of restraint before mentioning the permissible exceptions. This order teaches that the default attitude of a believer is one of modesty and control, not one of seeking to fulfil desire by any means.

=30=

After establishing the high standard of guarding chastity, the verse immediately clarifies the lawful exceptions: “except with their wives or those bondwomen in their possession, for then they are free from blame.” The faithful are not expected to suppress the natural desire entirely; rather, Allah has provided pure and honourable outlets.

The first and primary outlet is marriage to one’s wives. The marital relationship is described in the Quran as a sign of Allah’s mercy, characterized by love, tranquillity, and mutual rights. Within this bond, intimacy is not only permissible but an act that can bring reward when done with the right intention.

The second outlet, “those bondwomen in their possession,” refers to a specific historical legal category: female captives of war who were rightfully owned under the rules of Islamic warfare as it existed at that time. This was a regulated institution, not a licence for exploitation. The Quran and Sunnah set strict rules for the treatment of such women, including the fact that any children born to them were free and that their masters were responsible for their well-being. It is important to understand that this ruling was part of the social reality of the time, in a world where slavery was a universal institution. Islam did not initiate it; rather, it regulated it heavily and encouraged emancipation as a highly virtuous act. The permission here is coupled with the statement “for then they are free from blame,” meaning that within these two boundaries, fulfilling one’s desires is not sinful; rather, it is something natural and clean. The verse thus strikes a perfect balance between asceticism and licentiousness. It does not demand celibacy, which can be unnatural, nor does it allow promiscuity. The faithful channel their desires through these lawful means and thereby guard their chastity outside of them.

This is a profound wisdom that protects society, lineage, and individual honour.

=31=

The chapter closes the discussion on chastity with a firm boundary: “but whoever seeks beyond that are the transgressors.” The phrase “beyond that” means any sexual activity outside the two lawful categories of marriage and the bondwomen. Whoever crosses these limits has stepped into the realm of transgression.

The Arabic word “al-‘adoon” means those who exceed the bounds, who are aggressive in their violation of Allah’s laws. This includes adultery, fornication, homosexuality, and any other form of illicit relationship. The verse is absolutely clear that there is no third permissible category. In a time when modern ideas may try to blur these lines, the Quranic ruling stands firm. The transgression here is not a light matter; it is an act of injustice against one’s own soul, against the other person involved, and against the broader social order. The surah earlier described the Fire that summons those who turned away from truth and hoarded wealth. Sexual transgression is often linked in the Quran with these very spiritual diseases. It springs from the same impatient, gratification-seeking nature that cannot wait for what is lawful and wholesome. By calling them “transgressors,” Allah indicates that such actions are not merely mistakes; they are acts of rebellion against the limits set by the All-Wise.

The verse also serves as a warning to the believers not to be complacent. The faithful are not defined by never feeling desire, but by respecting the divine boundaries even when desire pulls them elsewhere. This requires the beautiful patience that the surah commanded the Prophet to have. The patient person restrains himself in the face of temptation, knowing that the lawful outlets are sufficient and that the Hereafter is far better. The connection to the Day of Judgment is again evident: on that Day, the transgressors will face the consequences of their excesses, while those who guarded their chastity will be among those held in honour in the Gardens, as described later. This verse completes the picture of personal purity that is foundational to the faithful character.

=32=

The surah continues to paint the portrait of the faithful by highlighting their moral integrity in social dealings: “also those who are true to their trusts and covenants.” The word “amanat” (trusts) covers a vast range of responsibilities—everything that is entrusted to a person by Allah or by other people. This includes the trust of faith itself, the trust of one’s body and faculties, the trust of secrets, property, positions of authority, and contracts.

The word “‘ahd” (covenants) refers to solemn agreements, pledges, and promises, whether made with Allah, such as the covenant of faith, or with fellow human beings, such as treaties, business agreements, and marriage contracts. The faithful are those who carefully observe and fulfil these obligations. They do not betray a trust, break a promise, or violate an agreement. This quality is a direct cure for the impatient, selfish nature described earlier. The “halu‘” person is primarily concerned with his own immediate interest; he might betray a trust if it benefits him or break a covenant if it becomes inconvenient. The believer, however, is anchored by the fear of Allah and the belief in the Day of Judgment. He knows that every trust and covenant will be examined on that Day. The Prophet, peace be upon him, was known as Al-Amin, the Trustworthy, even before his prophethood, and this verse reflects the highest standard of character that he exemplified. In the Meccan context, the disbelievers would often break treaties and betray trusts when dealing with the weak.

The surah establishes that the community of believers must be completely opposite. Trustworthiness in all matters, big or small, is a non-negotiable quality of those who pray, give zakat, and fear the punishment. It also connects to the earlier theme of wealth: giving the rightful share is part of fulfilling the trust of wealth. The faithful are reliable; their word is their bond. This creates a society of mutual trust and stability, which stands as a living proof of the truth of Islam.

=33=

A specific application of trustworthiness is highlighted next: “and who are honest in their testimony.” The Arabic phrase means they stand firm in their testimonies, meaning they give witness truthfully, without distortion, concealment, or bias. Testimony here includes formal legal witness in courts, but it also extends to any statement of truth that affects others’ rights or reputations. The faithful do not let personal feelings, family ties, wealth, or fear of powerful people sway their testimony. They say what is true, even if it is against themselves or their loved ones. This quality is deeply connected to the fear of the punishment of their Lord. A person who fears the Day of Judgment will not lie in testimony, because he knows the ultimate Judge hears every word. The surah earlier described the horrors of that Day, and the faithful keep those horrors in mind when they speak. The disbelievers, on the other hand, often bore false witness to mock the Prophet and oppress the weak. The verse calls the believers to be the exact opposite. This honesty is also a cure for the “halu‘” condition. The impatient, anxious soul might be tempted to twist the truth when it benefits him or to hide facts when they harm his interests. But the believer trains himself to put truth above immediate gain.

The placement of this quality after trusts and covenants emphasizes that truthful testimony is one of the most critical trusts. Many injustices in society occur because witnesses fail in their duty. The Quran tells the believers to be standard-bearers of justice, people whose words can be relied upon absolutely. This creates a community where truth is valued and falsehood is exposed. It is an essential part of the beautiful patience and the consistent prayer; a person who stands before Allah five times a day cannot then go and lie with his tongue. The mouth that recites Allah’s words must not be polluted with false testimony.

=34=

The list of the faithful’s qualities, which began with prayer, now concludes by returning to prayer: “and who are properly observant of their prayers.” At first glance, this might seem like a repetition of verse twenty-three, which said “consistently performing their prayers. ” However, a closer look reveals a different emphasis.

The Arabic here uses the word “yuhafizoon,” which means they guard, preserve, and protect their prayers. While consistency referred to the uninterrupted continuity of prayer, this proper observance refers to the quality, the conditions, the timings, and the spiritual presence within the prayer. The faithful do not just perform the prayer habitually; they guard it against negligence, haste, distraction, and anything that would diminish its reward. They maintain its pillars, its recommended acts, and its outward and inward etiquettes. They pray on time, with concentration, fulfilling the requirements of bowing and prostration, and striving for a heart that is present. This bookending of the list with prayer is profoundly beautiful. It shows that prayer is both the beginning and the end, the foundation and the pinnacle of all virtues. All the other qualities—charity, belief in the Day of Judgment, fear, chastity, trustworthiness, honesty—are nourished and protected by the prayer. And the prayer itself needs to be guarded. It is easy to start praying, but the challenge is to maintain its quality over a lifetime. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said, “The first thing the servant will be held accountable for on the Day of Judgment is his prayer. If it is sound, he will succeed; if it is corrupt, he will fail. ” This verse reminds the believer that the prayer is a precious treasure that can be lost through carelessness. Guarding it means arranging one’s life around the prayer times, not squeezing prayers into the gaps of a busy schedule.

In the context of the surah, this proper observance is what finally cures the impatient, hasty nature. The soul that learns to stand calmly and with full attention before Allah five times a day gradually sheds its restless anxiety and finds its true centre.

=35=

After detailing the beautiful qualities of the faithful, the surah announces their reward: “These will be in Gardens, held in honour.” The pronoun “these” points directly to those who embody the characteristics just listed, from prayer to charity to fear of punishment to guarding chastity and trusts. The reward is multifaceted. First, they will be in “Gardens,” the plural indicating expanses of lush, eternal bliss with all that the heart desires. The Garden is the ultimate place of security, the opposite of the restless anxiety of the “halu‘” nature. There will be no distress when touched with evil, and no need to withhold when touched with good, because all good will flow endlessly without fear of loss. Second, they are “held in honour.

” The Arabic word “mukramoon” means they are honoured, treated with generosity, and given high status. This is the exact opposite of the humiliation that the disbelievers will face on the Day of Judgment. The mocker who demanded the punishment will be summoned by the raging Flame and have his scalp ripped off, while the faithful believers will be honoured in the presence of Allah. This honour is not just an external gift; it reflects the inner nobility they cultivated through patience, generosity, and moral integrity. They guarded their chastity in private, so Allah will honour them in public. They were honest in their testimony when it was difficult, so Allah will grant them a station of truth. The contrast with earlier verses is powerful. The wicked person wished to ransom himself with his children, spouse, and entire clan. His relationships collapsed. The faithful, however, did not wait for that Day; they gave the rightful share to the poor, maintained their trusts, and prayed constantly. Now they are not bargaining with loved ones in terror; they are together in honour, perhaps reunited with those same loved ones who shared their faith. This verse serves as the climax of the portrait of the faithful, providing a vision of hope that motivates the believer to persevere in these qualities despite the mockery of the disbelievers that will be addressed next.

=36=

Having completed the description of the faithful and their reward, the surah turns sharply back to the mockers. The tone becomes confrontational and rhetorical: “So what is the matter with the disbelievers that they rush head-long towards you O Prophet?

” The Arabic phrase “fama li allatheena kafaru” expresses astonishment and rebuke. It is as if to say: after all that has been clarified about the Day of Judgment, the punishment, and the qualities of the saved, what excuse do these people have? Why do they behave so strangely?

The image is of the disbelievers “rushing” toward the Prophet. In Mecca, whenever the Prophet would recite the Quran or speak about the Hereafter, groups of disbelievers would crowd around him, not to listen sincerely, but to interrupt, to argue, and to mock.

The word “muhti‘een” implies that they come with their necks stretched out, eager and hasty, not with the calmness of a seeker of truth but with the agitation of a challenger. This mirrors the “halu‘” nature diagnosed earlier. Their very body language reflects impatience and arrogance. They do not sit and reflect; they rush to attack. The verse exposes their inner state. Their rush is not a sign of genuine interest but a symptom of their disease.

The question “what is the matter with them?” is meant to make the listener pause and realize the irrationality of their behaviour. If the Day of Judgment is truly impossible, as they claim, why do they exert so much energy mocking it? Why not simply ignore it? Their frantic rush to disprove the Prophet actually shows that the message is shaking something deep inside them. For the Prophet, this verse brings comfort; Allah is pointing out their absurd behaviour, validating his message and showing him that their mockery is a reflection of their own inner turmoil, not a weakness in the truth.

=37=

The description of the disbelievers’ behaviour becomes even more vivid: “from the right and the left, in groups to mock you?” They surround the Prophet from all sides. They come in separate bands, gathering together not for a unified, serious inquiry but to form mocking circles. The phrases “from the right and the left” indicate that they encircle him, leaving no direction free. This could refer to different factions of Quraysh, each approaching from its own angle, all converging on the Prophet with the same hostile intent. They sit in groups, making snide remarks, laughing at the Quran, and challenging his claims. The purpose is explicitly stated: “to mock you.” This historical scene reflects the intense psychological pressure the early Muslims faced. The Prophet was not attacked physically at this stage as much as he was subjected to a relentless campaign of ridicule. The verse exposes the pettiness of their actions. While the surah speaks of a Day fifty thousand years long and a raging Flame, these disbelievers are concerned with forming small groups to sneer. The contrast is deliberate. The faithful are busy with prayer, charity, and guarding their chastity, while the disbelievers are busy forming circles to mock.

The verse also carries a subtle warning: just as they now surround the Prophet from right and left in mockery, on the Day of Judgment the punishment will surround them from all sides with no escape. Their mocking groups are a faint and foolish imitation of the encirclement they will later experience. For the believers reading this later, the verse serves as a reminder not to be like those who gather to belittle the truth. Instead, believers should gather to support each other in faith. The mockery is a test of the beautiful patience that was commanded earlier. The Prophet is to endure, and this verse reassures him that Allah sees exactly what they are doing.

=38=

The surah now delivers a sharp rhetorical question that exposes the absurdity of the disbelievers’ aspirations: “Does every one of them expect to be admitted into a Garden of Bliss?” After detailing the demanding qualities of the faithful who will be in Gardens held in honour, Allah asks whether these mockers think they can attain the same reward without any of those qualities. They reject faith, mock the Messenger, neglect prayer, hoard wealth, and transgress moral boundaries, yet somehow they imagine that if there is a Hereafter, they will be among the honoured?

This is the height of self-deception. The question is a powerful piece of logical reasoning. The Garden of Bliss is for those who purified their souls through the beautiful patience and the consistent worship described earlier. The disbelievers have done none of that. By what logic, then, would they enter it?

The verse also challenges a common Meccan assumption. The Quraysh sometimes argued that even if resurrection happened, their wealth and status in this world indicated that they were favoured by God and would be favoured in the next as well. This verse demolishes that claim. Worldly prosperity is no sign of Divine approval; it is a test, and their response to the test—mockery and rejection—proves their failure.

The word “expect” (yatma‘u) implies a greedy hope, a desire without foundation. It connects back to the description of the human being as “halu‘”: the disbelievers want immediate gratification and also expect eternal bliss with no effort. The verse serves as a wake-up call. It tells them that the Garden of Bliss is not a random lottery; it is the reward for a specific way of life. If they are not willing to walk that path, their expectation is nothing but a delusion. This question also comforts the believers who see the prosperity of the mockers and wonder; Allah assures them that this prosperity does not translate into success in the Hereafter.

=39=

The answer to the previous rhetorical question is a decisive “But no!” (Kalla). The disbelievers will not be admitted to the Garden of Bliss with that attitude. Then Allah provides a reason that humbles them: “Indeed, they already know what We created them from.” This refers to their lowly, humble origin. The Quran repeatedly mentions that the human being is created from a drop of despised fluid—from dust and then from a sperm-drop. The disbelievers know this fact perfectly well; it is not a hidden secret. They have seen the process of birth and reproduction. They are aware that their bodies, of which they are so proud, originated from something so insignificant. How, then, can they be so arrogant as to mock the Messenger and feel entitled to the highest paradise? Their knowledge of their humble origin should have led them to humility and gratitude, not to rebellion and self-importance. The verse implies that their intellectual arrogance is inconsistent with their biological reality. If they reflect on their own creation, they will see the power of Allah who brought them from that lowly fluid to a fully formed human being. That same power can certainly resurrect them, and that same Creator deserves their worship, not their mockery.

This is a call to self-awareness. The “halu‘” person is so consumed by the present moment—his wealth, his status—that he forgets where he came from and where he is going. The faithful, on the other hand, are constantly reminded by prayer and belief in the Day of Judgment of their origin and their end.

The verse also connects to the opening theme: the challenger demanded a punishment, but he should have remembered that he is not a mighty being; he is a creation from a drop of fluid, utterly dependent on the Lord of the pathways of ascent. This knowledge is a proof against them; they have no excuse because the signs are within their own beings.

The surah now reaches a powerful oath that reaffirms the central theme: “So, I do swear by the Lord of all the points of sunrise and sunset that We are truly capable.” The oath is by Allah Himself, described with an attribute that highlights His complete mastery over the cosmos. The “points of sunrise and sunset” refer to the many positions on the horizon where the sun rises and sets throughout the year. As the earth’s tilt and orbit cause the sunrise and sunset points to shift gradually, they pass through numerous distinct locations. The Lord of all these points is the One who governs the entire celestial order with absolute precision. By swearing by this, Allah draws attention to the daily miracle of the sun’s movement, which the disbelievers take for granted. The One who manages this vast, complex system with such flawless regularity is surely “truly capable” of resurrecting the dead and replacing a disobedient people with others better than them. The oath is not made for the sake of the believers, who already believe; it is a rhetorical challenge to the disbelievers who see the Day as impossible. The sky, the sunrise, and the sunset are signs they witness every day. If they deny the resurrection, let them consider the sun’s journey.

The phrase “We are truly capable” is emphatic. The capability of Allah is not limited; He who can bring the sun from its many rising points and send it to its many setting points can easily bring forth a new creation.

This verse also connects back to the earlier image of the sky becoming like molten brass. The present order, including the orderly sunrise and sunset, will one day be transformed. The Lord of that order can end it and create a new one. The swearing by the Lord of the easts and wests is a profound way to ground the argument in observable reality, making the denial of the resurrection seem all the more unreasonable.
