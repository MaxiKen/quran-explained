=41=

Abraham’s magnificent prayer now turns from the establishment of worship to the deepest need of every soul: forgiveness. He implores, “Our Lord! Forgive me.” Even though he was a prophet of unshakeable faith and the intimate friend of the Merciful, Abraham did not consider himself beyond the need for divine pardon. He fully recognized that human beings, no matter how elevated their station, are never entirely free from shortcomings when measured against the infinite perfection of Allah. This humility before the Lord is the hallmark of true servanthood. He then extends the plea to his parents.

The Arabic word used is “wālidayya,” which refers to both his mother and father. This inclusion has drawn careful attention from scholars because Abraham’s father, Azar, was an idolater and ultimately died upon disbelief, as indicated in other Quranic passages. One well-grounded interpretation is that this prayer was offered earlier in Abraham’s life, before it became clear to him that his father would never embrace faith and was an enemy of Allah. When that reality became manifest, Abraham dissociated himself from his father and ceased seeking forgiveness for him, establishing the principle that one cannot pray for forgiveness for a polytheist after their death upon shirk. Another interpretation holds that “parents” here refers to his believing mother and father, as some traditions suggest his mother was a woman of faith, or it could refer to Adam and Eve as his ultimate parents. In any case, the verse teaches that praying for one’s parents is an act of profound righteousness, provided they are believers or died before the truth was clearly rejected. The supplication then expands to encompass “the believers,” demonstrating that the heart of a true servant does not stop at personal and familial concerns but embraces the entire community of faith across all times and places. Abraham finishes by anchoring the plea to a specific moment: “the Day when the judgment will come to pass.” He knows that forgiveness on that Day is the only real safety. This prayer is a timeless model for every Muslim, urging us to constantly seek forgiveness for ourselves, our parents, and the global community of believers while we still have the opportunity.

=42=

The surah turns from Abraham’s tender supplications to a direct reassurance addressed to the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. In the face of relentless persecution, mockery, and plotting by the Quraysh, a human heart might wonder why the wrongdoers seem to move freely without immediate punishment. The verse firmly dispels any such thought: “Do not think that Allah is unaware of what the wrongdoers do.” The divine attribute of complete awareness encompasses every hidden plot, every whispered slander, and every act of oppression. Nothing escapes His knowledge. The apparent freedom of the oppressors is not a sign of divine negligence; it is a calculated delay.

The phrase “He only delays them” reveals a profound divine strategy. The respite given to the wrongdoers is not for their benefit but an accumulation of evidence against them. Every additional day they persist in injustice increases their burden of sin. This delay leads to a specific, terrifying conclusion: a Day when their eyes will stare in horror.

The Arabic expression “tashkhaṣu fīhi al-abṣār” paints a vivid picture of eyes fixed and bulging, unable to blink or turn away from the overwhelming terror that confronts them. All the arrogance, the dismissive glances, and the haughty stares they once directed at the believers will be replaced by a frozen gaze of absolute fear. This verse comforted the early Muslims immensely, reminding them that the apparent victory of falsehood is temporary. The same principle applies today: when believers witness corruption and oppression flourishing unpunished, they must anchor their hearts in the certainty that the delay is only until a Day when the vision of the oppressors will be seized by horror. The verse connects directly to the preceding warnings of past nations and prepares the listener for the detailed scenes of the Day of Resurrection that follow, establishing that divine justice is never absent, only deferred to the wisest moment.

=43=

This verse completes the snapshot of the wrongdoers’ state on that Day of horror. After their eyes freeze in terror, they are depicted as “rushing forth.” They will move toward the summons in a frantic, helpless hurry, their previous composure completely shattered. They will run with heads raised unnaturally upward, a posture of disorientation and fear rather than dignity. The raised head is not a gesture of pride now but an involuntary reaction to the overwhelming awe of the scene. The description “never blinking” intensifies the earlier mention of staring eyes. The eyes remain open without the relief of a single blink, signifying a state of continuous, unrelieved shock. Blinking is a mercy that refreshes the eye and briefly shields it from a scene too intense; the removal of this mercy indicates the totality of the punishment. Then the verse reveals the inner condition: their hearts are void.

The Arabic word “hawā’” suggests an emptiness, a cavity devoid of substance. The hearts that were once filled with arrogance, schemes, love of the world, and hatred for the truth are now completely hollow. There is no courage to draw upon, no hope to cling to, and no intelligence to devise an escape. The void is a terrifying echo of the spiritual emptiness they cultivated in life when they filled their hearts with everything but the remembrance of Allah. On the Day when every inner secret is laid bare, they find nothing within to sustain them. This scene is the exact opposite of the believers whose hearts will be filled with peace and divine remembrance. For the contemporary reader, the verse serves as a powerful warning to examine what one is filling the heart with today. A heart fed on trivial distractions and arrogant denial will inevitably become a hollow void when faced with the ultimate reality, while a heart nourished by faith, humility, and the awe of Allah will find strength and firmness on that Day.

=44=

The Prophet is directly commanded to warn humanity of a Day that will arrive with sudden, overwhelming force. The punishment will overtake the wicked, and the verse zooms into their desperate reaction. From the depths of their torment, the wrongdoers will cry out with a plea that reveals a complete reversal of their worldly arrogance: “Our Lord! Delay us for a little while. ” In the world, they had decades of time, countless opportunities to listen and reflect, but they squandered them all. Now, when the reality has arrived, they beg for just a tiny extension—not years or even days, but a little while. Their promise is that if granted this brief return, they will respond to Allah’s call and follow the messengers.

This is the cry of a person who finally sees the truth with absolute clarity but has no time left to act upon it. However, the divine response cuts off all hope. It will be said to them, “Did you not swear before that you would never be removed to the next life?” In their earthly arrogance, the Quraysh and other deniers had sworn emphatic oaths that there was no resurrection, no afterlife, no judgment. They had mocked the very concept of being brought back to life and held accountable. Now that very resurrection they denied has become their inescapable prison. The rhetorical question exposes their hypocrisy and seals their fate. There is no going back. This verse serves as an urgent warning that the time for repentance is the present moment, not some imagined future moment on the brink of death or after it. The opportunity to respond to Allah’s call exists now through the message of the Prophet and the Quran. Once the Day arrives, the only thing that remains is the consequence.

The passage reminds every soul that oaths of denial spoken today will be the evidence used against one on the Day when the punishment overtakes.

=45=

The surah now directs the Meccan Arabs to a tangible, visible proof that they could not deny. As they traveled on their trade journeys to Yemen and Syria, they passed by the ruins of the peoples of ‘Ad, Thamud, the people of Lot, and others. These were not mythical tales but physical remains—crumbled palaces, empty wells, and abandoned cities—that they could see with their own eyes. The verse says these peoples “wronged themselves,” emphasizing that their destruction was not an arbitrary act of a capricious deity but the natural consequence of their own choices. The wrong they committed was primarily the denial of their messengers and the persistence in injustice, which corrupted their souls and societies.

The verse then states, “It was made clear to you how We dealt with them.” The clarity of this lesson was established through the stories in the Quran, the visible ruins, and the collective memory preserved among the Arabs. Allah did not destroy them without warning; He demonstrated the pattern repeatedly.

Finally, “We gave you many examples.” The examples encompass the detailed narratives, the parables, and the moral laws drawn from those histories. The provision of examples is a mercy designed to save people from repeating the same fatal mistakes. For the contemporary reader, this verse encourages the study of history through a spiritual lens. The ruins of past civilizations—whether ancient ones or more recent empires that collapsed due to moral decay—are silent testimonies to the divine law that injustice ultimately leads to downfall.

The verse also implicitly answers those who asked for a sign: the signs are already scattered across the landscape for those who travel with open eyes and an open heart. The lesson is that to ignore these clear examples and continue in the ways of those who wronged themselves is to walk blindly toward the same inevitable end.

=46=

After mentioning the ruined civilizations, the surah reveals another layer of their history: the intensity of their plotting against the messengers and the truth. The word “they devised every plot” indicates that the enemies of the messengers did not merely reject the message passively; they actively schemed using every available strategy—slander, economic boycott, threats of exile, and attempted assassination. The Quraysh themselves held secret meetings in Dār an-Nadwa to plan the murder of the Prophet, thinking their cleverness would erase the message. The verse immediately undercuts their pride: all of this plotting was “fully known to Allah. ” No secret meeting, no whispered conspiracy, no hidden strategy escapes the divine knowledge. The comfort for the Prophet lies precisely here: the enemy’s plans are not being woven in the dark, unseen; they are being woven under the full gaze of the Almighty who controls all outcomes.

The verse then declares that their plotting was not enough to overpower mountains, let alone Allah. The mountains in Quranic imagery represent immovable stability and immense mass. Even if the disbelievers’ schemes had the audacity to aim for the removal of mountains, they would utterly fail. The intended meaning is that their plots are pathetically weak compared to the power and plan of Allah. The mountains stand firm by Allah’s command; how then could a few human whispers and conspiracies defeat His divine will?

For the believer, this verse is an eternal source of peace. When facing the intrigues of those who oppose the truth—whether in personal life, in the workplace, or on a global scale—one can rest assured that every plot is known and that its ultimate reach is laughably limited. It cannot move the mountains of Allah’s decree. The plotting may cause temporary hardship, but it will collapse under its own weight at the time chosen by the All-Knowing.

=47=

Building on the previous assurance about the futility of plotting, the verse now directly addresses a thought that could potentially cross a human mind under severe pressure. The Prophet is told, “So do not think that Allah will fail to keep His promise to His messengers.” The promise here refers to the divine assurance given throughout the Quran that the messengers and their followers will ultimately be saved, and the persistent wrongdoers will be punished.

The word “khulf” for failing a promise implies a breach of trust, something absolutely impossible for Allah, who describes Himself as the One who never breaks His promise. The verse acknowledges the psychological weight of waiting for that promise to materialize. When years pass and the oppression continues, the human heart might feel a subtle whisper of doubt about the timing or the certainty of the victory. This verse slams the door on that whisper with a firm command: do not even entertain the thought. The reason is immediately given in the two closing attributes of Allah. He is Almighty, ‘Azīz, possessing irresistible power and honor. No force in the universe can prevent Him from executing His will. He is also “capable of punishment,” Dhū Intiqām, which translates more literally as the Possessor of Retribution. This attribute is not about uncontrolled anger but about perfect, calculated justice against those who deserve it. The promise to the messengers includes both the deliverance of the believers and the punishment of the wrongdoers, and both fall under the scope of His might and His right to retribution.

For every believer who has ever felt overwhelmed by the apparent dominance of falsehood, this verse provides a firm anchor. It redirects the mind from the apparent delay to the character of the One who made the promise. The victory may come in a form and at a time human beings do not expect, but it will certainly come, because behind the promise stands the Almighty who cannot be overpowered and who will inevitably settle all accounts.

=48=

This verse shifts focus to the cosmic scale of the Day of Judgment. The command “Watch for the Day” is a call to attentive anticipation, to live in constant awareness of that coming reality. The event is described with overwhelming imagery: the earth will be changed into a different earth, and the heavens as well. This transformation signals the complete end of the current order and the beginning of an entirely new creation. The earth we walk upon, with its familiar mountains, oceans, and cities, will be replaced by a new earth, flat and pristine, suitable for the gathering of all humanity. The heavens with their stars, galaxies, and celestial architecture will also be replaced. The hadith describes this new earth as a white, clean expanse with no landmarks, where no blood has been shed and no sin committed, a fitting arena for the supreme court of the Almighty. This change is not merely cosmetic; it marks the transition from the realm of testing to the realm of recompense.

The verse then declares that on that Day, all will appear before Allah. Every single human being, from Adam to the last infant, will stand exposed before the Creator. The names used for Allah here are deeply significant: the One, al-Wāḥid, emphasizes the absolute oneness of the deity before whom all false idols vanish. The Supreme, al-Qahhār, is the Irresistible Subduer who compels everything to submit to His will. On that Day, the supposed powers of kings, tyrants, and influential leaders will evaporate entirely. Only the One Supreme authority will remain.

For the believer, this verse reorients the perspective on all worldly struggles. The earth and heavens that seem so permanent are destined for a complete overhaul. The systems and powers that appear daunting today will be utterly dissolved, and every human will stand naked before the One who has no partner and no rival. This awareness cultivates a courage that is independent of earthly circumstances, rooted instead in the certainty of the final appearance before the One, the Supreme.

=49=

The description of the Day now moves from the cosmic to the specific and personal. The address “you will see” makes the scene vivid and immediate, as if placing the listener at the scene. The wicked, the mujrimūn, are those who earned the label of criminal through their persistent rejection of truth and their accumulation of sins. They will be bound together in chains. This binding is not random; it carries layers of meaning. On one level, it represents the physical restraint of those who once roamed the earth arrogantly, considering themselves free to do as they pleased. Now, their movement is completely controlled, their bodies weighed down and linked. On a deeper level, the chains connect partners in crime. Leaders and followers, those who led each other to sin and supported one another in defiance, will now be chained together in shared humiliation and punishment. The companionship of sin in the world, which they enjoyed and took pride in, becomes the chain of joint misery in the Hereafter.

The image also signifies the utter loss of individual power and the severance of any hope of escape. A chained person cannot flee, cannot fight, and cannot help. The proud chiefs of Mecca who once walked with their noses in the air will be dragged in chains.

For the believer, this verse serves as a stark caution about the company one keeps. If a friendship or an alliance is based on shared disobedience to Allah, that same bond will become an iron chain on the Day of Judgment. The verse therefore calls for a careful selection of companions in this world, urging the believer to form bonds of faith and righteousness that will, by contrast, become sources of light and comfort on that Day. The chains are the inevitable harvest of the sins they had woven together in life, now visible and inescapable.

=50=

The terrifying depiction continues with the clothing and appearance of the wicked. Their garments will be made of tar.

The Arabic word “qatirān” refers to a highly flammable, thick, pitch-black substance that melts and clings to the skin. When it burns, it produces an intense, sticky fire that cannot be easily extinguished. The garment is the direct opposite of the fine, luxurious clothing many of the arrogant wrongdoers flaunted in the world. They took pride in their silk and fine wool, using their attire to signal status and look down upon the poor believers. In Hell, their clothing is an instrument of torture, a sticky, burning layer that adheres to their bodies and intensifies the fire.

The verse then adds, “and their faces covered with flames. ” The face is the most honored part of the human body; a person’s dignity is closely tied to the face. To have the faces covered with flames indicates complete disgrace and the removal of any last shred of honor. The fire envelops the very features they once used to sneer at the believers, to utter lies against Allah, and to order others to sin. In the world, they might have protected their faces from the sun and wind, caring deeply about their appearance. Now, the fire wraps their faces, and there is no protection and no escape. This dual punishment—the tar garment below and the flame covering above—means the torment surrounds them entirely. The lesson embedded in this imagery is the principle of recompense in kind. Their inner arrogance, their outer display of luxury in disobedience, and their facial expressions of contempt are all met with an outward punishment that mirrors the sin. For the mindful believer, the verse prompts a deep reflection on the use of one’s body and wealth. Clothing is a blessing to be worn with gratitude and humility, and the face is a blessing to be used in worship and kind speech, never to be twisted in scorn against the truth.

=51=

This verse provides the overarching principle of divine justice that explains all the scenes of punishment described. The phrase “as such” refers back to the entire panorama of the Day of Judgment—the changed earth, the chained criminals, the garments of tar, and the flaming faces.

This is not arbitrary cruelty but a precise reward. “Allah will reward every soul for what it has committed. ” The word “soul” individualizes the accountability; no one will be punished for the sins of another, and no one’s good deeds will be lost.

The word “committed” encompasses every action, every word, every intention that was firmly held and acted upon in the world. The reward is perfectly calibrated, with no exaggeration in punishment and no reduction in reward.

This principle of individual, exact retribution is central to Quranic ethics and removes any basis for complaint from the inhabitants of Hell. They will see with their own eyes that what they are experiencing is precisely what their own hands sent ahead.

The verse then closes with a powerful statement: “Surely Allah is swift in reckoning.” The swiftness of divine reckoning is a dimension of Allah’s perfection. The judgment of billions of human beings, across all ages, with all their complex records, will be accomplished in an incredibly short span, without any error or delay. The swiftness also means that no one can escape or interrupt the process; once the reckoning begins, the verdict follows immediately.

For the believer, this verse is both a comfort and a warning. It comforts those who suffered injustice, knowing that the Reckoner will quickly and accurately settle all accounts. It warns those who are complacent, thinking judgment is a distant, slow process that can be delayed. The swiftness of the reckoning encourages urgency in repentance and in clearing one’s accounts with others. Every deed is being recorded, and when the time comes, the settlement will occur with a speed that leaves no opportunity for last-minute negotiations.

=52=

The surah that began with the revelation of a Book to bring people from darkness into light now reaches its majestic conclusion by reflecting on the nature and purpose of that Book. “This Quran is a sufficient message for humanity.

” The word “balāgh” means a proclamation, a clear and complete delivery of the message that leaves no excuse for the recipient. The Quran is not missing any essential guidance; it sufficiently covers all that humanity needs for its spiritual and moral well-being. The message is addressed to all humanity, not a single tribe or nation, underscoring the universal scope of the Prophet’s mission.

The first objective of this message is so that they may take it as a warning. The Quran is filled with warnings of the consequences of disbelief and disobedience, intended to shake the heedless heart into wakefulness.

The second objective is so that they may know that there is only One God. The knowledge of divine unity is the core of the entire revelation. Everything in the surah—the parables, the historical accounts, the signs in creation, and the scenes of judgment—points back to this singular, foundational truth.

The third objective is so that people of reason may be mindful. Here the verse identifies the true beneficiaries of the message. “People of reason,” ulu al-albāb, are those who possess sound intellects that are not clouded by blind prejudice or base desires. They are the ones who will hear the message, reflect deeply on its meanings, and allow it to transform their lives. Mindfulness, or dhikr, means a constant, active remembrance of Allah that shapes conduct. The verse, and with it the surah, ends by returning to the twin themes of warning and contemplation, forming a perfect circle with its opening which declared the Quran a means to lead people out of darkness and into light.

The final word leaves the reader in a state of introspection, asking whether they are indeed among those people of reason who have taken this sufficient message to heart.
