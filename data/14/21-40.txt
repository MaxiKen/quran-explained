=21=

After the strong warnings about the fate of the stubborn tyrants, the surah now draws back the curtain on a gripping scene from the Day of Judgment. The verse opens with the statement that all will appear before Allah, meaning every human being, from the most powerful ruler to the weakest follower, will stand exposed in the court of the Almighty. There will be no hierarchies, no intermediaries, just naked accountability. Then the focus narrows to a specific, heartbreaking exchange. The lowly followers, those who had been led astray and who had surrendered their will to the arrogant leaders, will turn to those very leaders and appeal. Their speech is laced with desperation: “We were your dedicated followers.” In the world, they had obeyed, imitated, and defended their chiefs and celebrities against every warning. Now, seeing the flames of divine torment, they cling to the faint hope that their leaders, who once seemed so powerful, might somehow shield them. They ask, “So will you protect us from Allah’s torment in any way?

” The phrase “in any way” reveals their utter helplessness; they are not demanding a full rescue, just even the tiniest portion of relief or intercession. The response from the arrogant leaders is crushing. They begin with a false theological excuse: “Had Allah guided us, we would have guided you. ” This is a twisted attempt to blame divine decree for their own choice of misguidance, implying that their leading others astray was somehow inevitable.

Yet the very fact that they are in Hell proves that Allah had sent guidance which they arrogantly rejected. The leaders then deliver the final blow: whether we suffer patiently or scream with impatience, it is all the same, for there is no escape. Their shared doom dissolves the worldly bond between the oppressor and the oppressed, the influencer and the follower. For the believers, this verse serves as a powerful reminder to choose one’s leaders, role models, and close companions with the utmost care, because blind following on earth will turn into bitter mutual abandonment on the Day when every soul will be too preoccupied with its own suffering to help another.

=22=

This is one of the most chilling and instructive speeches in the Quran. After the judgment has been finalized and the inhabitants of Hell are in their places, Satan—the root of all temptation—delivers an address to his followers. He begins by acknowledging a fundamental truth: “Indeed, Allah has made you a true promise.” Allah promised resurrection, judgment, Paradise for the obedient, and Hell for the disobedient. Every word of that promise has now materialized before their eyes. Satan then contrasts this with his own role: “I too made you a promise, but I failed you.” His promises—wealth, pleasure, safety from consequence, a long life free from accountability—were all glittering falsehoods. He admits his failure openly, not out of remorse, but to sever any remaining bond of expectation. He then clarifies the nature of his influence with the precise statement, “I did not have any authority over you.

” This is crucial. Satan does not possess the power to compel a human being to sin; his role is limited to whispering, decorating evil, and calling. He admits, “I only called you, and you responded to me. ” The call was an invitation, and the response was a free choice.

Thus, the blame, he argues, lies squarely upon themselves. The statement “So do not blame me; blame yourselves” is the ultimate end of every misguided follower who chased satanic temptations. Then, with brutal honesty, he declares the mutual helplessness: “I cannot save you, nor can you save me. ” The false god, the secret object of devotion, is as powerless as the devotee.

Finally, Satan makes a complete disavowal: “I denounce your previous association of me with Allah in loyalty.” In the world, people had obeyed Satan as they should have obeyed Allah, equating his whispers with divine command. Now he disowns that entire relationship.

The verse ends with a sober divine comment that the wrongdoers will indeed suffer a painful punishment. For the believer, this passage is a lifelong shield. When temptation calls, one remembers that the whisperer will not be there to share the blame; he will be the first to point the finger and walk away.

=23=

After the terrifying scenes of Hell and mutual disownment, the surah offers the antithesis—the serene and joyous outcome of the believers. The description is brief but breathtakingly beautiful.

The verse begins by linking two essential conditions: belief and good deeds. Faith is the root, the inner conviction that anchors the heart to its Creator, and good deeds are the fruit, the visible proof of that faith in action. Neither is sufficient without the other; empty faith without righteous work is dead, and deeds without faith are the ashes that the wind scatters, as described earlier in the surah. Those who combine the two will be admitted into Gardens. The plural “Gardens” indicates vastness and variety, multiple levels of bliss. The defining feature of Paradise is given: “under which rivers flow. ” Water in the desert environment of Arabia symbolizes life, freshness, and continuous, effortless provision. These rivers are not seasonal streams but perpetual blessings. The believers will stay there forever, an eternity untainted by any fear of eviction or death.

The phrase “by the Will of their Lord” is profoundly comforting; it means that their continued residence is not by their own merit alone but by the gracious permission and decree of Allah, who has willed this eternal honor for them. Finally, the verse mentions the greeting they receive: “Peace!

” This is not a mere word but a reality that pervades their existence. In this world, even moments of peace are fragile and temporary, interrupted by anxiety, illness, or conflict. But the “Peace” of Paradise is perfect, all-encompassing safety from every harm, and it comes from Allah Himself as well as from the angels. The contrast with the previous verses is stark: the followers and leaders in Hell exchange blame and despair, while the believers exchange a greeting of pure peace. This teaches that the companionship of faith, chosen in this world, blossoms into eternal harmony, while the alliances of sin dissolve into acrimony. It encourages the believer to prioritize the company of the righteous and to strive for that state where every fear is replaced by divine peace.

=24=

The surah now shifts to one of the most famous parables in the Quran. The question “Do you not see” is a rhetorical call to reflection, urging every listener to use their imagination and intellect to understand a deep spiritual reality through a natural image. Allah compares a kalimah tayyibah, a good word, to a good tree. The majority of commentators explain that the “good word” is the testimony of faith—La ilaha illa Allah—or more broadly any word of truth, wisdom, and remembrance that is pleasing to Allah. This word is likened to a tree that possesses two outstanding qualities. First, its root is firm. A tree with a deep, well-anchored root system can withstand storms and droughts; it is not easily toppled. The root of the word of faith is the firm knowledge of Allah’s oneness, deeply embedded in the heart, nourished by sincerity and understanding. This root remains unseen, much like the inner conviction of a believer, but it is the source of all life and stability. Second, its branches reach the sky. The tree is not stunted or confined to the ground; it grows upward, majestic and visible, spreading its limbs into the open air. The branches represent the good deeds, the noble character, and the beneficial impact that flow from a heart firmly rooted in faith. These deeds rise toward Allah, and their benefit extends outward to society, offering shade, fruit, and beauty. The upward reach also symbolizes the lofty aspirations of a believer, whose concerns are not limited to the dirt of worldly greed but constantly stretch toward the heavens. For the companions in Mecca, this image was powerfully reassuring: their word of faith, though seemingly fragile in a hostile environment, was like a tree whose roots, if nurtured with patience and prayer, would one day produce a canopy that touched the sky, a prophecy fulfilled as Islam spread beyond Arabia.

=25=

Continuing the description of the good tree, this verse adds a remarkable feature: it is always yielding its fruit in every season. In the natural world, most fruit trees are seasonal; they produce fruit at a specific time of the year and remain barren for the rest. But this special tree is perpetually productive. The believer’s good word, rooted in faith, similarly produces continuous fruit regardless of the changing seasons of life. Whether in times of ease or hardship, youth or old age, health or sickness, a heart firmly anchored in the testimony of faith never ceases to generate good deeds.

The phrase “by the Will of its Lord” is inserted to remind that even the finest tree does not bear fruit by its own power; it is Allah’s permission and creative command that causes the bounty to appear. This protects the believer from arrogance, knowing that every good word spoken, every act of charity, every moment of patience is ultimately a gift from Allah.

The verse then pauses to explain the purpose of such imagery: “This is how Allah sets forth parables for the people, so perhaps they will be mindful. ” A parable condenses a profound truth into a familiar picture, making it accessible to the common person and the scholar alike. The human mind often grasps abstract concepts better when they are linked to sensory experiences like a tree, its roots, and its fruits. The goal is mindfulness—tadhakkur—which implies a continuous, active remembrance that translates into practical conduct. The listener is meant to visualize this tree whenever they utter the kalimah or any good word, asking themselves whether their own word is bearing the kind of fruit that benefits themselves and others in every season. This parable also implicitly refutes the argument of the disbelievers who saw faith as a passing fad; it declares that the truth, once planted, yields everlasting, uninterrupted benefit.

=26=

To complete the contrast, the surah presents the opposite parable. The evil word—khabithah—is any statement of falsehood, from blatant shirk and denial of Allah to harmful gossip, lies, and corrupt ideologies. The parable given for it is that of an evil tree, one that is uprooted from the earth, having no stability.

The first and most striking characteristic is that this tree has been uprooted. It is not merely dead or withered; it has been completely torn from the ground. Its connection to the earth is severed, which means it cannot draw any nourishment or water. The root system of the evil word is not firm; it is inherently weak because falsehood has no basis in the reality that Allah created. A lie may appear powerful for a time, like a cut tree that retains its leaves for a few days, but it has no anchor in truth.

The phrase “having no stability” underscores its ultimate condition. An uprooted tree lying on the ground will inevitably decay, be blown by winds, and turn to dust. It offers no shade, no fruit, no shelter; it is only a lifeless obstruction.

This is the fate of every statement and ideology that contradicts divine guidance. It may command temporary followings, but it cannot stand the test of time or the storm of death and judgment. On the Day of Judgment, all evil words and the deeds built upon them will be shown as weightless and without foundation.

For the believer, this parable instills a deep caution about the words one speaks and the ideas one entertains. Every casual slander, every propagation of doubt, every defense of a corrupt system is a seed of an evil tree. Its fruits, if any appear, are bitter and poisonous. The verse implicitly invites the listener to uproot any remnants of evil speech from their own life before the final storm exposes all roots for what they are.

=27=

After the parables of the good and evil trees, the verse directly applies the lesson to the believers. The firm Word is understood as the testimony of faith, the good word that is rooted in the heart. Allah actively makes the believers steadfast, firm, and unwavering through this Word. The verb yuthabbitu indicates a continuous divine action; it is not a one-time event but an ongoing support. The steadfastness is granted in two distinct realms: this worldly life and the Hereafter. In the worldly life, this means that a believer who truly holds to the kalimah will be granted stability in the face of doubts, trials, and the pressures to abandon faith. When death comes, the firm Word grants the believer the ability to answer the angels’ questions in the grave with clarity and confidence.

In the Hereafter, it will be the cause of a steady footing on the bridge over Hell and a firm position before Allah. The famous hadith reported by Al-Bara’ ibn ‘Azib describes how Allah keeps the believer firm with the firm word at the time of questioning in the grave, which is a direct fulfillment of this verse.

The verse then presents the opposite divine law: Allah leaves the wrongdoers to stray. Those who reject the good word and cling to evil words are abandoned to the consequences of their choice. Their hearts, unanchored by truth, wander in confusion in this life and will stumble into perdition in the next.

The final sentence, “For Allah does what He wills,” is not an arbitrary statement but a declaration of His absolute sovereignty tied to His perfect wisdom. He wills to keep firm those who seek firmness from Him, and He wills to let stray those who turn away.

For the believer, this verse is a source of immense hope and a practical supplication: it inspires the constant prayer for steadfastness, recognizing that even the most deeply rooted faith requires the ongoing rain of divine support.

=28=

The surah returns to the stark contrast between gratitude and ingratitude that was established earlier with Moses’ people. The question “Have you not seen” invites reflection on a specific kind of disbeliever, one who has received abundant favours from Allah but responds with ingratitude. This ingratitude is not passive; it is an active meeting of blessings with denial, misuse, and rebellion. Instead of using the blessings to draw closer to the Giver, they employ them in disobedience and arrogance.

The verse then highlights the social dimension of their crime: they lead their own people to their doom. The word for “doom” here is al-bawār, which denotes utter destruction, ruin, and perdition. These are the leaders, influencers, and elites who do not merely go astray by themselves but become the cause of their entire community’s spiritual and sometimes physical downfall. The Qurayshi chiefs who organized opposition to the Prophet fit this description perfectly; they enjoyed Allah’s favours of wealth, honor, and the custodianship of the Ka‘bah, yet they met those favours with the ingratitude of shirk and actively dragged their followers into the battlefields of Badr and Uhud, leading them to worldly disgrace and, for those who died in disbelief, eternal ruin. This verse is a timeless warning against a mentality that sees worldly blessings as an end in themselves, unconnected to the responsibility of gratitude and guidance. It also warns the common person to be wary of leaders who display such ingratitude, for blind loyalty to them can drag one into the same abyss. In a contemporary setting, the “people” can be a nation, a social movement, or a digital following. The lesson remains that those who misuse their position of influence to promote ingratitude toward Allah are not just corrupting themselves; they are steering the masses toward destruction.

=29=

This brief verse delivers the inevitable consequence of the attitude described in the previous verse. Those who met Allah’s favours with ingratitude and led their people to doom will face a personal and collective punishment. The statement “In Hell they will burn” uses the verb yaslawnahā, which implies entering into the fire and suffering its intense heat directly. The burning is not a distant rumor but a lived reality they will taste.

The verse then adds a deeply emphatic exclamation: “What an evil place for settlement.” The word for settlement, maqarr, suggests a permanent abode, a place where one settles down and makes a home. By calling Hell an evil settlement, the Quran uses understatement and sarcasm to underline the horror. A home is supposed to be a place of rest, comfort, and security; for these ingrates, their eternal “home” will be a furnace of pain. The verse does not need lengthy descriptions here because the image of fire is universally understood as a source of suffering.

Yet the point is not to satisfy morbid curiosity but to jolt the conscience. The leaders who built luxurious homes in this world, who took pride in their palaces and estates, will find that their final residence is a pit of fire. This stark reversal is the fruit of their ingratitude. For the listener, the verse connects the concept of home—something dear to every human heart—with the afterlife. It forces a re-evaluation of priorities: is one spending a lifetime perfecting a temporary earthly home while risking an eternal home of torment? The condemnation of the settlement as “evil” is a divine judgment, not an expression of human emotion, which makes it all the more weighty.

The verse also serves as a final word on the fate of those who deliberately exchanged gratitude for ingratitude, leaving no ambiguity about the end of a life built on the denial of blessings.

=30=

This verse explains why the ingrates deserve such a fate. Their central crime is that they set up equals, andād, to Allah. These equals are not limited to carved idols; they include any object, person, ideology, or desire that is given the love, fear, and obedience that are due to Allah alone. The specific purpose mentioned is “to mislead others from His Way. ” The false gods were not merely personal superstitions; they were tools actively used to divert people from the path of monotheism. The Quraysh maintained the idols around the Ka‘bah not only out of misguided devotion but also because those idols brought economic and political benefits, and they used them to block the call of the Prophet.

The verse then instructs the Prophet to issue a chilling directive: “Say: Enjoy yourselves! Surely your destination is the Fire. ” This is not an encouragement to sin but a severe threat and a declaration of the futility of their worldly pleasures. The command “Enjoy yourselves” implies that their time is short and their pleasures are fleeting. They may eat, drink, and revel in their power, but the enjoyment is limited to a few days in this world.

The phrase “surely your destination is the Fire” serves as the final note, transforming every present enjoyment into a fleeting prelude to an inescapable end. There is a deep psychological impact in this verse: when a disbeliever hears that even their enjoyment is permitted by divine leave only to be followed by the Fire, it sours the taste of every pleasure and plants the seed of fear that could lead to repentance.

For the believer, the verse is a reminder never to envy the apparent enjoyment of those who reject faith, for it is merely the last meal of a condemned person. This verse also teaches that the mission of guiding others is obstructed when equals to Allah are erected, and the only correct response is to warn clearly, leaving the outcome to Allah.

=31=

In the midst of the severe warnings, the surah turns to the Prophet with a set of positive, urgent instructions directed to the believing servants. The phrase “My believing servants” is filled with tenderness and honor; Allah claims the believers as His own and addresses them with mercy.

The first command is to establish the prayer, iqām aṣ-ṣalāh, which implies performing it regularly, with all its conditions, with concentration, and as a communal obligation. Prayer is the central pillar that connects the servant to the Lord, and in a hostile environment it is the source of strength and steadfastness.

The second command is to donate from what Allah has provided for them. The spending, infāq, covers both the obligatory zakat and voluntary charity. The provision is described as “what We have provided for them,” reinforcing that the wealth they hold is ultimately Allah’s, entrusted to them as a test. The spending is to be done “openly and secretly,” covering both public charity that encourages others and private charity that protects sincerity and the dignity of the recipient. Then comes the powerful motivation: “before the arrival of a Day in which there will be no ransom or friendly connections.” The Day of Judgment will not be a marketplace where one can buy salvation with wealth, nor a social gathering where friendships or tribal alliances can intercede without Allah’s permission. The disbelievers who relied on their wealth and networks will find them utterly useless. For the believers, this is a call to invest in the one currency that will hold value on that Day: sincere prayer and charitable spending. The instruction to spend both openly and secretly also provides a balanced model that guards against showing off on one hand and completely hiding good deeds to the point of discouraging others on the other. The verse cultivates a sense of urgency, treating every day as an opportunity to send forward provisions for the inevitable journey.

=32=

After commanding the believers to pray and spend, the surah now calls all of humanity to reflect on the evidence of Allah’s power and generosity surrounding them. It begins with the grandest act: the creation of the heavens and the earth. These two vast realms, with their perfect order and staggering complexity, are the foundational signs.

The verse then moves to a closer, life-giving process: Allah sends down rain from the sky. Rain is a descending mercy, pure and fresh, which revives the dead land. From that water, fruits grow as a provision for human beings. The choice of the word “fruits” includes all agricultural produce that sustains life, and calling it “provision” directly links the natural cycle to the care of a Provider. Then the verse shifts to another domain: the sea. Allah has subjected the ships, heavy vessels laden with goods and people, to sail through the sea by His command. The ship, a marvel of human engineering, would be useless without the laws of buoyancy, wind, and water that Allah created and sustains.

The phrase “by His command” reminds that every voyage is under divine permission; a single storm can overturn the mightiest ship. Furthermore, Allah has subjected the rivers, making them fresh water highways and sources of irrigation, placed at the service of human civilization. In each of these—rain, fruits, ships, rivers—the repeated word “subjected” is crucial. The universe is not hostile or random; it has been made subservient to human needs by a deliberate act of the Creator. This reflection leads directly to gratitude, which is the essence of worship. For the Meccans, who were well-acquainted with trade caravans, sea routes, and the dependence on rain, these were undeniable daily miracles that they had taken for granted. The verse aims to reawaken their awe, steering them from the worship of creation to the worship of the One who subjected it all.

=33=

The list of subjected blessings continues, moving from the earth and its waters to the celestial bodies that govern time and life. The sun, that magnificent, blazing lamp, is subjected for human benefit. Its heat and light drive photosynthesis, warm the earth, and make the very existence of life possible. The moon, with its gentle light and its precise phases, is likewise subjected. Together, the sun and the moon are described as “both constantly orbiting.

” This description, revealed in a time when popular astronomical models varied, beautifully captures the constant, predictable motion of these bodies along their divinely set courses. They do not deviate; their orbits are a testimony to the meticulous order of the Creator.

The verse then adds the subjection of the day and the night. The alternation of day and night is not an accident but a deliberate provision: the day for work, activity, and seeking livelihood; the night for rest, coolness, and tranquility. The precise length of day and night, and the gradual change of seasons, are perfectly calibrated for the needs of life on earth.

The repetition of the word “subjected” drives home a single, overwhelming point: everything in existence is placed at the service of humanity by the decree of one Lord. For a person to then worship the sun, the moon, or to believe that these celestial bodies act independently, is to profoundly misunderstand the nature of the universe. The sun and the moon are not deities; they are obedient servants of Allah, assigned to a task that benefits humankind. This verse therefore serves both as a proof of monotheism and as a cultivation of gratitude. Every sunrise, every moonlit night, is a fresh invitation to recognize the Giver. The believer who reads this is encouraged to see the natural world not as mere background scenery but as a constant, speaking sign of divine care.

=34=

This verse reaches the summit of the list of blessings by making a sweeping statement. Allah has granted humanity all that they asked Him for. This can be understood in two profound ways, both valid. First, it refers to the innate needs and prayers of every human being. Even before a person articulates a request, Allah has already provided the essential requirements of life: air, water, food, family, and faculties. Second, it can refer to the countless specific supplications that are answered, whether in the exact form requested, in something better, or in the warding off of an equivalent harm. The expression encompasses all of it, highlighting that human existence is fundamentally a response to unspoken and spoken requests fulfilled by a Generous Lord. Then the verse challenges: “If you tried to count Allah’s blessings, you would never be able to number them. ” The blessings are so numerous and so deeply intertwined—from the breath in the lungs to the complex systems of the universe—that statistical enumeration is impossible. Every moment of health, every moment of safety, every intellectual insight, every instance of beauty experienced is a separate blessing. The challenge is not to dissuade counting but to inspire an overwhelming realization of infinite divine favour that should produce infinite gratitude. Despite this, the verse concludes with a sobering verdict on human nature: “Indeed humankind is truly unfair, totally ungrateful. ” The Arabic word for unfair, ẓalūm, denotes one who commits a great wrong, and the word for ungrateful, kaffār, is an intensive form indicating persistent, deep-rooted ingratitude.

This is not a condemnation of every individual in every state but a description of the dominant tendency of the human species when left to its own devices: to receive boundless blessings and yet respond with injustice and denial. The verse invites each person to break out of that pattern, to be among the few who are truly grateful, and to acknowledge that every blessing is an undeserved gift from the Most Merciful.

=35=

Having traversed from the creation to the pervasive ingratitude of humanity, the surah now brings forward the noble model of the opposite attitude: the profound gratitude and far-sighted prayers of Abraham, peace be upon him. The Meccans prided themselves on their connection to Abraham and his son Ishmael, yet they had fallen into the very idolatry Abraham feared. The verse calls to memory a specific supplication Abraham made. He begins with the intimate address “My Lord,” showing his close, personal relationship with Allah. His first request is for the city where he had settled part of his family—the barren valley that would become Mecca—to be made secure.

The word ḥaraman āminan indicates a sacred, safe sanctuary. This prayer was spectacularly answered: Mecca became a place where even animals and trees enjoyed a special protection, and within its boundaries, violence was prohibited. Abraham’s concern, however, went beyond physical security. He immediately added a second, deeper plea: “and keep me and my children away from the worship of idols.” Despite being a prophet of towering faith, Abraham did not feel immune to the trial of idolatry. He knew that the heart can be subtly drawn toward visible, tangible objects of devotion, and so he begged for divine protection from ever falling into or even approaching idol worship. His prayer encompasses his descendants, showing his concern for the spiritual well-being of all future generations.

This is a powerful refutation to the Meccan idolaters who claimed to follow Abraham’s religion while surrounding the Ka‘bah with idols. Abraham’s legacy, immortalized in this prayer, was precisely the opposite: a fervent desire for a pure monotheism free from every trace of shirk.

For every believer, this prayer becomes a timeless model. One should never feel overly confident in their own faith but should constantly implore Allah to keep them and their children far from the worship of anything other than Him, be it idols of stone or modern idols of status, wealth, and ego.

=36=

Abraham continues his heartfelt prayer by acknowledging the grim reality of idols. He says, “My Lord! They have caused many people to go astray.” Abraham does not hold any illusion about the neutrality of false gods; he speaks from either prophetic knowledge or observation of his own people’s history that idols have been the cause of mass misguidance.

This statement serves as a warning and an expression of sorrow. Then Abraham makes a declaration about his own relationship with those who will come after him. He states clearly: whoever follows me is with me.

This is a statement of solidarity and alliance. Those who adhere to his creed of pure monotheism share a spiritual bond with him that transcends time.

However, he immediately turns to the fate of those who will not follow him. Instead of invoking destruction upon them, Abraham’s words are soaked in prophetic mercy: “whoever disobeys me—then surely You are still All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” He does not say, “then destroy them” or “then they are doomed. ” He leaves the door of forgiveness wide open, acknowledging that those who strayed because of the idols might still repent and find Allah’s forgiveness. He entrusts their affair to the One whose capacity to forgive is infinite and whose mercy encompasses all things. This teaches a profound lesson in da‘wah: while being firm in the truth, the heart of a believer should be a vessel of mercy that hopes for the repentance of the sinners rather than seeking their immediate punishment. Abraham’s concern is not to condemn but to save. For the Quraysh who heard this, the message was clear: Abraham, whom you claim as your ancestor, would not have disowned you immediately but would have prayed for your forgiveness, yet you must turn from idols to the Lord of Abraham. The verse is also a comfort to every believer whose family members have strayed; it teaches that one can entrust them to the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful while remaining firm in one’s own faith.

=37=

This verse captures one of the most moving moments in Abraham’s life: the settlement of Hagar and the infant Ishmael in the dry, uncultivated valley of Mecca. Abraham addresses Allah with the repeated intimate call “Our Lord,” emphasizing his deep connection. He explains his action: he has settled some of his offspring in a valley that is barren, without cultivation, water, or visible means of survival. The location is near the Sacred House, even though at that time the Ka‘bah was not yet built; some scholars say the spot was already designated or that the prayer anticipated the future construction. The purpose of this settlement is purely spiritual: “so that they may establish prayer.” Abraham did not seek a thriving metropolis or a commercial center for his family; his primary goal was that they establish the worship of the One God in that isolated place. He then makes two specific requests. First, “make the hearts of believing people incline towards them. ” This prayer was fulfilled in a breathtaking manner as Muslims from every corner of the earth yearn to visit Mecca and feel a natural, inexplicable love for the city and its people. Second, “provide them with fruits.” In a barren valley, the request for fruits was a plea for a miracle of provision, which was also granted as Mecca became a crossroads where all kinds of produce were brought. The concluding phrase “so perhaps they will be thankful” reveals Abraham’s ultimate aim: all these provisions—spiritual and material—are means to an end, which is gratitude to Allah. This prayer is a blueprint for Muslim parents: the priority in choosing a home and raising a family should be the establishment of prayer and the cultivation of gratitude, not merely material prosperity. It also teaches that when one sacrifices worldly comfort for the sake of Allah, the divine compensation can come in unexpected forms, including the love that strangers feel for one’s descendants and the provision that flows into a barren land.

=38=

In this brief but profound verse, Abraham pauses his list of specific requests to acknowledge the vastness of Allah’s knowledge. He says, “Our Lord! You certainly know what we conceal and what we reveal.

” This statement is an act of complete surrender and honesty. Abraham is standing in a barren valley, his back turned on his wife and infant son, walking away in obedience to a divine command. The emotions of a father leaving his family in such a situation are deep, complex, and perhaps too painful to fully express. Abraham acknowledges that Allah knows the turmoil of his heart, the hidden fears for his son’s survival, the unspoken hopes, and the outward declarations of trust. He lays all of it before the One who already knows.

The verse then expands the scope from personal concealment to cosmic knowledge: “Nothing on earth or in heaven is hidden from Allah.” This universal statement anchors Abraham’s personal trust in a grand theological truth. If every atom, every whisper, every unseen thought across the expanse of creation is open and known to Allah, then surely the fate of a mother and child in a desert is within His perfect knowledge and care.

This verse teaches the believer the liberating practice of confessing one’s inner state to Allah, not because He does not know, but because the act of verbalizing and acknowledging His knowledge deepens the relationship of trust. It also provides immense comfort: in moments of loneliness, when no human understands the pain or anxiety one is feeling, the reality that nothing is hidden from Allah transforms isolation into a secret conversation with the All-Knowing. Abraham’s prayer thus becomes a model of how to anchor the heart in divine omniscience when facing the unknown.

=39=

After expressing the pain of separation and the depth of reliance, Abraham’s heart turns to pure, exuberant praise. He declares, “All praise is for Allah.

” This is a spontaneous eruption of gratitude that is not clouded by the hardship of his situation. The specific reason for the praise is articulated: “who has blessed me with Ishmael and Isaac in my old age. ” Abraham had yearned for a child for decades, and his wife Sarah was barren. When all natural hope had faded and his hair had turned gray, Allah granted him not just one son but two: Ishmael, the firstborn, through Hagar, and Isaac, the son of laughter, through Sarah in her even more advanced age. The blessing of children in old age is a double mercy; it provides support when physical strength wanes and ensures the continuation of a righteous legacy. Abraham does not take this for granted; he sees it purely as a gift from Allah. He then adds a statement of personal testimony: “My Lord is indeed the Hearer of all prayers.

” This is a powerful affirmation rooted in experience. Abraham had spent years making du‘a, long and fervent supplications, and he had now witnessed the answer in the most spectacular way. His proclamation is not a theoretical belief but a lived certainty. The verse highlights the divine name as-Samī‘, the Hearer, indicating that no prayer, however quiet or seemingly impossible, escapes Allah’s hearing.

For the believer, this verse instills a patience in du‘a that is grounded in the story of Abraham. When an answer seems delayed, one remembers the decades Abraham waited and how his prayer was finally answered with blessings greater than he could have imagined. It also teaches the etiquette of immediately turning praise to Allah when a long-awaited blessing arrives, recognizing the Giver before becoming absorbed in the gift.

Having praised Allah for the blessing of children, Abraham immediately returns to supplication, demonstrating that gratitude for a blessing should be followed by a request for the blessing to be made a source of continuous good. His request is deeply personal yet encompassing: “Make me an establisher of prayer.” Even as a prophet, Abraham does not assume he will always maintain the prayer without divine help. He asks Allah to make him one who consistently and perfectly establishes it. Then he extends the request to his descendants, specifically those who are believers. This shows that Abraham’s concern for his offspring is not based on mere blood ties but is linked to faith; his prayer is for the righteous among them to be steadfast in worship. The prayer is a legacy of prophethood.

The second part of the verse, “Our Lord! Accept my prayers,” is a humble plea that all his worship, including this very du‘a, be accepted by Allah. Even after a life of monumental service—building the Ka‘bah, sacrificing his comfort, facing Nimrod’s fire—Abraham fears that his deeds might not be accepted.

This is the epitome of ihsan, spiritual excellence, where one worships Allah with a combination of hope and awe, never feeling entitled to acceptance. The verse is an essential manual for every parent. It teaches that the greatest gift one can seek for one’s children is not wealth or status but the establishment of prayer. It also teaches that one should never stop praying for oneself, recognizing that steadfastness is an ongoing gift from Allah. The request for acceptance further purifies the intention, reminding that the goal is not the mere performance of rituals but the divine embrace of those rituals. Abraham’s prayer thus reaches its emotional peak here, blending intercession for generations with profound personal humility.
