=1=

This surah opens with the declaration that all genuine praise and heartfelt gratitude belong to Allah alone. The phrase "All praise is for Allah" is not merely a statement of fact; it is an act of worship that realigns the heart with its Creator. The One who deserves every tribute is the sole Owner of everything within the heavens and the earth. Nothing exists outside His dominion.

The mention of "whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth" brings to mind the vast galaxies, the angels, the mountains, the oceans, and every living being. All of it is His, so all of it points back to His majesty. The praise is not limited to this world; it extends into the life to come. "And praise be to Him in the Hereafter" assures us that just as the believers praise Him here, they will continue to praise Him in Paradise, where their final call will be "Praise be to Allah, Lord of all worlds." Even the judgment on that Day will unfold in a manner that reveals His perfect justice and wisdom, drawing praise from every corner of creation.

The verse ends with two majestic names: "All-Wise, All-Aware." His wisdom ensures that nothing He does is without purpose, and His awareness means that no thought, deed, or hidden motive escapes Him. For the Meccan disbelievers who are about to be addressed, this opening reminds them that the very universe they inhabit announces the praise of its Lord. The surah will go on to contrast grateful servants like David and Solomon with the ungrateful people of Sheba, and this first verse sets the foundation: gratitude and praise are the natural responses to recognising who Allah truly is.

=2=

After establishing Allah's ownership and praise, the surah draws attention to His perfect knowledge. "He knows whatever goes into the earth" covers every seed that is buried, every drop of rain that soaks into the soil, every treasure hidden underground, and every dead body that is laid in the grave. Nothing enters the earth without His complete awareness. Equally, He knows "whatever comes out of it": the sprouting plants, the gushing springs, the minerals and metals extracted by human hands, and ultimately the resurrection of the dead when they will emerge from their graves.

The second half of the verse turns to the sky. "Whatever descends from the sky" includes the life-giving rain, the decrees that descend with the angels, the blessings and provisions apportioned for every creature, and the divine revelation sent down to the prophets. And He knows "whatever ascends into it", such as the rising vapours that form clouds, the angels carrying the records of human deeds, and every sincere supplication that rises from a distressed heart. This comprehensive knowledge is not a cold, detached catalogue of data. The verse immediately follows it with "He is the Most Merciful, All-Forgiving." The One who knows every detail of our existence is also the One whose mercy envelopes everything and whose forgiveness is always open to those who turn back. This pairing creates hope: the same Lord who sees every buried seed can bring it to life, and the same Lord who knows our hidden sins can forgive them. As the passage moves towards the denial of the Hour, this verse prepares the ground by demonstrating that the One who tracks every particle can effortlessly bring the dead back to account.

=3=

The Meccan disbelievers are quoted making a bold, sweeping denial. "The Hour will never come to us" is not a statement of doubt but a defiant rejection of resurrection and judgment. They considered the very idea impossible after death and decay. In response, Allah instructs the Prophet to counter their denial with an oath that leaves no room for ambiguity. "Say, 'Yes—by my Lord, the Knower of the unseen—it will certainly come to you!'" The oath is taken by the Lord Himself, described as the Knower of the unseen, to emphasise that the Hour is part of the unseen realm which only He fully knows. The certainty is absolute.

The verse then provides the logical foundation for this certainty: nothing, not even the weight of the tiniest particle, is hidden from Him. The expression "an atom’s weight" translates the Arabic word dharrah, which refers to the smallest known thing, like a speck of dust or a tiny ant. In the heavens and the earth, every particle is known, and nothing smaller or larger than that escapes His record. This "perfect Record" is the Preserved Tablet, in which all events and details of creation are inscribed. If every atom of a decayed body is known and recorded by Allah, then reassembling it for resurrection is not only possible but entirely straightforward for the One who already knows its every detail. The verse dismantles the disbelievers' objection by rooting the argument in Allah's infinite knowledge. It also comforts the believers by anchoring their hope in the absolute certainty of the promise. The coming verses will explain why that Hour is necessary and what awaits each group.

=4=

This verse directly explains the purpose behind the establishment of the Hour. The word "So" connects the preceding promise of the Hour to its ultimate divine objective: "He may reward those who believe and do good." The Day of Judgment is not an arbitrary event; it is the necessary manifestation of Allah's justice and mercy. In this world, the righteous often suffer while the wicked prosper, and this imbalance demands a day when every account is settled. The verse singles out those who combine sincere faith with righteous action. Belief in the heart must be accompanied by deeds that demonstrate that belief, and together they make a person worthy of the promised reward. That reward is then described in two beautiful terms. "Forgiveness" comes first, because even the best of believers fall short and need their sins wiped away. The pardon from Allah removes the anxiety of standing before Him with an imperfect record. Then comes "an honourable provision." This provision is honourable because it is generous, pure, eternal, and completely free of any humiliation or hardship. The provision of this world, however abundant, is always mixed with toil, decay, and eventual loss. The provision of Paradise is different: it is noble, dignified, and never cut off. The verse sets up a clear contrast that will be developed in the following verses. Those who mocked the Prophet and denied the Hour are not simply holding an intellectual error; they are cutting themselves off from this forgiveness and honour. The promise here is deeply reassuring for the small, persecuted community of believers in Mecca. Their faith and good works were seen, and an incomparable recompense was waiting for them.

=5=

The other side of the coin is now presented, completing the contrast. "Those who strive to discredit Our revelations" translates an active, determined effort to oppose and undermine the signs of Allah. The Arabic expression carries the sense of racing and exerting oneself to render the revelations ineffective, to refute them, or to obstruct people from accepting them. This was the lived reality of the Quraysh leaders who tirelessly campaigned against the Prophet, calling his message magic, poetry, or fabrication. They did not merely disbelieve privately; they actively worked to extinguish the light of the Quran. The verse declares that their recompense will be a torment described as the worst kind, stemming from an agonizing pain. The wording emphasises that the punishment is not only severe in intensity but also humiliating in nature, perfectly fitting the arrogance of those who strove against Allah's words. There is a deep principle here: a person’s attitude towards divine revelation shapes their ultimate destiny. Rejecting the truth can be done with passive indifference, but actively fighting it draws a uniquely severe response. This served as both a warning to the Meccan elite and a consolation to the believers who saw their opponents apparently succeeding in their plots. The worldly success of the deniers was temporary; the real outcome would be revealed in the Hereafter. This pairing of reward for the believers and punishment for the active opponents sets the stage for the next verse, which explains who truly understands the value of what has been revealed.

=6=

Against the backdrop of the disbelievers' mockery, Allah directs attention to another group: "Those gifted with knowledge." This refers to people whose hearts and minds are sincerely open to the truth. Among the immediate audience were individuals like Abdullah ibn Salam and other learned Jews and Christians who recognised in the Quran the fulfilment of earlier scriptures. They could clearly see that what was revealed to the Prophet was indeed the truth from his Lord, not a fabrication or a product of insanity as the Quraysh alleged. Their knowledge was not merely intellectual information but a light that allowed them to discern the divine origin of the message.

The verse adds that this revelation "guides to the Path of the Almighty, the Praiseworthy." The path is the way of life laid out by the Quran, leading to Allah who is described with two majestic names. Al-Aziz, the Almighty, indicates that this path is backed by irresistible power and ultimate authority. Al-Hamid, the Praiseworthy, indicates that walking this path leads to a relationship with the One who is inherently worthy of all praise and gratitude. The link to the surah's opening is clear: the Quran guides to the very Lord to whom all praise belongs. For the believers in Mecca, this verse was a profound validation. It assured them that their faith was not blind but was shared by people of deep learning and insight. It also set a criterion: the truth is not measured by numbers or worldly status, but by the recognition of those whom Allah has granted true knowledge.

=7=

Having outlined the reward and punishment, the surah returns to expose the mentality of the deniers. This verse quotes their mockery in vivid terms. They say to one another, "Shall we show you a man..." as if pointing out a curiosity or a spectacle. Their words drip with ridicule. They refer to the Prophet, peace be upon him, in a belittling manner as "a man," deliberately stripping away any mention of prophethood or sincerity.

The phrase "utterly disintegrated" translates a vivid Arabic expression that pictures bones turning to dust and bodies being completely torn apart and scattered. To their minds, the idea that such scattered remains could be reassembled into a new creation was laughable. They could not look beyond the material processes of decay. Their mockery reveals a deeper spiritual sickness: they judged Allah's power by their own limited abilities and refused to accept that the One who created them from nothing could easily recreate them. This verse captures the social pressure and the climate of ridicule the early Muslims faced. The disbelievers tried to make the concept of resurrection seem foolish, hoping to shake the believers' faith. The Quran, however, does not shy away from quoting their words; it exposes them in full view, then systematically dismantles their objections. The very act of recording their mockery becomes a proof of the Quran's confident truthfulness.

The next verse will show how their insults boomerang back upon them.

=8=

The mockery escalates into direct accusations. The disbelievers pose two possibilities to explain the Prophet's message: either he has deliberately fabricated a lie against Allah, or he is insane. These two accusations were the standard attacks hurled at prophets throughout history. To call a prophet a liar is to accuse him of the worst moral failing, while to call him insane is to dismiss his message without having to engage with it rationally. The Quran does not pause to defend the Prophet's character here, because his entire life among them was the clearest refutation. He was known as Al-Amin, the trustworthy, and his sanity was evident to anyone who observed his conduct and speech. Instead, the verse turns the accusation back on the accusers with a powerful "In fact." It declares that the real problem lies not with the Prophet but with those who do not believe in the Hereafter. They are the ones "bound for torment" and they are the ones who "have strayed farthest from the truth." The depth of their straying is emphasised: they are not merely slightly mistaken but at the furthest extreme of error. This rhetorical reversal is profound. The very thing they mock—the Hereafter—is the reality that will judge them. Their denial of it is precisely what places them in such grave danger.

The verse teaches that rejecting resurrection is not an intellectual quibble; it is the root of moral and spiritual deviation. When a person believes there is no ultimate accountability, they drift further from truth and righteousness.

=9=

After exposing their mockery, Allah appeals directly to their senses. "Have they not then seen all that surrounds them of the heavens and the earth?" The verse invites them to look at the vast cosmos above and the stable earth beneath their feet. These are not inert backdrops but signs pointing to their Creator's power. The rhetorical question carries a rebuke: the evidence is all around them, yet they refuse to draw the right conclusion.

The verse then introduces a sharp warning. If they persist in their arrogance, Allah could simply command the earth to swallow them up, as happened to Korah, or cause pieces of the sky to fall upon them, as was threatened to other nations. Their feeling of safety on earth is not due to their own strength or intelligence; it is solely by Allah's forbearance.

This warning is meant to shake them out of their complacency. The verse concludes that in this observable universe and in these warnings there is a clear sign for "every servant who turns to Allah.

" The word munib describes someone who repeatedly turns back to their Lord in repentance and devotion. The sign is there, but it only benefits the one whose heart is oriented towards Allah. For the arrogant mocker, the sky and earth are just physical phenomena. For the humble seeker, they are pages of a divine book. This verse serves as a bridge to the stories of David and Solomon that follow, showing that those who truly turn to Allah receive extraordinary favours and recognise them as signs of His generosity.

=10=

From the general signs in creation, the surah turns to specific examples of grateful servants. David, peace be upon him, is the first example. He was granted "a great privilege" from Allah, a phrase that encompasses prophethood, kingship, wisdom, and the Psalms. Then a beautiful command was issued: "O mountains! Echo his hymns! And the birds as well." When David praised Allah with his melodious voice, the mountains would echo his words back, and the birds would join in chorus. This was a miracle that demonstrated how the entire creation is in harmony with those who worship Allah sincerely. It also showed that David's worship was so pure that even the inanimate and animal worlds resonated with it.

The second privilege mentioned here is that "We made iron mouldable for him." Iron, normally hard and requiring fire and heavy tools to shape, became soft and pliable in David's hands, allowing him to fashion it directly. This was a special gift that enabled him to craft chainmail and other items with ease. The combination of spiritual devotion and material skill is significant. David was not a monk withdrawn from the world; he was a king, a craftsman, and a worshipper all at once. His gratitude to Allah was expressed through his work as much as through his prayer. The story immediately sets up a contrast with the ingratitude of Sheba that will come later. David received remarkable blessings and responded with deeper worship and service.

=11=

Building on the gift of pliable iron, Allah gave David specific instructions. "Make full-length armour, perfectly balancing the links." The command was to craft comprehensive coats of mail, sabighat, that covered the body properly, and to fashion the rings with precise proportion so the armour would be both strong and flexible. This was not only a practical skill taught by revelation but also an economic provision for David and his family. It shows that manual craftsmanship, when done with excellence and for a good purpose, is a form of obedience to Allah. The instruction then broadens: "And work righteousness, O family of David!" The call to righteous action is addressed to David's household, which included his descendants and followers. They were not to rest on the honour of their lineage or the privileges they enjoyed. Righteousness was required of them in every aspect of life, whether in governance, craftsmanship, or personal worship.

The verse ends with a profound reminder: "Indeed, I am All-Seeing of what you do." No deed, public or private, escapes Allah's observation. This awareness is the foundation of true integrity. David's family was blessed, but the blessing came with heightened accountability. The detail about balancing the links of armour also carries a symbolic lesson: life requires balance, and actions must be properly proportioned. The reference to the family of David creates a link to Solomon, who will be mentioned next, as he carried forward this legacy of grateful service. The story demonstrates that divine favours are not a licence for complacency but a motivation for excellence and gratitude.

=12=

The favours granted to Solomon, peace be upon him, were even more spectacular. "We subjected the wind" to him, so that a single morning journey covered the distance of a month's travel, and the evening journey did the same. This meant Solomon could traverse vast territories with incredible speed, visiting far-flung regions of his kingdom and returning within a day. It was a symbol of how Allah can bend the laws of nature in service of His righteous servants.

Additionally, "We caused a stream of molten copper to flow for him. " This was a miraculous source of liquid metal that he could use for construction and industry without the need for mining or smelting. Further, the jinn were placed under his command, working for him by his Lord's Will. They performed diving, building, and other difficult tasks.

Yet this authority was not absolute; the jinn worked only by Allah's permission, and any who disobeyed or deviated from the command were punished with the torment of the blazing fire. This detail shows that even in a kingdom of such magnificence, justice was maintained and rebellion was checked. Solomon’s dominion was a foretaste of the authority humanity would be given when it lives in obedience to Allah. The entire array of gifts was a test of Solomon's gratitude, a test he famously passed when he prayed for a kingdom the like of which would not belong to anyone after him. His example stands as a towering rebuttal to the idea that wealth and power inevitably corrupt. With faith and gratitude, they can become channels of immense good.

=13=

The jinn worked on an impressive array of projects for Solomon. They constructed "sanctuaries," which may refer to places of worship or fortified palaces. They also crafted "statues," which in the law of Solomon were apparently permitted and not meant for worship, possibly depicting angels, prophets, or architectural ornamentation. Some scholars explain that these were non-living representations that did not violate the prohibition on idol-making, since Solomon's law was tailored to his time. The jinn also produced "basins as large as reservoirs," massive water troughs that could serve entire communities, and "cooking pots fixed into the ground," enormous stationary cauldrons to prepare food for his armies and guests. All this bounty came with a standing command that echoed through the house of David: "Work gratefully, O family of David!" The blessings were not for show or indulgence but for grateful service.

The verse then closes with a piercing observation: "Only a few of My servants are truly grateful. " True gratitude is rare. It is not a fleeting word of thanks but a constant orientation of the heart that channels every gift toward pleasing the Giver. Solomon and his righteous forefathers were among those few.

This statement casts a shadow forward to the people of Sheba, who failed this test miserably. It also prompts the listener to examine their own state: do I belong to the many who take blessings for granted, or am I striving to be among the few who live in deep, active gratitude?

=14=

This verse recounts a remarkable hidden lesson from Solomon's death. When Allah decreed that Solomon should pass away, he died while leaning on his staff, overseeing the jinn at work. Allah veiled his death from the jinn, and they continued their toilsome labour for a period, unaware that their master had departed from this world. The only thing that eventually revealed his death was a small, seemingly insignificant creature: a termite gnawing through the wooden staff. When the staff finally gave way, Solomon's body collapsed, and the truth became clear. This event exposed a critical falsehood. The jinn, and those humans who sought knowledge of the unseen through them, often claimed or implied access to hidden knowledge. But if the jinn had truly known the unseen, they would not have stayed in their humiliating servitude for all that time, working under a dead master.

The word used for "humiliating servitude" underscores the disgrace of their labour. The verse is a powerful refutation of every form of fortune-telling, soothsaying, and reliance on jinn for hidden information. The unseen belongs to Allah alone, and He shares it with none except what He chooses to reveal to His chosen messengers.

This story also carries a subtle reminder about the frailty of even the greatest worldly power. Solomon, the magnificent king, died like any other human being, and a tiny termite triggered the revelation of his death. It humbles every reader and reinforces that true knowledge and power rest solely with Allah.

=15=

The focus now shifts from grateful servants to an ungrateful nation. Sheba, an ancient people of Yemen, were given a magnificent sign in their own homeland. The valley in which they lived was flanked by two vast orchards, one on the right and one on the left, stretching as far as the eye could see. The land was lush, green, and abundantly productive. This was the result of the famous Marib dam, an engineering marvel that collected and distributed water with remarkable efficiency. Their prophets and scriptures conveyed a clear message: "Eat from the provision of your Lord, and be grateful to Him." The blessings were from Allah, and the proper response was to enjoy them lawfully while recognising the Giver. The message continued, "Yours is a good land and a forgiving Lord. " The land itself was tayyibah, wholesome and pleasant, free from the barren harshness of the surrounding deserts. And the Lord was described as Ghafur, the Forgiving, ready to pardon their shortcomings if they turned to Him. The stage was perfectly set for a life of prosperity and spiritual well-being.

The phrase "good land and a forgiving Lord" encapsulates the ideal combination of material comfort and divine mercy. This beautiful invitation makes their subsequent response all the more tragic. The verse invites every reader to consider their own circumstances and ask: when I am blessed with ease, do I respond with gratitude, or do I forget the One who provided it?

=16=

The response of Sheba was not one of mild forgetfulness but of deliberate turning away. "But they turned away" indicates a conscious rejection of the call to gratitude and worship. They grew arrogant with their prosperity and neglected their duties to Allah. The consequence was swift and catastrophic. Allah sent against them "a devastating flood," known as sayl al-‘Arim, the mighty flood that burst through the Marib dam and swept away their entire irrigation system, their gardens, and their homes. The water that had once been their lifeblood became their destroyer. The lush orchards were completely transformed. The verse paints a picture of utter desolation: "replaced their orchards with two others producing bitter fruit, fruitless bushes, and a few sparse thorny trees." Where once there were sweet fruits and pleasant shade, there was now bitter, inedible produce, wild scrub, and the scattered, prickly lote trees that symbolise desolation. The contrast is stark and heart-breaking. This was not a random natural disaster; it was the direct consequence of their ingratitude, as the next verse will explicitly state. The ruins of Marib remained as a visible lesson for the Arabs who passed by on their trade routes.

The story teaches that blessings are trusts, and when those trusts are met with persistent ingratitude, they can be withdrawn as suddenly as they were given. The physical landscape became a mirror of their spiritual state.

=17=

The verse states plainly that the devastation was a direct recompense. "This is how We rewarded them for their ingratitude." The word for ingratitude here is kufr, which also means disbelief. The two are deeply intertwined: rejecting Allah and rejecting His favours are two sides of the same coin. The punishment was not an arbitrary act of divine anger but a just consequence of their own choices. Then comes a rhetorical question that rings through the ages: "Would We ever punish anyone in such a way except the ungrateful?

" The answer is clearly no. This question establishes a universal principle. Allah does not delight in punishing His servants. Severe, sweeping punishment of this kind is reserved for those who have made ingratitude and denial their defining characteristic. It implies that if a people are afflicted with calamity, it does not necessarily mean they are being punished like Sheba, but persistent, arrogant ingratitude after repeated warnings invites devastating consequences.

The verse also offers comfort to the believer who suffers hardship: trials may be a test or expiation, not necessarily a punishment akin to that of Sheba. For the Meccan pagans listening, the message was clear: your own prosperity is not a sign of Allah's approval, and if you persist in rejecting the Prophet and his message, you are following the path of Sheba. The story is intended to spark a deep, urgent self-reflection.

=18=

Before the destruction, Sheba had enjoyed another immense favour. Allah had placed between their homeland in Yemen and the blessed cities of Syria a chain of small, visible towns. These towns were so closely spaced that they were "within sight of one another." A traveller could set out from one town and see the next one ahead, making the journey easy, safe, and psychologically comfortable. The travel distances were moderate, so a caravan could move from town to town without the fear of running out of supplies or getting lost in the desert. The divine invitation was to "Travel between them by day and night safely." The security and ease were extraordinary for that era, when long journeys typically meant danger, exhaustion, and isolation. This network of settlements turned their trade routes into pleasant, secure passages. They could travel morning or evening without anxiety. The blessing here was not merely the physical infrastructure but the peace of mind it brought.

This verse emphasizes that Allah's favours encompass every dimension of life, from the food on the table to the safety of the roads. For the Quraysh, who prided themselves on their own trade journeys to Yemen and Syria, this was a direct mirror. They too were travelling with remarkable security and prosperity, and the unspoken question was whether they would be more grateful than Sheba, or would repeat their error. The stage is set for Sheba's second act of ingratitude.

=19=

Incredibly, the people of Sheba grew bored with ease. They looked at the closely spaced towns and the comfortable distances and, with a perverse sense of arrogance, asked their Lord to make the journeys longer. They wanted the thrill and challenge of arduous desert travel, the long stretches between stops that would make their journeys feel more adventurous or prestigious. "Our Lord! Make the distances of our journeys longer" was a prayer that came from hearts that had lost appreciation for ease. By asking for this, they were "wronging themselves." Their ingratitude was not harming Allah but inviting their own ruin. Allah answered their request in a terrifying way: He dismantled the entire network of security and ease, scattered them across the land, and reduced them to "cautionary tales." People would say "as scattered as Sheba" to describe a group that had disintegrated into fragments. Their once-united and prosperous civilization became a memory and a proverb. The verse closes with the core lesson: "Surely in this are lessons for whoever is steadfast, grateful." The combination of steadfastness and gratitude is the key. Steadfastness (sabr) is needed to endure hardships without complaint, and gratitude (shukr) is needed to receive blessings without arrogance. The people of Sheba lacked both. The story is a permanent warning that comfort can corrupt, and that asking for unnecessary difficulty can result in losing everything. The believer is trained to say: "O Allah, grant us well-being and make us grateful."

=20=

After recounting the destruction of Sheba and the scatter of the ungrateful, the surah reveals the deeper spiritual dynamic behind such events. "Indeed, Iblis's assumption about them has come true." This refers to the moment when Iblis, after refusing to prostrate to Adam, declared that he would mislead the vast majority of humanity and prove that they were ungrateful to their Lord. Allah granted him respite and allowed the test to unfold. The story of Sheba, and countless other ungrateful nations, confirms that Iblis's prediction was accurate for most people. "So they all follow him" describes the general trend of humanity when it turns away from divine guidance. The attractiveness of worldly ease, the forgetfulness of blessings, and the rejection of messengers all stem from following Iblis's path.

Yet there is a crucial exception: "except a group of true believers." These are the servants of Allah whom Iblis himself acknowledged he could not mislead. They are the ones who combine sincere faith with gratitude, and they are the mukhlaseen, those whom Allah has purified and protected. The verse connects the entire preceding passage to the cosmic struggle between truth and falsehood. It also offers immense hope: no matter how widespread corruption becomes, there will always be a group of sincere believers who resist. For the persecuted believers in Mecca, this was a reassurance that their small numbers were part of a divinely protected remnant. The stage is now set for the next verse, which clarifies that Iblis has no real authority over humanity beyond whispering; the true test is to separate those who believe in the Hereafter from those who waver in doubt.
