=41=

The angels respond to Allah’s question with absolute clarity and a declaration of their own innocence. They begin with “Glory be to You!” which is an immediate exaltation of Allah above any association with the polytheists’ worship. This phrase alone rejects any notion that they deserved the devotion directed at them. They then affirm, “Our loyalty is to You, not them. ” The word translated as “loyalty” is wali, which speaks of a bond of allegiance, protection, and intimate friendship. The angels sever any imagined link between themselves and the idolaters and anchor their devotion exclusively in Allah. They then explain the real object of the polytheists’ worship, correcting a fundamental misunderstanding. It was not the angels that the pagans were truly serving, but rather the evil jinn, the devils, who whispered to them and beautified false beliefs. The angels state, “In fact, they only followed the temptations of evil jinn, in whom most of them had faith.” The pagan Arabs did not consciously think they were worshipping jinn; they erected idols as representations of angels, imagining they were drawing closer to Allah. But behind these practices were the devils, who inspired them to carve idols, to make offerings, and to seek intercession in forbidden ways. The angels’ testimony reveals that the entire system was a deception. The jinn received the worship indirectly, delighting in the misguidance.

The phrase “most of them had faith” indicates that the majority of the pagans were deeply entangled in this satanic web. This verse is a profound unveiling of the hidden reality of polytheism. It shows that idol worship is not just a mistaken theology but a spiritual manipulation where evil forces pose behind lifeless statues. On Judgment Day, this exposure brings immense shame upon the worshippers, who now see that their trusted gods were mere illusions and that the angels, whom they hoped would intercede, are the very ones testifying against them.

=42=

After the angels’ testimony, the final verdict is pronounced. The words “So Today” mark a decisive shift from the trial of life to the execution of justice. The address “neither of you” encompasses both the worshippers and whatever they falsely worshipped, including the jinn who misled them. Throughout the world, these entities may have enjoyed a bond of mutual validation: the idols gave a false sense of security, and the worshippers offered devotion. But on this Day, all that collapses. They cannot grant each other any benefit, not even a word of intercession, nor can they offer protection from the coming punishment. The complete breakdown of every false alliance is laid bare. Then Allah, the Sovereign Judge, speaks directly to the wrongdoers with a command that carries both rebuke and grim finality: “Taste the torment of the Fire, which you used to deny. ” The instruction to “taste” makes the suffering immediate and experiential; it is no longer a distant threat but a present, lived reality. The description “which you used to deny” connects the punishment directly to their past mockery. In the earlier parts of the surah, they scoffed at the idea of the Hour and asked when this threat would come. Now the very thing they called impossible and dismissed as a fabricated lie engulfs them. There is a terrifying precision in the phrase: they denied it, so they are made to taste it, so that they know with absolute certainty what they rejected. This verse demonstrates that the punishments of the Hereafter are not arbitrary cruelties but the natural harvest of persistent denial. The silence of the false gods and the finality of the divine command leave no room for appeal. It is a moment of perfect, unveiled justice.

=43=

The surah now returns to the Meccan scene to explain why such a severe punishment becomes deserved. It describes the reaction of the disbelievers whenever the clear, lucid verses of the Quran were recited to them. Instead of opening their hearts, they launched a multi-pronged attack on the Prophet and his message. Their first accusation was, “This is only a man who wishes to hinder you from what your forefathers used to worship. ” They framed him as a troublemaker seeking to divide families and disrupt ancestral traditions. This appeal to tribalism and heritage was designed to stir emotional resistance rather than intellectual engagement. Their second accusation escalated the attack: “This Quran is no more than a fabricated lie.” They dismissed the entire revelation as a forgery.

Yet this claim sat uncomfortably with their third and most contradictory accusation: “This is nothing but pure magic.” Calling it magic acknowledged that the Quran had an extraordinary effect that they could not match or explain away. Magic implies a supernatural but deceptive power, whereas a fabricated lie implies ordinary falsehood. The inconsistency of their charges revealed that they were not conducting a fair investigation; they were simply throwing any label they could at the truth to discredit it.

The phrase “of the truth when it has come to them” is significant. They were not rejecting an ambiguous claim; they were rejecting the truth itself after it had arrived with unmistakable clarity. Their hostility was not born of confusion but of stubborn refusal. This verse captures the psychology of those who feel their entire worldview threatened by divine revelation. Rather than contemplate its message, they try to frame the messenger as a homewrecker, a liar, or a magician, hoping to dissuade others from listening.

=44=

This verse exposes a crucial dimension of the Arabs’ situation that made their rejection particularly unjustifiable. The opening words, “They say so even though,” introduce a contrast. Their accusations were hurled not from a position of prior divine instruction that they could compare and find fault with, but from a background of scriptural emptiness. Allah states, “We had never given them any scriptures to study. ” Unlike the Jews and Christians, who had received the Torah and the Gospel and could debate theological nuances, the Arab polytheists had no earlier revealed book. They were a people without a written, detailed divine law for ages. They were not, therefore, rejecting the Quran in favour of an existing scripture they deemed superior; they had nothing.

Moreover, “nor did We ever send them a warner before you O Prophet. ” The long period between Ishmael and Muhammad, peace be upon them, is known as the fatrah, a gap in prophetic missions. The Meccans had not been exposed to a living prophet in centuries. So when the Prophet Muhammad came with a clear message, it should have been a moment of immense welcome and relief. Instead, they attacked him. Their lack of prior scripture made their denial even more indefensible, because they could not claim they were already following a clear, existing revelation. At the same time, the verse carries a note of mercy and justice: Allah does not punish a people until He has sent a warner. Now that a warner had come with a scripture, they had no excuse. This demolishes any potential argument they might have made about being taken by surprise. They were fully informed, and their rejection was purely out of arrogance and attachment to ancestral ways.

=45=

The surah now reinforces the warning by turning to history. The disbelievers of Mecca were not unique; they were following a well-worn path trodden by many before them. “Those destroyed before them denied as well.” Nations like Ad, Thamud, the people of Lot, and the Egyptians had all rejected their messengers. Despite their denials, they were utterly destroyed.

The verse then draws a stark comparison in material strength: “and these Meccans have not attained even one-tenth of what We had given their predecessors.” The earlier nations were granted far greater wealth, might, architecture, longevity, and power. The Ad were giants in stature and built high towers. Thamud carved homes into mountains. Pharaoh ruled a vast kingdom. The Quraysh, by comparison, were a small tribal merchant community. If those mighty civilizations could not withstand Allah’s punishment when they denied the messengers, how could the comparatively weak Meccans expect to escape? The rhetorical force is devastating.

The final exclamation drives the point home: “Yet when they denied My messengers, how severe was My response!” The vivid pronoun “My” adds a personal dimension, emphasizing the gravity of rejecting Allah’s own chosen envoys. This verse is designed to shatter any illusion of invincibility. It tells the pagans that their modest trade wealth and tribal alliances would be nothing before the divine punishment that had already toppled empires. It also comforts the believers, assuring them that the opposition they face is neither new nor unbeatable; the same false sense of security preceded past destructions, and Allah’s response proved decisive.

=46=

After the heavy historical warnings, the tone softens into a sincere, heartfelt appeal. The Prophet is instructed to say, “I advise you to do only one thing.

” This is an invitation stripped of demands, rituals, or immediate commitments. It is a call to a simple, honest process of introspection. “Stand up for the sake of Allah—individually or in pairs—then reflect.” The act of standing up implies a physical and mental posture of seriousness. They are to rise above their daily distractions, separate themselves from the crowd, whether alone or with a trusted companion, and dedicate a moment purely for the sake of truth, not for argument or tribal pride. Then they are to reflect, using their God-given reason, on the message and the messenger. The purpose of this reflection is stated plainly: to recognize that “Your fellow man is not insane.” They knew the Prophet as the trustworthy, sane, and upright man he had always been. A moment of honest reflection would confirm that his character had not suddenly transformed into madness. He was, in reality, “only a warner to you before the coming of a severe punishment. ” His role was not to dominate them or seize their wealth; it was to deliver a compassionate warning.

The phrase “your fellow man” evokes closeness and familiarity, breaking down the barrier the disbelievers had erected. This verse is a masterclass in da‘wah, showing that sometimes the most powerful call is to invite people to step back from the noise and think sincerely for themselves, with the Prophet’s well-known character as the primary testimony.

=47=

To further remove any suspicion of personal motive, the Prophet is instructed to publicly renounce any worldly compensation. “If I had ever asked you for a reward, you could keep it.” The hypothetical construction is generous: even if he had made such a request, they were free to refuse it. But the reality was that he had never asked for anything. He had endured years of hardship, ostracism, and persecution without ever seeking payment. This stood in stark contrast to soothsayers and poets who charged for their services. The declaration, “My reward is only from Allah,” is an assertion of complete trust and independence. He laboured solely for Allah’s approval, not for material gain.

This is a recurring marker of a true prophet, as mentioned in the stories of earlier messengers who also told their people they asked for no reward. The verse then seals the statement with “And He is a Witness over all things.” Allah is the ultimate observer of every intention, every secret, and every transaction. He knows the purity of the Prophet’s mission and the baselessness of the accusations against him. This verse is deeply comforting to the Prophet, who is told that his sincerity is witnessed by the One who matters most. For the audience, it removes a major psychological barrier: the fear that the Prophet was calling them in order to enrich himself or gain power. Once that is stripped away, they are left face to face with the message itself, with no distraction.

The verse teaches all callers to the truth that their sincerity must be transparent, and their reliance must be placed wholly on Allah.

=48=

The surah now gives a powerful image of how the divine message operates. The Prophet is to say, “Surely my Lord hurls the truth against falsehood.” The verb “hurls” paints a picture of a projectile, a stone or a spear, thrown with precision and force. Truth is not passive; it is an active force that confronts and shatters falsehood. When the Quran is revealed, it does not merely present an alternative view; it attacks the foundations of idolatry, corruption, and ignorance, demolishing them.

This is exactly what the Meccans were experiencing. The truth they tried to suppress kept breaking through their defenses.

The verse then adds the reason why truth is so effective: “He is the Knower of all unseen. ” Truth comes from the source of complete knowledge. Allah knows the hidden realities of the heavens and the earth, the secrets of hearts, and the ultimate outcomes of all affairs.

Therefore, the truth He reveals is based on absolute and comprehensive knowledge, whereas falsehood is always based on guesswork, limited perception, and hidden agendas. The link to the unseen also counters the disbelievers’ mocking questions about the Hour and resurrection. They claimed no one could know such things, but the Prophet’s Lord is precisely the Knower of the unseen. This verse builds on the earlier challenge to reflect: the truth has an inherent power to overcome doubt when a heart opens to it.

For the believer, this is a source of immense confidence. No matter how entrenched falsehood appears, the truth that Allah hurls against it will eventually prevail, guided by His perfect knowledge.

=49=

This is a concise, thunderous declaration of the ultimate outcome. “The truth has come” points to the arrival of Islam and the Quran. It is presented as a completed event, a reality that has already begun to unfold. “Falsehood will vanish” is a prophecy of its complete eradication.

The Arabic word for falsehood, batil, carries the meaning of something that is inherently perishable and unstable. Falsehood may appear imposing for a time, but it has no solid foundation.

The final clause, “never to return,” adds absolute finality. Falsehood will not simply retreat temporarily; it will be obliterated with no possibility of a comeback. In the immediate context, this was a promise that the idolatry dominating Arabia would be swept away. The Prophet, who was then a persecuted individual with a handful of followers, was instructed to proclaim the demise of the entire pagan system. History proved this proclamation true, as the idols were smashed and the Kaaba was purified within a few decades. But the verse also operates on a universal and spiritual level. In the cosmic struggle, truth is the original and permanent reality, while falsehood is a foreign intrusion destined for extinction. Every believer struggling against personal sins or societal corruption can draw strength from this verse: the falsehood they face has no sustainable future. It is only a matter of time before it vanishes.

The verse also serves as a warning: siding with falsehood means attaching oneself to something inherently doomed. The choice laid before the Quraysh was between the eternal truth and the vanishing lie.

=50=

After the bold proclamation of truth’s victory, the surah models the perfect humility of a true messenger. The Prophet is told to say, “If I am astray, the loss is only mine.

” This is a hypothetical that does not imply any real possibility of the Prophet being astray, but it demonstrates his complete submission to Allah’s judgment. He does not claim personal infallibility apart from what Allah grants. If, hypothetically, he were in error, he alone would bear the devastating consequences. Conversely, “And if I am guided, it is only because of what my Lord reveals to me.” He attributes all his guidance, righteousness, and knowledge to the revelation from Allah, not to his own intellect or effort. This strikes at the root of the arrogance the Quraysh displayed. The Prophet claims nothing for himself; his entire standing rests on divine grace.

The verse concludes with a beautiful description of Allah: “He is indeed All-Hearing, Ever Near.” All-Hearing means that Allah hears every supplication, every cry for guidance, and every word of slander. Ever Near means that His knowledge, help, and mercy are intimately close. He is not a distant, indifferent deity but the One who is qarib, near to His servants. For the sincere seeker, this nearness is a promise that guidance is accessible. The Prophet’s statement is both a disclaimer of ego and a profound teaching: the only lasting loss is self-inflicted misguidance, and the only real guidance is that which descends from the All-Hearing, Ever Near Lord. This verse naturally draws the listener to ask: am I humble enough to receive guidance from above, or do I insist on my own path?

=51=

The surah shifts back to the Day of Judgment with a vivid, emotion-laden scene. The phrase “If only you could see” invites the listener to imagine a sight so terrifying that words alone cannot fully convey it. On that Day, the disbelievers will be horrified—faza‘u, a sudden, overwhelming terror that seizes the heart.

The phrase “with no escape” means there will be no refuge, no mountain to hide behind, no ally to offer shelter. The terror will be absolute and inescapable. Then the verse adds a surprising detail: “And they will be seized from a nearby place.” The punishment does not come from some distant, abstract location; it seizes them from right where they are, from a place they thought was safe. Many commentators explain this “nearby place” as the very ground beneath their feet or the graves from which they are raised. Some interpret it as the plain of gathering itself, where they are surrounded and have no place to flee. The psychological impact is that danger is not remote; it is imminent and encroaching. While in the world they thought the Hour was far away or impossible, on that Day they find themselves caught with no distance left to run. The verse creates a sense of claustrophobic dread. It serves as a final warning to the Meccans who mocked the Prophet: the threat is not a distant myth but a reality that will spring upon them from the nearest point, leaving them in a state of complete horror.

For the believer, it reinforces the urgency of preparation, since the moment of seizure will be startlingly near.

=52=

As the horror grips them, the disbelievers will finally make the declaration they refused in the world: “We do now believe in it all.” They will profess faith in the resurrection, the judgment, the punishment, and the truth of the message they once mocked.

The word “now” is heavy with regret. It is a faith born of sight, not of genuine conviction.

The verse then shuts the door on their late belief with a piercing rhetorical question: “But how could they possibly attain faith from a place so far-off?” The “place so far-off” refers to the Hereafter in relation to the world. In the earthly life, faith is accepted because it is a choice made in the unseen. Once the soul departs and the veils are lifted, the test is over. The distance is not spatial but existential; the opportunity has passed.

This principle is a cornerstone of Islamic teaching: belief upon seeing the punishment is not accepted, as it is no longer a matter of free will but of compulsion. The verse evokes the deep tragedy of a chance forever lost. The Meccans were being offered the priceless opportunity to believe in the unseen, but they squandered it with mockery. The scene also warns every human being not to delay repentance, because the window of acceptance closes with death. The far-off place is as unreachable as the world is for the dead.

The image of them crying out their belief from an impossible distance highlights the futility of last-minute confessions.

=53=

The verse completes the symmetry of their loss. Not only are they now crying out from a far-off place in the Hereafter, but they had also spent their worldly lives in a state equally distant from the truth. “While they had already rejected it before” refers to their persistent denial during their earthly existence. “Guessing blindly” translates a word that implies throwing out conjectures without any foundation. Their rejection was not based on knowledge or investigation; it was built on pure guesswork, on following ancestors, on mocking without reflection.

The phrase “from a place equally far-away” reveals the tragic irony. In the world, they were spiritually far from the truth, lost in ignorance and falsehood, just as they are now physically and existentially far from any chance of salvation. They were distant from the Hereafter in their consciousness, living as if it would never come. So their whole existence was spent in a remote place of heedlessness, and now they find themselves in another remote place of irreversible regret. The verse demonstrates that their failure is double: they rejected the truth when it was near them in the form of the Quran and the Prophet, and now they attempt to grasp it when it is forever out of reach. This parallel structure is a rhetorical masterpiece, exposing the root cause of their doom. It also reminds believers that faith must be rooted in certainty, not guesswork. The one who follows their Lord with clear proof is in a completely different state from the one who flounders in blind conjecture.

=54=

The surah closes with a scene of complete deprivation. “They will be sealed off from whatever they desire.” A barrier, a hijab, will be placed between them and everything they long for. On that Day, their desires will include escape from the punishment, return to the world to do good, intercession from their false gods, or even annihilation from the torment. All of it will be denied. The sealing off is a final, irreversible separation from hope.

The verse then links this fate to a recurring historical pattern: “as was done to their counterparts before.” The same sealing off was applied to the disbelievers of earlier nations who refused their messengers.

This statement strips the Meccans of any illusion of exceptionalism; they are merely the latest link in a chain of those who chose doubt over faith. The final sentence diagnoses the underlying disease: “Indeed, they were all in alarming doubt.” Their entire opposition did not stem from a genuine intellectual difficulty but from a deep-seated, unsettling doubt that they nursed and fed. Doubt here is not a humble, searching uncertainty; it is a corrupting suspicion that rejects conviction and prefers to drift in skepticism. This doubt is described as mureeb, alarming and disquieting, because it offered them no peace in life and has now led them to an eternity of despair. The surah, which began with the praise of Allah and the certainty of the Hour, ends with the miserable consequence of living in doubt. It leaves the reader with a powerful resolution: to choose the clarity of faith, the gratitude of David and Solomon, over the ingratitude and skeptical arrogance of Sheba and their Meccan counterparts. The entire passage of Saba’ has thus been completed, taking us on a journey from cosmic praise to the intimate tragedy of the human heart that refuses to believe.
