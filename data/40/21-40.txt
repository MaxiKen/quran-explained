=21=

After establishing that Allah is the All-Hearing, All-Seeing who judges with truth, the surah poses a penetrating question to the Meccan disbelievers: have they not travelled throughout the land? The Quraysh were a trading people, their caravans journeying to Yemen in winter and Syria in summer, and they regularly passed the ruins of destroyed civilisations like ‘Âd and Thamûd. The verse appeals to this firsthand experience. It asks them to see what was the end of those before them. Those previous nations were far superior in might and richer in monuments. Their physical power, advanced buildings, and impressive structures surpassed anything the Quraysh possessed.

Yet none of that saved them. Allah seized them for their sins, a seizure that was sudden, comprehensive, and irrevocable.

The phrase "they had no protector from Allah" means that when divine punishment descended, no ally, god, or fortified city could repel it. The verse subtly reminds the Quraysh that their own wealth and strength are no guarantee against a similar fate. It also serves as a methodological lesson: travel and historical observation are a means to spiritual insight.

For the believer today, it calls for a reflective reading of history, not as mere entertainment but as a source of moral and spiritual lessons. The ruins of mighty empires testify that injustice and disbelief eventually lead to ruin, no matter how long they are deferred.

=22=

The surah explains why those earlier nations were destroyed. They were not punished arbitrarily. Their messengers came to them with clear proofs—miracles, sound arguments, and scriptures that left no room for honest doubt.

The phrase "clear proofs" indicates evidence so apparent that only stubbornness could reject it. Despite this, they persisted in disbelief. Their rejection was not a momentary slip but an entrenched refusal to accept the truth. The consequence is described with a familiar, terrifying refrain: "So Allah seized them.

" The word "seized" conveys a sense of being gripped with irresistible force, without escape. The verse concludes by affirming two divine attributes: "Surely He is All-Powerful, severe in punishment." His power guarantees that no one can overpower Him, and the severity of His punishment serves as a deterrent. This verse connects directly to the Prophet's situation, reminding him that the Quraysh's denial fits a historical pattern. It also comforted him, for it meant that divine justice would eventually deal with the rejecters as it had before. For all readers, it emphasises that the arrival of clear guidance leaves no excuse. Persistence in disbelief after understanding the truth makes one liable to a punishment that is both just and terrifying.

=23=

Moving from the general principle of destroyed nations, the surah now presents a detailed example: the story of Moses. The phrase "Indeed, We sent Moses" signals a shift to a narrative that will dominate much of the chapter. Moses was sent with "Our signs," referring to the miracles such as the staff turning into a serpent, the shining hand, and the other nine signs, as well as the Torah.

The term "compelling proof" translates sultān, which connotes clear authority and evidence that overwhelms any counterargument. The use of "We" speaks with divine majesty. Moses’ mission was to confront one of history’s greatest tyrants, and his story would resonate deeply with the Prophet Muhammad, who also faced a powerful, arrogant elite in Mecca. This introduction reminds the listener that Moses did not act on his own; he was equipped by Allah with everything necessary to establish the truth.

The verse also reassures that the struggle between truth and falsehood is ancient, and the outcome is always in favour of the messengers, even when appearances suggest otherwise. The transition from the general warning to a specific prophetic biography makes the lesson concrete and personal for the Prophet and the believers, as they see themselves mirrored in Moses’ experience.

=24=

The recipients of Moses’ mission are named: Pharaoh, Hamân, and Korah. Pharaoh was the tyrannical king of Egypt who claimed divinity. Hamân, according to the Quran, was his close advisor and minister, often associated with overseeing building projects. Korah (Qārūn) was a wealthy Israelite who aligned himself with Pharaoh’s cause, known for his arrogance and rebellion against Moses. These three represent different faces of power: political tyranny, bureaucratic enforcement, and economic might. When Moses came to them with clear signs, their response was not thoughtful consideration but immediate verbal abuse. They called him a magician and a total liar. Calling him a magician was their attempt to explain away his miracles, attributing them to trickery rather than divine power. Calling him a total liar was an attack on his character, intended to destroy his credibility. The juxtaposition of "magician" and "total liar" shows the confusion of the disbelievers: they cannot deny the extraordinary nature of his signs, so they label them magic, yet they also claim he is fabricating everything. This contradictory accusation reveals the irrationality that often accompanies arrogant disbelief. For the Prophet, this was a mirror of his own experience, when the Quraysh called him a poet, a soothsayer, or a madman.

=25=

The narrative escalates. When Moses presented not just signs but the truth—the clear message of monotheism and the call to free the Israelites—the response of Pharaoh and his elite became murderous. They ordered the killing of the sons of those who believed with him and the sparing of their women. This was a revival of the earlier policy of slaughtering male infants of the Israelites, now specifically targeting the families of the believers. It was a brutal attempt to crush the fledgling faith through intimidation and demographic engineering.

The phrase "keep their women" meant they would leave the women alive to be exploited and enslaved. Yet the verse immediately adds that the plotting of the disbelievers was only in vain. Their schemes, however carefully planned, could not succeed against divine protection. This verse taught the Prophet that the Quraysh’s persecution, including social and economic boycotts, was not new, and that just as Pharaoh’s plan failed, so would theirs. For all believers, it is a profound reassurance that no matter how formidable the enemy’s plans appear, they are ultimately futile if Allah wills to protect His cause and His people.

=26=

The personal animosity of Pharaoh reaches a climax. He arrogantly tells his council, “Let me kill Moses.” He is so convinced of his own power that he dismisses Moses’ connection to the divine with mockery: “and let him call upon his Lord!” implying that even Moses’ God cannot stop him. Then Pharaoh reveals his political motivation, cloaked in paternalistic concern: “I truly fear that he may change your traditions or cause mischief in the land. ” Pharaoh frames Moses’ call to monotheism as a threat to the established social and religious order. He equates faith with mischief, a classic tactic of tyrants who portray reformers as dangerous subversives.

The phrase “change your traditions” refers to the idolatrous religion and the oppressive system that kept Pharaoh in power. Pharaoh’s “fear” is not genuine care for his people but fear of losing control. The verse exposes how those in power often suppress truth under the pretext of preserving stability. For the Prophet, who was accused by the Quraysh of dividing families and attacking ancestral ways, this was a direct parallel and a source of comfort. It also warns believers that truth will often be labelled as disruption, but they must remain steadfast, trusting that the arrogance of falsehood will ultimately be its downfall.

=27=

In the face of Pharaoh’s murderous threat, Moses does not panic or respond with violence. He turns immediately to Allah with a profound statement of refuge. By saying “my Lord and your Lord,” Moses simultaneously affirms monotheism and reminds Pharaoh that the God he denies is, in reality, his own Lord, whether he acknowledges it or not. Moses seeks protection not only from Pharaoh specifically but from “every arrogant person who does not believe in the Day of Reckoning. ” This categorises Pharaoh as a type—the arrogant disbeliever—whose defining traits are pride and denial of accountability. Moses’ prayer teaches that the root of tyranny is arrogance combined with disbelief in the Hereafter. When a person does not fear a final judgment, there is no moral brake on their oppression.

The phrase “seek refuge” indicates that Moses, despite being a mighty prophet, knows that true protection comes only from Allah. This is a model for all believers facing powerful enemies: one’s first resort should be to Allah, and one should identify the spiritual illness behind the oppression.

The verse also subtly contrasts Moses’ humility and dependence on Allah with Pharaoh’s delusional self-sufficiency. For the Prophet in Mecca, this was a lesson in remaining dignified and reliant on Allah when threatened.

=28=

The surah now introduces a remarkable character: a believing man from Pharaoh’s own inner circle who had concealed his faith until this critical moment. He steps forward and argues with his people using calm, rational logic. His first point is that they are about to kill a man merely for saying “My Lord is Allah,” a statement that should not be a capital crime. He then points out that Moses has come with clear proofs—miracles that cannot be dismissed—so there is at least a possibility that he is truthful. He presents a pragmatic argument: if Moses is a liar, his lies will harm only himself, and they need not fear him. But if he is truthful, then killing him will bring upon them some of the punishment he is warning about.

This is a call for caution and risk assessment, based on reason rather than blind rage. He concludes by stating that Allah does not guide a transgressor and total liar, implying that Pharaoh’s character fits this description. The man’s speech is a masterclass in delivering truth in a hostile environment: he uses logic, appeals to self-interest, and avoids direct confrontation while still upholding the truth. His hidden faith also shows that believers can exist within corrupt systems, and there may come a time to speak out.

=29=

The believing man continues his speech, now addressing his people with a blend of acknowledgment and warning. He concedes that they currently hold authority and are dominant in the land, which should make them feel secure. But he immediately punctures that false sense of security with a piercing question: who would help them against the torment of Allah if it were to befall them? Their power, in the face of divine punishment, would be utterly useless. This rhetorical question is designed to make them reflect on their vulnerability. Pharaoh, sensing the effect of these words, interrupts with a manipulative assertion: “I am telling you only what I believe, and I am leading you only to the way of guidance.” Pharaoh poses as a sincere advisor, claiming that his own beliefs and his leadership are genuinely for their benefit.

This is a classic tactic of tyrants: to present their misguidance as the true path. The juxtaposition of the believing man’s sincere warning and Pharaoh’s deceptive reassurance sharpens the moral drama. It reminds us that when truth challenges falsehood, the proponents of falsehood will try to sound reasonable and caring, so discernment is crucial. The believer’s question remains a timeless wake-up call: political power is no shield against divine justice.

=30=

The believing man now intensifies his warning, shifting from general reflection to a specific historical analogy. He tells his people that he fears for them a doom like that of the earlier enemy forces.

The term "enemy forces" refers to those confederations of disbelievers who banded together to fight against the prophets, only to be wiped out by divine punishment. By using this phrase, he is implicitly comparing Pharaoh's regime to those doomed groups. He speaks with genuine concern, saying “I truly fear for you,” which reveals his compassion even for his disbelieving countrymen.

This is a hallmark of sincere faith: a believer does not wish destruction on others but warns them out of love and fear for their fate. The word "doom" conveys a sense of inescapable catastrophe, the kind of total ruin that leaves no survivors. For the Meccans, this was another reminder of the ‘Âd and Thamûd whose stories they knew well. The verse demonstrates that history is not just a record of events but a repository of divine patterns. Those who are mindful can see those patterns and avoid the mistakes of the past. The believer’s fear is not a personal anxiety but a prophetic-like concern rooted in knowledge of Allah’s unchanging way.

=31=

The warning becomes concrete as the man names specific destroyed nations: the people of Noah, ‘Âd, Thamûd, and those after them. Each of these nations had received clear warnings, rejected them, and were annihilated.

The mention of these names would resonate with any Arab audience because of their familiarity with these stories. The man then adds a crucial theological point: "For Allah would never will to wrong His servants. " This is both a reassurance and a basis for the warning. The destruction was not a random act of a tyrant deity but a just recompense for their own actions. Allah, being perfectly just, does not punish anyone without cause.

Therefore, if punishment came upon them, it was because they themselves had invited it through their disbelief and oppression. This statement also refutes any notion that the divine decree is unjust. It affirms that human beings are responsible for their choices, and that divine justice is meticulous. By citing this principle, the believing man is telling his people that if they follow the same path as those destroyed nations, they cannot expect a different outcome, because Allah's justice is consistent. The verse invites the listener to reflect on the justice of Allah, a justice that is neither capricious nor cruel but firmly rooted in cause and effect.

=32=

The man’s warning shifts from historical examples to the ultimate future event: the Day of Judgment. He says, "I truly fear for you the Day all will be crying out to each other.

" The phrase "crying out" refers to the mutual calling that will occur on that Day. People will call for help, call for their families, call for someone to save them, but all such calls will be in vain. The Day is described as a time of intense distress where normal communication breaks down into desperate shouts. The man’s fear is not a distant, theoretical concern; it is a vivid, heartfelt dread based on his faith in the unseen. He is trying to make the reality of that Day present in their minds, to break through their heedlessness. The fact that he says "I truly fear" again underscores his sincerity and compassion.

This is a model for all who call others to faith: the motivation must be genuine care, not a desire to win arguments or assert superiority. The verse also links to the earlier warning of the Day of Meeting in this surah, creating a coherent thread of eschatological warning that runs through the chapter. It serves as a stark reminder that the world’s conflicts and oppressions will be resolved on a day that no one can escape.

=33=

The description of that Day continues. When the judgment begins, the disbelievers will attempt to flee, but there will be nowhere to go. "You will try in vain to turn your backs and run away" paints a picture of panic, but the flight is futile. There will be no one to protect them from Allah, meaning no ally, no intercessor, and no physical shelter can shield them from the divine judgment. Their worldly power and connections will dissolve entirely. The man then adds a fundamental spiritual principle: "And whoever Allah leaves to stray will be left with no guide.

" This is not to say that Allah misguides people arbitrarily; rather, He leaves them to stray as a consequence of their own persistent rejection of guidance. Once Allah removes His guiding light from a person because of their arrogance, no one else can guide them.

The phrase is both a warning and a call to humility: one must cling to divine guidance now, because if it is withdrawn, the loss is irrecoverable. For Pharaoh's people, this was a direct challenge to their belief that their king was their guide.

The verse teaches that true guidance is a treasure that must be cherished and sought continuously. For the Prophet and the believers, it was a reminder that their guidance was a gift from Allah, not a product of their own intelligence.

=34=

The believing man now cites a historical precedent from their own land: the story of Joseph. Joseph was a prophet sent to Egypt long before Moses. He came with clear proofs, which included his virtuous character, the interpretation of dreams, and his miraculous survival and rise to power.

Yet the Egyptians never ceased to doubt what he brought. Their belief was not firm; they wavered and questioned. When Joseph died, they declared that Allah would never send a messenger after him, using his death as an excuse to close the door to further prophecy. This reveals a pattern of human self-deception: acknowledging a messenger’s truth but then, after his departure, dismissing the possibility of future guidance.

The verse concludes, "This is how Allah leaves every transgressor and doubter to stray. " The combination of transgression and doubt is deadly. The transgressor violates moral boundaries, and the doubter refuses to commit to faith even when evidence is clear. As a result, Allah allows them to wander in error. For the Quraysh, who might have acknowledged earlier prophets but rejected Muhammad, peace be upon him, this was a mirror. It shows that claiming to respect past prophets while rejecting the contemporary one is a hollow excuse that leads to ruin.

=35=

Continuing the description of those left to stray, the verse identifies them as those who dispute Allah’s signs without any valid proof. Their arguments are not based on evidence but on stubborn resistance and empty rhetoric.

The phrase "with no proof given to them" exposes the intellectual bankruptcy of their position. Then comes a powerful exclamation: "How despicable is that for Allah and the believers! " This disputation incurs immense hatred from Allah because it is a direct assault on His truth, and it is also despised by the believers who see the truth being maligned.

The verse ends with a terrifying statement: "This is how Allah seals the heart of every arrogant tyrant." A sealed heart is one that cannot receive guidance, locked in a state of spiritual blindness. The cause is twofold: arrogance and tyranny. Arrogance prevents a person from submitting to the truth, and tyranny makes them oppress others and fight against the message. The sealing is both a consequence and a punishment; it is the natural result of repeated rejection.

This verse concludes the believing man’s speech with a sobering diagnosis of Pharaoh’s condition. For the listener, it serves as a warning to guard against arrogance, which is the gateway to a sealed heart.

=36=

In response to the believing man’s profound arguments, Pharaoh does not reflect or relent. Instead, he displays his arrogance in a spectacularly absurd command. He orders Hamân, his minister, to build him a high tower. The purpose? To reach the pathways.

The word "pathways" (asbāb) can mean the routes or means of access to the heavens. Pharaoh, who claimed to be a god, now wants to physically ascend to the sky to confront or inspect the God of Moses.

This command reveals his delusional state. He is so intoxicated with power that he believes he can build a structure tall enough to pierce the heavens and challenge the divine. The demand also shows his ignorance of the natural world; he treats the sky as a physical ceiling that can be reached with enough bricks.

Historically, this reflects the ancient building projects of Egypt, like the pyramids, which were meant to connect the earthly and divine realms. But here, it is portrayed as an act of defiance, not piety. For the Prophet, this was a vivid example of how far a tyrant will go in his arrogance.

The verse also serves as a timeless reminder that those who reject faith often resort to grand but futile projects, attempting to assert their dominance over a universe that is entirely under Allah’s control.

=37=

Pharaoh clarifies his intention: he wants to ascend to the heavens and look for the God of Moses. His statement "although I am sure he is a liar" reveals a contradiction. If he is so sure Moses is lying, why go to such lengths? It betrays an inner turmoil, a fear that perhaps Moses is not lying, and he must prove it to himself. But his arrogance will not allow him to admit the truth. Then the verse explains a profound psychological and spiritual principle: "And so Pharaoh’s evil deeds were made so appealing to him that he was hindered from the Right Way." His own actions, his tyranny, his claim to divinity, his oppression, were beautified in his eyes. This beautification is a form of divine punishment, where a person’s evil choices become so attractive to them that they see no need to change. It acts as a barrier, preventing them from even considering the truth.

The verse concludes, "But the plotting of Pharaoh was only in vain." His tower, his schemes, his military might—all came to nothing. The verse reaffirms that no matter how attractive falsehood may seem to its proponents, it is ultimately doomed.

For believers, this is a lesson in not envying the apparent success of evildoers, for their path is a trap that leads to destruction.

=38=

While Pharaoh busies himself with his futile tower, the believing man steps forward again, this time with a direct and courageous invitation. He says, "O my people! Follow me, and I will lead you to the Way of Guidance.

" This is a striking statement. A single individual, from the oppressed minority, stands before the ruling elite and calls them to follow him to the truth. He does not claim to be a prophet but a guide who points to the path of the prophets. His invitation is personal and urgent.

The phrase "Way of Guidance" refers to the straight path that leads to Allah’s pleasure and eternal success. This man embodies the role of a true caller: he does not merely criticise the existing system but offers a clear alternative. He models the courage required to speak truth in the face of power. For the Prophet and his companions, this was a source of inspiration, showing that even a single believer within a tyrannical system can be a beacon of guidance. The man’s call also implies that following him is not blind allegiance but a shared journey towards divine guidance, a contrast to Pharaoh’s demand for blind obedience.

The verse teaches that every believer has a responsibility to invite others to the truth, using wisdom and sincerity.

=39=

The believing man now provides the fundamental reason to prioritise his call. He describes this worldly life as only a fleeting enjoyment.

The word "fleeting" captures the temporary, deceptive nature of worldly pleasures. They appear delightful but vanish quickly, like a crop that flourishes then withers.

In contrast, the Hereafter is truly the home of settlement. The term "settlement" implies permanence, stability, and true abode. This world is a passage, a corridor, while the next life is the destination where one will dwell forever. The man is asking his people to weigh the temporary against the eternal. Pharaoh’s court was immersed in luxury, power, and monuments, all of which were the epitome of worldly enjoyment. By labelling it fleeting, the man undermines its value and redirects attention to what lasts.

This is a core Quranic theme, and it was especially important for the early Muslims who were giving up worldly benefits for their faith. The verse is a powerful tool for reorienting one’s priorities: every decision should be made in light of eternity. It does not call for the abandonment of the world but for recognising its true status so that one uses it for the Hereafter rather than being enslaved by it.

=40=

The man concludes this segment of his speech by stating the divine principle of justice and mercy. The first part establishes that whoever does an evil deed will only be paid back with its equivalent. The recompense is exactly proportionate; there is no injustice, no amplification of punishment beyond what is deserved. This assures that divine judgment is not vengeful but perfectly balanced.

The second part brings immense hope: whoever does good, whether male or female, and is a believer, will enter Paradise. The explicit inclusion of both genders underscores that spiritual worth and reward are not based on gender but on faith and righteous deeds. The condition of being a believer is crucial; good deeds without faith do not yield eternal reward in the Hereafter because they lack the necessary foundation of monotheism and divine acceptance. Once admitted to Paradise, they will be provided for without limit.

The term "without limit" means without reckoning, without end, and without any reduction. This contrasts sharply with the fleeting enjoyment of the world. The verse beautifully balances justice and grace: evil receives exactly what it earns, while good is rewarded with limitless bounty. For Pharaoh’s people, this was an invitation to trade a life of temporary luxury for an eternal, boundless provision. For every listener, it clarifies that no act of goodness, however small, is lost, and that Paradise is the ultimate, unrestricted gift for those who combine belief and virtue.
