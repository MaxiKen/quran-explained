After listing the blessings of cattle, ships, and the mastery over land and sea, the surah issues a direct challenge to the human conscience. “And He shows you His signs.” The present tense “shows” indicates that the display of signs is continuous, not a one-time event. Every sunrise, every rainfall, every beat of the heart, every piece of provision is a sign. The signs are not hidden mysteries but clear pointers to the Creator’s existence, power, and mercy. They are shown in the vast horizons and within the human soul itself. Then comes the piercing question: “Now which of Allah’s signs will you deny?” This rhetorical question is both an accusation and an invitation. It forces the listener to review the countless signs they have witnessed and to pinpoint which of them they can rationally reject. The expected answer is none, for denial of even one sign after clear display would be irrational.

The question also carries a tone of gentle rebuke: after all that He has shown you, how can you persist in ingratitude? For the Quraysh, who relied on Allah’s signs for their trade, their health, and their very existence, yet worshipped other gods, this was a call to intellectual honesty.

The verse teaches that disbelief is not due to a lack of evidence but a refusal to accept it. The question remains alive for every generation: which of the signs, from the microcosm of the cell to the macrocosm of the galaxies, can one legitimately deny? None, because they all testify to the same truth. This verse connects seamlessly to the preceding mention of cattle and ships, which were themselves specific signs, and now broadens the scope to include all signs in creation and revelation, inviting a response of faith, gratitude, and submission.

=82=

The surah returns to the theme of historical observation, repeating the call already sounded in verse 21, but with added emphasis. “Have they not travelled throughout the land” is a question that appeals to the Quraysh’s own experience. Their trade journeys took them past the ruins of ‘Âd, Thamûd, and the people of Lot. The verse asks them to see what was the end of those who were destroyed before them. Those nations were not weak; they were far superior in might and richer in monuments. Their buildings, their irrigation systems, their military strength were legendary. The Arabs knew these stories and saw the remnants.

Yet “their worldly gains were of no benefit to them. ” When divine punishment arrived, their wealth, their walls, their armies could not repel it. The verse highlights a profound truth: material superiority is no protection against spiritual failure.

The repetition of this call in the surah indicates its importance. It establishes a universal law: civilisations that reject divine guidance, however advanced, eventually face ruin.

This is not just a warning to the Quraysh but a principle for all times. The verse also implies that if one does not learn from the past, one is doomed to repeat it.

The word “monuments” evokes the impressive remains that the Quraysh passed, which should have served as sobering reminders. Yet they turned these ruins into tourist curiosities rather than signs for reflection. The verse invites the reader to travel with insight, to read history as a divine textbook, and to understand that true security lies not in might but in faith and righteousness.

=83=

The surah now explains the inner mechanism of those destroyed nations. “When their messengers came to them with clear proofs,” they faced a choice. The proofs were undeniable, yet the people responded not with humility but with arrogance. They were “prideful in whatever worldly knowledge they had. ” This refers to their expertise in material sciences, technology, architecture, or worldly philosophies that made them feel self-sufficient. They thought their knowledge rendered them independent of divine revelation.

This is a timeless portrait of the attitude that dismisses spiritual truth because of confidence in human achievement. They trusted their own understanding and belittled the message of the prophets. Then the verse delivers the outcome: “and were ultimately overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule.” The very punishment, resurrection, or moral laws they mocked became their reality.

The word “overwhelmed” indicates being encircled and drowned by the thing they laughed at. This is a precise description of what happened to Pharaoh, who ridiculed Moses and then was drowned, and to the Quraysh, who mocked the warning of the Fire and will face it if they do not repent.

The verse teaches a critical lesson: intellectual arrogance is a precursor to destruction. When a people value their own achievements over divine guidance, they become blind to truth and eventually are destroyed by the consequences of their rejection. The “worldly knowledge” could be any body of human learning that fosters pride, including modern science or philosophy if it leads to the denial of the Creator. The antidote is humility before the revealed word, recognising that human knowledge, however vast, is limited and must bow to the wisdom of the All-Knowing.

=84=

The scene shifts to the moment of crisis. When the promised punishment descended upon them, the disbelievers made a sudden declaration of faith. They cried, “Now we believe in Allah alone and reject what we had been associating with Him!

” The word “Now” is crucial. Their belief comes at the eleventh hour, when the punishment is already in sight and there is no room for genuine choice. In the depths of the Flood, at the approach of the howling wind, or as the sea closes over them, they finally affirm the oneness of Allah. Their confession is theologically perfect: they declare exclusive faith in Allah and a complete repudiation of their former idols.

Yet, the verse immediately implies that this confession, though true in content, is worthless because it is coerced. It is not a free turning of the heart but a recognition of an undeniable reality. Faith at the moment of death or during the descent of punishment is not accepted, a principle established elsewhere in the Quran. This scene echoes Pharaoh’s final declaration of belief in the God of the Israelites as he drowned, which was rejected. The verse demonstrates that Allah gives ample time and sends clear warnings so that people can believe freely and sincerely. When that window closes, belief no longer benefits. For the Quraysh, this was a warning not to wait until they saw the promised punishment, whether in battle or at death. For all humanity, it is a call to embrace faith now, while life offers the opportunity for genuine, voluntary submission. The delay of repentance is a gamble with eternal consequences.

=85=

The surah concludes the story of the destroyed nations with a divine verdict. “But their faith was of no benefit to them when they saw Our torment.

” The phrase “of no benefit” indicates that their late belief brought them neither reprieve from the worldly punishment nor salvation in the Hereafter. The door of acceptance had closed.

The verse then states that this is not an arbitrary rule but “Allah’s way of dealing with His wicked servants.” The sunnah of Allah, His established and unchanging pattern, is that once punishment arrives, the chance for faith is over.

This is the way He has dealt with all previous nations who persisted in wickedness until the very end. The term “wicked servants” describes those who go beyond the bounds in sin and rebellion.

The verse ends with a comprehensive statement: “Then and there the disbelievers were in total loss.” Their loss is absolute; they lost the world, their lives, their wealth, their false gods, and their chance at eternal salvation. “Then and there” captures the moment of finality, the point of no return. This conclusion serves as a powerful reinforcement of the warnings that run throughout the surah. For the Prophet and the believers, it was a confirmation that the Quraysh’s apparent safety was an illusion; if they continued in their ways, they too would face a moment when faith would not avail. The verse calls every listener to urgency: the time to believe is now, before the sight of the torment, when belief can still be a genuine transformation rather than a desperate, rejected cry. The entire surah up to this point has built a comprehensive case for faith, and with this verse, the warning reaches its climactic finality.
