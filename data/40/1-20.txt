=1=

These two letters, Ḥâ and Mĩm, stand at the opening of this surah as part of the disjointed letters that begin twenty-nine chapters of the Quran. Their precise meaning is known fully only to Allah, and they serve as a reminder that this revelation is composed of the very letters of the Arabic language, yet no human can produce anything like it. When the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, recited these letters, the Arabs who prided themselves on eloquence were immediately alerted that something beyond their capacity was about to follow. The letters challenge the listener to reflect on how such ordinary components can form a message of extraordinary power, wisdom, and beauty. Scholars have offered various insights, one of which is that these letters draw attention to the miraculous nature of the Quran, as if to say, “From these simple letters comes a Book that guides humanity.” In this particular surah, Ḥâ-Mĩm also introduces a group of chapters known as the Ḥawâmîm, all of which begin with these same two letters, and they share themes of revelation, warning, and the mercy of Allah. By starting with these letters, the surah immediately establishes that the message is not from the Prophet’s own mind but is a divine sending. The very first hearers would stop and listen, recognising that something solemn was being proclaimed.

This opening also connects beautifully to the next verse, which declares the source of the Book, moving from the letters themselves to the One who revealed them. It teaches us that the Quran, though using the language of human beings, originates from the Almighty, and even its smallest units carry divine purpose.

=2=

Having captured attention with the disjointed letters, the surah now states plainly that the revelation of this Book is from Allah. The term “revelation” indicates a sending down, a gradual descent from the highest heavens to the heart of the Prophet. It corrects any notion that the Quran is Muhammad’s own composition or a product of human culture. The verse describes Allah with two mighty names: the Almighty and the All-Knowing.

The name “Almighty” assures the believer that the One who sent this Book has absolute power to enforce its message, to protect it, and to hold to account those who reject it. The name “All-Knowing” assures that the guidance within the Book is perfect, based on complete knowledge of creation, human nature, past and future, and the unseen. Together, these attributes address a common question: how can a Book revealed centuries ago remain eternally relevant? Because it comes from the One whose knowledge encompasses all times and places, and whose power guarantees its preservation. The Prophet and the early Muslims faced fierce opposition in Mecca, and this opening verse fortified their hearts, reminding them that they were not following a human philosophy but a revelation from the One who is irresistible in might and infinite in knowledge. For every reader, this verse roots the entire surah in divine authority; before any command, story, or warning is given, the source is established, so that the heart receives what follows with submission.

The verse also subtly refutes the claims of the disbelievers who alleged that the Prophet fabricated the Quran, for no human, however intelligent, could produce a scripture characterised by such unmatched depth and accuracy.

=3=

After stating that the revelation is from the Almighty, the All-Knowing, the surah further expands the names of Allah in a way that perfectly balances hope and fear. He is the Forgiver of sin and the Accepter of repentance. The two descriptions are closely linked yet distinct. “Forgiver of sin” means He can wipe away the worst of transgressions, however enormous they may be, out of His sheer grace even before repentance is offered, and His forgiveness is a recurring attribute throughout the Quran. “Accepter of repentance” emphasises His welcoming of those who turn back to Him; He not only pardons but actively receives the repentant, transforming their misdeeds into good.

This is immediately followed by “the Severe in punishment,” reminding the listener that forgiveness is not a licence to persist in rebellion, for the same Lord who embraces the penitent also exacts justice on those who arrogantly reject truth. Then comes “Infinite in bounty,” an attribute that covers all blessings, spiritual and material, bestowed without any lack on His part.

The verse then presents the core of faith: “There is no god worthy of worship except Him,” negating all false deities and affirming that worship, in all its forms, belongs solely to Allah. The final phrase, “To Him alone is the final return,” fixes the destination of every soul, making the entire human journey a return to the One who gave life. The Meccan polytheists struggled with this exclusivity, and the verse dismantles their idolatry by linking forgiveness, severity, bounty, and ultimate return to the one true God. It also reassures the Prophet that those who reject his call will not escape, for they will return to a Lord who is both merciful and just.

=4=

The surah now turns to address a painful reality the Prophet faced: the constant disputation of the Quraysh leaders against the Quranic verses. The phrase “none disputes the signs of Allah except the disbelievers” draws a sharp boundary. A person who genuinely seeks truth will not engage in stubborn argumentation; rather, they will reflect and be guided. Disputation here refers to quarrelling with the aim of undermining the message, not honest inquiry. The verse identifies such behaviour as a hallmark of disbelief, because the signs of Allah, whether in scripture or in creation, are clear enough for a sincere heart. Then comes a direct instruction to the Prophet and, by extension, to the believers: “so do not be deceived by their prosperity throughout the land.” The disbelievers’ wealth, trade, comfortable homes, and political influence could easily give the impression that they are favoured or that their way of life is sustainable. The believers in Mecca were often poor, persecuted, and marginalised, and a superficial look might suggest that the disbelievers were on the right path. Allah cautions against this deception. Prosperity is not proof of truth, nor is hardship a sign of falsehood.

The verse reminds us that worldly success can be a test and a respite, allowing people to increase in sin before a sudden seizure. This teaching protected the early Muslim community from envy and discouragement, anchoring their confidence in divine standards rather than material appearances. It also serves as a timeless warning: a society that disputes divine guidance may appear powerful, but its prosperity is temporary and no indicator of ultimate acceptance by Allah.

=5=

To strengthen the warning, the surah reminds the Prophet and his opponents of earlier nations. The people of Noah are mentioned first, for they were the first community to fall into persistent denial after the great flood, and their story was well known even among the Arabs. After them, “enemy forces” refers to various confederations of disbelievers who banded together to oppose their messengers, like the ‘Âd, Thamûd, and the people of Lot.

The verse reveals a consistent pattern: every community plotted against its prophet to seize him, meaning they conspired to kill, imprison, or expel the messenger. They argued in falsehood, hoping to discredit the truth with it. Their aim was to demolish the message by any means, not to find truth.

The surah then uses a powerful shift in person: “So I seized them.” Suddenly Allah speaks in the first person, bringing the terrible consequence into sharp focus. The seizure was sudden, decisive, and inescapable. The rhetorical question “And how horrible was My punishment!” adds weight to the warning, urging the listener to imagine the terror of those who once walked the earth arrogantly and were then wiped out. For the Quraysh, who knew the ruins of these civilisations along their trade routes, the verse was a direct threat that history could repeat itself. It also comforted the Prophet, showing that opposition to messengers was not new and that divine justice, though it may be delayed, is certain. Every believer facing mockery or persecution can draw strength from this, knowing that the plots of falsehood ultimately collapse under the weight of divine decree.

=6=

Having described the destruction of earlier nations, this verse draws the logical conclusion that Allah’s decree concerning the disbelievers is just as inevitable. The phrase “your Lord’s decree has been proven true” indicates that the word of punishment, which was already spoken in the divine knowledge and in the scriptures, has been fulfilled in history and will be fulfilled again in the Hereafter. The decree is not arbitrary; it is the result of their own persistent disbelief and rejection after clear warnings. The verse specifies that they will be the inmates of the Fire, a designation that points both to the eternal abode of the disbelievers in the afterlife and to the moral state of being a companion of the Fire. This serves as a sobering transition from the discussion of worldly punishment to the mention of angelic prayer in the following verses. The early Meccan Muslims would reflect on this and find their hearts a mixture of fear and hope: fear of meeting the same fate should they falter, and hope because they were on the path of belief that leads away from the Fire.

The verse also underscores a fundamental Quranic theme: divine justice is not only about immediate consequences in this world but also about the ultimate recompense. For the Prophet, this was reassurance that while the Quraysh appeared to escape earthly ruin for the time being, a far greater punishment awaited them unless they repented. It ties tightly to the next verses, which contrast the lot of the disbelievers with that of the believers for whom the angels pray.

=7=

The surah now draws back the curtain on the celestial realm, describing the angels who carry the Throne and those around it. These are among the loftiest of created beings, yet their occupation is continuous glorification of their Lord and faith in Him.

The mention of their faith is significant: even these magnificent angels are described as believers, which teaches that faith is the highest honour for any creature. After glorifying and believing, they do something astonishingly merciful: they seek forgiveness for the believers on earth. Their prayer is recorded: “Our Lord! You encompass everything in Your mercy and knowledge.” They begin by acknowledging the vastness of Allah’s attributes, which assures us that no sin is beyond His mercy and that He knows the sincerity of those who repent. Then they ask, “So forgive those who repent and follow Your Way,” linking forgiveness to turning back and adhering to the path revealed by the Prophet. They also pray for protection from the torment of Hellfire. This angelic intercession highlights the dignity of the believers, for whom the highest angels are concerned. It also demonstrates that the angels, despite their purity, do not stand aloof from the struggles of humanity; they actively pray for the faithful. The verse thus instills immense hope: if the bearers of the Throne are praying for our forgiveness, we must strive to be among those who repent and follow the way, confident that mercy encompasses us.

=8=

The prayer of the angels continues, moving from forgiveness and protection to the positive reward. They ask Allah to admit the believers into the Gardens of Eternity, which are already promised to them.

The phrase “which You have promised them” reveals the angels’ complete trust in the divine promise; they are merely asking for its fulfillment. The prayer beautifully extends beyond the individual: “along with the righteous among their parents, spouses, and descendants. ” This teaches that true salvation is not solitary; a believer naturally yearns for the well-being of family members. The angels’ supplication includes them, provided they too are righteous. This encourages believers to work for their own faith and to care for the spiritual state of their families.

The mention of parents, spouses, and descendants covers the closest human relationships, and the promise of reunion in Paradise is one of the Quran’s most moving consolations. The prayer ends by extolling Allah: “You alone are truly the Almighty, All-Wise.” The Almighty can accomplish whatever He wills, so admitting multitudes into eternal gardens is effortless for Him. The All-Wise does nothing without purpose and places each soul in its rightful station. This pairing of power and wisdom assures the believer that their admission into Paradise is not an arbitrary gift but the fruit of divine justice and mercy perfectly combined.

=9=

The angels conclude their prayer with a plea for a comprehensive protection: “And protect them from the consequences of their evil deeds.” Even the believers are not free from sins and missteps, and the angels, knowing the terrors of the Day of Judgment, ask that those sins not be held against them or not lead them into punishment. This does not mean that sin is encouraged; rather, it reflects the recognition that human beings are weak and that Allah’s forgiveness is essential.

The verse then explains the outcome of such protection: whoever is shielded from the evil consequences of their deeds on that Day has indeed been shown Allah’s mercy. The choice of words “will have been shown Your mercy” indicates that this protection is itself a manifestation of mercy, a grace that envelops the servant. Then comes the powerful declaration: “That is truly the ultimate triumph.” True success is not wealth, power, or even a comfortable life, but being saved from the Fire and admitted to Paradise with one’s sins forgiven. The angels, who witness the realities of the unseen, testify that no triumph can compare to this. For the early Muslims enduring persecution, this angelic prayer reassured them that the whole cosmos is on their side so long as they remain faithful.

The verse also inspires every believer to value the Hereafter above all else and to trust that the mercy of Allah can overcome any shortcoming.

=10=

After the hopeful imagery of angelic prayers, the surah returns to the severe fate of the disbelievers. This verse describes a dreadful announcement that will be made to them in the Hereafter. It draws a comparison: during worldly life, when they were invited to believe in Allah, they responded with contempt, mockery, and hatred for the message and the messengers. That contempt is now contrasted with the mutual hatred and blame they will feel for one another on the Day of Judgment when they realise their leaders and deities led them astray. The verse announces that Allah’s contempt for them, meaning His displeasure and rejection, was far worse than their own mutual contempt.

This is a profound psychological and spiritual torment. Their own dislike for each other is nothing compared to the realisation that the Creator Himself has rejected them utterly.

The phrase “as you disbelieved when invited to belief” underlines that the cause of this divine contempt was their own choice; they were given a clear invitation and they refused it arrogantly. This serves as a warning to the Quraysh and to all who hear the call to faith. The contempt of Allah is not a petty human emotion but a just withdrawal of mercy and an expression of His wrath due to their persistent rejection.

The verse reminds us that what we sow in this world in terms of attitude toward the truth, we will reap in the next.

=11=

The scene intensifies as the disbelievers, now in the grip of torment, turn to Allah with a desperate plea. They acknowledge, “You made us lifeless twice, and gave us life twice.” Scholars have explained this in two main ways.

The first interpretation is that the first lifelessness is the state before creation when they were nothing, then Allah gave them life at birth. The second lifelessness is death, and the second life is the resurrection on the Day of Judgment. Others hold that it refers to being lifeless in the loins of their fathers, then given life in the womb, then caused to die after the first stage, then brought to life at birth, and then final death and resurrection. Whichever precise meaning is intended, the point is their recognition of Allah’s complete power over life and death. They follow this with a confession: “Now we confess our sins. ” In the world they denied, but facing the reality of punishment, truth becomes undeniable.

However, this confession comes too late to benefit them. The plea “So is there any way out?” is filled with anguish. They are asking for a return to the world to do good, or for relief from the Fire. This shows the depth of their regret, but the door of return is permanently closed.

The verse teaches us that the time for faith and repentance is the present life; once death comes and the unseen becomes seen, belief no longer avails. It is a powerful call to urgency in turning to Allah before the opportunity ends.

=12=

The answer to their desperate plea is a firm refusal, and the reason is laid bare. They are told that their punishment is not arbitrary but rooted in their fundamental spiritual corruption: whenever Allah alone was invoked, they staunchly disbelieved, turning away with pride and denial.

However, when others were associated with Him in worship, when idols, saints, or other intermediaries were mentioned, they readily believed. This describes the polytheists of Mecca exactly. They found the worship of one invisible God without images or intermediaries unacceptable, yet they enthusiastically embraced the worship of their numerous idols. The verse exposes the heart of their disbelief: they were not against worship in principle, but they rejected the exclusive worship of Allah. Then the divine verdict is announced: “So Today judgment belongs to Allah alone—the Most High, All-Great.” On that Day, the false gods will vanish, and Allah’s sole authority will be undeniable. The names “Most High” and “All-Great” remind them that He is exalted above all partners and that His greatness dwarfs everything they had associated with Him.

This is a lesson for all times: associating partners with Allah while claiming to believe is a contradiction. True faith requires that Allah alone be worshipped and that His commands be held supreme.

=13=

From the scene of the Hereafter, the surah returns to the present world, drawing attention to Allah’s continuous signs. He is the One who shows you His signs. These signs include the verses of the Quran, the wonders of creation, and the events of history. They are everywhere, displayed to every human being, yet not everyone benefits from them. Then a specific sign is mentioned: He sends down rain as a provision from the sky. Rain is a profound sign of mercy and power. It revives dead land, provides drinking water, and enables agriculture. Without it, life would cease. The verse calls it “a provision,” linking the physical sustenance directly to the Provider. Despite this unmistakable blessing, the verse ends with a sobering note: “But none will be mindful except those who turn to Him.” Mindfulness, or remembrance, is not automatic. It requires a heart that is oriented toward Allah, one that turns away from heedlessness and towards its Lord.

The mention of rain is particularly powerful for the Meccan audience, whose life depended on the scarce rainfall. They acknowledged that Allah sent rain, yet they still associated partners with Him. This verse challenges them to allow this simple observation to lead them to full mindfulness and devotion. The lesson is that the natural world is replete with reminders, but only those who regularly turn to Allah in repentance and reflection truly absorb them.

=14=

Having highlighted the signs and the failure of many to be mindful, the verse issues a direct command: “So call upon Allah with sincere devotion.” The phrase “sincere devotion” translates ikhlas, which means purifying one’s worship for Allah alone, without any partner, hidden agenda, or desire for worldly recognition.

This is the essence of faith. The command is not just to pray, but to make one’s entire religion exclusively for Allah.

The verse adds a challenging phrase: “even to the dismay of the disbelievers.” The Quraysh polytheists were deeply offended by the open proclamation of Allah’s oneness and the abandonment of their idols. Their dismay, however, is not a reason to compromise or hide one’s faith. The early Muslims were often pressured to soften their stance or to acknowledge the idols in some way to please their opponents. This verse gave them the courage to worship openly and purely, regardless of the anger it provoked. The principle remains timeless: a believer’s devotion to Allah should not be watered down by fear of displeasing people, whether they are family, society, or those in power. The dismay of disbelievers is a natural reaction when falsehood is challenged, but the believer’s duty is to remain steadfast, trusting that Allah’s pleasure is the ultimate goal. The verse sets the stage for the next, which describes the majesty of the One being worshipped.

=15=

The description of Allah continues, affirming His incomparable majesty. “Highly Exalted in rank” means that His status is beyond any comparison or elevation. No one shares His rank or can approach His grandeur. He is “Lord of the Throne,” which is the mightiest of created things, encompassing the heavens and the earth. His lordship over the Throne symbolises His absolute authority over the entire cosmos. Then the verse moves to revelation: He sends down the revelation by His command to whoever He wills of His servants. This establishes that prophethood is a divine choice, not a human achievement. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, was chosen by Allah’s will, and the Quran is a descent from Him. The purpose of this revelation is “to warn all of the Day of Meeting.” The Day of Meeting is when all of creation, the earlier and the later generations, will meet their Lord and meet one another to witness judgment. The warning function of the Quran is central to this surah, and the Prophet is being reminded that his task is to deliver this warning, no matter the reaction.

The phrase “of His servants” also shows that prophets are honoured servants, not divine beings, refuting any tendency to deify them. For the believer, this verse inspires awe of Allah’s exaltedness and gratitude that He sends guidance to prepare us for that immense gathering.

=16=

This verse expands on the Day of Meeting. On that Day, all will appear before Allah, meaning every human being, jinn, and even the angels will be brought forth, exposed, with no possibility of concealment.

The phrase “nothing about them will be hidden from Him” underscores the completeness of divine knowledge. Every thought, secret, and deed will be laid bare. Then a majestic question will be posed: “Who does all authority belong to this Day?

” The answer follows immediately: “To Allah—the One, the Supreme!” In the world, many arrogate authority to themselves: kings, rulers, the wealthy, and even false gods are given allegiance. But on that Day, all false claims will collapse, and the reality of Allah’s exclusive sovereignty will be universally recognised. The description “the One, the Supreme” highlights that He is uniquely singular, with no partner, and He is irresistibly dominant over all. This verse serves as a powerful rebuke to the Quraysh who refused to submit to Allah’s authority while granting it to their idols and tribal leaders. It also comforts the oppressed believers by promising a day when true justice will prevail. Every believer can live with this perspective, knowing that the temporary powers of this world are nothing compared to the absolute sovereignty that will be manifest on the Day of Judgment.

=17=

The surah continues the description of the Day of Judgment, now focusing on the principle of recompense. “Today every soul will be rewarded for what it has done.” This emphasises individual accountability. Every action, whether good or evil, large or small, open or secret, will meet a just reward. There is no collective punishment; each soul stands alone with its record. Then comes the emphatic declaration: “No injustice Today!” This means that no one will be wronged by having their good deeds decreased, their evil deeds increased, or being punished for something they did not do. The divine court is perfectly fair. To further reassure, the verse ends with “Surely Allah is swift in reckoning.” Swiftness here does not imply haste that compromises accuracy; rather, it means that Allah’s knowledge and power are such that the judgment of all humanity will be completed with ease and speed, without any delay or confusion. For the Meccan believers who were often cheated and oppressed, the promise of absolute justice brought immense hope. For the oppressors, it was a terrifying warning.

The verse also reminds every believer to focus on their own deeds and to trust that no act of righteousness will be forgotten. The balance between fear and hope is maintained: fear of the record of our bad deeds, and hope in the swift and just reward for our good ones.

=18=

The command to warn the people intensifies with a vivid description of the Day’s horrors. The Prophet is told, “Warn them of the approaching Day.

” The word “approaching” indicates the certainty and nearness of the Hour from the perspective of eternity, making it feel imminent. The specific image is terrifying: “when the hearts will jump into the throats. ” This is a graphic metaphor for extreme fear and anxiety. In moments of intense terror, it feels as if the heart has risen to the throat, choking the person. They will be “suppressing distress,” meaning they will try to hold back their grief and panic, but it will be overwhelming.

The verse then states that the wrongdoers will have neither a close friend nor intercessor to be heard. In worldly life, a person in trouble can rely on a loyal friend or someone to speak on their behalf. On that Day, all such relationships will break, and no intercession will benefit unless Allah permits it for the believers. The verse specifically targets the wrongdoers, those who persisted in disbelief and oppression, cutting off their false hopes. This powerful warning was meant to shake the Quraysh out of their complacency and remains a stark reminder for all to prepare for a day when human support vanishes entirely.

=19=

After describing the helplessness of wrongdoers, the verse highlights the completeness of Allah’s knowledge, which makes escape from judgment impossible. Allah knows the sly glances of the eyes—the furtive, treacherous looks that people exchange without others noticing.

The phrase “sly glances” refers to those secretive signals of mockery, conspiracy, or lust that are hidden from most human observers. Beyond the eyes, He knows “whatever the hearts conceal,” the innermost thoughts, intentions, doubts, and hidden feelings that never reach the tongue or the limbs. This verse was particularly relevant to the Meccan disbelievers who would secretly plot against the Prophet and exchange mocking glances during his recitation. They thought their hidden schemes were unknown, but Allah was fully aware. This knowledge is not limited to the wrongdoers; it encompasses every human being. The believer finds both comfort and caution in this. Comfort, because Allah knows the sincerity and struggle of the heart even when others misunderstand. Caution, because no secret sin or hidden arrogance escapes His sight. The verse thus calls for the purification of the inner self, for it is the heart that will be judged. It also connects to the theme of the Day of Meeting: nothing will be hidden, so one must live with constant awareness of Allah’s all-encompassing knowledge.

=20=

The final verse of this batch contrasts divine judgment with the impotence of false deities. Allah judges with the truth, meaning His judgment is always based on absolute fairness, perfect knowledge, and unerring wisdom. There is no error, bias, or inconsistency in His decisions.

The phrase “with the truth” also means that His judgment upholds and manifests the truth, vindicating the believers and condemning the disbelievers. In stark contrast, those idols they invoke besides Him cannot judge at all. Idols are lifeless objects, and even the so-called gods, saints, or angels that people call upon have no authority to judge or decide anything on the Day of Judgment. They do not even possess the faculty of judgment.

The verse then concludes with two divine names: “Indeed, Allah alone is the All-Hearing, All-Seeing. ” He hears every plea, every cry, every statement, and sees every action and circumstance. Because He is All-Hearing and All-Seeing, His judgment is perfectly informed. The Meccan polytheists, who called on their idols for help and believed they could intercede, are reminded that their deities lack the very attributes necessary for judgment.

This verse also strengthens the believer’s trust: one can rely completely on Allah’s judgment, knowing that He hears the oppressed and sees the oppressor, and He will deliver flawless justice.
