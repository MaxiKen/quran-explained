=61=

After the powerful invitation to call upon Allah and the warning against arrogance, the surah turns to the signs surrounding human beings every single day. The verse states that it is Allah Who has made the night for you to rest in and the day bright. The night is described with a specific purpose: rest. The darkness, the quiet, the natural slowing of the world’s activities, all of it is a mercy designed for sleep and recovery. The day is made bright, allowing people to see, to work, to seek provision, and to carry out their lives. The alternation of night and day is so familiar that people rarely stop to see it as a deliberate act of a Compassionate Creator. If daylight were permanent, rest would be impossible; if night were endless, life would be unbearable. The perfect balance speaks of a Lord who knows the needs of His creation intimately.

The verse then declares, “Surely Allah is ever Bountiful to humanity,” using the word fadl, which encompasses all kinds of unearned favour. The night and day are just two examples of a generosity that fills every moment of existence.

Yet the verse ends with a tragic note: “but most people are ungrateful.” Instead of recognising the Giver and devoting themselves to Him, they take the blessings for granted, sometimes even attributing them to nature or chance. This ingratitude is the root of disbelief, and the verse invites the listener to break that cycle by consciously receiving each sunrise and sunset as a gift.

=62=

The surah now draws a conclusion from the signs just mentioned. “That is Allah, your Lord.” The One who arranged the night and the day is not a distant, uninterested force; He is your Lord, nurturing, sustaining, and guiding.

The verse then amplifies the description: “the Creator of all things. ” Everything that exists, without exception, owes its origin to Him. This universal creation is the strongest proof of His sole right to worship.

Therefore, “There is no god worthy of worship except Him.” This is the natural outcome of acknowledging Him as Creator. If He alone creates, He alone should be worshipped. Then comes a penetrating question: “How can you then be deluded from the truth?

” The word “deluded” indicates that turning away from this clear truth is not a result of sound reasoning but of deception. The deceiver could be one’s own desires, the whispers of Satan, or the blind following of societal norms.

The question is rhetorical and jolting: after witnessing all the signs, after knowing that Allah is the Creator and Lord, what excuse remains for worshipping false gods or living in disobedience? For the Quraysh, who acknowledged Allah as the Creator of the heavens and the earth yet worshipped idols alongside Him, this was a devastating argument. The verse calls every human being to examine what exactly has turned them away from the most obvious truth, and to realise that it is a delusion that must be shaken off.

=63=

Lest anyone think that the Quraysh were unique in their self-deception, the verse immediately places them in a historical continuum. “This is how those who used to reject Allah’s signs were also deluded.” The pattern is ancient and consistent. Nations before them received clear signs, yet they turned away, deceived by their wealth, power, or intellectual arrogance. The verse connects the present to the past, normalising the Prophet’s experience while also issuing a warning: the outcome of previous deluded nations was destruction.

The phrase “Allah’s signs” encompasses both the revealed verses and the signs in the created world. Their rejection was not based on a lack of evidence but on a spiritual ailment that made the truth appear ugly and falsehood beautiful. This verse consoles the Prophet by showing that his opponents are not acting out of a rational position but out of a spiritual sickness that has afflicted many before. It also serves as a mirror for every generation: when people turn away from clear guidance, they are not being clever or enlightened; they are repeating the same delusion that led earlier civilisations to their ruin. The antidote is to break the cycle through sincere reflection and by seeking Allah’s protection from being deluded.

=64=

The surah continues listing blessings, moving from night and day to larger cosmic favours and personal creation. Allah made the earth a place of settlement, stable and spread out, not shaking constantly, with soil for planting and surfaces for building. The sky is made a canopy, a protective ceiling that holds back harmful rays, provides an atmosphere for breathing, and presents a breathtaking view. Then the attention turns inward: “He shaped you in the womb, perfecting your form. ” The stages of embryonic development are a profound miracle; every human being is sculpted into a balanced, beautiful form, with limbs, senses, and faculties precisely fitted for life. After creating humans, He has provided them with what is good and lawful from foods, drinks, and resources. The provision is not only abundant but also good and lawful, emphasising that Allah intends purity and benefit for His servants.

The verse then reiterates, “That is Allah—your Lord,” reinforcing the personal relationship. It concludes with a majestic exclamation: “So Blessed is Allah, Lord of all worlds.

” The word “Blessed” indicates that Allah is the source of all goodness, perpetually worthy of praise. The progression from earth and sky to personal creation and provision invites every human to look at both the universe and their own body to find overwhelming evidence of a Benevolent Creator, rendering disbelief a profound ingratitude.

=65=

The description of Allah reaches its summit with a foundational attribute: “He is the Ever-Living.” Unlike everything else that experiences life, death, or dependence, Allah possesses life in its absolute, eternal, and self-sustaining sense. He never dies, never sleeps, never weakens. This attribute is the basis of all divine actions; only the Ever-Living can be the Creator, the Provider, and the Responder. From this, the logical conclusion follows: “There is no god worthy of worship except Him.” If He alone is the Ever-Living, then all worship must be directed to Him alone.

The verse then returns to the command to supplicate: “So call upon Him with sincere devotion.” The sincere devotion means purifying one’s worship from any hidden polytheism or worldly motives. The call is to be accompanied by the words, “All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds.” This connects supplication with gratitude. The one who calls upon Allah should already be filled with praise, recognising His lordship over everything. The verse is a comprehensive summary of faith: believe in the Ever-Living God, worship Him alone, call on Him with a pure heart, and fill your tongue with His praise. For the early Muslims, this was both a theology and a spiritual practice that fortified them against the surrounding idolatry.

The verse also refutes those who worshipped beings that were dead or dependent, reminding them that only the Ever-Living can hear and respond.

=66=

The surah now instructs the Prophet to make a clear, public declaration of his mission. “Say, O Prophet, ‘I have been forbidden to worship those idols you worship besides Allah.’” The use of “forbidden” indicates that this is a divine prohibition, not a personal preference. The Prophet is not seeking a compromise; the line is drawn decisively. The reason is given: “since clear proofs have come to me from my Lord. ” The revelation he has received leaves no room for ambiguity; the truth has been established so convincingly that any association of partners with Allah would be a betrayal of that knowledge. Then he declares, “And I have been commanded to fully submit to the Lord of all worlds.” Submission is the core of Islam. The Prophet’s entire being is oriented towards obeying the Creator of everything. By adding “Lord of all worlds,” the verse emphasises that Allah’s lordship is universal, covering not just humans but the angels, jinn, animals, and all creation. This declaration served both as a statement of the Prophet’s unwavering position and as a model for every believer. It teaches that once faith is founded on clear proofs, one must openly disavow false worship and commit to complete submission, regardless of societal pressure.

The verse also draws a sharp contrast between the confusion of polytheism and the clarity of monotheism, inviting the listener to choose the path that is based on evidence and reason.

=67=

After the declaration of monotheism, the surah returns to the signs within human life itself, narrating the journey of human creation. “He is the One Who created you from dust.” This refers to the origin of the human race, Adam created from dust, and by extension every human being is composed of the elements of the earth. Then the verse moves to the individual origin: “then from a sperm-drop,” a humble, fluid beginning that carries the genetic blueprint. This develops into “a clinging clot of blood,” the embryo that attaches to the uterine wall. These stages, described in a 7th-century scripture, resonate with modern embryology and point to the impossibility of the Prophet knowing these details without divine revelation. Then “He brings you forth as infants,” taking the person from the hidden world of the womb into the visible world, weak and dependent. The life stages continue: reaching prime, full of strength and intellect, and then old age, with its characteristic decline.

The verse adds that some die sooner, before reaching old age, but all proceed toward “an appointed time,” the fixed term decreed by Allah. The purpose of this detailed description is stated: “so perhaps you may understand Allah’s power.” The entire life cycle, from dust to death, is a display of power and wisdom. The verse calls on people to reflect on their own biography as evidence of the Creator who can just as easily resurrect them for judgment.

=68=

Following the description of human life stages, the surah summarises the ultimate divine prerogative. Allah gives life and causes death. These two realities frame human existence; every living being owes its life to Him and will return to Him through death. No one else possesses this power.

The verse then reveals the ease with which Allah creates and commands: “When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, ‘Be!’ And it is!” The command “Be” is not a spoken word in a human language but a representation of His absolute will. There is no delay, no process, no need for materials or assistance. The instant He wills something, it comes into existence.

This is the answer to the Quraysh who found the resurrection incredible. If Allah can give life and cause death at will, resurrection is simply another command.

The phrase also applies to all divine decrees: the creation of the universe, the sending of revelation, the help given to messengers, and the punishment of the disbelievers are all brought about by His effortless command. This verse fills the believer’s heart with awe and trust; the One who commands “Be” is not constrained by any obstacle. It also comforts the Prophet by assuring him that the victory promised by Allah is just a single divine command away, needing no army or political power to be realised.

=69=

The surah shifts back to the fate of the stubborn disbelievers, connecting to earlier warnings. The verse asks, “Have you not seen how those who dispute Allah’s signs are turned away?” The address is to the Prophet, but it extends to all believers.

The question invites reflection on a visible phenomenon: those who argue against the revealed signs without sound evidence are being diverted from the truth, often without realising it. Their disputation is not only a cause of misguidance but also a symptom of it.

The phrase “turned away” indicates that they are being prevented from reaching faith, sometimes gradually, as a divine consequence of their arrogance. This verse echoes the earlier descriptions of sealed hearts and beautified evil deeds. The believers are to observe this pattern without being disheartened; it is part of Allah’s just way.

The verse also implicitly warns the listener not to become one of those who dispute the signs, because the moment one starts arguing against truth with no intention of being guided, they risk being turned away permanently. The question form is powerful, engaging the mind to recognise a spiritual law that plays out in every era. The Quraysh leaders, who debated the Prophet endlessly, are a prime example of this turning away. The verse thus reassures the Prophet that their rejection is not his failure but a divine pattern.

=70=

The verse identifies those who dispute Allah’s signs as “the ones who reject this Book and all scriptures We sent Our messengers with.” Their disbelief is comprehensive; they do not simply reject the Quran but the entire chain of divine revelation. By denying the final scripture, they in effect deny all previous ones because they reject the prophet who confirms them.

The phrase “this Book” refers to the Quran, which is present before them, challenging them. Their rejection is not based on any deficiency in the Book but on their own unwillingness to submit. Then comes a threat: “So they will know the consequences.

” This is a short, ominous sentence. The knowledge of the consequences will come to them in a way that leaves no room for doubt, either in this world through defeat and punishment, or in the Hereafter when they face the Fire.

The verse reminds the believers that those who reject the Book may appear to be free and unpunished for a while, but a time will come when they will know the truth of what they denied. For the early Muslims, this was a promise that the mockery and opposition they endured would eventually turn into regret for the mockers. It serves as a universal warning: rejecting God’s word is not a trivial act; it carries consequences that will be known with devastating certainty.

=71=

The surah now provides a graphic preview of those consequences. The scene shifts to the punishment of the disbelievers. “When shackles will be around their necks and chains on their legs.” The imagery is of complete restraint and humiliation. Shackles on the neck indicate submission and disgrace, while chains on the legs prevent any movement or escape.

This is a physical manifestation of their spiritual state in the world, where they were shackled by their arrogance and the chains of sin that they refused to break through repentance. The verse then says, “They will be dragged.” This dragging is not a gentle leading but a violent, forceful pulling, likely by the angels of punishment, through terrain and into the depths of Hell. The absence of any mention of dignity highlights their utter helplessness. The mighty leaders of Quraysh, who walked proudly in the markets of Mecca, will be dragged in chains like the lowliest of prisoners. This picture was meant to shake the disbelievers out of their complacency. For the believers, it provided a sense of justice: those who oppressed them and arrogantly rejected the truth would face a punishment befitting their crimes. The verse uses sensory language to make the warning vivid, appealing to the imagination so that the listener can almost feel the cold metal and the violent motion.

=72=

The description of the punishment intensifies. After being shackled and dragged, they will be pulled “through boiling water.” The water is at an extreme, unbearable temperature, scalding their skin and causing unimaginable pain. Then they are “burned in the Fire as fuel. ” This phrase is chilling: they become the very fuel that sustains the Fire. In this world, they lived as if they were the centre of existence, consuming resources for their pleasures.

In the Hereafter, they become the consumed, feeding the flames with their own bodies. The punishment is multi-layered: the boiling water, the dragging, the chains, and the burning Fire all combine to create a torment that matches their multiple crimes. The verse implies that just as they used their bodies and faculties to sin and to oppose the messengers, those same bodies will be the material for the punishment. It also recalls the earlier verse where they plotted against the prophets to seize them; now they are the ones seized and dragged. The Quranic descriptions of Hell always carry an ethical message: the punishment fits the crime, and no one is wronged. The terrifying sequence serves as a powerful deterrent, intended to stir the conscience before it is too late. The listener is urged to imagine these horrors not as myth but as impending reality for those who persist in denial.

=73=

The torment is not only physical but also psychological and moral. After being dragged and thrown into the Fire, they will be asked a humiliating question: “Where are those idols you used to associate with Allah?

” The question is a rebuke, exposing the futility of their lifelong devotion. In the world, they proudly called upon their idols, sacrificed for them, and fought to defend them. Now, when they are in desperate need, the idols have vanished.

The question is posed by the angels or by a divine command, and it forces the disbelievers to confront their own folly. It is not a question seeking information; it is a taunt that adds to their regret.

The word “associate” points to the fundamental sin of shirk, the association of partners with Allah. The verse reminds the listener that every false object of worship will be stripped away on that Day, leaving the polytheist utterly alone. The unfinished sentence, which continues in the next verse, creates a dramatic pause, as if the disbelievers themselves are looking around in vain, hoping to find their gods, but seeing nothing. For the believers, this is a warning to purify their worship completely, so that they never face this humiliating question. It also highlights the absurdity of idolatry, which is built on something that will ultimately desert its followers.

=74=

The question is completed, and the disbelievers answer with a mixture of despair and confession. They cry out, “They have all failed us.” The idols they trusted, the false gods they called upon, have abandoned them completely. Not a single one comes to their aid. Then they make a deeper admission: “In fact, we did not invoke anything real before.

” This is a profound realisation, though too late. They acknowledge that the beings they worshipped were not real deities; they were figments of imagination, lifeless objects, or beings that had no power. Their entire religious life was built on a vacuum. This confession strips away all their former justifications and leaves them with the naked truth.

The verse concludes, “This is how Allah leaves the disbelievers to stray.” Their straying is a consequence of their own choices. Allah leaves them to stray because they preferred falsehood over truth. This leaving is not an arbitrary act but a just response to their persistent rejection. The verse closes the loop on the theme of misguidance: those who dispute Allah’s signs are left to wander, and their end is utter loss. For the believers, it reinforces the value of divine guidance and the necessity of clinging to it, for without it, one is left with nothing real to hold onto. The verse is both a warning and a call to reflect on the objects of one’s devotion, ensuring they are grounded in truth.

=75=

The divine address continues, now explaining the reason for the punishment. They will be told that this punishment is a direct result of two related sins: being prideful on earth unjustly and acting arrogantly. Pride in the Quran is not mere self-esteem; it is a rejection of truth and a looking down on others. The addition of “unjustly” indicates that their pride was not based on any real merit that would justify such behaviour; it was a false sense of superiority rooted in rebellion against Allah. Arrogance is the outward expression of that pride, manifest in behaviour, speech, and treatment of the messengers and the weak. Pharaoh, Hamân, and Korah are prime examples, but the principle applies to all tyrants and rejecters of faith.

The verse shows that Hell is not an arbitrary punishment but the natural consequence of a heart that inflated itself beyond its proper size and refused to humble itself before the Creator. By linking the punishment directly to pride, the Quran teaches that humility is the key to salvation. The proud person cannot enter Paradise, for Paradise is the abode of those who humbled themselves.

The verse also consoles the oppressed believers by validating their dignity: the arrogant who made their lives miserable will be told that their arrogance is the very reason for their torment. This is divine justice made manifest.

=76=

The sentencing concludes with a command that is both terrifying and definitive. “Enter the gates of Hell, to stay there forever.” The gates of Hell are multiple, each designated for different categories of sinners, but the common destination for the disbelievers is an eternal stay.

The word “forever” extinguishes any hope of eventual escape or annihilation; the punishment is perpetual. Then comes the final, piercing remark: “What an evil home for the arrogant!

” The word “home” is used ironically. In the world, the arrogant built luxurious homes and felt secure. Their true home, however, is Hell, a place of torment rather than comfort. The specific mention of the arrogant again ties the punishment to its cause. The verse summarises the entire narrative arc: arrogance leads to the Fire, and humility leads to salvation. For the Prophet and the believers, this was a source of comfort, knowing that the oppressive aristocracy of Mecca would not escape justice. For all who hear this verse, it serves as a stark warning to guard against the slightest arrogance in the heart, for its final abode is the most dreadful of all.

=77=

Following the terrifying descriptions of the disbelievers’ fate, the surah turns once again to comfort the Prophet. The command “So be patient” echoes verse 55, reinforcing that the way of the messenger is one of steadfast endurance. The reason for patience is “Surely Allah’s promise is true.” That promise includes both the punishment of the wicked and the victory of the believers.

The verse then addresses a possible concern: the Prophet might not live to see the full punishment descend upon the Quraysh. Allah says, “Whether We show you some of what We threaten them with, or cause you to die before that, to Us they will all be returned.” In either case, the outcome is certain. If he sees the punishment in this life, it will be a vindication. If he dies before that, they will still face their reckoning before Allah, and the Prophet will witness their punishment in the Hereafter. This verse liberated the Prophet from feeling that he must see immediate worldly results. His mission was to deliver the message; the timing of justice was in Allah’s hands. For every caller to truth, this is an essential teaching: do not measure success by visible worldly outcomes, but trust that the promise of Allah is infallible, and all accounts will be settled eventually.

=78=

To further reassure the Prophet, the surah places his experience within the broader history of prophethood. Allah has sent many messengers before him, some whose stories have been related in the Quran, and others whose tales remain untold. This reminds the Prophet that he is not alone; he belongs to a long, honourable line.

The verse then clarifies a crucial point: “It was not for any messenger to bring a sign without Allah’s permission. ” The Quraysh demanded specific miracles, but the messengers did not perform signs at the whims of their people. Miracles occur solely by Allah’s will and wisdom. If Allah chooses to delay a visible sign, it is not a failure on the Prophet’s part. Then the verse points to the ultimate moment of judgment: “But when Allah’s decree comes, judgment will be passed with fairness. ” The decree may refer to the final punishment or the Day of Judgment. The judgment is with perfect fairness, meaning everyone will receive what they truly deserve. The result is that “the people of falsehood will then be in total loss.” Their loss is comprehensive: they lose their false gods, their worldly gains, and any hope of salvation. This verse is a powerful anchor for the believer: do not be impatient for visible victory, for the divine schedule is perfect, and the ultimate outcome is never in doubt.

=79=

After the strong theological and eschatological passages, the surah returns to the tangible blessings of daily life, grounding faith in the familiar. “It is Allah Who made cattle for you.” Cattle refers to camels, cows, sheep, and goats, animals central to Arabian life. The verse mentions two primary benefits: riding and eating. Camels, in particular, were the ships of the desert, enabling travel across vast distances. Cattle also provided meat, a staple food. The phrasing “made for you” indicates purposeful creation. These animals were not products of human engineering but gifts from a Creator who designed them with specific utilities for human beings. Their docile nature, their ability to be domesticated, their nutritious flesh, all point to a Provider who anticipates human needs. The verse calls for gratitude and recognition of the Creator’s wisdom. For the desert Arab, a camel was a lifeline, and the Quran uses this familiarity to argue that the One who provided the camel also provides guidance. If they are grateful for cattle, they should be infinitely more grateful for revelation.

The verse also subtly refutes those who considered certain cattle sacred or forbidden without divine sanction, reminding them that Allah created cattle for their benefit, and it is His law, not human superstition, that determines their use. It is a call to see the ordinary as extraordinary signs of divine care.

=80=

The surah expands the list of blessings related to cattle. Besides riding and eating, there are “other benefits” in them. These include their milk, their wool for clothing and tents, their skins for containers and shelter, and their use as beasts of burden carrying heavy loads.

The phrase “other benefits” is deliberately open-ended, encompassing all the uses that humans have discovered and will discover. The verse then highlights transportation: “by means of them you may reach destinations you desire.” Travel in the pre-modern world was dependent on animals, and reaching a desired destination safely was a significant blessing. Then the verse makes a subtle shift: “And you are carried upon some of them and upon ships.” By mentioning ships alongside cattle, it broadens the horizon. Ships, though not directly created from cattle, are also made possible by Allah’s creation of the seas, the wind, and the materials for building vessels.

The mention of ships would resonate with the Quraysh trading caravans that also knew sea travel. The verse thus connects the blessings of land and sea, all under Allah’s provision.

The sequence teaches that human ingenuity in using animals and building ships is itself a gift from Allah, who created the raw materials and gave the intelligence. The overarching message is one of comprehensive divine favour, inviting the listener to ask: if Allah has provided all this, what response other than worship and gratitude is appropriate?
