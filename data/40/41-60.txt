=41=

The believing man continues his speech, now turning the tables on his people with a poignant and bewildered question. He asks, “How is it that I invite you to salvation, while you invite me to the Fire?

” The word “salvation” here translates najāh, which means deliverance from destruction and entry into eternal bliss. His invitation is to faith, to the worship of Allah alone, and to moral uprightness—a path that leads directly to Paradise. Their invitation, on the other hand, is for him to abandon his faith, to return to the worship of Pharaoh and the idols, which leads to the Fire. The contrast is stark and logical: he is calling them to safety, yet they call him to doom.

The question “How is it?” expresses astonishment at their irrationality. Why would anyone choose destruction over salvation? This rhetorical question is meant to make them reflect on the utter absurdity of their position. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, these words were a mirror of his own situation in Mecca, where he called his people to the worship of the One God and they invited him back to their ancestral idolatry. The verse demonstrates that the true believer sees clearly the ultimate outcomes, while the disbelievers are blinded by their immediate desires. It also teaches us that invitations are not equal; a call to salvation is a blessing, whereas a call to misguidance is an assault. The believer must remain firm, even when outnumbered, because the destination of each path is worlds apart.

=42=

The man now clarifies the content of the two invitations. Their call to him is to disbelieve in Allah, which is the very denial of the Creator, and to associate with Him what they have no knowledge of.

The phrase “what I have no knowledge of” is a powerful admission: idolatry is based on ignorance, conjecture, and inherited custom, not on any revealed proof or rational evidence. They cannot produce any scripture or clear sign that justifies worshiping Pharaoh, the sun, or stone idols.

In contrast, his invitation is to the Almighty, the Most Forgiving. He describes Allah with these two specific names for good reason. “Almighty” addresses their love of power; if they want real might, they should turn to the One who is truly irresistible, not Pharaoh who is merely a man. “Most Forgiving” opens the door of hope: no matter how many sins they have committed, Allah is ready to forgive. So his call is not just to a dry doctrine but to a Lord who combines overwhelming power with boundless mercy. This pairing of fear and hope is the hallmark of prophetic preaching.

The verse reveals the hollowness of idolatry: it is based on knowledge-less claims, while faith is rooted in knowledge of Allah’s attributes. For every believer, this serves as a reminder that inviting others to Islam must be grounded in knowledge and wisdom, highlighting both the majesty and the mercy of Allah, never reducing the message to mere argument.

=43=

The man now delivers a categorical verdict on their false gods. He states with absolute certainty that whatever idols they invite him to worship “are not worthy to be invoked either in this world or the Hereafter.” This means their idols have no power of benefit or harm in the present life, and they certainly cannot intercede or help in the next life.

The phrase “no doubt” underscores that this is not a matter of personal opinion but a clear truth. He links this to the certainty of the return to Allah: “our return is to Allah.” All human beings, including Pharaoh and his followers, will eventually stand before their Creator. The idols will vanish, and the reality of monotheism will become undeniable. He then concludes that “the transgressors will be the inmates of the Fire. ” The term “transgressors” identifies those who overstep the boundaries set by Allah, especially by associating partners with Him.

This is a sobering end to his sermon, leaving his audience with a clear choice. The verse reinforces a core Quranic theme: false gods lack any ontological reality that could render them worthy of worship; they are not even capable of hearing supplications, let alone answering them.

For the believer, it solidifies the exclusive devotion that must be directed to Allah alone, in times of ease and hardship alike.

=44=

The believing man closes his speech with a prophetic warning and an act of complete reliance. He says, “You will remember what I say to you.

” This is not a threat from his own ego but a statement of fact: the day will come when they will recall his words and recognise their truth, but by then it will be too late. That day could be the moment of their drowning, or the Day of Judgment. His certainty is rooted in faith. Then he declares, “I entrust my affairs to Allah.” Having delivered the message, he now puts his full trust in his Lord, regardless of how they react.

This is tawakkul, the Islamic concept of trusting in Allah after taking necessary means. He had spoken the truth courageously, and now he hands the outcome over to the One who controls all. He justifies this trust by affirming that “Allah is All-Seeing of all His servants.” Allah knows his sincerity, their obstinacy, the conspiracies they may hatch, and the final just recompense. This verse is a model for anyone who has done their duty in calling to truth: after conveying the message with wisdom, one should not despair over the rejection of others but place their trust in Allah, who sees all. It also echoes the Prophet’s own experience, teaching him that his task is delivery, and the results belong to Allah.

=45=

The narrative now reveals the divine response to the believing man’s stand and the tyrant’s schemes. “So Allah protected him from the evil of their schemes.” Scholars say this refers to the believing man escaping the murderous plans of Pharaoh. Some traditions identify him as a believer from the family of Pharaoh who hid his faith and ultimately was saved, possibly by leaving with Moses or by martyrdom that nonetheless secured his place in Paradise, but the Quranic focus is on the divine protection. The protection was comprehensive, shielding him from all harm they intended.

In contrast, “Pharaoh’s people were overwhelmed by an evil punishment.” The word “overwhelmed” indicates that the punishment enveloped them completely. While the believer was saved, the tyrant and his followers were subjected to a terrible chastisement. This juxtaposition illustrates the constant Quranic principle that the final outcome favours the righteous, even if they are a lone voice. For the Prophet and the early Muslims, this was a direct reassurance that the Quraysh’s plots would not succeed, and that those who sincerely believed would be guarded. It is a call to rely on Allah and to understand that temporary worldly power is no indicator of divine favour. The punishment mentioned here unfolds in the next verse, linking the worldly drowning to the ongoing punishment in the realm of the unseen.

=46=

This verse unveils an aspect of the unseen: Pharaoh’s people, after their drowning, are exposed to the Fire in their graves morning and evening. This is a reference to the punishment of the barzakh, the intermediary state between death and resurrection.

The phrase “morning and evening” indicates a continuous, recurring torment, a preview of the Hellfire that awaits them. The grave becomes a place where their souls are shown their ultimate destination, causing unending dread. Then, on the Day the Hour is established, a command will be given: “Admit Pharaoh’s people into the harshest punishment of Hell.” This shows an escalation: the grave punishment is only a prelude, and the full, unrelenting torment of the Hereafter is far worse. The verse serves as a powerful deterrent, making the reality of punishment tangible. It also confirms the Prophet’s teaching about the punishment of the grave, which is part of Islamic creed. For the Quraysh, it was a warning that death is not the end, but a transition to a conscious state of suffering or bliss. The connection to the previous verse is clear: the evil punishment that overwhelmed them in this world was just the beginning, and their true catastrophe continues and will intensify. It also explains why the believing man was in such earnest fear for his people: he knew what awaited them beyond this life.

=47=

The surah now moves to describe a future scene of mutual blame in Hell. The verse says, “Consider the Day when they will dispute in the Fire.” This invitation to consider is meant to make the listener vividly imagine the scene. The lowly followers, meaning the masses who blindly obeyed their elite, will turn to the arrogant leaders—people like Pharaoh, Hamân, and the chieftains—and plead desperately. They will say, “We were your dedicated followers, will you then shield us from a portion of the Fire?” They acknowledge their subservience, hoping that the leaders who commanded them can now protect them.

However, this plea is futile. The leaders are themselves helpless. The verse exposes the false bonds of loyalty that exist in this world. Those who follow tyrants and false guides assume they will have support, but on that Day every alliance of misguidance will shatter. This scene directly links back to the earlier verse about Allah’s contempt for the disbelievers being worse than their contempt for one another. It is also a warning to the Quraysh who followed their chiefs in rejecting the Prophet. The Quran is teaching that blind following in matters of faith is a recipe for eternal regret. Every individual must use their own intellect and choose guidance, for no leader will bear another’s burden on the Day of Judgment.

=48=

The response of the arrogant leaders is a blunt declaration of their shared doom. They will say, “We are all in it!” There is no superiority left, no hierarchy of privilege. The leaders who enjoyed prestige and power in the world are now equal victims of the Fire with their followers. Their statement also acknowledges the justice of Allah’s judgment: “For Allah has already passed judgment over His servants.” They admit that the divine decree has been justly implemented, even though they deny it no longer. There is a tone of resigned despair. They cannot help, and they know their excuses will not be heard. This verse dismantles the idea that worldly status translates into spiritual advantage. It reinforces the Quranic theme that all human distinctions vanish before divine justice. For the believers who were oppressed, this scene is a source of consolation; the arrogant who lorded over them will face humiliation and will be unable to shield anyone.

For the disbelievers, it is a stark warning to reassess their loyalties before it is too late. The verse also highlights that on the Day of Judgment, there will be no new negotiation; judgment is final, based on the record of deeds and the state of hearts.

=49=

Having failed to get help from their leaders, the inmates now turn to the keepers of Hell, the angels tasked with administering the punishment. They plead, “Pray to your Lord to lighten the torment for us even for one day!” This request is remarkable for several reasons. First, they now recognise the angels as beings with a direct connection to the Lord they once denied. Second, they are not asking for complete removal of the punishment, only for a lightening, and merely for a single day. This shows the unbearable nature of the torment; a moment of relief would be a priceless mercy. The plea also reveals their continued miscomprehension: they still hope that intercession might work outside the limits Allah has set.

In the Hereafter, the system of intercession is entirely under Allah’s control, and no intercession will benefit the disbelievers. The angels, who are absolutely obedient, will not act without Allah’s permission. The verse demonstrates that the punishment is not only physical but also psychological: the constant, unrelenting nature of the pain, with no hope of reprieve, is part of the torment. For the living, this serves as a terrifying reminder that the time to seek mercy is now, through repentance, not when facing the keepers of Hell.

=50=

The response of the keepers is a straightforward, devastating reminder of the justice of their situation. They ask, “Did your messengers not constantly come to you with clear proofs?

” The use of “constantly” indicates that the messengers came repeatedly, with ample, unmistakable evidence. The inmates can only answer truthfully: “Yes they did. ” They cannot claim ignorance or lack of warning. Then the keepers deliver the final blow: “Then pray! Though the prayer of the disbelievers is only in vain.

” This is not an invitation to a successful supplication but an ironic statement. You can call on your Lord, but your prayer now will never be answered because the door of repentance has closed.

The phrase “only in vain” means their supplication is lost, futile, producing no benefit. This completes the circle: they had the chance in the world, they rejected it, and now even their cries for a single day’s relief are denied. The scene shows that divine mercy was offered abundantly in the world through the messengers, but they chose to disbelieve. For the listener, it underscores the urgency of responding to the call of the messengers before death. The Prophet is being told that his warning is a mercy, and those who ignore it will face a similar, irreversible regret.

=51=

After the terrifying depictions of Hell, the surah turns back to words of reassurance for the Prophet and his followers. The verse declares a divine principle: “We certainly help Our messengers and the believers.” This help encompasses both this worldly life and the Hereafter. In the world, this assistance may come in various forms: victory over enemies, steadfastness under persecution, the establishment of the faith, or even a noble death. On the Day the witnesses stand forth, the help will manifest as successful intercession, a good reckoning, and entry into Paradise. The “witnesses” refers to the prophets who will testify about their nations, the angels recording deeds, and even the believers’ own limbs bearing witness. The emphatic “certainly” is a promise from Allah, and Allah never breaks His promise. This verse directly addresses the fears and anxieties of the early Muslims in Mecca who were severely oppressed. It assures them that the apparent triumph of the disbelievers is temporary and that ultimate victory belongs to the believers. It also serves as a guiding principle for all generations: as long as one stands with the truth, divine help is near, even if delayed according to human perception. The verse beautifully connects the two spheres of assistance, reminding believers that even if worldly help seems absent, the Hereafter’s help is guaranteed and far greater.

=52=

The verse continues describing the Day of the witnesses, but now from the perspective of the wrongdoers. On that Day, their excuses will be of no benefit to them. In this world, people offer countless justifications for their disbelief and misdeeds: they were following their forefathers, they were deceived by leaders, they did not understand, or they did not have enough time. All these excuses will be rendered null and void. Their own tongues and limbs will testify against them, and the truth will be laid bare.

The phrase “they will be condemned” means they will be cursed and driven away from Allah’s mercy. The “worst outcome” is the eternal Fire, the most dreadful of all ends. The verse starkly contrasts the help and honour given to the believers with the condemnation and loss suffered by the wrongdoers. This pairing is common in the Quran, highlighting the two ultimate destinies. For the Prophet, this was a confirmation that the Quraysh leaders who mocked him would soon be silenced, and their sophistry would not save them. For the believers, it is a warning to stay away from wrongdoing and to ensure that their own record is clean, because on that Day, no eloquence or clever argument will substitute for righteous faith and deeds.

=53=

The surah now returns to the story of Moses to reinforce the theme of divine help and to provide a concrete example. Allah says, “Indeed, We gave Moses true guidance.” This guidance includes the law, the clear miracles, and the steadfastness to confront Pharaoh. It was a comprehensive, unerring direction from Allah. Then, “We made the Children of Israel inherit the Scripture.” After the destruction of Pharaoh and his host, the Torah was given to Moses and the Children of Israel became its inheritors. They were entrusted with the divine law.

The mention of inheritance indicates that the Scripture was passed down through their generations, a blessing and a test. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a reminder that just as Moses was given guidance and his followers inherited the Book, so too would he receive the Quran and his community would become its bearers.

The verse also serves as a warning that inheriting a scripture is a great responsibility, and the Children of Israel’s subsequent history shows what happens when that trust is betrayed. For the believers, it instills a sense of honour and duty regarding the Quran: they have inherited the final, preserved revelation, and they must live by it. The verse smoothly connects the past narrative to the present, preparing for the direct address to the Prophet in the next verse.

=54=

This short verse describes the nature and purpose of the Scripture given to Moses, and by extension, all revelation. It is “a guide” (hudan), meaning it shows the correct path in belief, worship, ethics, and law. It is also “a reminder” (dhikrā), meaning it awakens the innate human knowledge of Allah, stirs the conscience, and recalls people from heedlessness.

The phrase “to people of reason” (li-ulī al-albāb) specifies that the Quran and earlier scriptures benefit those who use their intellect. The “people of reason” are not merely those with high IQs, but those who employ their minds to reflect on the signs of Allah in scripture and creation, free from the blind following of desires and traditions. The verse emphasises that revelation is not irrational; it appeals to and elevates the intellect.

For the disbelievers of Mecca who rejected the Quran, this was a subtle challenge: if they truly possessed reason, they would recognise the Quran as a guide and reminder, just as the Torah was for those who used their intellect. The verse also encourages believers to approach the Quran as active thinkers, not passive reciters. The guidance and reminder are only truly accessed by those who engage their faculties.

This is the natural disposition of the human being: reason and revelation work in harmony, not opposition.

=55=

The surah now turns to directly comfort and instruct the Prophet. The command “So be patient” is the natural conclusion from the stories and principles discussed. The persecution, the denial, the plots—all of it requires steadfastness. The reason for patience is given: “Allah’s promise is certainly true. ” That promise includes help for the messengers, punishment for the disbelievers, and the ultimate victory of truth. The Prophet is then told to “seek forgiveness for your shortcomings.” This might refer to minor human oversights that even prophets, as human beings, may experience, though they are protected from sin. It is a call to constant humility and turning to Allah.

The command also serves as a model for the believers; if the Prophet is commanded to seek forgiveness, how much more the ordinary Muslims. Finally, he is instructed to “glorify the praises of your Lord morning and evening,” meaning to engage in continuous remembrance, prayer, and praise, especially at the ends of the day. This regular worship is a source of strength and spiritual nourishment. The verse weaves together patience, reliance on divine promise, repentance, and worship as the comprehensive recipe for enduring hardship and succeeding in one’s mission.

For every believer, this is a timeless prescription for navigating life’s challenges.

=56=

The surah returns to the characteristics of the opponents. Those who dispute Allah’s signs without proof are not truth-seekers. Their motive is exposed: “they have nothing in their hearts but greed for dominance.

” The Arabic term kibr, translated here as greed for dominance, signifies arrogance and the lust for power and superiority. Their rejection of the Prophet is not intellectual; it is psychological and political. They fear losing their status, their leadership, their control over the people. The verse assures that “they will never attain” the dominance they seek; ultimately, the message will prevail. Then the Prophet is instructed to “seek refuge in Allah” from such people and their machinations.

This is both a spiritual shield and a declaration of dependence on Allah. The verse ends by affirming that Allah is the All-Hearing, All-Seeing. He hears their false arguments and sees their conspiracies, so He will deal with them appropriately. For the believers, this verse provides a profound insight into the psychology of many who oppose the truth: it is not about evidence but about power. It also teaches that the correct response to the arrogant disputation of others is not to become consumed by it, but to seek refuge in Allah and trust in His hearing and sight, which encompass all things.

=57=

The surah presents a logical argument for the resurrection, a concept the Meccans found incredible. It states that the creation of the heavens and the earth is certainly greater than the re-creation of humankind. The vastness, complexity, and magnificence of the universe are undeniable. If Allah could create such an immense cosmos from nothing, then bringing human beings back to life after death is comparatively easy. The initial creation serves as evidence for the resurrection, a theme found repeatedly in the Quran.

The phrase “but most people do not know” indicates that this simple logical deduction is missed because people are heedless or refuse to think. The verse challenges the disbelievers to reason: how can the One who originated the heavens and the earth be incapable of reassembling bones and giving life to a human being?

For the believer, reflecting on the universe strengthens faith in the Hereafter. It shifts perspective from the narrow, materialistic worldview to a grand, divine order. This also ties back to the earlier mention of Pharaoh’s people and their punishment; the resurrection is the guarantee that all accounts will be settled.

=58=

The surah presents a fundamental moral and spiritual equation. “Those blind to the truth and those who can see are not equal.” This blindness is not physical but spiritual, a heart that cannot perceive guidance. The one with sight is the believer who recognises Allah’s signs and follows them. Then another pairing: “nor are those who believe and do good equal to those who do evil.” The believer is defined not only by faith but also by righteous action; the disbeliever by evil deeds. These two categories cannot be equated, neither in this world nor in the Hereafter. Their conditions, values, and destinies are utterly distinct.

The verse ends with a lament: “Yet you are hardly mindful.” Most people do not deeply reflect on this truth, so they live as if all ways of life are equal. This call to mindfulness is a call to discriminate between truth and falsehood and to make a clear choice. For the Prophet and the believers, it was a reminder not to be swayed by the majority who were blind and evildoers, and to value the distinction that Allah has made. It also teaches that relativism in spiritual matters is a sign of heedlessness; the difference between faith and unbelief is as stark as light and darkness.

=59=

The surah reaffirms the certainty of the Hour, the Day of Judgment. “There is no doubt about it” is an absolute statement leaving no room for scepticism. The signs of its approach, the testimony of all prophets, and the very justice of Allah demand that such a Day come.

Yet, despite this certainty, most people do not believe. This disbelief is not based on rational refutation but on a desire to live without accountability and a failure to grasp the unseen. The verse highlights a recurring human tragedy: the truth is evident, but many choose to reject it. For the early Muslims, this was a comfort; the denial of the Quraysh was not a sign that the message was false, but a fulfilment of what the Quran itself predicted. The verse calls every listener to awaken to the reality that this world is not the end, and that every action is moving toward a final judgment. The certainty of the Hour should shape one’s entire conduct, encouraging righteousness and discouraging heedlessness.

=60=

The surah closes this section with a magnificent divine promise and warning. Allah Himself proclaims, “Call upon Me, I will respond to you.

” This is an open, unlimited invitation to supplication (du‘ā). The verse uses the term “call upon Me” which encompasses both the prayer of need and the worship of the heart. The response is guaranteed; no sincere prayer is ever lost. The response may come in the form requested, or something better, or the aversion of a harm, or a reward stored for the Hereafter. After this beautiful promise, a severe warning follows: “Surely those who are too proud to worship Me will enter Hell, fully humbled.” The arrogance that prevents a person from praying, bowing, and supplicating is the same arrogance that leads to the Fire. There is a profound contrast: the one who calls upon Allah is honoured by His response; the one who is too proud to call will be humiliated in Hell.

The word “fully humbled” indicates that they will be reduced to the lowest state of disgrace, exactly the opposite of their worldly arrogance. For the Prophet, this was a powerful reminder to persist in prayer and not be grieved by the arrogance of the disbelievers.

For every believer, it is a call to make du‘ā a central part of life, never feeling too self-sufficient to ask Allah, and a warning that abandoning worship is a path to ruin. The verse beautifully combines hope and fear, encouraging a life of humble devotion.
