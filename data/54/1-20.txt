=1=

The surah opens with a thunderous declaration that links a cosmic event witnessed by the Quraysh with the ultimate cosmic event that is yet to come. The Hour, the Day of Judgment, has drawn near. Its approach is not a distant speculation but an impending reality, and one of the signs of its nearness is that the moon was split.

The Arabic verb "inshaqqa" means to be split apart, to be cleaved. According to numerous authentic hadiths narrated in both Bukhari and Muslim, this verse refers to a miracle that occurred in Mecca during the lifetime of the Prophet Muhammad. The Quraysh came to the Prophet and demanded a sign to prove his prophethood. They asked him to split the moon. The Prophet turned to Allah in supplication, and Allah caused the moon to split into two distinct halves, with Mount Hira visible between them. The Prophet called out to the Quraysh, "Witness!" They saw the miracle with their own eyes.

Yet, despite witnessing this undeniable sign, they turned away and called it magic, as the next verse will state. The splitting of the moon is thus both a miracle and a warning. It is a miracle because it demonstrated the Prophet's truthfulness and Allah's absolute power over the celestial bodies that the pagans sometimes worshipped. It is a warning because it signalled that the natural order is not permanent, that the cosmos is obedient to its Creator, and that the great disruption of the Hour is both possible and near. The verse pairs the nearness of the Hour with the splitting of the moon because the latter is a foretaste of the former. If the moon can be split by divine command, then the heavens can be rent asunder, the stars can fall, and the entire universe can be dismantled when the Hour arrives. The believer reflects on this and feels a profound sense of awe. The same moon that hangs serenely in the night sky is not an independent deity or an eternal fixture; it is a creature under the command of its Lord, and its splitting was a sign of both mercy and warning to a people who were given every chance to believe.

=2=

This verse captures the tragic and obstinate response of the Quraysh to the miracle of the moon splitting. Despite seeing an undeniable sign, something that defied the laws of nature and could only be the work of the Creator, they turned away.

The Arabic "yu'ridu" means to turn one's side, to show disregard, to walk away. They did not pause to reflect, they did not fall down in prostration, they did not acknowledge the truth. Instead, they immediately reached for a label that would allow them to dismiss it. They called it "same old magic.

" The Arabic "sihrun mustamirr" can mean continuous magic, powerful magic, or magic that is passing away and will soon vanish. Each of these meanings captures a different aspect of their desperate psychology. By calling it continuous magic, they implied that the Prophet was a persistent and skilled magician whose tricks were becoming tiresome. By calling it magic that would pass away, they comforted themselves with the thought that this sign, like all supposed magic, was temporary and its effect would fade. This reaction reveals the terrifying power of a heart that has sealed itself against the truth. When a person is determined not to believe, no evidence is sufficient. They will always find a label, an explanation, a rationalisation that allows them to continue in their state of denial without confronting the reality. The verse is also deeply instructive for believers. It shows that the demand for signs is often not a sincere search for truth but a tactic of avoidance. The Quraysh asked for a sign, received the most dramatic of signs, and still rejected it. The problem was never the lack of evidence; the problem was the state of their hearts. The believer learns from this that faith is not simply the passive result of seeing miracles. It is a choice of the heart, a choice to submit to the truth when it is presented, rather than to turn away and invent comfortable excuses.

The repetition of this pattern throughout history, where every nation that rejected its prophet did so with similar dismissive labels, is a source of comfort for the believers who face mockery and a warning to those who mock.

=3=

This verse diagnoses the root cause of the Quraysh's denial and delivers a decisive verdict on its consequences. They rejected the truth, and they followed their own desires.

The Arabic "kadhdhabu" means they called it a lie. Their rejection was not a passive disbelief but an active denunciation. They did not simply withhold belief; they declared the truth to be false.

The second half of the diagnosis is even more revealing: they followed their desires. The Arabic "ahwa'ahum" is the plural of "hawa," the whims, inclinations, and passions of the self.

This is the engine that drives rejection. People do not disbelieve primarily because of intellectual objections; they disbelieve because the truth conflicts with their desires. The Quraysh wanted the freedom to worship their idols, to maintain their social status, to engage in their lucrative but sometimes unjust trade practices, and to live without the accountability that the Day of Judgment implies. The pure monotheism and moral discipline of Islam threatened their entire way of life. Following their desires was easier than submitting to the truth.

The verse then delivers a short, powerful sentence that closes the matter: "and every matter will be settled." The Arabic "kullu amrin mustaqirr" means that every affair has a point of settlement, a final outcome, a conclusion that will inevitably be reached. The matter of their rejection will not remain in suspense forever. It will be settled, either in this world through punishment or on the Day of Judgment when they face the full consequences of their choice. This phrase is a profound source of comfort for the oppressed and a dire warning for the oppressor. The injustice, the mockery, and the denial may persist for a time, but they are moving toward a definitive conclusion. The believer trusts that the matter is in Allah's hands and that the final settlement will be perfectly just. Nothing remains unresolved in the divine plan.

=4=

This verse presents an additional layer of evidence that the Quraysh had no excuse for their rejection. The stories of the destroyed nations had already come to them. They knew about the people of Noah, the people of 'Ad, the people of Thamud, the people of Lot, and the people of Pharaoh. These were not obscure tales from distant lands. The ruins of 'Ad and Thamud were in their own peninsula. The caravans of the Quraysh passed by the devastated region of Lot's people on their way to Syria. The stories of these nations, detailing how they rejected their prophets and were subsequently annihilated, were a sufficient deterrent, a "muzdajar," meaning something that should have stopped them in their tracks, a warning that should have prevented them from following the same path. The historical evidence was right before their eyes.

Yet, they looked at the ruins of the past and did not take heed. They saw the empty dwellings of Thamud and did not connect their own behaviour to the behaviour of Thamud's deniers. The verse implies that the Quraysh had all the information they needed to make the right choice. The stories of the destroyed nations served as a clear and sufficient warning. If a person ignores a warning sign that says "Danger: Cliff Ahead" and walks off the cliff, the fault is not with the sign but with the person who ignored it. The Quran is here stating that the historical evidence was a clear sign, and their disregard of it is wilful.

For the believer, this verse underscores the importance of studying history with the eye of faith, not merely as a collection of dates and events but as a repository of divine lessons. The stories of the Quran are not entertainment; they are warnings and guidance. The believer who reads about 'Ad and Thamud should feel a shiver of fear and a renewal of commitment to obey the prophets. The surah is about to recount these very stories, reminding the Quraysh of what they already know but refuse to internalise.

=5=

This verse comments on the nature of the Quran and the tragic state of the deniers. The Quran is described as "hikmatun balighah," wisdom that is profound, complete, far-reaching, and that has reached its ultimate point of perfection.

The word "hikmah" is profound, encompassing knowledge, wisdom, sound judgment, and the ability to put things in their proper place. The Quran is not a collection of myths or poetry; it is the distillation of divine wisdom, a guidance that addresses every aspect of human life and provides the most rational, most just, and most beneficial teachings. The adjective "balighah" adds that this wisdom is not partial or incomplete; it has reached its goal, it is fully mature and effective. If a person approaches it with an open heart, it will transform them utterly.

Yet, the verse immediately follows this description with a lament: "but warnings are of no benefit." The Arabic "nudhur" refers to the warnings, the wake-up calls, the admonitions that are meant to stir the heedless from their sleep. Despite the profound wisdom of the Quran, these warnings do not benefit the deniers. The problem is not with the Quran; the problem is with the recipients. Their hearts are so sealed, so hardened by their desires and their arrogance, that the most powerful wisdom in existence bounces off them like rain off a stone.

This is a profound and sobering truth. The same Quran that transforms a savage, drunken, tribal society into the most righteous and unified community in history, the same Quran that makes grown men weep and souls soar, is utterly ineffective for those who have chosen to close themselves off from it.

The verse teaches the believer that guidance is ultimately a gift from Allah. The external means, the Quran, is perfect and complete. But the internal receptivity, the opening of the heart, is what determines whether the wisdom takes root or is rejected. The Prophet is being comforted; he is not failing in his delivery. The wisdom he brings is perfect, but the hearts he addresses have turned to stone.

=6=

After establishing that the deniers are impervious to warnings, the Quran gives the Prophet a difficult but necessary command: turn away from them. The Arabic "fa tawalla 'anhum" means to turn aside, to leave them, to cease the exhausting effort of trying to convince those who have made it abundantly clear that they will not listen.

This command is a mercy to the Prophet. His heart was heavy with grief over their rejection, and he was in danger of destroying himself with sorrow because they would not believe. Allah, in His care for His messenger, relieves him of that burden. The responsibility of the messenger is to convey, not to compel. Once the message has been delivered with clarity and the response is persistent mockery and denial, the messenger is justified in withdrawing his emotional investment and entrusting the matter to Allah.

However, this turning away is not a cancellation of the warning; it is a transition to a different phase. The verse then instructs the Prophet to wait for the Day when the caller will summon them to something horrifying. The caller is the angel Israfil, or another appointed angel, who will blow the trumpet on the Day of Judgment. The call will summon all of humanity to the plain of gathering.

The word "nukur" describes something horrifying, unprecedented, terrifying beyond all human experience. The Day of Judgment is a day unlike any other, a day whose horrors the mind cannot fully comprehend. The command to wait is both a comfort and a subtle threat. The Prophet waits with serene patience, knowing that the justice of Allah will manifest. The deniers, meanwhile, are being given a final warning. The turn away from them is a sign that their case has now been deferred to the ultimate court, and the court date is set for a Day that will be horrifying beyond their worst nightmares.

For the believer, this verse teaches the wisdom of disengagement from fruitless arguments. There comes a point when the best thing to do for one's own spiritual health is to turn away, leave the judgment to Allah, and focus on those who are receptive.

=7=

This verse paints a vivid and terrifying picture of the resurrection. The deniers will emerge from their graves with their eyes humbled and downcast.

The Arabic "khushsha'an absaruhum" means their eyes will be in a state of extreme humility, abasement, and terror. They will not dare to look up. The arrogance that characterised their worldly life, the strutting and the proud glances, will be utterly extinguished. They will be like the lowest of the low, their eyes fixed to the ground in shame and fear.

The second part of the verse compares their emergence to swarming locusts. Locusts, when they swarm, are a chaotic, teeming mass that spreads across the land in overwhelming numbers, moving with a frantic, undirected energy. The comparison is profoundly apt. Like locusts, the resurrected human beings will be countless, emerging from every corner of the earth, covering the plains. Like locusts, they will be in a state of confusion and dispersion, driven by the call to gather, unable to resist. Like locusts, they will rise from the ground, from their graves, after a period of being hidden in the earth. The imagery also conveys a sense of weakness and vulnerability. Locusts are fragile creatures, easily crushed. The mighty kings and tyrants of history will rise from their graves as helpless, teeming beings with downcast eyes, stripped of all their power and pretense. The believer, reading this, is filled with a sense of the majesty of Allah and the utter insignificance of human power before Him.

The verse also provides a contrast: the believers will rise with faces radiant and eyes full of hope, as described in other surahs. But for the deniers who laughed and mocked, the resurrection will be a scene of unmitigated horror and humiliation. The comparison to locusts is a sharp reminder that the human being, no matter how impressive in this world, is but a creature, and when the Creator commands, the creature rises in a state of complete subjection.

=8=

This verse continues the scene of the resurrection, describing the movement of the resurrected and their verbal response. They will be rushing headlong, necks stretched out, hastening towards the caller.

The Arabic "muhti'in" means speeding eagerly or being driven compulsively, unable to stop or slow down. They are drawn by the irresistible summons of the trumpet, like iron filings to a magnet. There is no choice, no hesitation, no possibility of refusal. The call of the divine summons overrides all other wills. The scene is one of total, unstoppable motion toward the place of judgment. Then comes the cry of the disbelievers: "This is a difficult Day! " The Arabic "yaumun 'asir" means a day of hardship, severity, and constriction.

This is their first verbal admission of the truth they spent their lives denying. In the world, they mocked the idea of the Day of Judgment. They asked when it would come and scoffed at its terrors. Now, faced with its reality, they can only utter this cry of anguish.

The word "difficult" barely scratches the surface of the horrors they are experiencing. The admission comes too late. In the world, they could have avoided this difficult Day by believing and doing good. Now, the acknowledgment of its difficulty does not alleviate its suffering. The verse captures the essence of the tragedy of disbelief: the truth is denied when it could save, and it is acknowledged when it can only condemn.

For the believer, this verse serves as a powerful motivation to take the warning seriously while there is still time. The Day will indeed be difficult, but for the believers, Allah will make it easy. The Prophet said that the Day of Judgment will be made easy for the believer to the extent that it will be like the time between the noon and afternoon prayers. The contrast could not be greater. The same Day that is "difficult" beyond imagination for the disbelievers will be a period of ease and relief for the righteous. The cry of the disbelievers, "This is a difficult Day," is the sound of a soul encountering the reality it refused to prepare for. The surah is about to recount the stories of nations who faced a foretaste of this difficult Day in their worldly destruction.

=9=

The surah now begins the series of historical examples that serve as warnings to the Quraysh. The first is the people of Noah. They denied the truth and rejected Our servant, meaning Noah. The title "Our servant" is an honour. Despite being rejected and mocked by his people, Noah was elevated by Allah with this noble designation.

The Arabic "'abdana" signifies a close, devoted servant, someone who belongs entirely to Allah. The people of Noah did not merely disagree with him; they accused him of being insane.

The word "majnun" was hurled at Noah, just as the Quraysh hurled it at Muhammad. This is the first of many parallels that the Quran draws between the Prophet and his predecessors. The accusation of insanity is the last refuge of the denier who cannot refute the message.

The verse adds that Noah was intimidated. The Arabic "wazdujira" means he was rebuked harshly, threatened, driven back with insults and menaces. His people did not just ignore him; they actively bullied him. They told him that if he did not stop his preaching, he would be stoned or expelled. The pressure on Noah was immense. He was one man standing against an entire society entrenched in idolatry and sin.

This description serves to comfort the Prophet Muhammad. He is not the first messenger to be called insane. He is not the first to face threats and intimidation. His brother Noah, a mighty prophet, endured the same for nearly a thousand years. The believer learns from this that the path of da'wah is often a path of persecution. The truth is rarely welcomed by those whose power and desires it threatens. The reaction of Noah's people, calling him insane and trying to silence him, is the classic response of falsehood when it cannot counter the argument. The verse sets the stage for Noah's supplication and the divine response, which will demonstrate the pattern of patient calling followed by ultimate deliverance for the messenger and destruction for the deniers.

=10=

After centuries of patient preaching, after enduring mockery, threats, and physical intimidation, Noah finally turned to his Lord with this deeply moving supplication. The Arabic "da'a rabbahu" means he called upon his Lord, he supplicated earnestly and desperately. The words he used are recorded in other surahs and summarised here: "I am helpless, so help me.

" The word "maghlub" means overpowered, defeated, vanquished. Noah was not admitting a personal failure of effort; he was stating the reality of his situation. He had exhausted every possible means of persuasion. He had called his people openly and secretly, by day and by night. He had used every argument, every approach. But they had overpowered him with their numbers, their ridicule, and their threats. He was helpless to change their hearts, because guidance is ultimately in Allah's hand. So he turned to the only source of help. The supplication is a beautiful model of prophetic humility. Noah did not ask for their destruction out of personal vengeance. He simply laid his state before his Lord and asked for help.

The Arabic "fantasir" means to grant victory, to come to the aid of the oppressed, to exact justice on behalf of the one wronged. It is a cry for divine intervention. This supplication marks a turning point. After it, the era of warning came to an end, and the era of punishment began.

The verse teaches the believer that there is a time for patient endurance and a time to pour out one's heart to Allah in desperate supplication. Allah is the only true helper. When all human means are exhausted, the believer turns to Allah, not with despair but with hope, knowing that His help is near. Noah's supplication was answered in the most dramatic way imaginable, as the following verses will describe. The believer learns that no situation is hopeless when one has a direct line to the Lord of the worlds. The cry of the oppressed is heard, and the response may come in ways that shake the very foundations of the earth.

=11=

The divine response to Noah's supplication was swift and overwhelming. The gates of the sky were opened.

The Arabic "fatahna abwab as-sama'" paints the picture of the heavens being thrown wide open, not a gentle shower but a deluge. The rain that came down was not ordinary rain. It was "ma'in munhamir," pouring water that descended in torrents, in sheets, without pause or mercy. This was not the rain that brings life to the earth; this was the rain of destruction.

The image of gates being opened is a powerful one. Gates imply control. Normally, Allah sends down rain in a measured amount, a blessing that sustains life. But when the gates are opened, the measure is removed, and the water becomes an unstoppable flood. This verse and the next one describe the two sources of the floodwater: the sky and the earth. Water came from above and water gushed from below, surrounding the disbelievers from every direction. There was no escape. The flood was not a random natural disaster; it was a precisely timed act of divine retribution.

The opening of the gates of the sky is also a sign of Allah's absolute power over the elements. The same sky that holds back its water during a drought can release it in a torrent that destroys a civilization. The Quraysh, who sometimes experienced both drought and floods, were being reminded that the natural world is not a self-regulating machine. It is under the direct command of its Creator. The rain that they took for granted or that they sometimes feared is entirely in Allah's hand.

The verse also carries a symbolic meaning. The flood was not just physical water; it was a cleansing of the earth from the filth of disbelief and sin. The earth was being washed of a corrupt humanity, making way for a new beginning. The believer reflects on this and recognises that what appears as a natural phenomenon is often a divine act, and that the boundary between mercy and punishment is crossed when human beings transgress the limits set by their Lord.

=12=

The second source of the flood was the earth itself. The earth burst with springs.

The Arabic "fajjarna al-ard 'uyunan" means the earth was split open, and water gushed forth from every fissure. It was as if the entire planet had become a fountain. The combination of torrential rain from above and gushing springs from below meant that the water rose rapidly, covering everything. There was no high ground to flee to, no mountain that could provide refuge. The waters met, the rain and the spring water, converging to fulfil a command that was already decreed.

The phrase "for a fate already set" translates the Arabic "li amrin qad qudir. " This means that the flood was not a chaotic, random event. It was a matter that had been preordained, written in the divine decree. The amount of water, the duration of the flood, the exact moment it began, and the exact number of those who would perish were all determined by Allah's precise measure.

This is a profound theological point. The destruction of the disbelievers was not a loss of control; it was the execution of a perfect, preordained plan. The universe operates on divine law, and the flood was part of that law.

The verse also highlights the helplessness of human power against the forces of nature when they are unleashed by divine command. The people of Noah may have been strong, wealthy, and numerous, but they could not close the gates of the sky or seal the springs of the earth. Their technology, their buildings, and their physical strength were utterly useless. For the Quraysh, this was a clear warning. They too were subject to the same Lord who drowned the people of Noah. The flood was a historical event, but the principle behind it remains. When a people persist in their rebellion, they are at the mercy of a universe that is entirely obedient to its Creator. The believer learns to fear the Lord who controls the sky and the earth and to seek His protection rather than relying on any human security.

=13=

Amidst the overwhelming description of the flood, the Quran turns to the means of salvation for Noah and the believers. Allah says "We carried him," taking direct credit for the rescue. The ark was not merely Noah's construction; it was a vessel that sailed by divine will and protection. The ark is described in humble, tangible terms: it was made of planks and nails.

The Arabic "dhat alwahin wa dusur" strips the ark of any mythical grandeur. It was a simple wooden boat, built with boards and fastened with nails or ropes.

This is a lesson in itself. The greatest salvation does not require the most sophisticated technology. What matters is that the construction is done under divine guidance and that the vessel is boarded by those who have faith. The simplicity of the ark also highlights the contrast between the material resources of the deniers and the believers. The deniers had grand buildings, lofty towers, and all the wealth of their civilization. The believers had a wooden boat held together with nails. But the boat survived, and the grand civilization was utterly destroyed.

The mention of planks and nails makes the story tangible and credible. It is not a fairy tale about a magical ship; it is a historical account of a real vessel whose remains some traditions say were seen on Mount Judi.

The verse also emphasises Allah's direct involvement. "We carried him" is a statement of intimate care. Noah was not abandoned to the flood. He was personally transported to safety by his Lord. The ark, with its planks and nails, became the locus of divine mercy.

For the believer, this verse is a reminder that material simplicity, when combined with faith and obedience, is a fortress that no flood can destroy. The planks and nails of the ark teach us that what makes something a means of salvation is not its outward form but the command of Allah that accompanies it. The believer trusts in the planks and nails of their own life, the simple acts of obedience and the straightforward faith, knowing that Allah can carry them through any storm.

=14=

The ark sailed under the watchful eyes of Allah. The Arabic "bi a'yunina" means under Our eyes, under Our direct gaze and protection.

This is the same phrase used to comfort the Prophet in a previous surah. The ark was not drifting aimlessly on a chaotic sea. It was under the constant supervision and care of Allah. Every wave that rose, every current that flowed, every movement of the vessel was known and controlled.

This is an image of profound intimacy and security. In the midst of the greatest flood the world has ever known, a small wooden boat was the safest place on earth because it was under the eyes of the Lord.

The second half of the verse states the purpose of this event: it was a punishment on behalf of the one they denied. The one they denied was Noah. The flood was divine justice executed in response to their rejection and mockery of Allah's servant. The punishment was not arbitrary; it was a response to a specific crime.

The Arabic "jaza'an li man kana kufir" means a recompense for the one who was disbelieved. This shows the honour that Allah bestows upon His prophets. To reject a prophet is to invite divine wrath. To support a prophet is to board the ark of salvation. The verse masterfully combines the themes of mercy and justice. For Noah and the believers, the flood was an act of rescue and protection. For the deniers, the same flood was an act of punishment. The water that carried the ark to safety drowned the mockers. The believer learns from this that Allah's actions in the world are not one-dimensional. A single event can be a punishment for one group and a mercy for another, depending on their relationship with Allah. The eyes of the Lord that watched over the ark are the same eyes that saw the drowning of the wicked. The believer seeks to be on the side of the ark, under the watchful eyes of mercy, not on the side of the flood, under the eyes of justice.

=15=

The flood of Noah was not just an event that happened and was forgotten. Allah deliberately left it as a sign for future generations.

The Arabic "taraknaha ayatan" means We left it, the story or the ark itself, as a sign. The sign is multi-layered. The story itself, preserved in scripture and oral tradition, is a sign that teaches about the consequences of disbelief. The physical remains of the ark, which according to the Quran rested on Mount Judi, were left as a sign for people to see and reflect upon. Some scholars mention that the early generations knew the location of the ark's remains. The entire narrative, with its vivid details, is a sign that points to the reality of divine justice and the truth of the prophetic message. Then the verse poses a powerful, recurring question that will become the refrain of this surah: "So is there anyone who will be mindful?

" The Arabic "fa hal min muddakir" is an invitation, a challenge, a plea. Muddakir comes from dhikr, meaning remembrance, reflection, taking heed.

The question is: is there any soul out there who will take this sign to heart? Who will remember the lesson of Noah and apply it to their own life? Who will recognise that the same Lord who drowned the mockers of old is the Lord who will judge the mockers of today? This refrain is a direct appeal to the human conscience. It bypasses intellectual argument and addresses the heart's capacity for taking warning.

The question implies that the signs are abundantly clear and that the only thing missing is a receptive heart. The believer, upon hearing this verse, should feel a stirring in the heart, a desire to be among those who are mindful, who do not let the signs pass them by as mere stories but who internalise their lessons and change their lives accordingly.

The repetition of this refrain throughout the surah will hammer this invitation home, giving the listener multiple opportunities to respond. It is one of the most beautiful and haunting features of Surah Al-Qamar.

=16=

This verse is the second refrain of the surah, and it complements the first. While the first refrain invited reflection, this one expresses the severity of what happens when the warning is ignored. "How dreadful were My punishment and warnings!

" The Arabic "kayfa kana 'adhabi wa nudhur" is a rhetorical question that implies an answer too terrible to fully articulate. The punishment was dreadful beyond description. The warnings that preceded it were clear, persistent, and merciful. The combination of the two creates a picture of complete justice. The warnings gave every opportunity for repentance. The punishment was the inevitable consequence of rejecting those warnings. The verse is spoken in the first person by Allah, which adds to its power. It is not a narrator describing the punishment; it is the Lord of the worlds Himself reflecting on the fate of those who defied Him. This personal involvement underscores the reality of the punishment. It was not an impersonal natural disaster; it was "My punishment. " The use of "My" also highlights that the warnings were an act of mercy. Allah sent "My warnings" through Noah, a gesture of care and concern. When those warnings were mocked, "My punishment" became an act of justice.

For the believer, this refrain inspires a healthy fear of Allah. The punishment of Noah's people is not a myth; it is a historical demonstration of what happens when warnings are ignored. The believer hears this and renews their commitment to heed the warnings of the Quran, knowing that the same Lord who punished the people of Noah is the Lord who will judge all humanity. The refrain also serves as a comfort to the oppressed believers. The punishment that fell on the mockers of Noah was a vindication of Noah and his followers. The same Lord who punished the oppressors then will punish the oppressors now. Justice may be delayed, but it is never cancelled.

=17=

This verse is the third refrain and perhaps the most central to the surah. It declares that Allah has made the Quran easy for remembrance, for taking heed, for being stored in the heart and recalled in the mind.

The Arabic "yassarna al-Qur'ana li dhikr" is a profound statement about the accessibility of the divine word. The Quran is not a book of obscure mysteries that only a scholarly elite can understand. Its core message is simple, clear, and easily remembered. Its stories are vivid, its arguments are logical, its moral teachings are intuitive, and its language is rhythmic and memorable. Millions of people, young and old, literate and illiterate, have memorised the entire Quran, a phenomenon unique among the world's scriptures. This ease of remembrance is a miracle in itself and a fulfilment of the divine promise. The purpose of making the Quran easy is "li dhikr," for remembrance, for being a constant reminder. The Quran is meant to be a living companion, a book that is frequently recited, reflected upon, and used as a source of guidance in daily life. It is not meant to sit on a shelf gathering dust.

The verse then repeats the question: "So is there anyone who will be mindful? " The ease is provided, the sign is clear, the reminder is accessible. The only missing component is a human being who is willing to take heed, to remember, to internalise.

The question is a gentle reproach and an urgent invitation. What excuse does anyone have? The book has been made easy. The path to guidance has been smoothed. If a person still fails to be mindful, the fault lies entirely within their own soul.

For the believer, this verse is a tremendous encouragement. It assures them that the Quran is not beyond their reach. They can understand it, memorise it, and live by it. It also instils a sense of responsibility. Since the Quran has been made easy, the believer has no excuse for ignorance. The verse is a call to engage actively with the book of Allah, to recite it, to study it, and to become one of those who are mindful.

The repetition of this refrain after each story of destruction creates a powerful rhythm: warning, punishment, and then the open door of the easy reminder, inviting every listener to enter.

=18=

The surah now moves to the second example of a destroyed nation: 'Ad. They also rejected the truth.

The Arabic "kadhdhabat 'Ad" indicates their active denial of the message brought by their prophet Hud. Just like the people of Noah, just like the Quraysh, the people of 'Ad were given clear warnings, and they responded with disbelief and mockery. The verse is extremely concise, assuming the listener is already familiar with the story of 'Ad from other parts of the Quran. 'Ad were a mighty people, known for their tall stature and impressive buildings. They lived in a region of sand dunes and were blessed with physical strength and material prosperity. But they became arrogant, worshipped idols, and oppressed the weak. Prophet Hud called them to monotheism, but they rejected him, boasting that no one was stronger than them. The verse immediately follows their rejection with the refrain: "Then how dreadful were My punishment and warnings!" The structure is identical to the story of Noah. The rejection is followed by the rhetorical question about the severity of the punishment. This creates a pattern that drives the lesson home. The punishment of 'Ad is described in the next verses, but the refrain here serves as a preview, building suspense and dread. The listener knows that something terrible is about to be described. The refrain also emphasises that the warnings were just as clear and just as severe as the punishment. The people of 'Ad had no excuse. Their prophet Hud was one of their own, speaking their language, calling them to what was good. They had the historical example of Noah's people before them.

Yet they persisted in their denial. The verse serves as a direct parallel to the situation of the Quraysh. They too have received clear warnings. They too have a prophet from among themselves. They too have the examples of previous nations. If they continue in their rejection, the refrain will apply to them as well. The believer, reading this, feels a renewed urgency to take the warnings seriously and to avoid the fate of the 'Ad, who once walked the earth in strength and now exist only as a cautionary tale.

=19=

The punishment of 'Ad is now specified. Allah sent against them a furious wind.

The Arabic "rihan sarsaran" is a wind that is fiercely cold, howling, and violent, a wind that makes a terrifying sound. This was no ordinary gust; it was a sustained, supernatural wind sent by divine command.

The phrase "on a day of unrelenting misery" further describes the horrific nature of the punishment. The Arabic "yaumi nahsin mustamirr" can mean a day of constant calamity, a day that was inauspicious and utterly devoid of any blessing or relief. The wind blew for seven nights and eight days without pause, as mentioned in Surah Al-Haqqah. There was no respite, no moment of calm. The misery was continuous and unrelenting.

This is a key detail. In the world, suffering is often interspersed with periods of relief. Even in the worst disasters, there are moments of hope or rest. But the punishment of 'Ad was designed to be a continuous, escalating horror. The wind did not let up. It grew fiercer and more destructive with each passing hour. The choice of wind as the instrument of punishment is also significant. 'Ad were a people who prided themselves on their physical strength. They thought their powerful bodies and their solid buildings could withstand anything. Allah destroyed them with something intangible, something they could not fight or build a wall against. The wind slipped through every crack, toppled every tower, and tore apart every stronghold. It demonstrated that human strength, when pitted against the forces of nature unleashed by Allah, is utterly negligible. For the Quraysh, who also prided themselves on their fortifications and their martial prowess, this was a clear warning. The wind that destroyed 'Ad was not a myth. The ruins of their civilization bore witness. The believer reads this and is reminded of the fragility of human power. The same wind that cools the skin on a hot day can, by Allah's command, become an instrument of annihilation.

=20=

This verse describes the devastating effect of the wind on the people of 'Ad. It snatched them up, tearing them from the ground where they stood, hurling them into the air, and dashing them against the earth.

The Arabic "tanzi'u an-nas" means to pull them out violently, to uproot them from their places. The wind was so powerful that human beings were no more stable than leaves in a gale. They were lifted, tossed, and destroyed.

The verse then gives a hauntingly vivid image: they were left like trunks of uprooted palm trees. The Arabic "a'jaz nakhlin munqa'ir" compares their bodies to the trunks of palm trees that have been pulled out of the ground and lie scattered. A palm tree trunk is tall, heavy, and seemingly sturdy. But when it is uprooted, it lies helpless on the ground, its roots exposed, its life extinguished. The comparison is particularly apt for 'Ad because they were known for their tall, powerful physiques. They were like palm trees in their stature and their strength.

Yet, they were uprooted and scattered like dead wood. The image also conveys a sense of utter devastation. A palm grove after a hurricane, with its majestic trees lying flattened and lifeless, is a scene of complete ruin.

This is what happened to the civilization of 'Ad. A people who were once the envy of their neighbours became scattered debris. The specific mention of palm trees is also a subtle reminder of the blessings they had enjoyed. Palm trees were a source of life in the desert, providing dates, shade, and material for building. The same wind that destroyed them also destroyed their trees, turning their fertile land into a barren wasteland.

For the believer, this verse is a graphic reminder of the end of arrogance. The people of 'Ad walked tall and proud, but they were thrown down like dead tree trunks. The lesson is clear: do not be deceived by your strength, your height, your physique, or your worldly standing. All of it can be uprooted in a moment by the command of the One who is truly powerful.
