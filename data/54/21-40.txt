=21=

This refrain repeats after the description of ‘Ad’s destruction, exactly as it did after the story of Noah. The repetition is not a mere stylistic choice; it is a deliberate and powerful pedagogical tool. Each time the refrain appears, it drives the same truth deeper into the heart of the listener. The punishment was dreadful. The warnings were clear. The combination of the two establishes the perfect justice of Allah. No nation can claim they were destroyed without fair notice. ‘Ad received their warner, Hud, who spoke their language and shared their culture. They saw the signs he brought. They heard his warnings about a painful punishment if they did not repent. They responded with arrogance and mockery. When the punishment came, it was exactly as dreadful as they had been warned, and perhaps far more dreadful than their imaginations could grasp. The rhetorical question “how dreadful” invites the listener to pause and contemplate the sheer magnitude of the calamity. The mind, unable to fully comprehend the horror of a screaming wind that uproots bodies like dead palm trunks, is left in a state of awe.

The repetition of this refrain after each story creates a cumulative effect. The listener is repeatedly confronted with the same stark truth: rejecting the divine warning leads to an unspeakable outcome. The refrain also serves as a comfort to the believers. The punishment of the oppressors is an act of divine justice. The believers who suffered at the hands of ‘Ad were vindicated. Their cries were heard. The refrain is a promise that the same justice will manifest for every believer who is oppressed and for every denier who mocks the truth. The fact that Allah says “My punishment” and “My warnings” personalises the entire event.

This is not an impersonal law of karma. This is the direct, personal response of the Creator to His creation. The believer finds in this a relationship of both fear and intimacy. They fear the punishment of their Lord, and they feel the closeness of His concern in sending the warnings. The refrain forces the listener to take a side: will you be among those who heed the warnings, or among those upon whom the dreadful punishment falls?

=22=

This refrain also repeats, and its placement after the story of ‘Ad is deeply significant. The story of ‘Ad is terrifying. The wind, the uprooting, the devastation. The human heart, after hearing such a tale, might feel a sense of despair, a fear that perhaps it is too difficult to avoid such a fate. Immediately, the Quran offers the antidote: the reminder has been made easy. The Quran is accessible. Its stories are recounted so that they may be remembered and taken to heart. The path to safety is not hidden or arduous. It is laid out clearly in the pages of the Quran and in the teachings of the Prophet.

The repetition of “And We have certainly made the Quran easy to remember” after each story of destruction is a lifeline thrown to the listener. It is as if Allah is saying: I destroyed those nations, but I do not wish to destroy you. I have given you a book that makes guidance easy. All you need to do is be mindful.

The Arabic “yassarna” emphasizes that this ease is a deliberate act of divine facilitation. The Quran’s language, its structure, its rhythmic verses, its vivid imagery, its logical arguments, all work together to make it a book that can be memorised, understood, and applied. It does not require a degree in theology to grasp its essential message. A simple Bedouin hearing the Quran could understand its call to monotheism and righteous living.

The question that follows, “So is there anyone who will be mindful?”, is a direct challenge to the listener. Will you be that person? Will you be the one who breaks the cycle of heedlessness that destroyed ‘Ad and Noah’s people?

The question is open-ended, hanging in the air, waiting for a response from the heart. The believer answers this question by turning to the Quran with renewed commitment, by engaging in dhikr, by making the Quran a daily companion. The refrain transforms the surah from a mere historical account into a living, breathing invitation. Every listener, in every era, is being asked in this very moment: will you be among the mindful? The ease has been provided. The stories have been told. The choice is yours.

=23=

The surah now turns to the third example: the people of Thamud. The pattern established with Noah and ‘Ad continues. Thamud also rejected the warnings.

The Arabic “kadhdhabat Thamudu bi an-nudhur” is almost identical to the phrasing used for Noah and ‘Ad. This deliberate repetition of structure underscores the uniformity of human behaviour across time. The faces change, the names change, the geography changes, but the fundamental drama remains the same. A prophet comes with clear warnings. The people, entrenched in their ways, reject him. The consequences follow. Thamud were the people of Prophet Salih. They lived in the region of Al-Hijr, where they carved magnificent homes into the mountains. They succeeded ‘Ad in the land and inherited their civilization. They were known for their engineering skills, their agricultural prosperity, and their material advancement. But like those before them, they turned the blessings of Allah into objects of pride and rebellion. They worshipped idols and oppressed the weak. Prophet Salih was sent to them from among their own people. He called them to worship Allah alone and to cease their corruption. He was known for his wisdom and integrity, and his people had high hopes for him before his prophethood.

Yet when he began to deliver the warning, they turned against him. The verse does not yet detail their specific arguments; it simply states the fact of their rejection. This brevity is powerful. It tells the listener that the specific excuses do not matter. Whether a people reject a prophet for one reason or another, the core sin is the same: they rejected the warnings. The Quraysh, listening to this, are meant to see their own reflection. They too have a prophet from among themselves. They too have been warned. Their rejection places them in the same category as Thamud. The believer, reading this, feels a sombre recognition that the human soul, when left to its desires, follows the same destructive patterns century after century. The only way to break the pattern is through mindfulness, through the very thing the refrain is constantly inviting.

=24=

This verse records the specific argument that the people of Thamud used to reject Prophet Salih. Their objection was not based on the content of his message; it was based on his humanity. They were scandalised by the idea that a mere human being, one of their own, could be a prophet.

The phrase “one average human being from among us” is deeply revealing. The Arabic “basharan minna wahidan” means a single human being from among us. They could not accept that Allah would choose a man from their own community rather than sending an angel or choosing someone of higher social status. They expected that divine communication would bypass ordinary humanity and come in a more grandiose form.

This is a recurring theme in the objections of the deniers throughout the Quran. They could not conceive that a human being who eats, drinks, and walks in the market could be the recipient of divine revelation. They then declared that if they were to follow such a person, they would be “misguided and insane.

” The Arabic “fi dalalin wa su’ur” means they would be in error and in a state of madness. This is a classic psychological projection. They are the ones who are misguided, worshipping stones they carved with their own hands. They are the ones who are insane, following the superstitions of their ancestors without any rational proof.

Yet they accuse Salih of being the cause of misguidance and madness. This reversal of reality is a hallmark of the defiant denier. The truth seems crazy to a person whose heart is steeped in falsehood. The believer learns from this that the accusations hurled at the prophets are not new, nor are they rational. They are the desperate flailings of a soul that refuses to submit. The Quraysh were using the exact same argument against Muhammad: “He is just a man like us. ” The Quran is showing them that they are merely repeating the words of a destroyed people, and if they do not change, they will share their fate.

=25=

The people of Thamud continued their objections with another argument rooted in envy and pride. They asked, with indignation, whether the revelation had been sent down only to him out of all of them.

The Arabic “a ulqiya adh-dhikru ‘alayhi min baynina” reflects their astonishment that Allah would single out Salih from among the entire tribe. This question reveals their fundamental misunderstanding of prophethood. Prophethood is not a prize that is distributed according to human standards of wealth, lineage, or popularity. It is a divine gift bestowed upon whom Allah wills, based on His knowledge of the purity and capacity of that soul. The people of Thamud could not stomach the idea that Salih, whom they considered ordinary, was exalted above them. Their pride was wounded. They then compounded their denial by accusing Salih of being a “boastful liar.

” The Arabic “kadhdhabun ashir” combines two accusations: that he is an arrogant liar who fabricates things against Allah, and that he is a boaster who seeks to elevate himself above others. Again, the accusation is a projection. It was they who were arrogant and boastful about their mountain carvings and their power. It was they who lied against Allah by worshipping idols. Salih was the epitome of humility and truthfulness. This verse exposes a recurring dynamic in the confrontation between prophets and their people. The prophet calls to humility and submission to God. The people, addicted to their status and their desires, accuse the prophet of the very sins they are committing. The Quraysh similarly called Muhammad a liar and accused him of seeking power and prestige, when he lived a life of simplicity and was known as Al-Amin, the trustworthy.

For the believer, this verse is a lesson in recognizing the nature of the attacks against the truth. The attacks often reveal more about the attackers than about the truth itself. The believer remains steadfast, knowing that the accusations are false and that history has vindicated every prophet who was similarly maligned.

=26=

Allah responds to the accusations of Thamud by addressing Salih directly, offering comfort and a definitive promise. The phrase “They will soon know who the boastful liar is” is a powerful, terse statement of impending judgment.

The Arabic “sa ya’lamuna ghadan mani al-kadhdhabu al-ashir” uses the word “ghadan,” meaning tomorrow, to indicate that the truth will be revealed in the very near future. The matter is not going to remain ambiguous for long. The accusation they hurled at Salih will be turned back upon them, and the true identity of the boastful liar will be made manifest to everyone.

This is a classic Quranic rhetorical move. When the disbelievers make a false accusation, Allah does not always provide a lengthy logical refutation. Instead, He often defers the matter to the near future, when events themselves will prove who was truthful and who was lying. The punishment that is about to descend upon Thamud will be the ultimate proof. When they are destroyed and Salih and the believers are saved, the entire world will know that Salih was the truthful messenger and they were the boastful liars. The promise of “soon” is a source of immense comfort for every believer who is slandered for their faith. The false accusations, the misrepresentations, the mockery, all have an expiration date. The day of judgment, whether in this world or the next, will bring absolute clarity. The believer does not need to obsess over defending their reputation against every slander. They trust that Allah, in His timing, will make the truth prevail.

The word “ashir” (boastful) is also significant. Salih was not a boaster. He lived a humble life. The boasters were the ones who arrogantly displayed their wealth and their mountain palaces. The punishment would show that their boasting was built on a foundation of sand. This verse is a small but powerful interjection of divine assurance in the midst of the narrative, reminding the listener that Allah is fully aware of the lies spoken against His prophets and that He will deal with them decisively.

=27=

The divine plan for Thamud now takes a concrete form. Allah informs Salih that He is sending the she-camel as a test.

The Arabic “inna mursilu an-naqati fitnatan lahum” indicates that the camel is a fitnah, a trial, a test that will expose the true nature of their hearts. The story of the she-camel is elaborated in other surahs. The people of Thamud had demanded a sign from Salih, specifically that a pregnant she-camel emerge from a specific rock. Salih prayed to Allah, and the rock split open, bringing forth a magnificent, miraculous she-camel that was unlike any ordinary animal. This camel was not merely a sign of Allah’s power; it was a specific test of their obedience and their sense of justice. They were given clear instructions: they must not harm the camel, and they must share the water with her on alternate days. The test was designed to see whether they would respect the sign of Allah or whether their arrogance would drive them to destroy it.

The verse then instructs Salih to “watch them closely, and have patience.” The Arabic “fa artiqibhum wa astabir” is a dual command. He is to observe them, to see what they will do, to witness their response to the test. And he is to be patient, steadfast, enduring whatever mockery or hostility they direct at him.

This is a profound lesson for every believer. When a trial or a sign is presented, one must watch and wait, not jumping to conclusions or acting impulsively. Patience is required, because the outcomes of tests are often delayed. The she-camel was a walking miracle, a living proof of Salih’s prophethood.

Yet Salih knew that the test might not lead to their belief but to their destruction. He was commanded to watch and wait with a patient heart, trusting in Allah’s wisdom.

For the believer, this verse is a reminder that life is full of tests. Some people are given signs, blessings, or challenges. The response to these tests determines their fate. The believer watches their own response and remains patient with the responses of others, knowing that the final judgment belongs to Allah alone.

=28=

This verse details the specific command given to the people of Thamud regarding the she-camel. Salih is instructed to tell them that the drinking water must be divided between them and the camel.

The Arabic “anna al-ma’a qismatun baynahum” establishes a clear, just arrangement. The water was a precious resource in the arid region of Thamud. The miraculous camel, being a creation of Allah, had a right to drink from the water that Allah provided. The division was fair: one day for the camel, one day for the people. On the camel’s day, the people were not to touch the water. On their day, the camel would not drink. This arrangement was a test of their willingness to share, to recognise the rights of a creature that was a sign from Allah, and to abide by a divinely imposed rule that curtailed their absolute control over resources. The test was not just about belief in the abstract; it was about a tangible, practical sacrifice. They had to give up half of their water supply. For a desert people, water was life. To share it with an animal, even a miraculous one, required a surrender of their greed and their sense of entitlement. The command was a training in humility and obedience. The believer reflects on this and understands that faith often demands concrete sacrifices. It is not enough to say “I believe” in the heart. The belief must manifest in actions, in giving up something of value for the sake of Allah. The just division of water is also a lesson in the ethics of resource sharing. The rights of animals, the environment, and other human beings are to be respected. The people of Thamud were not being asked to give up all the water, only to share it equitably. Their refusal to do so, as the next verses will show, revealed the depth of their selfishness and their rebellion. The simple, practical command became the pivot upon which their eternal fate turned. The believer learns that obedience in small, tangible matters is often the key to success in the great, eternal matters.

=29=

The response of Thamud to the test was one of utter defiance and brutality. Instead of honouring the arrangement, they decided to eliminate the sign. The verse says they “roused a companion of theirs,” meaning they called upon one of their own, a man known for his boldness and wickedness.

The Arabic “fa nabaw as-sahibahum” implies they summoned their comrade, incited him, and urged him to do the foul deed. They chose the most wretched among them to carry out the act.

This detail shows that the killing of the camel was not the act of a lone rogue. It was a collective decision, endorsed by the society. The entire community was complicit. The man, empowered by their support, dared to kill the she-camel.

The Arabic “fa ta’ata fa ‘aqar” means he approached her and hamstrung her, cutting the tendons of her legs, a brutal method that brought the animal down. This act was a direct challenge to Allah. It was a declaration of war against the divine sign. They did not merely reject the message intellectually; they physically attacked and destroyed the living miracle that had been sent to them. This was the point of no return. The killing of the she-camel was the culmination of their arrogance and their contempt for Allah’s command. It was the equivalent of the Quraysh mocking the moon splitting or plotting to kill the Prophet. When a people reach the point of actively seeking to destroy the signs of Allah, their doom is sealed. The verse captures the moment of their fatal choice. They roused their companion. He dared to kill. The language is terse and grim. There is no long description, just the cold facts of their crime.

For the believer, this verse is a chilling warning about the dangers of collective sin. When a society as a whole supports and encourages wrongdoing, the consequences are collective as well. The man who struck the blow was the agent, but the entire people shared the guilt. The lesson is to never be silent or complicit in the face of evil, but to stand on the side of the truth, even if one stands alone.

=30=

The refrain strikes again, this time with even greater force following the heinous crime of the she-camel’s killing. The question is posed: how dreadful were My punishment and warnings? The punishment of Thamud, which will be described in the next verse, was swift and devastating. The warnings that preceded it were persistent and clear. Salih had warned them repeatedly, and the she-camel herself was a walking warning. The juxtaposition of the crime and the punishment, framed by this refrain, creates a profound sense of divine justice. The punishment was not arbitrary; it was a direct response to their transgression. The “how dreadful” invites the listener to contemplate the terror of the blast that would soon annihilate them. It also serves to highlight the mercy inherent in the warnings. The warnings were an opportunity to avoid the dreadful punishment. The people of Thamud were given every chance. They had a prophet among them. They had the she-camel. They had the clear command to share the water. They had time. But they chose to reject all of it. The refrain, when read in the context of the crime, becomes almost a lament. It is as if Allah is expressing the tragic waste of a people who were given so much and who threw it all away. The believer, upon reading this, feels a combination of fear and gratitude. Fear of the dreadful punishment, and gratitude that they have been guided to heed the warnings. The refrain also serves as a transition, preparing the listener for the specific details of the punishment that are about to be recounted.

The question is rhetorical, but the answer is written in the ruins of Thamud’s stone dwellings. The punishment was dreadful indeed. The believer takes this to heart and resolves to never be among those who make the warnings of their Lord a subject of mockery.

=31=

The punishment of Thamud is now described with stark brevity. Allah sent against them only one mighty blast.

The Arabic “sayhatan wahidatan” refers to a single, thunderous cry, a shriek, or an explosive sound. It was not a prolonged campaign of punishment. It was one blast, delivered at a specific moment. This emphasises the ease with which Allah can destroy a civilization. No army was needed, no siege engines, no prolonged war. One sound, and the mighty Thamud were no more. The blast is often interpreted as a terrifying noise from the sky, accompanied by an earthquake, that shattered their hearts and levelled their bodies. It was the voice of divine wrath. The result of that single blast is described with a graphic image: they were left like the twigs of fence-builders.

The Arabic “ka hashimi al-muhtazir” refers to dried, broken twigs and branches that a person collects to build a fence or an enclosure for animals. These twigs are brittle, scattered, and worthless. The comparison is devastating. The people of Thamud, who had carved magnificent palaces out of solid rock, who were so proud of their engineering and their permanence, were reduced to the level of dry, broken twigs trampled underfoot. Their once-impressive civilization became a scattered heap of debris, indistinguishable from the random sticks collected by a shepherd.

The image is one of utter trivialisation of human power. All their strength, their art, their technology, their wealth, was turned into meaningless rubble in a single moment. For the Quraysh, who admired the ruins of Thamud as they passed by, this was a direct warning. Their own grand houses, their trade, their fortified positions in Mecca, could all become like twigs if they continued to reject the warner. The believer reflects on this and feels the fragility of worldly existence. The only true permanence is with Allah. The works of human hands, no matter how impressive, are ultimately as fragile as dry twigs before the power of the Creator.

=32=

Once again, after the devastating account of Thamud’s destruction, the refrain of mercy and invitation reappears. The Quran has been made easy to remember. After telling the listener that a mighty civilization was turned into broken twigs, the Quran immediately offers the tool to avoid such a fate: the easy reminder.

The placement of this refrain here is profoundly merciful. It is as if Allah is saying: I have just shown you what happens to those who reject the warning. But I do not want that for you. I have made the Quran easy, specifically so that you can be mindful and escape that terrible end. The ease of the Quran is a recurring theme in this surah, but here it takes on a particular urgency. The people of Thamud were not illiterate fools. They were intelligent enough to carve cities out of mountains. But intelligence without mindfulness is useless. The Quran provides the mindfulness they lacked. It tells the stories, it explains the signs, it calls to reflection.

The question “So is there anyone who will be mindful?” is asked again. The listener is given another chance to respond.

The repetition of this refrain throughout the surah is a reflection of Allah’s infinite patience. He could have told the story once and then condemned the deniers. Instead, He tells story after story, each time pausing to ask if anyone is willing to take heed.

This is the character of the Most Merciful. He does not desire the destruction of His servants. He desires their mindfulness, their remembrance, their return to Him. The believer, hearing this refrain for the third time, feels their heart soften further. They want to be that person who is mindful. They want to seize the easy rope that Allah has extended. The refrain becomes a personal call to action, a moment of introspection in the midst of the surah’s powerful narrative.

=33=

The surah now presents the fourth example: the people of Lot. The phrase is identical in structure to the previous introductions. The people of Lot also rejected the warnings.

The Arabic “kadhdhabat qawmu Lutin bi an-nudhur” places them squarely in the same category as Noah’s people, ‘Ad, and Thamud. The pattern continues unbroken. The specific sin of Lot’s people was different from the sins of the previous nations, but the root cause was the same: they rejected the warnings. They had a prophet among them, Lot, the nephew of Abraham. He called them to purity, to the worship of Allah alone, and to the abandonment of their abominable practices. They were the first people to commit acts of homosexuality openly and as a society. They also engaged in highway robbery, lewd gatherings, and the mockery of virtue. Their wickedness was comprehensive. But the Quran here does not immediately detail their sins. It focuses on their rejection of the warnings.

This is significant. The gravity of their sin was not merely in the acts themselves but in their response to the messenger who came to correct them. When Lot warned them, they rejected him, just as the previous nations had rejected their prophets. The pattern is the key. The Quraysh, listening to this, are being shown that their rejection of Muhammad is part of a long, dark tradition of rejecting warners. They are not unique. They are not special. They are walking a well-trodden path that leads to a well-known destination. The believer, reading this, understands that the root of all societal destruction is the rejection of divine guidance. Specific sins may vary, but the underlying rebellion is the same. The verse is a sombre introduction to a story that will detail one of the most dramatic and unique punishments in the history of divine retribution. The listener is prepared for another variation on the theme of warning and punishment, but the specific form of the punishment of Lot’s people will provide new dimensions of horror and lessons.

=34=

The punishment of Lot’s people is described. Allah unleashed upon them a storm of stones.

The Arabic “inna arsalna ‘alayhim hasiban” indicates a violent shower of stones, a hailstorm of baked clay. This was not ordinary hail. The stones were heated, hard, and sent with precise aim. The storm was unrelenting, covering the entire area and destroying every single one of the criminals. The form of the punishment matched the nature of their crime. They had inverted the natural order, so a punishment rained down from the sky, turning the very earth upside down as well, as the Quran mentions elsewhere. The unleashing of the stones was a manifestation of divine wrath, a deliberate and targeted act of destruction. Then, the verse immediately provides the crucial contrast: the family of Lot, the believers, were delivered.

The Arabic “illa ala Lutin najjaynahum” means that Lot and his household were saved. The exception is clearly stated. The punishment was not indiscriminate. The righteous were separated from the wicked.

This is a consistent principle in all the stories of destruction. Noah and the ark, Salih and the believers, and here Lot and his family, all were saved before the punishment descended.

The verse adds the specific timing: “before dawn.” The Arabic “bi sahar” refers to the last part of the night, the time just before the break of dawn. It was in the darkness before the first light that the believers were led out of the city to safety. This timing is significant. The punishment came with the morning, as the next verses will mention. The believers were rescued in the quiet, dark hours, while the criminals slept, unaware that their doom was moments away.

This is a lesson in the way Allah’s help arrives. Often, deliverance comes in the darkest hour, when hope seems thinnest. The believer is reminded that the mercy of Allah operates with perfect timing, separating the righteous from the wicked with precision that no human judge could achieve.

=35=

The deliverance of Lot and his family is further explained: it was a blessing from Allah. The Arabic “ni’matan min ‘indina” means a favour, a grace, a gift directly from Us. The believers did not save themselves through their own cunning or strength. Their salvation was an act of divine generosity. The verse frames this deliverance as part of a general divine principle: “This is how We reward whoever gives thanks.

” The Arabic “kadhalika najzi man shakar” establishes a direct link between gratitude and reward. Lot and his family were grateful to Allah. They believed in Him, worshipped Him, and followed His messenger. Their gratitude was not just a verbal expression but a life lived in obedience.

Therefore, Allah rewarded them with salvation from the most horrific punishment. The concept of shukr, gratitude, is central to the Quranic worldview. The opposite of gratitude is kufr, which is also the word for disbelief. The disbelievers are those who are ungrateful for the blessings of Allah. The believers are those who recognise the blessings, attribute them to Allah, and use them in His service. The deliverance of Lot’s family is presented as a reward for their gratitude. This teaches the believer that gratitude is not a passive emotion; it is an active force that attracts more blessings and provides protection from calamity. When a believer thanks Allah sincerely, especially in times of difficulty, they are aligning themselves with the divine current of mercy.

The verse also serves as a reassurance to the believers in Mecca. They were a small, persecuted minority, surrounded by a hostile majority. The story of Lot tells them that no matter how small the group of the grateful, Allah’s reward for them is certain and complete. They should continue to be grateful, continue to worship, continue to obey, and their deliverance will come, just as it came for Lot’s family in the darkness before dawn. The verse transforms the historical narrative into a timeless promise.

=36=

This verse adds a crucial detail about the events leading to the destruction of Lot’s people. Lot had already warned them of Allah’s crushing blow.

The Arabic “wa laqad andharahum batshatana” means that Lot explicitly warned them of the severity and power of divine seizure. The term “batsha” refers to a sudden, violent seizing, a crushing grip. Lot did not merely give vague spiritual advice. He warned them of a specific, terrible punishment that would befall them if they did not repent. He used strong language, invoking the fear of Allah’s might.

This warning was an act of mercy, giving them every opportunity to change. Yet their response was to dispute the warnings.

The Arabic “fa tamaraw bi an-nudhur” means they argued about them, they cast doubt upon them, they engaged in intellectual and verbal gymnastics to dismiss them. They did not simply ignore Lot; they actively disputed his message, probably mocking the idea that stones could rain from the sky or that their cities could be overturned.

This is the same behaviour exhibited by the Quraysh, who disputed the Quranic warnings, asking when the punishment would come and calling it old magic. The verse highlights the tragedy of a people who are warned clearly and directly, and who use the gift of speech and reason not to accept the warning but to argue against it. Dispute becomes a tool of self-destruction. The believer learns from this that when a clear warning from Allah reaches the heart, the proper response is not to argue or to rationalise it away but to accept it with humility and to change one’s behaviour. The story of Lot shows that the time for dispute eventually runs out. When the crushing blow comes, the arguments evaporate, and all that remains is the terrible reality they denied. The verse is a call to take the warnings of the Quran with the utmost seriousness, bypassing the impulse to dispute and heading directly to the response of obedience.

=37=

This verse describes the specific event that sealed the fate of Lot’s people. The angels had come to Lot in the form of handsome young men. Lot received them with hospitality and protected them in his home. The wicked people of the city, upon hearing of the visitors, rushed to Lot’s house and demanded that he hand the guests over to them for their evil purposes.

The Arabic “wa laqad rawaduhu ‘an dayfihi” means they sought to seduce or forcefully take his guests. This was the ultimate act of depravity. They had reached a level where they would violate even the most sacred laws of hospitality and morality. They saw no shame in publicly demanding the guests for their sinful acts. At this moment, the angels revealed their true nature to Lot and reassured him. Then Allah blinded their eyes.

The Arabic “fa tamasna a’yunahum” means We wiped out their sight, We sealed their eyes. The exact nature of this blinding is a miracle. Some reports say the angels struck their faces, causing them to lose their sight, and they stumbled around, unable to find the door. The physical blindness was a fitting punishment for their spiritual blindness. They could not see the truth of Lot’s message, so their physical sight was taken as an immediate prelude to the greater punishment to come. After they were blinded, they were told, “Taste then My punishment and warnings! ” This is the first time in the surah that the deniers are directly addressed with these words during the punishment itself. The taste of punishment is no longer a distant threat; it is a present reality. The command to “taste” is deeply experiential. The punishment is not just a concept; it is a flavour, a sensation that fills their entire being.

For the believer, this verse is a terrifying illustration of how close punishment can be. The people of Lot moved from demanding the guests to blindness to total destruction in a single night. There was no long delay. The divine response was swift and decisive. The lesson is clear: when sin reaches a certain level of brazenness, the punishment can descend with shocking speed.

=38=

The timeline of the punishment is now specified. The events of the previous verse, the blinding and the warning to taste the punishment, occurred at night. Then, by the early morning, the final, total destruction came.

The Arabic “wa laqad sabbahahum bukratan” means the morning came upon them, and with it, the punishment that settled and remained. The word “bukrah” refers to the early part of the day, the time just after dawn. The punishment was an “adhab mustaqirr,” an unrelenting, established torment that took hold of them permanently. This was not a temporary disaster that they could recover from. It was a terminal punishment that ended their existence in this world and transferred them to the punishment of the Hereafter. The contrast between the night and the morning is stark. The night was filled with the crime and the blinding. The morning brought the final cataclysm. The people of the city probably woke up thinking it was a normal day, but for them, the sun rose on a scene of utter devastation. The stones rained down, and the cities were flipped. The torment settled upon them and never lifted.

The word “mustaqirr” echoes the earlier verse about every matter being settled. The matter of Lot’s people was settled on that morning. Their long record of sin, their rejection of the warnings, their assault on the honour of the guests, all was settled in one devastating morning.

For the believer, this verse instils a profound sense of the suddenness of divine punishment. A person can go to sleep in sin and wake up in the grip of torment. The only safety is to have one’s record settled in a state of repentance and faith before the morning of punishment arrives.

The verse also highlights the mercy shown to Lot and the believers, who were far away by that morning, safe and witnessing the smoke of the destruction from a distance. The same morning that brought death to the wicked brought freedom to the righteous.

=39=

The command to taste is repeated, but with a subtle and significant variation. In verse 37, the command was given after the blinding: “Taste then My punishment and warnings!

” The word “then” implied a sequence. Now, after the full destruction has occurred, the command is given again: “Taste now My punishment and warnings! ” The repetition emphasises that the tasting is an ongoing experience. The punishment is not a one-time event. It continues, from the immediate blinding through the destruction and into the eternal torment of the Hereafter. The deniers are being made to taste the full, cumulative consequence of their rejection.

The use of “My punishment and My warnings” together is a powerful rhetorical device. The warnings and the punishment are paired. The very warnings they mocked have now become part of the punishment, because they will remember those warnings forever as the missed opportunities for salvation. Every moment of their suffering will be accompanied by the bitter memory of Lot’s voice calling them to safety, and their own laughter in response. The command “Taste!” is an address from a position of absolute authority and justice. It is a declaration that the punishment is deserved, that it is the direct result of their own choices. There is no room for appeal.

For the believer, this repeated command is one of the most chilling parts of the surah. It is a reminder that the words of the Quran are not just information; they are a living address. The same Lord who commanded the people of Lot to taste the punishment is the Lord who speaks to us through these verses. The believer hears this and prays for protection from ever hearing those words addressed to them.

The repetition also serves to reinforce the theme of the surah: the warnings have been delivered, the punishment is real, and the only way to avoid tasting it is to taste the sweetness of faith and obedience now, in this life, while the door of mercy is still open.

=40=

The refrain returns for the fourth time, after the harrowing account of Lot’s people. The placement is again an act of profound mercy. The listener has just heard about the storm of stones, the blinding, the unrelenting torment, and the command to taste the punishment. The heart is trembling. The mind is picturing the terror. And then, immediately, the Quran offers the balm and the solution: the reminder has been made easy. This refrain, appearing after each story of destruction, is the consistent lifeline. It says to the listener: you have seen what happens to those who reject the warnings. Now, will you use the tool I have given you to avoid that fate? The Quran is easy to remember. Its stories, like the ones you have just heard, are vivid and unforgettable. Its lessons are clear. Its guidance is practical. There is no excuse for heedlessness.

The question “So is there anyone who will be mindful?” is asked again, and with each repetition, the urgency and the pathos of the question increase. It is as if a merciful teacher, having shown a student a series of catastrophic failures, turns to the student and asks, with sincere concern, “Will you not learn from their mistakes? ” The word “muddakir” is related to dhikr, remembrance. The Quran is the ultimate reminder. To be mindful is to remember Allah, remember the Hereafter, remember the stories of the past, and let that remembrance transform one’s actions. The believer, hearing this refrain for the fourth time, feels the weight of the invitation. They renew their commitment to be among the mindful, to not let the Quran be mere words that pass through the ears but a living reality that shapes the life. The refrain is a pause, a deep breath, a moment of decision, before the surah continues to the next example, the people of Pharaoh, and then to the direct address to the Quraysh, driving the message home with relentless power.
