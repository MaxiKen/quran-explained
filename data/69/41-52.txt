=41=

This verse begins the refutation of the specific accusations leveled against the Prophet: “And it is not the word of a poet — little is what you believe.” The disbelievers, unable to deny the eloquence and power of the Qur'an, sought to dismiss it by claiming that the Prophet was a poet and that the Qur'an was merely poetry. The verse categorically denies this. The Qur'an is not poetry, and its messenger is not a poet, for the source, the purpose, and the nature of the two are utterly different.

The verse points to the profound difference between poetry and revelation. Poetry, whatever its beauty, is the product of the human imagination, reflecting the moods, desires, and experiences of its author. It speaks of the fleeting and the subjective, and it aims to move the emotions. The Qur'an, by contrast, is the word of the Creator of the universe, conveying eternal truths, moral guidance, and the knowledge of the unseen. Its purpose is not to entertain but to transform, to guide humanity from darkness into light. The two are of a completely different order.

The phrase “little is what you believe” is a rebuke to the disbelievers for the weakness of their faith. They had been given abundant signs and clear arguments, yet they persisted in denial. Their rejection was not based on genuine doubt but on obstinacy and the refusal to accept the implications of the truth. The Qur'an laments that their belief is so little, so shallow, and so easily overturned by the whisperings of their own desires.

This verse is an affirmation of the unique nature of the Qur'an. It is not a work of literature to be appreciated and then set aside, but a divine address to be received with reverence, reflected upon with humility, and acted upon with devotion. The believer's relationship to the Qur'an is fundamentally different from the reader's relationship to a poem, for the Qur'an is the living word of Allah, speaking to every generation and every soul.

=42=

This verse refutes the second accusation made against the Prophet: “Nor is it the word of a soothsayer — little is what you remember.” The disbelievers, in their attempt to explain away the Qur'an, also claimed that the Prophet was a fortune-teller and that the Qur'an was the kind of mysterious, rhyming speech uttered by the soothsayers of Arabia. The verse denies this outright. The Qur'an is not the word of a soothsayer, and its message has nothing in common with the mumblings of those who claimed knowledge of the unseen through the jinn.

The difference between the Qur'an and the speech of soothsayers is vast. The soothsayer's words were vague, ambiguous, and designed to impress and manipulate, offering glimpses of the future in exchange for payment. The Qur'an is clear, coherent, and uncompromising in its call to truth, and it came to liberate humanity from superstition and falsehood, not to exploit it. It condemns the very practice of soothsaying and calls people to the worship of Allah alone, who alone knows the unseen.

The phrase “little is what you remember” parallels the rebuke of the previous verse, but with a subtle difference. The disbelievers did not believe, and they did not remember. Their denial was compounded by their heedlessness — they neither accepted the truth nor even reflected upon it. The Qur'an laments that their remembrance, like their belief, is so little, so fleeting, and so easily distracted by the concerns of this world.

This verse, together with the previous one, defends the honor of the Prophet and the divine origin of the Qur'an against every accusation. The message is clear: the Qur'an is neither poetry nor soothsaying, and its messenger is neither a poet nor a fortune-teller. It is the pure, unadulterated word of Allah, and it deserves to be received as such. The believer is reminded to approach the Qur'an with the respect due to the speech of the Lord of the worlds.

=43=

This verse delivers the definitive answer to all the accusations and doubts: the Qur'an is “a revelation from the Lord of the worlds.” This single sentence resolves the entire question of the Qur'an's origin. It is not from a poet, a soothsayer, or any human source. It is a tanzīl — a sending down, a revelation — from the Lord of all that exists. The word tanzīl emphasizes the divine descent of the Qur'an, its origin in the heights of divine majesty and its transmission down to humanity through the noble Messenger.

The title “Lord of the worlds” is significant. It establishes the absolute sovereignty and authority of the One who revealed the Qur'an. The One who created the heavens and the earth, who sustains every living thing, who holds the destinies of all nations in His hand — it is He who has sent this Book. Its authority is therefore absolute, its truth beyond question, and its guidance binding upon all humanity. The Qur'an is not one voice among many but the definitive word of the ultimate Sovereign.

The verse is the climax of the passage's argument. The oaths by the seen and the unseen, the affirmation of the noble Messenger, and the refutation of poetry and soothsaying all lead to this single, powerful conclusion: this is revelation from the Lord of the worlds. Everything else in the passage serves this central truth. Once this is established, the obligation to believe and to follow is inescapable.

This verse is the foundation of the believer's relationship to the Qur'an. Because it is from the Lord of the worlds, the believer receives it with total trust, submits to its guidance without reservation, and holds it as the ultimate criterion of truth. The believer's entire life is oriented around this revelation, for it is the word of the One who created them, who knows them, and who alone can guide them to what is best in this world and the next.

=44=

This verse introduces a powerful argument for the divine origin of the Qur'an by way of a hypothetical: “And if he had fabricated against Us some sayings.” The “he” refers to the Prophet, peace be upon him, and the verse considers the possibility — purely hypothetical — that the Prophet had invented something and attributed it to Allah. The very framing of this hypothesis is a testament to the Qur'an's confidence in its own truth, for it is willing to entertain the accusation in order to refute it decisively.

The verse speaks of “fabricating against Us,” that is, against Allah. This framing underscores the gravity of the hypothetical act. To attribute words to Allah that He did not speak is the most serious of offenses, a crime against the divine majesty itself. The Qur'an elsewhere declares that if the Prophet were to invent anything in Allah's name, he would be seized with punishment. The verse thus establishes the impossibility of such a thing, not by assertion alone but by describing the inevitable consequence that would follow.

The hypothetical also serves to refute the disbelievers' claim that the Prophet had fabricated the Qur'an. If he had truly invented it and attributed it to Allah, then Allah would have punished him, as the following verses describe. But the Prophet was not punished; rather, he was supported, protected, and vindicated throughout his life, and the Qur'an remained intact. This is presented as proof that the Qur'an is genuinely from Allah, for a fabricated scripture could not have endured and its fabricator could not have escaped divine retribution.

This verse is a powerful example of the Qur'an's method of argumentation. It confronts the doubts of the skeptics directly, entertains their accusations, and refutes them with reason and with reference to the divine justice. The believer is thus equipped with certainty, and the skeptic is left without excuse, for the truth has been demonstrated from every angle.

=45=

This verse describes the first part of the punishment that would have befallen the Prophet had he fabricated anything in Allah's name: “We would have seized him by the right hand.” The image is one of immediate, powerful, and inescapable seizure. The right hand, associated with strength and authority, is seized to render the person completely helpless. There would be no opportunity to resist, no chance to flee, and no possibility of escape. The punishment would be swift and decisive, commensurate with the gravity of the crime.

The seizure by the right hand is understood by the scholars to be a figurative expression of the exercise of divine power, conveying the idea that the culprit would be grasped with overwhelming force and rendered powerless. It is the gesture of one who seizes another to execute judgment upon them, a prelude to the severing described in the next verse. The image is vivid and terrifying, leaving no doubt about the certainty and severity of the divine response to such a crime.

The verse underscores a fundamental principle: the Prophet was not free to say whatever he wished in Allah's name. He was a Messenger, entrusted with a message, and his entire authority rested upon his faithful transmission of that message. The notion that he could fabricate revelation and escape punishment is unthinkable, for Allah protects His revelation and holds His messengers to the highest standard of fidelity. The Prophet's integrity is thus guaranteed by the very nature of his office.

This verse, together with the next, serves to convince the believer of the absolute reliability of the Qur'an. If the Prophet could not have fabricated it without being seized by Allah, and if he was not seized, then the Qur'an is authentically from Allah. This argument from divine protection is a powerful source of certainty for the believer, grounding their trust in the revelation in the wisdom and power of the One who sent it.

=46=

This verse completes the description of the punishment that would have befallen a fabricator of revelation: “Then We would have cut from him the aorta.” The aorta, al-watīn, is the main artery that carries blood from the heart, and its severing means instant death. The image is of a swift, decisive, and fatal judgment — the fabricator would not merely be reprimanded but struck down at the very seat of his life. This is the fate that would inevitably have overtaken the Prophet had he invented anything in Allah's name, and the fact that he was not struck down is proof of his truthfulness.

The mention of the aorta specifically may also carry a subtle irony. The disbelievers had accused the Prophet of fabricating the Qur'an, and some had even suggested that his claim to revelation was a matter of the heart, a kind of internal impulse. The Qur'an responds that if this were a fabrication originating from within him, the very source of that impulse — the heart and its vessels — would have been cut off. The verse thus answers their accusation with a striking image that turns their own logic against them.

The severing of the aorta is a reminder of the seriousness with which Allah guards His revelation. The word of Allah is not a matter to be trifled with, and the one who would presume to speak falsely in His name invites destruction upon himself. This principle applies not only to the Prophet but to all who would distort or fabricate in the name of religion. The sanctity of divine truth demands absolute fidelity from all who convey it.

This verse, with its vivid and memorable image, drives home the certainty of the Qur'an's authenticity. The Prophet lived and died, not struck down by Allah but honored and protected, his message spreading and enduring. This is presented as undeniable evidence that he was no fabricator, but the faithful Messenger of Allah, conveying a revelation whose truth is beyond all doubt.

=47=

This verse concludes the hypothetical scenario with a decisive statement: “And there is none of you who could have prevented it from him.” Having described how a fabricator would have been seized and struck down, the Qur'an now declares that no one among the Prophet's companions, supporters, or the whole of humanity could have shielded him from that punishment. Had Allah willed to seize His servant, no power in the universe could have intervened. The verse thus establishes both the certainty of the punishment for fabrication and the utter powerlessness of all creation before the divine will.

The verse addresses the disbelievers and all who doubt, challenging them to consider: if the Prophet had truly fabricated the Qur'an, who could have saved him from Allah's punishment? The answer, obvious to all, is no one. The fact that he lived out his life protected and victorious, conveying the message with unwavering consistency, is therefore a sign that he was under divine protection — a protection granted only to those who speak the truth on behalf of their Lord.

The verse also reinforces the theme of divine sovereignty that runs throughout this passage. Allah is the Lord of the worlds, the revealer of the Qur'an, and the guardian of His own word. His power is absolute, and His judgment cannot be deflected. The one who is true to Him is under His care, while the one who fabricates against Him has no refuge. This is a source of immense comfort to the believer, who knows that the message they follow is protected by the power that sustains the universe.

This verse completes the argument for the authenticity of the Qur'an with unassailable logic. The Qur'an is not the word of a poet or a soothsayer, but a revelation from the Lord of the worlds, conveyed by a noble Messenger whom Allah protected from any possibility of fabrication. The believer's certainty is thus grounded not in blind faith but in the manifest signs of divine protection that accompanied the Prophet throughout his mission.

=48=

This verse states the purpose of the Qur'an: “And indeed, it is a reminder for the God-fearing.” The Qur'an is here described as tadhkirah, a reminder, a counsel, and an admonition. Its function is to remind human beings of what they already know deep within their souls — the truth of their Lord, the reality of their purpose, and the certainty of their return to Him. The Qur'an does not impose alien truths but awakens the innate knowledge that Allah has placed within every heart, calling it back to life after heedlessness has buried it.

The verse specifies that the Qur'an is a reminder “for the God-fearing” (al-muttaqīn). This does not mean that others cannot benefit from it, but that its guidance is received fully only by those who possess taqwā — the awareness of Allah that produces care and caution in one's deeds. The God-fearing are those whose hearts are open, who approach the Qur'an with humility, and who allow its reminders to transform them. For such people, every verse is a fresh reminder that deepens their awareness and strengthens their resolve.

The description of the Qur'an as a reminder is significant because it captures the essence of revelation. Human beings are prone to forgetfulness — they forget their Lord, their purpose, and their destination. The Qur'an is the antidote to this forgetfulness, repeatedly calling humanity back to the truth. Its reminders are not redundant but necessary, for the heart, like a field, must be constantly tended, and the soul, like a traveler, must be constantly reoriented towards its destination.

This verse is an invitation to approach the Qur'an with the heart of the God-fearing, to read it not as a historical document but as a living reminder addressed to oneself. The believer is urged to make the Qur'an a constant companion, to recite it with reflection, and to allow its reminders to renew their faith day by day. For the Qur'an is, above all, a reminder, and the one who heeds it is guided to the straight path.

=49=

This verse acknowledges a sobering reality: “And indeed, We know that among you are deniers.” Having affirmed the truth of the Qur'an and its role as a reminder, Allah now addresses the mixed response it has received. Not everyone who hears the Qur'an accepts it. Some deny it, and Allah knows who they are. This knowledge is presented not as a threat but as a statement of fact, a recognition that the message has always divided humanity into believers and deniers, and that this division is part of the divine wisdom.

The verse is addressed to the Prophet's audience, but its message extends to every generation. Among those who hear the Qur'an, there will always be those who accept and those who reject. This is not a failure of the message but a consequence of human free will. The Qur'an is clear, but clarity does not compel acceptance, for belief is a matter of the heart's choice. Allah, who knows all that is in the hearts, knows precisely who believes and who denies, and His knowledge encompasses every soul.

The acknowledgment that some will deny is also a consolation to the Prophet and to the believers. The Prophet's mission was to convey the message, not to force acceptance. The presence of deniers does not diminish the truth of what he brought, nor does it mean his efforts were in vain. His duty was fulfilled, and the response of each soul is its own responsibility. The believer, likewise, is reminded not to despair over those who reject the truth, for guidance is in Allah's hand.

This verse teaches a balanced perspective on the reception of the truth. The believer is neither surprised nor discouraged by denial, knowing that it is part of the divine decree. At the same time, the believer remains committed to conveying the message with wisdom and patience, leaving the outcome to Allah. For Allah knows who among the people are deniers, and He knows who will respond to the truth, and His knowledge is complete and unfailing.

=50=

This verse describes the effect of the Qur'an upon the disbelievers: “And indeed, it is a regret for the disbelievers.” The word ḥasrah denotes deep sorrow, anguish, and remorse — the grief of one who has lost something of infinite value. For the disbelievers, the Qur'an will become a source of profound regret, because it is the very thing that testifies against them. They heard its message, recognized its truth at some level, and yet turned away. On the Day of Judgment, they will see what they have lost, and their regret will be complete.

The regret of the disbelievers has two dimensions. In this world, they may feel the sting of the Qur'an's exposure of their falsehood, its refutation of their arguments, and its call that disturbs their complacency. But the full measure of the regret is reserved for the Hereafter, when they will see the believers entering Paradise while they are driven to the Fire. On that Day, they will wish they had believed, wished they had followed the Messenger, and wished they had never denied the truth. But their wishing will avail them nothing.

The Qur'an is thus a two-edged reality: it is a reminder and a mercy for the God-fearing, and a regret for the disbelievers. The same verses that illuminate the hearts of the believers become, for the deniers, a witness against them. This is the nature of truth — it cannot be neutral. It either guides or it condemns, depending upon how it is received. The Qur'an is the clearest of all truths, and therefore the most decisive in its effect.

This verse is a warning and an invitation. It warns the disbelievers of the regret that awaits them if they persist in denial, and it invites them to avert that regret by accepting the truth while there is still time. For regret in this world can still be remedied through repentance, but regret in the Hereafter is eternal. The wise person heeds the Qur'an now, when heeding it can still change their destiny, rather than lamenting it later, when lament is of no use.

=51=

This verse reaches the summit of certainty: “And indeed, it is the truth of certainty.” The Qur'an is here described as ḥaqq al-yaqīn, the absolute, certain truth — truth of the highest and most unshakeable kind. The phrase conveys a certainty that admits of no doubt whatsoever, a truth so firmly established that it is the very standard by which all other claims are measured. The Qur'an is not merely true; it is truth itself, the ultimate reality from which all other realities derive their meaning.

The progression of this passage is striking. The Qur'an was described first as the word of a noble Messenger, then as a revelation from the Lord of the worlds, then as a reminder for the God-fearing, and now as the truth of certainty. Each description builds upon the last, culminating in this absolute affirmation. The reader is led step by step to the recognition that this Book is not one opinion among many but the definitive expression of reality, the word of the One who is the truth itself.

The certainty of the Qur'an is grounded in its source. It comes from Allah, who is al-Ḥaqq, the Truth, and who knows all things with perfect knowledge. Whatever He reveals is therefore certain, beyond the reach of error or falsehood. The believer's certainty in the Qur'an is not a leap in the dark but a resting upon the most solid of foundations — the truthfulness of Allah Himself. This is why the believer's faith can withstand every doubt and every trial, for it is anchored in that which cannot be shaken.

This verse is an invitation to the deepest level of faith, the certainty that transforms the heart and governs the life. The believer who attains certainty in the Qur'an is freed from the anxiety of doubt and the instability of wavering. They know, with a knowledge that penetrates to the core of their being, that the Qur'an is the truth of certainty, and this knowledge becomes the compass of their entire existence.

=52=

This final verse of the sûrah issues its concluding command: “So glorify the name of your Lord, the Most Great.” Having established the truth of the Qur'an with absolute certainty, the sûrah now directs the believer to the natural response to that truth: the glorification of Allah. The word tasbīḥ means to declare Allah free from all imperfection, to exalt Him above all that is unworthy of His majesty, and to honor His name with praise. It is the worship that springs spontaneously from the recognition of who Allah is.

The description of Allah as “the Most Great” (al-ʿAẓīm) echoes the earlier mention of the disbeliever's failure to believe in “Allah, the Most Great.” The sûrah thus concludes where its message reached its height, calling the believer to do what the disbeliever refused to do: acknowledge the greatness of Allah. The glorification of the name of the Most Great is the opposite of the arrogance that led the disbeliever to ruin, and it is the mark of the believer's submission and love.

The command to glorify Allah is also the appropriate conclusion to a sûrah that has emphasized the reality of the Hereafter and the certainty of the revelation. When a believer reflects upon the Day of Judgment, upon the fate of the two parties, and upon the truth of the Qur'an, the overwhelming response is the glorification of Allah. The heart that has taken all of this in cannot but burst into praise of the One who created, revealed, and will judge. Worship is the fruit of knowledge, and this sûrah leads its reader to that fruit.

This verse is a fitting end to Sûrah Al-Ḥāqqah, bringing its themes to their resolution in worship. The believer is left with the command to glorify the name of their Lord, the Most Great, to fill their life with His remembrance, and to live in the awareness of the realities this sûrah has so powerfully conveyed. It is an ending that is also a beginning, for the glorification of Allah is the essence of the believer's ongoing journey towards their Lord.
