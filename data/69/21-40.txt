=21=

This verse begins the description of the reward that awaits the one who receives their record in the right hand: “So he will be in a pleasant life.” The phrase “pleasant life” (īshatin rāḍiyah) describes a life that is completely satisfying — a state of being in which the person is fully content, where every desire is met, every joy is complete, and nothing is lacking. It is the opposite of the anxiety, dissatisfaction, and restlessness that characterize so much of human experience in this world, where even the most fortunate are haunted by unfulfilled longings and the fear of loss.

What makes this life pleasant is not merely its external comforts but its perfect harmony with the soul. In this world, a person may possess wealth, health, and status, yet remain inwardly miserable. In Paradise, the inner state and the outer environment are in perfect accord. The believer's heart, purified of envy, greed, fear, and sorrow, finds itself in surroundings that answer its every pure desire. There is no conflict between what one wants and what one has, no gap between hope and fulfillment.

The word rāḍiyah also carries the connotation of mutual satisfaction — a life that is pleasing, and a person who is pleased. This points to the deeper reality that the believer has earned the pleasure of Allah, and this is the source of all contentment. When Allah is pleased with a servant, that servant's life, in this world and the next, becomes truly pleasant. The believer's contentment is the reflection of Allah's contentment with them.

This verse is a glimpse of the ultimate success that faith leads to. It reminds the believer that the trials, sacrifices, and acts of self-restraint of this life are the price of an eternal life of pure contentment. What the believer gives up in this fleeting world is as nothing compared to what they receive in the Hereafter — a life so pleasant that no eye has seen it, no ear has heard it, and no human heart has imagined it.

=22=

This verse adds the second element of the believer's reward: “in an elevated Garden.” Paradise is here described as jannah ʿāliyah, a Garden that is lofty and exalted. The elevation is both physical and spiritual. Physically, the Gardens of Paradise are raised in tiers, one above another, with the highest levels reserved for those nearest to Allah. Spiritually, the elevation signifies honor, dignity, and the raising of the believer to a station of closeness to their Lord that surpasses anything achievable in this world.

The pairing of “pleasant life” with “elevated Garden” paints a complete picture of Paradise. The pleasant life speaks to the inner state of contentment, while the elevated Garden speaks to the outer reality of beauty and honor. Together they show that Paradise satisfies both the heart and the senses, both the soul's longing for peace and its longing for splendor. The believer is not given a bare sufficiency but an overflowing abundance, a home whose beauty increases without end.

The elevation of Paradise also carries a message of encouragement. In this world, the believer is often looked down upon, dismissed as naive or backward for their faith. The Qur'an repeatedly consoles the believers by reminding them that true honor belongs to the Hereafter, where the scales of this world are overturned. The one who was humble for the sake of Allah is raised, while the one who was arrogant is brought low. The elevated Garden is the reward of those who lowered themselves in worship.

This verse, brief as it is, encapsulates the destination of the righteous: an elevated Garden, lofty in rank and beautiful beyond description, where the believer dwells in the company of the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous — and what an excellent company they are. It is a promise that gives the believer strength to persevere through the humiliations and hardships of this world.

=23=

This verse describes one of the delights of the elevated Garden: “Its fruits are near at hand.” The phrase conveys the idea that the fruits of Paradise hang low and within easy reach, so that the believer may pluck them without effort, whether standing, sitting, or reclining. There is no labor, no ladder, no thorn, and no toil. Every fruit is offered freely and generously, a symbol of the complete ease that characterizes life in Paradise, where hardship has been permanently lifted.

The accessibility of Paradise's fruits is often understood by the scholars to mean that its blessings are not restricted to a few. In this world, the best things are often scarce, guarded, and available only to a privileged minority. In Paradise, abundance is the norm, and every inhabitant enjoys the fruits of the Garden without competition, without rationing, and without the fear of depletion. The generosity of Allah towards His servants in the Hereafter is limitless, and the fruits near at hand are but one expression of that generosity.

The nearness of the fruits also points to the immediacy of joy in Paradise. In this world, pleasure is always delayed, mediated, and interrupted — one must wait, work, and struggle for every satisfaction, and even then it is fleeting. In Paradise, desire and fulfillment meet instantly. The believer has only to wish, and the object of the wish is present. This is part of the meaning of the divine promise that the people of Paradise will have therein whatever they desire.

This image, drawn from the orchards and gardens familiar to the Arabs, is a way of making the unseen delights of Paradise comprehensible to human imagination. It assures the believer that the Garden is not an abstract spiritual state but a real, tangible, and beautiful place, filled with sensory delights that surpass anything known in this world. The fruits near at hand are a promise of ease, abundance, and immediate joy that awaits those who enter it.

=24=

This verse records the gracious invitation that will be extended to the people of Paradise: “Eat and drink in satisfaction for what you sent ahead in the days that have passed.” The invitation to eat and drink in contentment is the highest expression of hospitality, and here it is Allah Himself, the Lord of the worlds, who extends it to His servants. The word hanī'an describes food and drink that is wholesome, delightful, and free from any harm or consequence — the perfect enjoyment that leaves no regret and no ill effect, unlike the pleasures of this world which are always mixed with some cost.

The phrase “for what you sent ahead in the days that have passed” is the key to the verse. It refers to the good deeds the believer performed during their earthly life, which are now returning to them as eternal reward. This world is the field in which the seeds of the Hereafter are sown, and the days of life are the currency with which the believer purchases Paradise. Every act of worship, every charity, every kindness, and every act of self-restraint was something “sent ahead” to be enjoyed now, in the life that never ends.

The verse teaches a profound truth about the relationship between this world and the next. Nothing is lost. The believer who gives in the way of Allah is not depriving themselves but investing. The sacrifices made for the sake of Allah are not losses but deposits, stored with the most trustworthy of guardians, to be returned multiplied beyond measure. The people of Paradise eat and drink now because they gave in the days that have passed, and their enjoyment is the harvest of their earlier sowing.

This is also a message of encouragement and consolation for the believer in this life. The difficulty of obedience, the restraint of desire, and the patience under trial all have an end, and that end is this divine invitation. The believer who feels the weight of the struggle should look forward to the day when they will be told to eat and drink in satisfaction, and the memory of their past efforts will sweeten every delight.

=25=

The Qur'an now turns to the opposite case, describing the one who is given his record in his left hand. In stark contrast to the joy of the believer, this person cries out in bitter regret: “I wish I had not been given my record!” The left hand, associated in Arab culture with dishonor and misfortune, signals the failure of a life. The record that is now placed in his hand is a complete account of his sins, his denials, and his wasted opportunities, and he wishes desperately that it had never come into his hands.

His cry reveals the depth of his despair. In this world, such a person did not want to be reminded of accountability. He turned away from the warnings, mocked the idea of a reckoning, and lived as though his deeds would never be brought to light. Now, on the Day when all is revealed, he can no longer ignore the truth. The record is in his hand, undeniable and complete, and he wishes he could escape its testimony. But the time for turning away has passed.

The contrast between this verse and the earlier description of the believer is deliberate and devastating. The believer cried, “Here, read my record!” with joy, while the disbeliever cries, “I wish I had not been given my record!” with despair. The same record — the book of deeds — is a source of honor for one and of humiliation for the other. The difference lies entirely in what each one wrote during the days of life, and in the awareness with which each one lived.

This verse is a stark warning to every human being. The record of our lives is being written whether we acknowledge it or not. The question is not whether we will receive it, but how we will receive it. The wise person lives now in a way that will make the receiving of the record a moment of joy rather than regret, so that on that Day they may cry out in happiness rather than wish they had never been handed the truth.

=26=

The despair of the one given his record in the left hand deepens in this verse, as he laments: “And I did not know what my account was.” In this world, he lived without awareness of the reckoning, without ever stopping to consider the consequences of his deeds. He did not know, or refused to know, that every action was being recorded and that a day would come when he would be called to answer for it all. Now, faced with the reality he ignored, he wishes he had remained ignorant — for the knowledge that now floods upon him brings nothing but terror.

The lament “I did not know my account” is not an excuse but a confession. It is the admission of a life lived in heedlessness, a life in which the Hereafter played no part in shaping decisions. Such a person may have been intelligent, educated, and worldly-wise, yet they were ignorant of the one knowledge that truly mattered: the knowledge of their Lord and the reality of the day of return. This is the ignorance that the Qur'an warns against, the blindness of the heart that sees the world but cannot see the truth.

The verse teaches that true knowledge is not the accumulation of information but the awareness of one's ultimate purpose and destiny. A person may know a great deal about the world and yet know nothing of what matters most. The believer is distinguished not by worldly knowledge but by the knowledge of Allah and the awareness of accountability, which illuminate every other kind of knowledge and direct it towards good.

This verse is an invitation to wakefulness. It calls upon the reader to acquire now the knowledge that the disbeliever acquires too late, to live with awareness of the reckoning while that awareness can still change the course of a life. For once the record is in hand, knowledge comes too late, and the cry “I did not know my account” is heard only as a futile expression of eternal regret.

=27=

This verse continues the anguished cries of the one who receives his record in the left hand: “I wish that it had been the final end.” The “it” refers to death, and the wish is that death had been the absolute conclusion, with no resurrection, no reckoning, and no afterlife. This is the most despairing wish imaginable, for it reveals a person so terrified by what awaits them that they would prefer total annihilation to continued existence. They now long for the very thing they once doubted — the permanent end of being — because they cannot bear what is before them.

The verse is profoundly ironic. In this world, the disbelievers often dismissed the afterlife as impossible, insisting that death is the end and there is nothing after it. Some even longed for death as an escape from suffering, believing it would bring oblivion. Now, faced with the reality of the Hereafter, they discover that death was not the end at all, and they wish it had been. The very belief they held in this world becomes the thing they desperately hope for in the next, but it is too late.

The suffering of the Hereafter is so severe that it makes non-existence seem desirable. This is the measure of the punishment that awaits the disbelievers, a punishment from which there is no escape and no relief. The verse thus conveys, with terrible clarity, the magnitude of the loss suffered by those who turn away from their Lord. They have lost not only Paradise but the possibility of any peace at all, and their existence itself has become a burden they wish to be rid of.

This verse is a warning of the utmost seriousness. It confronts every human being with the choice between a life that leads to eternal contentment and a life that leads to eternal regret. The wish for annihilation is the end of all hope, and it is the destination of those who die in heedlessness. The believer is urged to reflect upon it now, while the choice is still open.

=28=

The disbeliever's lament continues as he surveys the ruins of his worldly life: “My wealth has not availed me anything.” In this world, he had placed his trust in his wealth, believing that it would protect him, secure his future, and grant him whatever he desired. He spent his life accumulating it, and perhaps he used it to deny the truth, to oppress others, and to purchase the pleasures that distracted him from his Lord. Now, on the Day of Judgment, his wealth stands helpless before the reality of his reckoning, and he confesses its utter uselessness.

This confession is universal and timeless. Wealth is one of the greatest tests of human life, and one of the most common objects of misplaced trust. The Qur'an repeatedly warns that wealth and children are ornaments of the worldly life, and that they will avail nothing on the Day of Judgment except for the one who comes to Allah with a sound heart. The disbeliever's cry “my wealth has not availed me” is the painful discovery that everything he accumulated is now worthless, left behind in a world he has departed, unable to accompany him or to intercede for him.

The verse does not condemn wealth itself, for wealth can be a great good when earned lawfully and spent in the way of Allah. What is condemned is the attachment to wealth, the reliance upon it, and the pursuit of it to the neglect of the Hereafter. The believer who uses wealth as a means to please Allah will find it transformed into a source of reward. The disbeliever who made wealth his god will find it has abandoned him at the moment of his greatest need.

This verse is a reminder to weigh carefully where one places one's trust. Everything of this world will be left behind at death, and only the deeds done for the sake of Allah will remain. The wise person, therefore, does not make wealth their security but uses it to purchase what truly endures, so that on the Day of Judgment they may say, with the believer, that their efforts have availed them, rather than cry out that their wealth has availed them nothing.

=29=

The disbeliever continues his confession of loss with the words: “My authority has perished from me.” The word translated as “authority” (sulṭān) is rich in meaning, referring to power, dominion, influence, argument, and standing. In this world, the disbeliever had all of these. He wielded power over others, commanded respect, and used his position to reject the truth and to oppress those who called him to Allah. His authority seemed unassailable. Now, on the Day of Judgment, all of it has vanished, stripped away in an instant, and he stands before his Lord naked, powerless, and utterly alone.

The perishing of authority is part of the great reversal of the Day of Judgment. In this world, the arrogant are exalted and the humble are looked down upon. But on that Day, the scales are overturned. The tyrant who ruled with an iron fist becomes a helpless supplicant. The one who used his authority to deny the truth finds that his authority has no standing before the Lord of all worlds. All the hierarchies of this world collapse, and only one sovereignty remains — that of Allah, the One, the Irresistible.

The verse also reminds us that authority in this world is a trust and a test. It is given by Allah, and it will be taken away by Allah. The one who uses it for justice, mercy, and the service of truth will find it a source of honor in both worlds. The one who uses it for tyranny and the rejection of truth will find it a source of ruin. Power, like wealth, is temporary, and it is the deeds done with it that endure.

This verse, together with the preceding one, paints a complete picture of the disbeliever's bankruptcy. His wealth has failed him, and his authority has perished. Everything he relied upon in this world has abandoned him at the moment of reckoning. He is left with nothing but the record of his deeds, and that record condemns him. The believer, by contrast, invested in what cannot perish — faith and good deeds — and finds those investments intact and multiplied.

=30=

Having heard the disbeliever's cries of regret, the Qur'an now describes the command that will be given concerning him: “Seize him and shackle him.” This is the voice of divine authority addressing the angels of punishment, ordering them to take hold of the one who rejected his Lord and to bind him in chains. The sudden shift from the disbeliever's lament to this imperative is jarring and effective, conveying the swiftness and finality of the divine judgment. There is no appeal, no negotiation, and no escape — only the execution of a just sentence upon one who has earned it.

The command to seize and shackle reflects the reversal of the disbeliever's fortunes. In this world, he was free, moving about with arrogance and rejecting the truth without consequence. He imagined that his freedom was absolute and his power unchallengeable. Now he is seized and bound, unable to resist, and his former arrogance is replaced by utter helplessness. The hands that once reached for sin are now chained, and the feet that once walked proudly in defiance are now fettered.

The verse is a reminder that the justice of Allah is real and certain. The punishment described here is not arbitrary but the direct consequence of the disbeliever's own choices. He was given guidance and warning, and he rejected both. He was shown the truth and turned away. The shackling is the fruit of his own deeds, the harvest of a life of rebellion. The Qur'an emphasizes throughout that Allah wrongs no one; the people wrong themselves.

This verse, and the verses that follow, describe the punishment of the Hereafter in vivid, physical terms. These descriptions are not meant to be dismissed as metaphor but to be taken with the utmost seriousness as warnings of a real and terrible fate. They are also a mercy, for the warning itself is an opportunity to turn back before it is too late. The one who hears these words while still in this world is given the chance to avoid the seizure and the shackling by turning to Allah in repentance now.

=31=

The command of punishment continues: “Then drive him into the Blaze.” The word translated as “drive” (ṣallū) carries the sense of compelling or hurling forcefully, indicating that the disbeliever is not led gently but cast into the Fire against any resistance. After being seized and shackled, he is thrown into the blazing Fire of Hell, the punishment that awaits those who rejected their Lord and persisted in their rebellion. The image is one of overwhelming force and inescapable doom.

The mention of the Blaze (al-jaḥīm) is significant. It is one of the many names of Hell, emphasizing its fierce, raging heat. The Qur'an describes this Fire in terms that make it far more intense than any earthly fire: its fuel is men and stones, its flames leap up over the hearts, and its inhabitants beg for relief that never comes. The one who is driven into it enters a torment of such severity that the mind struggles to comprehend it, and the body cannot endure it, yet it never ends.

The sequence of the punishment — seizure, shackling, and then the Fire — reflects the comprehensive nature of divine justice. The disbeliever's crimes were many: he denied Allah, rejected the Messenger, and committed evil without restraint. His punishment is therefore layered, combining humiliation, physical restraint, and burning torment. Each element corresponds to an aspect of his rebellion, so that the punishment fits the crime with perfect justice.

This verse is a powerful call to heed the warning of the Qur'an before the opportunity passes. The Fire is real, and those who deny it are not escaping it but hastening towards it. The believer is urged to reflect upon these descriptions not with complacency but with the fear that produces action, seeking refuge in Allah from the Blaze and striving to be among those who are kept far from it.

=32=

This verse adds a further element to the punishment: “Then bind him with a chain whose length is seventy cubits.” The chain described here is of colossal size, far beyond anything known in this world, and it serves to convey the magnitude of the punishment prepared for the disbelievers. The number seventy is often used in Arabic and in the Qur'an to indicate a great multitude or an extreme degree, so the chain of seventy cubits symbolizes a binding of immense and terrifying scale, from which there is no possibility of escape.

The chain binds the disbeliever within the Fire, ensuring that he can neither flee nor move freely within his torment. Having been seized, shackled, and driven into the Blaze, he is now bound with this massive chain, so that his suffering is made complete and permanent. The image of the bound sinner within the Fire is one of utter helplessness, the final state of one who used his freedom in this world to defy his Creator.

The severity of this punishment is proportionate to the severity of the crime. The disbeliever rejected the truth of his Lord and the guidance of His Messenger, the greatest of all matters. He was given a lifetime of opportunities to turn back, and he squandered them all. The chain of seventy cubits is the material expression of the weight of his denial, a punishment whose enormity mirrors the enormity of his sin.

The vivid physicality of this description is a hallmark of the Qur'an's warnings about the Hereafter. It confronts the imagination with concrete images that leave no room for evasion. The believer is meant to feel the horror of the scene and to be moved to seek Allah's protection from it. These verses are thus not merely informational but transformative, intended to shake the heedless heart into awareness and to strengthen the resolve of the believer to remain upon the path of truth.

=33=

The Qur'an now states the reason for this terrible punishment, explaining the crime that earned it: “Indeed, he used not to believe in Allah, the Most Great.” The first and greatest sin is identified as the refusal to believe in Allah, the Most Great. This is the root of all other sins, for when a person loses faith in the Creator, they lose the foundation of all morality and the purpose of all existence. Every other evil flows from this first denial, and it is for this that the disbeliever is bound and cast into the Fire.

The description of Allah as “the Most Great” (al-ʿAẓīm) is significant in this context. The disbeliever's sin is precisely that he refused to acknowledge the greatness of his Lord. He made himself great, exalting his own desires, his own opinions, and his own authority above the authority of Allah. He lived as though there were no power greater than his own, no will more authoritative than his own. The punishment is the corrective to this arrogance, demonstrating in the most unmistakable way the greatness that he denied.

The verse also implies that the disbeliever's problem was not a lack of evidence but a lack of submission. The signs of Allah's existence and greatness are everywhere — in the heavens and the earth, in the creation of the human being, in the revelation of the Qur'an. The disbeliever saw these signs but refused to submit to them, because submission would have required abandoning his arrogance and his desires. His disbelief was thus not intellectual but moral, a rebellion of the will against the truth.

This verse is a reminder that the heart of the matter is faith in Allah. Everything else — the good deeds, the avoidance of sin, the hope for Paradise — rests upon this foundation. The believer is urged to renew and deepen their faith in Allah, the Most Great, to exalt Him in their heart and their life, and to beware of the arrogance that leads to denial. For it is the refusal to acknowledge Allah's greatness that leads, in the end, to the chain of seventy cubits and the Fire.

=34=

This verse identifies the second crime of the disbeliever: “Nor did he encourage the feeding of the poor.” This single shortcoming, mentioned alongside the refusal to believe in Allah, reveals the deep connection between faith and compassion. Belief in Allah naturally produces care for His creation, especially the most vulnerable. The one who truly believes in the Most Great recognizes that all provision comes from Him and that the wealth one holds is a trust to be shared with those in need. The disbeliever, having rejected Allah, also rejected this obligation, and his failure to feed the poor is the visible proof of his hardheartedness.

The verse specifically says he did not “encourage” the feeding of the poor, which is a subtle and significant point. It is not only that he himself failed to feed the poor, but that he did not urge others to do so either. He was indifferent to the hunger of others, and he discouraged, by his example and his attitude, any collective effort to alleviate it. This active indifference is singled out as a cause of his punishment, showing that in Islam, the neglect of the poor is not a minor omission but a grave sin that testifies against the one who commits it.

The mention of feeding the poor in the context of the disbeliever's punishment is a recurring theme in the Qur'an. In Sûrah Al-Ḥāqqah's companion passage, the people of the right hand ask the people of the left, “What has brought you into the Fire?” and they answer, among other things, “Nor did we feed the poor.” This theme emphasizes that the practical expression of faith is compassion, and that a faith that does not manifest in care for the needy is hollow.

This verse is a powerful admonition to the believer to be attentive to the needs of the poor. Feeding the hungry, clothing the naked, and caring for the orphan are among the dearest deeds to Allah, and their neglect is among the causes of ruin. The believer is urged not only to give but also to encourage giving, to be a voice for the needy, and to build communities in which no one is left to go hungry while others are able to help.

=35=

This verse declares the final isolation of the disbeliever: “So today he has no close friend here.” The word ḥamīm refers to an intimate, devoted friend — one who is close in affection and loyal in times of need. In this world, the disbeliever may have been surrounded by friends, allies, and associates, bound together by shared interests and pleasures. But on the Day of Judgment, all these bonds dissolve. He stands alone, with no friend to console him, no ally to defend him, and no companion to share his fate.

The verse reflects a fundamental truth about the Hereafter: every person will stand before Allah alone, responsible only for their own deeds. The friendships of this world, whatever their warmth, are temporary and conditional. They cannot transfer from this life to the next, and they cannot intercede for a person on the Day of Judgment. The Qur'an elsewhere describes how even the closest relationships will be severed on that Day, with the believer's own relatives fleeing from them in terror, and how the disbelievers will disown one another and turn on one another in mutual recrimination.

The loneliness of the disbeliever is contrasted with the blessed company of the believer. The believer, too, stands alone in their individual accountability, but they are surrounded by the mercy of Allah, the intercession of the Prophet, and the companionship of the righteous in Paradise. The disbeliever has none of this. He has severed his connection to Allah, and in doing so he has cut himself off from the only friendship that could have saved him. His earthly friends are of no use to him now, and he is utterly alone.

This verse is a reminder to choose one's companions wisely and to invest in relationships that endure. The friendship that matters is the friendship rooted in faith and in the love of Allah, for it is this that survives death and blossoms into the eternal companionship of Paradise. The believer is urged to befriend the righteous, to love for the sake of Allah, and to remember that on the Day of Judgment, the only abiding friendship will be the friendship of Allah and His beloved servants.

=36=

This verse describes the only “food” available to the disbeliever in the Fire: “Nor is there any food except from ghisleen.” The word ghisleen refers to the filth, discharge, and festering fluid that flows from the wounds of the inhabitants of Hell, mixed with the foulest of matter. This is the sustenance of the one who rejected his Lord, a “food” so repulsive that its very description is meant to horrify. It is the complete inversion of the wholesome food and drink that the believer enjoys in Paradise, described only a few verses earlier.

The contrast between the believer's feast and the disbeliever's ghisleen is deliberate and devastating. The believer is invited to “eat and drink in satisfaction” of the finest delights of Paradise, while the disbeliever is given nothing but the filth of Hell to consume. The same mouth that once consumed the lawful and unlawful pleasures of this world without gratitude now receives the repulsive nourishment of the Fire. The pleasure that was sought in defiance of Allah is repaid with a repulsiveness beyond description.

The verse also highlights the spiritual dimension of the punishment. The disbeliever's soul, in this world, fed upon the filth of sin — upon lies, injustice, and the gratification of base desires. In the Hereafter, this inner filth is made manifest in the outer reality of his punishment. He is given to consume the very thing his deeds produced. The ghisleen is the materialization of a life lived in moral decay, and the punishment is the perfect mirror of the crime.

This verse, like the others describing the punishment of Hell, is a warning of the utmost seriousness. It is meant to awaken the heedless and to strengthen the resolve of the believer to remain upon the path of purity. The believer is reminded that what one consumes in this world — both physically and spiritually — shapes one's destiny in the next, and that the choice between the delights of Paradise and the filth of Hell is made now, in the choices of every day.

=37=

This verse concludes the description of the disbeliever's food in Hell: “None will eat it except the sinners.” The word used for sinners, khāṭi'ūn, refers to those who deliberately committed sin, who knowingly transgressed the bounds set by Allah and persisted in their wrongdoing. This is not the accidental slip of the one who strives to do right, but the willful rebellion of the one who chose sin over obedience. It is for these, and these alone, that the repulsive food of Hell is prepared, and they are its only consumers.

The verse makes clear that the punishment is not arbitrary but is precisely matched to its recipients. The food of Hell is for the sinners, just as the food of Paradise is for the righteous. Each receives what their deeds have earned. This principle of perfect justice runs throughout the Qur'an: no one is wronged, and no one is punished except for what they themselves have done. The repulsiveness of the ghisleen is the just recompense for the repulsiveness of a life lived in willful sin.

The exclusivity of the statement — “none will eat it except the sinners” — is also a source of hope for the believer. It means that the one who sincerely repents, who turns back to Allah and reforms their life, is not counted among the khāṭi'ūn. The mercy of Allah is such that a sincere repentance can erase even a lifetime of sin, transforming the former sinner into one of the people of Paradise. The verse thus simultaneously warns the unrepentant and consoles the repentant.

This verse brings the description of the two destinies to its culmination. On one side stand the people of the right hand, feasting in an elevated Garden on fruits near at hand, greeted with peace. On the other side stand the sinners, bound in chains and fed on the filth of Hell. The choice between these two is offered to every human being, and the Qur'an has made the path to each unmistakably clear. It is for each soul to choose, while the choice still remains.

=38=

With this verse, the sûrah turns from the description of the reckoning to a powerful affirmation of the divine origin of the Qur'an: “So I swear by what you see.” The oath introduces a section that establishes the truth of the revelation and refutes the accusations of the disbelievers against it. The word “so” (falā) connects this oath to what has preceded: since the Day of Judgment is a reality, and since the destiny of every soul depends upon its response to the truth, the Qur'an now swears by the visible and the invisible to confirm the truth of its message.

The object of the oath — “what you see” — encompasses all that is visible to human perception: the heavens, the earth, the sun, the moon, the mountains, the oceans, and every sign of Allah's power and wisdom in creation. By swearing by the visible world, the Qur'an appeals to the evidence that is before every eye. The very universe that surrounds humanity is a witness to the existence and greatness of its Creator, and the one who reflects upon it cannot but recognize the truth of the revelation that calls to Him.

The oath is a characteristic device of the Qur'an, used to draw attention to the importance of the statement that follows and to invoke the testimony of creation itself. When Allah swears by His creation, it is a mark of the solemnity and certainty of what is being affirmed. The reader is meant to feel the weight of the declaration that follows, to recognize that it is not an ordinary claim but a truth established by the testimony of all that exists.

This verse, together with the next, frames the affirmation of the Qur'an's origin within the grandeur of creation. The visible world and the invisible world together bear witness to the truth, and the Qur'an, as the word of the Creator of both, is the most certain of all realities. The believer is invited to contemplate the signs around them and to allow that contemplation to deepen their certainty in the revelation.

=39=

This verse completes the oath begun in the previous one: “And what you do not see.” Having sworn by the visible world, the Qur'an now swears by the invisible — the realm of the angels, the unseen worlds, the realities of the Hereafter, and all that lies beyond the reach of human perception. This oath by the unseen is a profound assertion that reality is not limited to what the senses can grasp. There is a vast, hidden dimension of existence, known fully only to Allah, and it is as real and as certain as the world that is seen.

The pairing of the seen and the unseen encompasses the totality of existence. Everything that exists falls into one of these two categories: what human beings can perceive and what they cannot. By swearing by both, the Qur'an affirms that the whole of creation, visible and invisible, testifies to the truth of what is being revealed. The believer is reminded that their knowledge is partial, and that beyond the horizon of perception lies a reality of which the Qur'an speaks with authority.

The oath by the unseen is also a rebuke to the disbelievers who demanded to see proof of the Hereafter before believing. Their insistence that only what is visible can be real is exposed as narrow and arrogant, for the visible world itself testifies to the existence of a hidden order. The believer accepts the unseen not blindly but on the basis of the trustworthiness of the One who informs them of it, and on the basis of the signs in the visible world that point towards it.

This verse invites the reader to expand their understanding of reality. The life of this world is only a small portion of what exists, and the Hereafter, though unseen, is more real and more enduring than everything that is seen. The believer lives with this awareness, seeing the visible world as a sign pointing to the invisible, and the invisible as the ultimate home towards which all existence is moving.

This verse states the central affirmation of the passage: “Indeed, it is the word of a noble Messenger.” The “it” refers to the Qur'an, and the “noble Messenger” is understood by the majority of commentators to be the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, who conveyed the revelation to humanity. The verse affirms that the Qur'an is not the invention of the Prophet but the word of Allah conveyed through him, a message delivered faithfully by a Messenger of the highest honor and trustworthiness.

Some commentators understand the “noble Messenger” here to refer to the angel Gabriel, who brought the revelation from Allah to the Prophet. Both interpretations are valid and complementary, for the chain of revelation descends from Allah through Gabriel to the Prophet, and both are “noble messengers” in the delivery of the divine word. The essential point is the same: the Qur'an is not a human production but a divine message, conveyed through noble intermediaries of impeccable integrity.

The emphasis on the nobility of the Messenger is a refutation of the disbelievers' accusations. They had accused the Prophet of being a poet, a soothsayer, or a madman, and they alleged that the Qur'an was his own composition. The Qur'an responds by asserting the nobility and honor of the one who conveyed it, and by implication the baselessness of the accusations against him. A noble Messenger, entrusted by Allah with His word, does not fabricate or distort; he delivers faithfully.

This verse is a reminder of the sacredness of the Qur'an and the honor of those who carried it. The believer's reverence for the Qur'an is rooted in the recognition that it is the word of Allah, conveyed through His noble Messenger. This recognition demands not only belief but also gratitude, for the revelation is the greatest gift ever bestowed upon humanity, a light that guides from darkness to the path of the Most Praiseworthy.
