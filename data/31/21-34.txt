=21=

Building directly on the end of the previous verse, which condemned those who dispute about Allah without knowledge, this verse exposes the root of their stubbornness: blind adherence to ancestral tradition. When the polytheists are invited with a simple, clear call to “Follow what Allah has revealed,” meaning the Quran and the message of pure monotheism, their immediate reaction is a flat refusal. They do not engage with the evidence, nor do they ask for clarification. Instead, they retreat into the familiar comfort of their forefathers’ ways, declaring, “We only follow what we found our forefathers practicing. ” This reply reveals a mindset that equates truth with heritage, as if the passage of time alone sanctifies belief. The Quran challenges this by posing a devastating rhetorical question: would they still cling to those inherited practices even if, in reality, Satan is the one inviting them through that legacy to the torment of the blazing Fire?

The question implies that ancestral customs, when they contradict divine revelation, are not neutral but actually become tools in Satan’s hand. Satan beautifies the old ways, making them seem noble tradition, while their ultimate end is destruction. This verse cuts to the heart of a human tendency found in every age: the unquestioning veneration of the past. It reminds us that the truth is not determined by how many generations held a belief, but by whether it aligns with what Allah has revealed. The early Meccan Muslims faced immense pressure to abandon their faith and return to the religion of their fathers; this verse armed them with a rational and spiritual rebuttal. It also teaches that true respect for ancestors means wanting for them the truth, not blindly copying their errors. The logic is simple and piercing: if a path leads to hellfire, then following it is madness, no matter who walked it before.

=22=

In contrast to those who follow their forefathers into error, this verse presents the portrait of the truly successful person. The phrase “fully submits themselves to Allah” translates the Arabic yuslim wajhahu ilā Allāh, which literally means to turn one’s face entirely towards Allah in surrender. This implies complete devotion, directing one’s entire being—intentions, desires, and actions—to the Creator alone. This submission is paired with being a good-doer, muhsin, meaning one who performs deeds with excellence and sincerity, just as described in the early verses of the surah. The result of such a comprehensive Islamic posture is that the person has “grasped the firmest hand-hold,” al-ʿurwah al-wuthqā.

This is a powerful metaphor: imagine a climber who finds an unbreakable, secure grip on a solid rock, ensuring they will not fall. That hand-hold is the testimony of faith, the commitment to tawḥīd, and the life of obedience built upon it. It is the most trustworthy anchor in a world of shifting sands.

The verse then closes with a profound reassurance: “And with Allah rests the outcome of all affairs.” No matter how chaotic events seem, the final resolution and judgment of every matter belong to Allah alone. This connects beautifully with the previous counsel of Luqmān, who rooted his son’s character in submission and patience. The believers who hear this are reminded that their grip on faith is their safety, and whatever temporary victories the disbelievers seem to enjoy, the ultimate outcome is with their Lord. The verse balances personal responsibility—submitting and doing good—with trust in divine decree, offering both a call to action and a deep sense of peace.

=23=

Having described the secure condition of the believer, the surah turns to console the Prophet Muhammad directly, and by extension every caller to truth, regarding those who reject faith. The human heart naturally feels sorrow when people reject guidance, especially when they are beloved community members. The Prophet’s deep concern for his people was well known, and this verse lifts that emotional burden. The command “do not let their disbelief grieve you” is a divine balm, releasing the messenger from a weight he was never meant to carry alone. The reason follows: their final destination is not in the caller’s hands but “to Us is their return.” Allah will handle the reckoning. He will inform them of everything they did, with a knowledge so complete that it encompasses even the most hidden secrets of the heart.

The verse ends by affirming that Allah knows best what is in the chests, the inner recesses of thought, motive, and feeling that no human can access. This serves both as a warning to the disbelievers—they cannot hide their stubbornness or insincerity—and as a comfort to the believer, knowing that the injustice and mockery they suffered are fully recorded.

This principle is echoed in many places in the Quran, reinforcing that the ultimate role of a prophet and his followers is to convey, not to compel. The lesson for all who invite others to faith is profound: care deeply, but do not allow the rejection to consume you. Your task is the delivery; the accounting belongs to the All-Knowing. The flow from verse 22 is clear: those who submit grasp the firmest handhold; those who do not are in God’s hands for judgment, not yours.

=24=

This verse continues the address about the disbelievers, explaining the apparent delay in their punishment. The worldly prosperity or pleasure that the rejecters enjoy is a brief respite granted by Allah.

The phrase “a little while” emphasizes the fleeting nature of this life compared to eternity; all the wealth, power, and amusement that distract them will vanish. The verb “We allow them enjoyment” indicates that even this temporary enjoyment is by divine permission, not by their own power, and it serves as a test. Then, when the appointed term ends, the tone shifts dramatically. The verse says “We will force them” using a word that conveys being driven or compelled, eliminating any possibility of escape. The torment described as “harsh” is severe, unrelenting, and painful, the direct opposite of their prior ease. This juxtaposition of a short, pleasant interval against an overwhelming, forced punishment serves as a stark warning. It reminds believers not to envy the temporary comforts of those who reject faith, for those comforts are like the small, sweet bait that hides a deadly trap. The knowledge that the punishment is harsh and inevitable gives the oppressed community patience. It also answers a perennial question: why do the wicked sometimes seem to prosper? The Quran’s answer is consistent—it is merely a brief extension that only increases their ultimate ruin, a principle stated elsewhere that Allah grants respite so that they may increase in sin and thus deserve a heavier recompense. The verse thus links to the previous consolation: the Prophet should not grieve because their enjoyment is fleeting, and a just and severe outcome awaits.

=25=

The surah now exposes a glaring contradiction in the pagans’ belief system. Even the Meccan idolaters, if asked directly about the ultimate origin of the heavens and the earth, would immediately answer “Allah.” They recognized that the supreme Creator was Allah alone, and their idols were only intermediaries. This acknowledgement is presented as a rhetorical point: their own admission establishes that the true Creator and Lord is Allah. The instruction to the Prophet is to say “Al-ḥamdu lillāh,” all praise belongs to Allah, a phrase that here serves as an expression of gratitude for this residual truth and as a declaration of Allah’s uniqueness.

Yet the verse immediately follows with a sharp observation: “In fact, most of them do not know.” They possess the raw knowledge, but they fail to grasp its implications. If Allah is the Creator of all things, then worship, devotion, and absolute obedience must be directed to Him alone. Their knowledge is superficial because it does not translate into proper action and pure monotheism.

This is a crucial insight: knowledge that does not lead to correct practice is incomplete and effectively a form of ignorance. The verse ties back to Luqmān’s first counsel against shirk, showing that shirk is often rooted in a failure to follow logic to its conclusion. The argument invites the listener to complete the reasoning: if Allah created everything, what have the idols created? That challenge was already laid out in verse 11. Here, the pagans’ own testimony is used against them, a method that demonstrates the power of engaging people with their own stated beliefs. It also highlights that true faith is not just verbal acknowledgment but a wholehearted acceptance that shapes one’s life.

=26=

After recording the pagans’ partial admission that Allah is the Creator, this verse declares the full and absolute ownership of Allah over everything that exists in the heavens and the earth. The statement “to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth” is comprehensive, covering all beings, objects, resources, and even the idols the pagans worship, since those idols are mere objects within Allah’s creation. This ownership is not merely legal; it is the complete dominion of the Creator who sustains, controls, and governs all.

The verse then closes with two divine names: Al-Ghanī, the Self-Sufficient, and Al-Ḥamīd, the Praiseworthy. Allah is Self-Sufficient in that He has absolutely no need for any of His creation, nor for their worship or gratitude. The entire cosmos adds nothing to His majesty, and its absence would subtract nothing. Simultaneously, He is inherently Praiseworthy; all praise in the heavens and the earth ultimately returns to Him because every instance of beauty, goodness, and perfection is a reflection of His attributes or a gift from His hand. This pairing of names echoes verse 12, where Luqmān was told that whoever is ungrateful, Allah is Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy.

The repetition reinforces the lesson: human ingratitude does not diminish Allah in any way. The verse also serves as a firm theological foundation against shirk. If Allah owns everything and is self-sufficient, then associating partners with Him is not only offensive but utterly irrational—what can a created idol give to the One who owns all and needs nothing? This truth invites the believer to worship Allah purely out of love and recognition of His perfection, not because He requires it.

=27=

This magnificent verse uses a striking visual metaphor to convey the limitless nature of Allah’s words, knowledge, and creative power. The image is of all the trees on the planet transformed into writing pens, and the vast ocean turned into ink, and then that ocean being replenished by seven more oceans, an Arabic expression indicating infinite multiplication. Even with such an unimaginable supply of writing tools and ink, the Words of Allah would still not run out.

The term “Words of Allah” refers to His speech, His knowledge, His decrees, and the manifestations of His creative command. Every leaf, every galaxy, every law of nature is a word from Him. As one hadith explains, if the oceans were ink for the words of my Lord, the ocean would be depleted before the words of my Lord are exhausted, even if we brought similar oceans to replenish them.

The verse concludes by affirming that Allah is Almighty, so He can actualize His words without limitation, and All-Wise, so His words are full of perfect wisdom. This not only expands the believer’s sense of awe but also answers a potential objection: how could the Quran, a finite book, encompass all guidance? The Quran is a selection from Allah’s infinite knowledge suited to humanity’s needs.

The verse also connects to the earlier description of the Book as “rich in wisdom”; its wisdom flows from an infinite source. The lesson for the believer is humility before the vastness of divine knowledge. We can never fully wrap our minds around all of Allah’s wisdom, but we trust that what He has revealed is sufficient.

This verse also serves as a rebuttal to those who demand more signs; even if all creation were ink, it could not capture all the signs Allah could give.

=28=

After establishing the vastness of Allah’s words and knowledge, the surah addresses another common doubt: the resurrection of the countless dead across all ages. The verse draws a powerful analogy to demonstrate Allah’s absolute power. The creation of all of humanity and their subsequent resurrection is, for Allah, exactly like the creation and resurrection of a single soul.

The phrase “as simple as” reflects the Arabic illā ka-nafsin wāḥidah, meaning it is only like one soul. This demolishes the idea that a large number of beings poses any difficulty for the Creator. Human manufacturing and effort multiply with quantity, but divine power faces no such constraints. When Allah wills a thing, He merely says “Be,” and it is, whether one soul or billions.

The verse then links this immense power to two divine attributes: All-Hearing and All-Seeing. Allah hears every voice that calls upon Him and sees every scattered particle of the decayed bodies in the earth. Nothing escapes His perception, so the reassembly of every individual is fully within His knowledge and capability.

This is both a comfort for the believers awaiting justice and a warning for those who deny the resurrection. The Meccan pagans often mocked the idea that bones turned to dust could be brought back to life; this verse removes the perceived impossibility by pointing to the One whose power is not limited by scale. It also subtly ties back to Luqmān’s teaching that Allah will bring forth even a mustard seed’s weight of a deed; the One who can resurrect the entirety of humanity with a single command can surely account for the finest details of each person’s actions.

=29=

This verse directs the attention once more to the observable phenomena of the universe, calling the listener to reflect. The merging of night into day and day into night is a daily miracle. The transition is gradual, smooth, and continuous; the darkness slowly yields to light and vice versa, without a sudden cut. This gradual merging shows control, balance, and wisdom. The sun and the moon are described as being subjected, meaning they are compelled to follow the precise orbits and laws set for them. Their movement is not random but governed, and each runs for an appointed term—a predetermined span until the end of this cosmic order. This reminded the pagan Arabs, who worshipped the sun and moon in some cases, that these celestial bodies are not deities but servants of Allah, running their courses by His command.

The verse ends with a jarring personal note: “and that Allah is All-Aware of what you do. ” The shift from cosmic order to personal accountability is deliberate. The same meticulous precision that governs the heavens also governs the recording of human deeds. The One who maintains the orbits of the sun and moon does not neglect the actions of His servants. This link is meant to awaken a deep sense of being watched and to instill a reverence that leads to righteous conduct. The verse seamlessly connects natural signs with moral responsibility, a recurring Quranic pattern. It builds on the earlier creation verses, reinforcing that the cosmos is not a meaningless machine but a theatre of signs pointing to a purposeful, aware Creator.

=30=

This verse provides the underlying reason for the cosmic order and divine control just described. “That” refers to the reality of Allah’s management of the universe and His perfect awareness. The reason is rooted in the very nature of divinity: Allah alone is the Truth, the ultimate Reality whose existence is necessary and unchanging, while everything invoked besides Him is falsehood, bāṭil, meaning it is vanishing, ineffective, and unreal in its claim to divinity. The contrast is absolute and leaves no room for a middle ground. The idols, saints, or forces that people call upon have no independent existence or power; they are false, destined to perish.

The verse then reiterates two majestic attributes: Allah is the Most High, Al-ʿAlī, transcending all creation in His essence and authority, and the All-Great, Al-Kabīr, possessing supreme greatness that dwarfs everything. This declaration logically follows the preceding signs: only the True God could orchestrate the merging of night and day and the subjection of the sun and moon. False gods could never design or maintain such a system.

The verse also echoes the theme of Luqmān’s central advice to avoid shirk, because shirk is fundamentally an attachment to falsehood. By naming Allah as the Truth and everything else as falsehood, the surah draws a clear line that forces a decision. The human heart must cling to the One who is Real, or it will chase illusions that lead to ruin.

For the believer, this verse is a statement of profound security, anchoring the soul in the only reality that never fails.

=31=

Moving from the celestial signs to a maritime one, this verse directs attention to the ships that glide across the sea. The phrase “by the grace of Allah” points out that the smooth sailing is not merely a result of human engineering or favorable winds; it is a continuous divine favor. The water that could easily swallow the vessel is held in check, and the wind that could destroy it is made gentle, all by Allah’s mercy. The purpose is “so that He may show you some of His signs,” meaning that every successful voyage is a lived experience of divine care, a sign crafted from the fabric of daily life.

The verse then specifies that these signs are truly visible only to a particular type of person: every ṣabbār shakūr—whoever is steadfast and grateful. Steadfastness here implies patient endurance and consistency in reflecting and obeying, while gratitude indicates a heart that continually acknowledges the Giver. These two qualities form a complete response to life’s tests: patience in hardship and thankfulness in ease. When a person sails on a ship and reaches their destination safely, the grateful heart recognizes the sign and responds with praise, while the patient heart is reminded to trust Allah in all journeys.

The mention of ships is especially poignant for the Meccan audience, whose livelihood depended on trade caravans and maritime knowledge of others. The verse also foreshadows the next scene of a storm, where human nature is stripped to its core.

Thus the smooth sailing becomes both a gift and a test, preparing the soul for moments of crisis when the true nature of faith is revealed.

=32=

This verse paints a dramatic scene that exposes the raw human instinct for God. When a ship is surrounded by towering waves, described as being like mountains—an image of terrifying, overwhelming danger—the polytheists on board abandon their idols and cry out to Allah alone.

The phrase “in sincere devotion” translates the Arabic mukhliṣīn lahu al-dīn, meaning they purify their faith entirely for Him at that moment, forgetting all intermediaries. Their innate nature, the fiṭrah, surfaces when all worldly supports vanish. But the test comes after the rescue. When Allah delivers them safely to dry land, their response splits. The verse says “only some become relatively grateful,” meaning even those who show gratitude do so half-heartedly or temporarily, while the majority revert to their old ways.

The final statement declares a universal principle: none persistently and arrogantly rejects Allah’s signs except a person who is deeply treacherous, khattār, and habitually ungrateful, kafūr. The term khattār conveys constant betrayal and breaking of promises, describing someone who makes a pact with God in distress and then reneges once safe.

This is a powerful moral diagnosis. The storm at sea is not just a historical anecdote; it is a metaphor for the crises of life that reveal our true dependencies. The verse challenges the listener: will you be like the one who calls sincerely in trouble and then forgets, or will you be steadfast and grateful in all conditions? It ties back to Luqmān’s wisdom: gratitude must be constant, not circumstantial. The warning is clear—being among those described as deceitful and ungrateful leads to ruin.

=33=

After the parable of the sea, the surah issues a universal call to all humanity, marked by the direct address “O humanity.” The command is to have taqwā, a mindful consciousness of the Lord that leads to obedience and protection from His displeasure. The warning then focuses on the Day of Judgment, where the usual bonds of family, the strongest relationships in human life, will be completely severed in terms of intercession or help. No parent will be able to help their child, and no child will be able to help their parent in the slightest, unless Allah permits intercession for the believers. This shattering image dismantles the false security people place in family ties and tribal solidarity, which the Quraysh depended on heavily. The statement “Surely Allah’s promise is true” anchors the warning in certainty. Following this, two specific deceptions are identified against which humanity must be on guard. First, the life of this world must not deceive you, meaning its fleeting pleasures, distractions, and false sense of permanence should not lead one to neglect the eternal life. Second, the Chief Deceiver, al-gharūr, which refers to Satan, must not deceive you about Allah. Satan’s greatest trick is to make people feel secure in their sin by exploiting Allah’s mercy—“Allah will forgive you anyway”—or to make them despair of mercy altogether. Both are traps. This verse is a climactic warning that gathers the themes of the surah: gratitude, accountability, and the danger of self-deception. It serves as the final push before the closing verse on divine knowledge, reminding us to live with an eternal perspective.

=34=

The surah concludes with a verse that encompasses five keys of the unseen, matters of exclusive divine knowledge that no prophet or angel possesses independently. The Prophet Muhammad said in a well-known hadith reported by Al-Bukhari: “The keys of the unseen are five, and no one knows them but Allah,” and then he recited this very verse.

The first key is the knowledge of the Hour, the exact time of the Day of Judgment, which remains hidden so that every generation lives in readiness. The second is the sending down of rain: despite modern meteorology, the precise timing, location, and life-giving effect of rain are ultimately known only to Allah in their full detail and decree.

The third is knowledge of what is in the wombs, encompassing the complete development, gender, future traits, and destiny of the unborn child at every stage—far beyond what ultrasound images can reveal. The fourth key is that no soul knows what it will earn for tomorrow, meaning the future events, gains, losses, and specifically one’s deeds are entirely hidden. This demolishes any arrogance that assumes continued life and success. The fifth key is that no soul knows in what land it will die. The place and time of death are completely sealed from us, teaching humility and constant preparation.

The verse ends by affirming that Allah is All-Knowing, All-Aware, tying these exclusive domains to His perfect knowledge. This final message beautifully aligns with Luqmān’s advice to his son about Allah’s subtle, comprehensive knowledge. The surah that opened with the wise Book now closes by inviting the reader to rest in the One who alone knows the full picture, encouraging trust, humility, and readiness for the inevitable meeting with Him.
