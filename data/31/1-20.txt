=1=

The surah opens with the three separate Arabic letters Alif, Lām, and Mīm, a pattern found at the beginning of several chapters of the Quran. These are known as the disconnected letters, and their precise meaning remains a matter of the unseen known fully only to Allah.

Yet their placement here is profoundly meaningful. They immediately capture attention and remind the listener that this revelation is composed of the very letters of the Arabic language, the language of the Arabs who prided themselves on eloquence. Despite possessing these same letters, no one could produce anything like the Quran.

Thus the letters serve as a silent challenge, demonstrating that while the tools are ordinary, the resulting Book is miraculous. When recited aloud, Alif-Lām-Mīm introduces a tone of reverence and contemplation, preparing the heart for what follows. Many companions and early scholars also noted that the disconnected letters function as names for the Quran itself, or as oaths by which Allah swears by the building blocks of His speech.

In this surah, they introduce a message rich in wisdom, directing attention toward Luqmān’s advice and the broader themes of gratitude, monotheism, and accountability. The mystery of these letters reminds us that not all knowledge is accessible to the human mind and that humility before divine wisdom is the foundation of faith. Accepting that some matters belong to Allah alone keeps the believer grounded, never arrogantly assuming the intellect can uncover everything. So the very first verse sets the stage: listen with an open heart, for the words that follow are not ordinary speech but a wise and merciful guide.

=2=

After the opening letters, Allah immediately describes the revelation as the verses of the Book, meaning the Quran, which is characterised as being rich in wisdom. The word used for wisdom in Arabic is ḥakīm, which also conveys the sense of something made perfectly precise, well-constructed, and containing profound judgment.

This description tells us that the Quran’s teachings are not merely rules or stories, but every word is placed with deep insight and purpose, aiming to correct human thought and behavior. Wisdom here implies that the Book distinguishes truth from falsehood, places everything in its right context, and leads to the most beneficial outcome for those who follow it. The verses are living guidance that helps a person navigate life’s complexities, addressing both inner purification and outward conduct. The fact that the Book itself is called wise also indicates that its source is the All-Wise, for only a wise Creator can produce such perfectly measured revelation.

This description comforts the believers, assuring them that the path they tread is not based on conjecture but on divine wisdom that sees the past, present, and future simultaneously. Every prohibition, permission, story, and parable is an expression of that wisdom. For the skeptic who hears “Alif-Lām-Mīm,” this verse answers the natural question: what are these letters? They are the building blocks of a wise scripture. The connection between the two verses invites us to move from the recognition of the letters to the appreciation of the profound message they form, a message that will later unfold in the counsel of Luqmān and the descriptions of creation that follow.

=3=

The Quran is then further qualified as a guide and a mercy, but the verse restricts this benefit to the good-doers, those known as al-muḥsinīn. This description is crucial because it shows that while the Quran is potentially a source of guidance for all, only those who sincerely incline towards goodness and righteousness will truly benefit from its light.

The word hudan, meaning guidance, indicates that the Book leads humanity out of the darkness of ignorance, doubt, and misguidance into the clarity of faith. The word raḥmah, mercy, reveals that the teachings are not a burden but a compassionate gift, bringing peace and salvation in this life and the Hereafter. By specifying the good-doers, the verse teaches that a person’s receptivity matters. Someone who already possesses the quality of iḥsān—the desire to do what is beautiful and excellent—will find in the Quran the direction and the merciful healing they seek. This does not mean the Quran is exclusive, but that a closed, arrogant heart cannot extract its benefits. Throughout the Quran, Allah describes various human responses; here, the focus is positive, setting up the description of those who establish prayer and pay zakāh. The early Meccan context is vital, as the believers were a small group facing rejection and hardship. This verse assured them that the divine words were personally tailored as a mercy for them, strengthening their resolve. It also invites every listener to examine their own heart: are they among the good-doers whom the Quran will guide and envelop in mercy, or are they blocking themselves from receiving this gift?

=4=

The good-doers mentioned in the previous verse are now identified by three defining qualities. First, they establish prayer, meaning they perform the ṣalāh regularly, correctly, and with inward devotion, not merely going through motions.

The use of the word “establish” (yuqīmūn) indicates that they make prayer a firm pillar in their lives, observing its conditions, timings, and spiritual concentration. This was a central act of worship even in Mecca before the full five daily prayers were prescribed during the Night Journey. Second, they pay the alms-tax, the zakāh, which purifies their wealth and souls by giving a portion to those in need. This cultivates compassion, reduces greed, and builds a community of mutual care. Third, they have sure faith in the Hereafter; the Arabic yaqīn conveys absolute certainty, not mere hope. They live with a clear awareness that this world is temporary and that they will stand before their Lord for judgment. This certainty naturally affects their decisions, making them prioritize eternal outcomes over fleeting pleasures. Together, these three traits cover a person’s relationship with Allah (prayer), relationship with creation (charity), and inner conviction about the ultimate reality (faith in the Hereafter).

The verse presents a balanced picture of faith that is neither purely ritualistic nor purely intellectual; it manifests in bodily worship, financial sacrifice, and a mindset shaped by the unseen. This balance is the wisdom of the Book in action, preparing the reader for the later advice of Luqmān to his son, where prayer and humility feature prominently.

=5=

Having described their qualities, Allah now declares the outcome for such people. They are truly guided by their Lord, which means they are upon a clear path that originates from Allah Himself. The guidance is not self-generated; it is a divine gift bestowed upon those who show sincerity and effort.

The repetition of “it is they” emphasizes exclusivity, distinguishing the true recipients of guidance from those who merely claim belief. Then comes the promise of success, falāḥ, a comprehensive term in the Quran that encompasses salvation from Hell, entry into Paradise, and a state of flourishing in both worlds. The successful ones are not necessarily the wealthiest or most powerful by worldly standards, but they achieve the ultimate triumph that every soul deep down desires. This verse reassures the struggling Meccan believers that despite their minority status and persecution, they are the ones on the right track, and their future is secure. It also sets up a contrast with the following verses about those who trade guidance for misguidance. The structure of the surah carefully establishes a model of true success before exposing its opposite, so that the listener can clearly see the two paths. The link to Luqmān’s story is subtle: Luqmān was also guided by his Lord through wisdom, and his success lay in gratitude and good counsel. The early Muslims could see their own reflection in this verse, and every generation after them can measure themselves against these standards, asking whether they truly embody the traits that lead to divine guidance and ultimate success.

=6=

After the portrait of the sincere good-doers, the surah turns to a contrasting group: those who use idle tales, or theatrics, to mislead people from the path of Allah. The Arabic term lahw al-ḥadīth refers to diverting speech or entertainment that captures attention and distracts from what truly matters. Historical reports, including narrations from Ibn ʿAbbās and others, indicate that this verse was revealed concerning a man like Al-Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith, who would travel to Persia, purchase books of legends and fables, and then recite them to the Quraysh, boasting that his stories were more entertaining than the Quran. He deliberately used captivating narratives to draw people away from listening to revelation.

The phrase “without any knowledge” exposes his ignorance; he had no real understanding of divine truth, yet he arrogantly set himself up as an alternative guide. Moreover, he made a mockery of Allah’s Way, ridiculing the message and those who followed it. The verse warns that such actions invite a humiliating punishment, one that brings disgrace because their arrogance and mockery will be met with severe, debasing torment in the Hereafter. This serves as a timeless warning against the misuse of media, entertainment, and storytelling to undermine faith. Even today, some forms of content may not openly attack religion but simply consume so much time and attention that people drift away from spiritual growth and reflection. The punishment is described as humiliating because they sought honor and attention through their theatrics, so they will be recompensed with the opposite. This verse bridges the earlier praise of the believers with the ensuing emphasis on revelation and gratitude, showing the consequences of treating divine guidance lightly.

=7=

This verse describes the reaction of the same people when the Quranic verses are recited. They turn away with an arrogance that is not just physical but deeply psychological. The comparison to deafness is striking: they behave as if they did not hear the words at all, not because their ears malfunction, but because their hearts are blocked by pride.

The phrase “as if there is deafness in their ears” employs a powerful simile; outwardly their hearing is intact, but the truth does not penetrate. It is a chosen deafness, a refusal to engage sincerely. The command to the Prophet, “So give them good news of a painful punishment,” uses irony to deliver a severe warning. Normally, “good news” conveys glad tidings, but here it is the announcement of what awaits them. This rhetorical device jolts the listener, making them realize the gravity of the situation. The painful punishment is a direct consequence of their turning away and mockery mentioned in the previous verse.

The sequence shows that rejecting divine signs after they have been clearly presented is not a neutral act; it invites calamity. For the believers enduring taunts, this verse provided reassurance that justice would ultimately be served. For all readers, it serves as a sobering reminder to approach the Quran with humility and openness, lest a hardness of heart render one spiritually deaf. The connection to Luqmān’s advice is evident later: he counsels his son to be humble in posture and voice, the exact opposite of the arrogance displayed here.

=8=

In stark contrast to the humiliating punishment of the mockers, the surah returns to the reward of the faithful. The opening word “surely” (inna) conveys certainty and emphasis. The description “those who believe and do good” pairs inner conviction with outward righteous deeds, a recurring Quranic principle that faith must be accompanied by action to bear fruit. The promised reward is the Gardens of Bliss, jannāt al-naʿīm, which denote gardens of perpetual delight, comfort, and joy where every desire is fulfilled and no hardship exists.

The word “naʿīm” specifically evokes ease, luxury, and pleasure, painting a picture that contrasts powerfully with the painful punishment mentioned just before. This alternating pattern of warning and glad tidings is characteristic of the Quran’s method: it shakes the heart with fear of punishment and then soothes it with hope of reward, so that the believer lives between hope and fear. The believers in Mecca, who faced social ostracism and economic boycott, could lift their eyes to this promise, knowing that their struggles were temporary and that eternal bliss awaited them.

The mention of “Gardens” also connects to the imagery of Paradise throughout the Quran, where lush greenery, flowing rivers, and cool shade represent the ultimate homecoming. This verse prepares the ground for the subsequent affirmation that Allah’s promise is true, reinforcing that the believers’ hope is not a wishful fantasy but a guaranteed reality from the Almighty, All-Wise. It is a direct encouragement to continue in their faith and good works, no matter the opposition.

=9=

The description of the reward continues with the crucial detail that the believers will abide in those gardens eternally. The permanence of Paradise removes any fear of loss or ending, which magnifies the bliss infinitely. A temporary pleasure, however intense, is always shadowed by the sorrow of its cessation, but eternal bliss is complete joy.

The verse then grounds this promise in the truthfulness of Allah: “Allah’s promise is true. ” This short sentence is a profound anchor for the believer. Human beings may break promises, but the Creator, free from all deficiency, never fails in His word. The two concluding divine names, the Almighty and the All-Wise, are particularly fitting. He is Almighty, so He has absolute power to fulfill this promise; no force can prevent Him from admitting the believers to Paradise and granting them everlasting life. He is All-Wise, so this promise is made with perfect knowledge and justice, not arbitrarily.

The pairing of might and wisdom assures that Paradise is not a wishful myth but the deliberate, powerful decree of the One who governs all existence. This verse completes the portrait of the believers’ destination that began in verse 8, offering a profound sense of security. It also subtly links back to the opening verses: the Book is rich in wisdom, and the One who revealed it is All-Wise, ensuring that its glad tidings are not empty words but a binding covenant.

For those who suffer because of their faith, meditating on this verse renews patience and hope, reminding them that their Lord’s promise is unbreakable.

=10=

The focus shifts from the Hereafter to the visible signs of Allah’s power and wisdom in creation. The verse begins by drawing attention to the heavens, which are raised without visible pillars.

The phrase “as you can see” engages the observer directly, inviting reflection on the sky that stretches above, held by invisible divine command. In a time with no telescopes, the vast, unsupported expanse was a clear sign of the Creator’s ability. Then the verse moves to the earth, where firm mountains are described as stabilizers, preventing the earth from shaking violently with its inhabitants. Modern geology acknowledges mountains’ deep roots, but the primary point is that Allah’s design ensures a stable habitat. Next, the scattering of all types of creatures indicates the diversity of life—animals, birds, insects—each a testament to creative brilliance.

The final part mentions rain descending from the sky, causing the growth of every kind of fine, pleasing plant. The Arabic “zawj karīm” suggests plants that are noble, beneficial, and beautiful, providing food and delight.

This sequence—heavens, earth, creatures, plants—is a concise yet comprehensive tour of the cosmos, emphasizing that everything is deliberately created and sustained. The verse prepares for the rhetorical question that follows, challenging the idolaters to show what their false gods have created. It also connects deeply with Luqmān’s later advice to his son to be aware that Allah sees all things, for the same Creator who established these massive systems is intimately aware of every tiny deed.

=11=

After listing the wonders of creation, Allah delivers a direct challenge to the polytheists. “This is Allah’s creation” is a simple, undeniable statement. The heavens, the mountains, the creatures, and the plants are all observable realities. The command “Now show Me what those other than Him have created” is a rhetorical demand that exposes the emptiness of idol worship. The idols, whether stone statues, celestial bodies, or ancestral spirits, have created nothing—not even a fly. This challenge invites rational reflection: if these beings cannot create anything, how can they be worthy of worship?

The verse then concludes with a statement of fact: the wrongdoers are clearly astray. Their misguidance is not hidden or excusable; it is evident in the light of creation’s clear testimony. This argument appears repeatedly in the Quran, such as in Surah Al-Ḥajj where Allah challenges the idols to create a fly. The Meccan pagans themselves acknowledged Allah as the Creator of the heavens and earth, a point made later in this very surah. Their contradiction was obvious, and this verse exposes it with unassailable logic. This rhetorical style serves as a powerful tool for believers in any era to defend monotheism. It teaches that faith is not blind; it is rooted in observation and reason, guided by revelation. The transition to Luqmān’s story is now seamless: he, as a wise man, recognised this truth and imparted it to his son, beginning with the most fundamental command: never associate partners with Allah. The stage is set for a living example of wisdom responding to creation’s signs.

=12=

With the evidence of creation established, the surah introduces Luqmān, a figure renowned for his wisdom. The majority of scholars hold that Luqmān was a righteous man, not a prophet, possibly of African descent, known for his sound judgment and insightful sayings. Allah says He blessed him with wisdom, and that wisdom is immediately linked to gratitude. The command “Be grateful to Allah” is the first expression of that wisdom. Gratitude is presented as the proper human response to the countless favors mentioned in the previous verses.

The verse then clarifies a central principle: gratitude benefits only the grateful person. Allah is in no need of thanks; the act of being grateful elevates the soul, increases blessings, and brings contentment. Conversely, ingratitude does not harm Allah at all, for He is Self-Sufficient and Praiseworthy. He is completely free of any need, so human rebellion diminishes nothing from His majesty, and He is inherently deserving of praise regardless of whether people acknowledge it.

This teaching dismantles the arrogance of those who withhold gratitude as if they are withholding something from God. Wisdom, therefore, begins with recognizing one’s total dependence on the Creator and responding with heartfelt thanks. This aligns with the earlier depiction of the good-doers, who are grateful servants.

The mention of Luqmān’s wisdom sets the stage for the series of counsels he gave his son, which are not just paternal advice but divinely approved gems that the Quran records for all generations.

=13=

The Quran now relays the actual words of Luqmān’s counsel, beginning with the most critical matter: pure monotheism. The affectionate address, “O my dear son,” shows the loving, concerned tone of a father who wants the best for his child. The prohibition is against shirk, associating partners with Allah in worship, belief, or lordship. Luqmān emphasizes this because it is the foundation upon which all other deeds rest. The verse explains why: associating others with Allah is a grave wrong, in fact the greatest wrongdoing.

The Arabic term “ẓulm” means placing something in its wrong place, and giving worship, which belongs exclusively to the Creator, to created things is the ultimate injustice. This wrong is not merely against oneself; it is a transgression against the rights of Allah, against one’s own soul, and against the truth of existence. The warning against shirk is the first and most emphatic point because if a person’s concept of God is corrupted, every other aspect of life becomes distorted. Luqmān’s wisdom aligns perfectly with the message of every prophet, showing that monotheism is the natural, rational response to the signs of creation described earlier. The heavens and earth without pillars, the mountains, and the plants all point to One Creator; therefore, turning to idols or other beings is absurd and deeply wrong. This verse serves as the headline of Luqmān’s advice, with the subsequent counsels flowing from the root of true faith. It also reminds parents of their prime responsibility: to nurture sound belief in their children with love and clarity.

=14=

Having recorded Luqmān’s advice against shirk, the Quran now interweaves a direct divine command concerning parents, creating a beautiful link. Tawḥīd is followed by kindness to parents, a pairing found throughout the Quran. The command to honor parents is universal, but the verse singles out the mother for special mention, highlighting her unique sacrifices: she bore the child through hardship upon hardship, from pregnancy’s physical strain to the pains of labor, and then nursed the child for up to two years. This detailed mention of pregnancy and weaning appeals to the conscience, leaving no doubt about the immense debt a child owes the mother. The command to be grateful to Allah and to parents places parental gratitude right after divine gratitude, elevating the status of parents, especially the mother.

Yet this gratitude has a condition, as the next verse clarifies. The final sentence, “To Me is the final return,” serves as a gentle reminder that this relationship is accountable; the child will answer to Allah for how they treated their parents, and the parents will answer for how they raised their child. It also consoles children who may suffer from difficult parents, for ultimate justice lies with Allah. This verse echoes Luqmān’s own advice and frames it within Allah’s own speech, showing that Luqmān’s wisdom was divinely inspired. It prepares for the exception that follows, ensuring that obedience to parents never overrides obedience to the Creator.

=15=

The command to honor parents is immediately balanced with a crucial boundary. If parents strive, with utmost effort, to force their child into shirk—associating with Allah something for which there is no proof or knowledge—the child must not obey them in that specific matter.

The phrase “what you have no knowledge of” underlines that shirk is baseless; there is no sound evidence for it, only blind imitation. Yet even in this conflict, the child is not allowed to sever ties or treat them harshly. The instruction is clear: still keep their company in this world courteously. That means maintaining kind speech, financial support if needed, and gentle companionship, all while firmly refusing to compromise on faith.

This is a profound balance that many struggle with. The verse then instructs to follow the way of those who turn to Allah, meaning the path of the prophets and righteous believers, the community of faith that reinforces monotheism.

The final sentence brings back the theme of accountability: all will return to Allah, and He will inform them of the true nature of their deeds. This comforts the believing child who faced pressure, knowing that Allah saw their struggle and will reward their patience. It also warns the misguided parents that their coercion will not go unjudged. This verse directly connects to the core theme of gratitude and warns against the blind following of ancestors, a topic picked up later in the surah.

=16=

The surah returns to Luqmān’s words to his son, now focusing on Allah’s all-encompassing knowledge and justice. The image is breathtakingly precise: a deed as light as a mustard seed, the proverbial smallest weight. Even if that tiny deed were hidden inside a solid rock, or lost somewhere in the vastness of the heavens, or buried deep within the earth, Allah will bring it forth on the Day of Judgment.

This statement leaves no room for any sin or good act to be overlooked. The Prophet Muhammad confirmed this concept in a well-known narration describing that on the Day of Resurrection, even the smallest good and evil will be presented. The two divine names ending the verse are perfectly matched: Al-Laṭīf, the Most Subtle, indicates that Allah’s knowledge penetrates the most hidden, fine, and inaccessible matters without any difficulty, while Al-Khabīr, the All-Aware, signifies comprehensive awareness of every detail and inner reality. Together they assure that divine judgment is based on perfect, unobstructed knowledge. For a young person, this teaching instills a profound sense of personal accountability; one cannot hide from Allah, not in the darkest cave nor the farthest galaxy. This awareness naturally encourages sincerity, because even a private act of charity or a secret sinful thought is fully known and will be brought to light. The lesson links back to the creation verses: the One who could create heavens without pillars and scatter creatures throughout the earth can surely keep track of every atom of human action.

This principle shapes moral behavior when no human eye is watching.

=17=

With the foundation of faith and awareness of accountability firmly laid, Luqmān moves to the practical pillars of righteous living. First, establish prayer, making it a constant, properly performed act of worship. The same word “establish” used earlier for the believers shows consistency. Prayer is the daily renewal of one’s connection with Allah, and it fuels the strength to carry out the next commands. Second, encourage what is good and forbid what is evil. This elevates the son’s role from personal piety to social responsibility; a true believer is not passive but actively promotes virtue and gently corrects wrongdoing in the community.

The sequence is important: one must first be grounded in prayer before being able to effectively enjoin good, because the task is demanding and requires spiritual energy. Third, endure patiently whatever befalls you. Fulfilling these duties invites opposition, criticism, and hardship, so patience is essential.

The word “ṣabr” implies steadfastness without complaint. The concluding phrase “surely this is a resolve to aspire to” means these matters are among the great, firm resolutions that Allah commands, the kind of determination that separates the strong in faith from the weak. They are not optional extras but essential goals that require serious commitment. This advice encapsulates the core of the believer’s mission: maintain your link with Allah, reform society, and hold steady through trials. It resonates deeply with the Meccan context where the Prophet and his followers were patiently persevering in the face of persecution while calling people to monotheism.

=18=

Luqmān’s counsel now addresses inner character and external behavior, specifically the disease of pride. The first prohibition is against turning one’s cheek away from people in scorn, a gesture of contempt and superiority.

The second is walking upon the earth with an exaggerated, proud gait, as if to demonstrate one’s importance. Together, these cover the attitude of the heart and the demeanor of the body. The verse explains the reason: Allah does not love every arrogant, boastful person.

The Arabic “mukhtāl fakhūr” describes someone who is deluded by self-admiration and constantly boasts about their qualities, lineage, or achievements. This is the opposite of the humility that faith demands. Arrogance was the sin of Iblīs, who refused to bow to Adam, and it is the trait that often keeps people from accepting the truth. The verse connects to the earlier description of those who turned away in arrogance from the Quran’s recitation. True wisdom, as Luqmān teaches, produces modesty. Knowing that all blessings come from Allah and that every human being is created from humble origins cures pride.

Moreover, the prohibition of boastfulness reminds a person that any good they possess is a gift from Allah, not a personal accomplishment to flaunt. In daily life, this translates to treating everyone with respect, walking modestly, and maintaining a quiet confidence that does not seek to belittle others.

The following verse extends this theme of moderation into the realm of voice and gait.

=19=

This verse continues the theme of humility and dignified conduct, now focusing on moderation in walking and lowering one’s voice. The instruction to be moderate in pace means not to walk too slowly in a manner of arrogance, nor too hastily in a restless, undignified way, but to adopt a balanced, purposeful gait. This teaches that even a mundane action like walking can be an act of worship when done mindfully. The command to lower the voice aims at avoiding loud, harsh, or boisterous speech. It includes refraining from shouting unnecessarily, raising the voice in argument beyond need, or speaking in a tone that intimidates or annoys others. The vivid comparison that follows is striking: the most repulsive of voices is the braying of a donkey. This powerful simile leaves a strong impression; a raised, harsh voice is compared to an animal known for its unpleasant, loud sound. The wisdom behind this is that loudness often stems from arrogance, anger, or a desire to dominate, all of which are unworthy of a believer. Soft speech reflects a calm heart and respect for others. The Prophet was known for his gentle, measured speech, never raising his voice indecently. This advice encompasses everything from table talk to public discourse, encouraging an atmosphere of peace and mutual respect. Both verses 18 and 19 provide a practical code of social conduct that shapes a personality beloved to Allah and to people. They complete Luqmān’s recorded advice, leaving a timeless manual for raising children with wisdom and love.

=20=

After the intimate counsel of Luqmān, the discourse returns to a direct address to humanity at large, calling attention to Allah’s universal favors. The rhetorical question, “Have you not seen,” invites reflection on the observable world. Everything in the heavens—the sun, moon, stars, and clouds—and everything on earth—the animals, rivers, and vegetation—has been made subservient to human beings, meaning it is designed to benefit them. This subjection is not by human power but by divine decree, a gift that should inspire awe and gratitude.

The verse then mentions that Allah has lavished His favors in abundance, both those that are seen, like health, wealth, and nature’s beauty, and those that are unseen, like faith, intellect, safety, and divine protection. The word “lavished” implies overflowing generosity that far exceeds basic needs.

Yet despite this overwhelming kindness, some people still dispute about Allah’s oneness, power, or the truth of revelation. They do so without any foundation: no knowledge, no genuine guidance, and no enlightening scripture. This threefold negation demolishes their position entirely; they argue from ignorance, follow no reliable source of guidance, and possess no revealed book to support their claims. The contrast between Allah’s tangible favors and their baseless disputation exposes the unreasonableness of ingratitude and denial. This verse bridges the grateful wisdom of Luqmān with the stubbornness of the polytheists, setting up the next verse’s critique of blind ancestral adherence. The pattern is clear: abundant signs call for humble acceptance, not empty argument.
