=41=

The believing companion continues his warning by presenting another dimension of vulnerability that the wealthy man had overlooked. “Or its water may sink into the earth.” The river that flowed between the two gardens was the source of life for all that vegetation. Without water, the gardens would be nothing. The believer points out that this water is not an independent resource but a gift that God can withdraw at any moment. He imagines a scenario where the water disappears deep underground, beyond the reach of any digging or machinery.

The phrase “and then you will never be able to seek it out” emphasizes the absolute helplessness that would follow. The verb “tabtaghiya” suggests searching with effort and determination, but the outcome would be total failure. No human power, no amount of wealth, and no manpower could retrieve what God had caused to vanish.

This warning is especially powerful because it moves the threat from a dramatic thunderbolt from the sky to a quiet, hidden disappearance beneath the earth. The man’s pride was in what was visible and measurable—the grapes, the palms, the crops, the flowing river. But the true source of it all was invisible, resting in God’s command. If the water simply sank away, the beauty would wither silently, without a sound, and the proud owner would be left helplessly wringing his hands. The believer is teaching a fundamental lesson: all means of sustenance are held by God’s hand. The water we rely on, the air we breathe, the health we enjoy—all are divine trusts that can be recalled. The wealthy man had spoken as if his property was eternal and his wealth guaranteed, but the believer dismantles that illusion by reminding him of the hidden, fragile threads that hold worldly success together. The warning is not given with spite but with sincere concern, hoping that the man will awaken to reality before it is too late.

=42=

The warning of the believing companion was not mere speculation. “And so all his produce was totally ruined” signals the swift execution of divine decree.

The word “uḥīṭa” conveys the sense of being completely encircled and destroyed. Everything he owned perished. His fruits, his crops, his vines—all gone. The reaction is painted in a vivid, poignant image: “so he started to wring his hands for all he had spent on it.” Wringing one’s hands is a universal gesture of extreme regret, grief, and helplessness. He was physically expressing the pain of seeing his investment and effort vanish.

The phrase “for all he had spent on it” highlights his attachment to the material cost—the money, labour, and time he had poured into the gardens. His heart was entirely bound to these expenditures. The scene is made more devastating by the detail “while it had collapsed on its trellises.” The garden structures, the wooden frameworks that once supported the vines, had fallen, and the vines themselves lay in ruin. What was once a picture of order and beauty was now a scene of wreckage. Then comes his cry of realization: “Alas! I wish I had never associated anyone with my Lord in worship!

” The Arabic “yā laytanī” is a lament of irreversible loss. In the moment of disaster, he finally understood the root of his error. He recognized that his boastful attitude, his reliance on his own power, and his denial of the afterlife were forms of associating partners with God. He had made his wealth a partner in his sense of security, his manpower a partner in his strength, and his own self a partner in control. His regret, however, came too late. This verse is a sobering illustration that the veils of pride often fall only when the punishment arrives, and then repentance is no longer accepted. It is a call to repent while the opportunity of life remains.

=43=

The verse zeroes in on the complete and utter isolation of the wealthy man in the face of divine decree. “And he had no manpower to help him against Allah.” Earlier, he had boasted of being superior in manpower, meaning his retinue of family, servants, and followers. He thought that his numbers made him strong and secure. But when the calamity struck, none of them were of any use. No army of servants could restore the ruined gardens. No family could reverse what God had decreed.

The word “fi’ah” means a group or faction prepared to assist, but against the will of God, all such groups dissolve into nothingness. The second part intensifies the helplessness: “nor could he even help himself.

” The Arabic “wa mā kāna muntaṣiran” implies that he could not defend or rescue himself. There was no inner resource, no clever strategy, no personal strength that could save him. The very self that he had placed at the centre of his existence was powerless.

This is the inevitable endpoint of every arrogance that fails to recognize the sovereignty of God. The verse functions as a universal principle: when God’s punishment or a trial arrives, the props that a person relied on—wealth, social connections, intelligence, status—all crumble.

This reality was not only a lesson for the disbeliever in the parable but a warning to the Quraysh and all who read the Quran. The early Muslims, who faced the might and wealth of the Quraysh, were reminded that those who appeared invincible could be reduced to absolute weakness in a moment. It also serves as a personal reflection for every believer: do I place my trust in my own resources, or in the One who controls all resources? The collapse of the man’s false sense of security is now complete, setting the stage for the declaration of true support in the following verse.

=44=

From the ruins of the disbeliever’s false hopes, the Quran draws the ultimate lesson. “At this time, support comes only from Allah.

” The phrase “at this time” (hunālika) refers to the moment of judgment, either in this life when calamity strikes, or on the Day of Resurrection, or both. When all else fails, the only real protection and aid is with God.

The word “al-walāyah” implies loyalty, support, and guardianship. It belongs exclusively to Allah, “the True Lord.” His attribute of al-Ḥaqq, the True, contrasts with all false supports. Wealth, manpower, and personal abilities are fleeting falsehoods; God alone is the permanent Reality whose help truly matters.

The verse then adds a beautiful conclusion: “He is best in reward and best in outcome.” God’s reward for the believers is far superior to anything the world can offer. The gardens of Paradise eclipse any earthly garden. His outcome, meaning the final consequence for those who rely on Him, is the best possible ending. Even for the wealthy man in the parable, the best outcome would have been to turn to God, but he lost it through his own choice. This verse is a direct echo of the earlier description of the believers’ reward and a reaffirmation of the surah’s central theme: reliance on God alone. For the Prophet’s companions who had left their wealth and homes for the sake of faith, this was a powerful reassurance. They might have lost worldly support, but they had gained the support of the True Lord. The lesson extends to all believers: in moments of crisis, when human support vanishes, one must turn to Allah with full certainty that His guardianship is real, His reward is beyond imagination, and His planned outcome is always the best, even if immediate appearances suggest otherwise.

=45=

The story of the two gardens naturally leads into a broader, universal parable about the nature of worldly life itself. The Prophet is instructed, “And give them a parable of this worldly life.” The parable serves to cement the lesson in the listener’s mind through a scene they witness every season. “It is like the plants of the earth, thriving when sustained by the rain We send down from the sky. ” Rain falls, and the earth bursts into life. Vegetation becomes dense, green, and vibrant, looking magnificent and permanent. This stage corresponds to youth, health, wealth, and worldly prosperity when a person feels they are at their peak. Then comes the inevitable turn: “Then they soon turn into chaff scattered by the wind. ” The lush plants dry out, become yellow and brittle, and are broken into pieces. The wind blows, and the fragments scatter, leaving the ground bare as if nothing was ever there.

The word “hushīman” describes dry, broken stubble, and “tadhrūhu ar-riyāḥ” paints the picture of the wind dispersing it without any power to resist. This is the lifecycle of every worldly beauty and achievement.

The phrase “And Allah is fully capable of doing all things” serves as both a comfort and a warning. He has the power to give and to take away, to bring life and to cause death, to create and to resurrect. This parable distills the entire message of the surah into a single, memorable image. The cave youths, the wealthy man’s gardens, and all human civilizations follow this pattern. The believer who internalizes this parable holds the world with an open hand, enjoying God’s blessings without being fooled by their apparent permanence, always preparing for the eternal life to come.

=46=

Having given the parable of vegetation, the Quran now specifies two of the most potent attractions of worldly life: “Wealth and children are the adornment of this worldly life.” Wealth and offspring are specifically highlighted because they are the primary measures of status and success in almost every society. The wealthy man in the parable had both, and they were the source of his boast.

The word “zinah” means adornment or ornament; it is something beautiful on the surface but not the substance. An adornment can be taken away, and it does not define the true worth of the thing it decorates. This reframes the entire human pursuit of these things. They are not evil in themselves, but they are tests, decorations that can distract from the real purpose of life. Then the verse provides the alternative: “but the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord in reward and in hope. ” Al-bāqiyāt aṣ-ṣāliḥāt, the enduring righteous deeds, are acts of worship, charity, prayer, remembrance of God, and good character that outlast death. Their reward is “khayrun ‘inda rabbika,” better in the sight of your Lord in terms of recompense. And they are “khayrun amalan,” better as a source of hope. A person can place their ultimate hope in their deeds done for God, whereas hope placed in wealth and children is often disappointed. This verse does not call for the abandonment of family or wealth; it calls for a correct ordering of the heart. The believer may enjoy the adornment of the world, but their true investment and hope lie in the deeds that will endure beyond the grave.

This is the balanced Quranic worldview that prevents both materialistic obsession and escapist monasticism.

=47=

The surah now turns to a vivid scene from the Day of Judgment, connecting the themes of the transient world and eternal reward. The verse begins with an implicit warning: “Beware of the Day We will blow the mountains away.” The mountains, symbols of stability, permanence, and immense weight, will be reduced to dust and blown away.

This image is meant to shake the listener’s confidence in the material world. If mountains can be annihilated, then what of the gardens and palaces that people boast of? Then, “you will see the earth laid bare.” The earth will become a flat, empty plain without any of its landmarks, vegetation, or structures. Everything that adorned it will be gone, echoing the earlier verse about reducing the earth’s adornments to barren ground. “And We will gather all humankind, leaving none behind.” The gathering is universal and complete. Every human being who ever lived will be resurrected and assembled. No one will be forgotten, missed, or able to hide.

The Arabic “fa-lam nughādir minhum aḥadan” emphasizes absolute inclusivity. This verse is a direct rebuttal to the wealthy man’s doubt about the Hour. The scene transitions seamlessly from the destruction of the world to the presentation before God, forcing the listener to visualize their own standing on that plain. The mountains that seem so permanent are mere illusions; the only reality that endures is God and His promise.

For the believer, this verse inspires a profound sense of accountability and a reordering of priorities, knowing that the world they see will one day be utterly transformed.

=48=

The scene on the Day of Judgment continues with the image of humanity standing before God. “They will be presented before your Lord in rows.

” The word “ṣaffan” indicates that people will be lined up, orderly and exposed, with no one able to hide behind another. There is no hierarchy, no wealth, no followers to protect a person. Each stands as an individual before the Creator. The address that follows is directed at those who denied the resurrection: “You have surely returned to Us all alone as We created you the first time.

” The phrase “all alone” (furādā) hammers home the reality of individual accountability. They come back stripped of wealth, family, and all the worldly things they relied upon, just as they were born naked and empty-handed.

This is the ultimate refutation of their arrogance. Then the statement adds a sharp note of rebuke: “although you always claimed that We would never appoint a time for your return.” Their lifelong mockery of the Hour is thrown back at them.

The word “za‘amtum” implies a claim that was always false. Standing in that majestic and terrifying presence, they face the undeniable truth of what they used to ridicule. This verse serves as a powerful reminder that the denials and arguments we make in this life will be answered by the sheer reality of the Hereafter. For the Prophet’s opponents who scoffed at the idea of bones being reassembled, this was a direct warning. The imagery is designed to make the listener reflect: on that day, there will be no companion, no wealth, and no argument, only the deeds one has sent forward.

=49=

After the presentation in rows, the deeds are brought forth. “And the record of deeds will be laid open.” The book containing every action, word, and intention is placed before each person, completely accessible. The focus then turns to “the wicked” (al-mujrimīn), the criminals who rejected faith. They are described as “in fear of what is written in it.

” The Arabic “mushfiqīn” conveys a trembling, anxious fear. They do not want to look, yet they cannot look away. Upon seeing the exhaustive record, they cry out in shock and horror: “Woe to us! What kind of record is this that does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted?” The exclamation “yā waylatanā” is an expression of utter despair. They are stunned by the meticulous precision of the record. No minor sin (ṣaghīrah) is overlooked, and no major sin (kabīrah) is left out. Everything is counted, everything is present. This directly counters the human tendency to dismiss small sins or to assume that some deeds will be forgotten. Then the verse states: “They will find whatever they did present before them.” There is no escape; their entire life’s work is laid out in front of their eyes.

The verse ends with a declaration of perfect divine justice: “And your Lord will never wrong anyone.” The punishment they face is not an act of injustice but the direct consequence of their own actions. God’s record is accurate, and His judgment is fair. This verse is a profound meditation on the all-encompassing nature of divine surveillance. Every thoughtless word, every hidden deed, every forgotten sin is recorded.

For the believer, it instills a state of muraqabah—a constant awareness that nothing escapes God’s knowledge, which leads to carefulness in conduct and swift repentance when one falls short.

=50=

The surah now shifts to the primordial story of Iblis, connecting his rebellion to the arrogance displayed in the previous parables. “And remember when We said to the angels, ‘Prostrate before Adam.’” This prostration was an act of honour and greeting for the first human, commanded by God. All the angels obeyed immediately, but “not Iblis. ” The verse clarifies a critical point: Iblis “was one of the jinn. ” He was not an angel but a jinn who, through his devotion, had been elevated to the company of angels.

However, his inner nature, made of smokeless fire, was different. His refusal is described as “he rebelled against the command of his Lord.

” The word “fasaqa” means he stepped outside obedience with defiance and arrogance. The question then is posed to humanity: “Would you then take him and his descendants as patrons instead of Me, although they are your enemy?” It is the height of irrationality and ingratitude to abandon the worship of the loving Creator and instead follow a sworn enemy whose only goal is human destruction. Iblis is not a neutral figure; he is an active enemy. Taking him and his offspring as awliyā’ means obeying his whispers and making him a partner in allegiance. The verse calls this “an evil alternative for the wrongdoers to choose.” It is a bad exchange (badal), trading the mercy of God for the enmity of Satan.

This verse establishes the cosmic backdrop of the human struggle: there is a clear enemy dedicated to leading people astray, and following him is a willful act of self-harm. The stories of the cave youths and the believing companion demonstrate resistance to Satan, while the arrogant wealthy man exemplifies falling into his trap. The connection reminds the believer that the battle against arrogance and materialism is part of this larger spiritual war.

=51=

This verse continues the theme of Iblis and his offspring by underlining their complete lack of authority or partnership with God in the act of creation. Allah declares, “I never called them to witness the creation of the heavens and the earth or even their own creation.” The creation of the cosmos and of their own selves was a matter of God’s exclusive will. Neither Iblis nor any other being was present as an observer, advisor, or assistant. They were themselves created, so how could they have witnessed their own coming into existence? This rhetorical point demolishes any basis for taking them as associates. If they had no role in the grandest acts of creation, what power or authority could they possibly have to merit worship or obedience? Then the verse concludes: “nor would I take the misleaders as helpers. ” The word “muḍillīn” refers to those who mislead others. God does not need the help of those who lead astray. His plan is carried out without their support, and He does not depend on any creature.

This is a direct refutation of the idea that Satan or his followers have any hidden power or that they are a necessary counterpart to divine guidance. Everything occurs by God’s will and power alone. This verse reinforces the absolute sovereignty of God and the utter helplessness of all false objects of worship.

For the believer, it is a powerful reminder that the spiritual forces of misguidance have no independent power. They cannot create, they cannot harm without God’s permission, and they are certainly not partners with the Almighty. All reliance should be placed solely on the One who created the heavens and the earth without any need for witnesses or helpers.

=52=

The scene returns to the Day of Judgment with a specific episode of humiliation for the polytheists. “And beware of the Day He will say, ‘Call upon those you claimed were My associate-gods.’” The command is a form of rebuke. God, in His knowledge, knows that these supposed partners are false, but He instructs the disbelievers to call upon them in their moment of need, just as they used to do in the world. The polytheists, in desperation, will turn to the idols, saints, or beings they worshipped and call out for help. “But will receive no response.

” The Arabic “fa-da‘awhum fa-lam yastajībū lahum” conveys the absolute silence. There is no answer, no aid, no intercession without God’s permission. The idols are lifeless objects, and even those worshipped among the jinn or righteous humans will disown their worshippers.

The verse then states: “And We will make them all share in the same doom.” The word “mawbiqan” can mean a barrier of destruction, a shared place of ruin, or a valley in Hell. The worshippers and their false gods will be thrown together into the same punishment. For example, the stone idols will be heated and pressed against their worshippers, adding to the torment. The false gods become a cause of additional suffering, not rescue. This scene is a powerful deterrent against associating anything with God. It exposes the bankruptcy of shirk on the day when it matters most. The warning “beware of” reminds the listener that this Day is certain and one must prepare for it by purifying their faith now. It connects back to the earlier warnings and the parable of the gardens, showing the eternal consequences of what may seem like minor compromises in this life.

=53=

The punishment intensifies. “The wicked will see the Fire and realize that they are bound to fall into it.” The verb “ra’ā” indicates seeing with their own eyes, not just knowing theoretically. The reality of Hell will be directly in their sight.

The phrase “they are bound to fall into it” (annahum muwāqi‘ūhā) means they have a firm conviction that there is no escape and that they are inevitably heading into it. This is the moment of dreadful certainty before the actual entry. The psychological torment of seeing the destination and knowing it is inescapable is a punishment in itself. Then, “and will find no way to avoid it. ” The word “maṣrifan” means a place to turn away, a detour, or an escape route. They will desperately look for any exit, any way to divert from this fate, but none will exist. The Fire will surround them just as the walls of the Fire surrounded the wrongdoers described earlier. This verse represents the culmination of a life spent in heedlessness. Every warning they ignored, every opportunity for repentance they rejected, now stands before them as an unyielding reality.

For the believer, the vividness of this scene serves as a powerful motivator to seek God’s protection from the Fire and to live a life of obedience and repentance. It is an act of divine mercy that the Quran paints such images so that we might avoid them while there is still time.

The verse also completes the picture of the judgment scenes: the gathering, the presentation of the books, the confrontation with false gods, and now the sight of the final abode, all leading to an unchangeable fate.

=54=

The Quran now reflects on its own method, having just presented parables, narratives, and warnings. “We have surely set forth in this Quran every kind of lesson for people.

” The word “ṣarrafnā” implies variety, repetition, and careful arrangement. The Quran contains stories, laws, parables, threats, promises, and logical arguments, all designed to reach every type of heart and mind. No essential guidance is omitted. This rich tapestry of lessons is a mercy, providing multiple avenues for people to understand and return to their Lord.

Yet, despite this comprehensive presentation, there is a tragic human response: “but humankind is the most argumentative of all beings.” The word “jadal” refers to argumentation, disputation, and quarrelling. Instead of receiving the lessons with humility and acting upon them, many people use their intellect to argue against the truth, raise objections, and seek loopholes. This was evident in the Quraysh who questioned the Prophet about the cave sleepers and other matters not to learn but to test and dispute. It is also a general characteristic of those whose hearts are not receptive. They will argue about trivial details, like the number of the sleepers, while ignoring the profound lesson of resurrection. The verse serves as a mirror for every reader: when we engage with the Quran, is our approach one of seeking guidance with an open heart, or one of debating and finding excuses? The contrast between God’s exhaustive guidance and human disputatiousness highlights the depth of divine mercy and the tragedy of human stubbornness.

The following verses will illustrate the consequences of this argumentative attitude and clarify the role of messengers in the face of it.

=55=

The verse diagnoses the real barrier to faith. “And nothing prevents people from believing when guidance comes to them and from seeking their Lord’s forgiveness…” The Quran and the Prophet have brought clear guidance, and the door to forgiveness through repentance is wide open. There is no intellectual or practical obstacle on God’s side. So what is it that stops them?

The answer is a single, stubborn condition: “except their demand to meet the same fate of earlier deniers or that the torment would confront them face to face.” The disbelievers were not asking for more evidence; they were essentially demanding that God bring upon them a visible, overwhelming punishment like the flood of Noah, the winds of ‘Ad, or the thunderbolt of Thamud, or that they should see the torment of the Hereafter directly in this life. They said things like, “O Allah, if this is the truth, then rain down stones upon us!” They were mocking and challenging, not sincerely seeking. But when such punishment arrives, it is too late to believe; it is the end. The verse exposes the irrationality of this stance. It is like a person who refuses to believe a warning about a collapsing building unless the roof falls on his head. True guidance requires acting on the unseen with faith, not demanding to see the punishment first. By linking this attitude to the fate of earlier deniers, the Quran reminds the Quraysh and all such people that history is filled with examples of nations that made the same demand and were destroyed when the punishment came. This verse is a warning that God’s mercy is available now through faith and seeking forgiveness, but persisting in the demand for punishment will only bring about that very punishment, leaving no room for return.

=56=

This verse clarifies the mission of the messengers in light of the previous argument about punishment. “We do not send the messengers except as deliverers of good news and warners.” The role of the Prophet is not to bring the punishment on demand. He is a mubashshir (bearer of glad tidings) for those who believe, and a nadhīr (warner) for those who reject. His purpose is to convey, not to compel. The authority to send punishment belongs solely to God, according to His wisdom and timeline.

This is a consolation for the Prophet, who was grieved by their rejection and their demands for immediate torment. Then the verse describes the disbelievers’ reaction: “But the disbelievers argue in falsehood, hoping to discredit the truth with it. ” Instead of accepting the good news and heeding the warning, they engage in debate using false arguments (bāṭil). Their goal is not to find the truth but to refute and overcome it (li-yud’ḥiḍū bihi al-ḥaqq). They twist words, raise irrelevant questions, and employ sophistry. They also “make a mockery of My revelations and warnings. ” They turn the signs of God and the warnings of punishment into objects of ridicule, laughing at them instead of trembling before them. This behaviour is the height of spiritual corruption. The verse highlights the patience required of the messenger; his duty is simply to continue delivering the message with the qualities of a bearer of glad tidings and a warner, leaving the outcome to God.

For the believer, this verse teaches that the truth will always face arguments based on falsehood, and the proper response is steadfastness, not despair. It also underscores the seriousness of mocking divine signs, a sin that deeply hardens the heart.

=57=

The verse describes the most severe type of wrongdoing. “And who does more wrong than those who, when reminded of their Lord’s revelations, turn away from them and forget what their own hands have done?” The rhetorical question implies that no one is more unjust. They are reminded of the truth, but they actively turn away (a‘raḍa). Their turning away is not passive ignorance but deliberate avoidance.

Moreover, they “forget what their own hands have done.” They suppress the memory of their own sins, making them unwilling to face the need for repentance. Because of this persistent rejection and heedlessness, a divine consequence follows: “We have certainly cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend this Quran—and deafness in their ears. ” The veils (akinnah) on the heart prevent intellectual and spiritual understanding. The deafness (waqran) in the ears blocks the ability to hear it with benefit.

This is not an arbitrary punishment but the natural outcome of their repeated choice to turn away. When a person constantly ignores the truth, their spiritual faculties atrophy and become sealed. Then the final, sobering judgment: “And if you invite them to true guidance, they will never be rightly guided.

” This is not a statement that God’s mercy is limited, but that for those who have reached this stage of sealed hearts, no external effort will change them. The Prophet’s role is to convey, but guidance belongs to God. This verse is both a warning about the danger of persistent sin and a comfort for the Prophet that their rejection is not his failure. It closes the passage on the deniers with a note of finality, before transitioning to God’s mercy and the next story.

=58=

Immediately after the severe warning about sealed hearts, the Quran balances the discourse with a reminder of God’s attributes of mercy. “Your Lord is the All-Forgiving, Full of Mercy.” The words “Al-Ghafūr” and “Dhū ar-Raḥmah” emphasize that forgiveness and mercy are central to His dealing with creation.

The verse then presents a profound explanation for why the disbelievers are not immediately destroyed despite their crimes: “If He were to seize them immediately for what they commit, He would have certainly hastened their punishment. ” If God were to exact instant retribution for every sin, no human being would remain on the earth. The fact that they are given respite, enjoy provisions, and have time to reflect is a direct manifestation of His mercy.

However, this respite should not be mistaken for approval or weakness. “But they have an appointed time, from which they will find no refuge. ” There is an “ajal,” a fixed term decreed by God for individuals and nations. When that term arrives, there is no escape, no shelter, and no delay. The mercy of God offers a window of opportunity for repentance, but once it closes, justice prevails. This verse provides a complete framework for understanding the delay of punishment in this life. It is an act of grace, giving people chance after chance to turn back. The Prophet is being taught to view the persistence of the disbelievers through this lens: God is giving them time, so he should not grieve himself to death over their current state.

For the believer, it is a call to never despair of God’s mercy for oneself, while also never feeling secure from His plan. The appointed time is certain, and preparation must happen before it arrives.

=59=

The verse points to history as evidence for the principle just stated. “Those are the societies We destroyed when they persisted in wrong.

” The word “tilka al-qurā” refers to the well-known towns and nations of the past—‘Ad, Thamud, the people of Lot, and others—whose ruins were known to the Arabs along their trade routes. These societies were not destroyed arbitrarily.

The phrase “when they persisted in wrong” (lammā ẓalamū) indicates that the punishment came as a result of their continuous injustice and oppression, despite warnings. The root ẓulm includes disbelief, idolatry, and moral corruption. They crossed the limits and refused to reform. Then the verse adds, “and We had set a time for their destruction. ” The destruction had an appointed term (maw‘idan). It was not an impulsive act; God had predetermined the moment when their respite would end and their punishment would begin. This reinforces the message that the current respite given to the Meccan disbelievers is part of the same divine pattern. The history they see around them is a direct warning.

The verse also serves as a comfort to the believers who might wonder why the tyrants seem to get away with their injustice. The pattern is clear: the term is fixed, and when it comes, the destruction is complete.

The next section of the surah will seamlessly transition into the story of Moses and Al-Khaḍir, which further explores the theme of divine wisdom and the hidden timing of events. The destruction of the unjust societies and the appointed times all operate under a knowledge that human beings may not immediately grasp, a perfect introduction to the lessons Moses will learn about patience with the unseen wisdom of God.

=60=

The surah now begins the third major story, that of Moses and the righteous servant of God, traditionally known as Al-Khaḍir. The verse opens, “And remember when Moses said to his young assistant.” The young assistant, identified in hadith as Yusha‘ ibn Nūn (Joshua), was in the service of the great prophet Moses. Moses makes a solemn declaration of intent: “I will never give up until I reach the junction of the two seas, even if I travel for ages. ” The “junction of the two seas” (majma‘ al-baḥrayn) is the location where Moses was divinely informed he would meet a servant of God who possessed knowledge different from his own. The determination in Moses’ words is striking: “I will never give up” (lā abraḥu) means he will not cease his journey.

The phrase “even if I travel for ages” (aw amḍiya ḥuquban) indicates that he was prepared to go for a very long time, possibly years or decades, without stopping. This shows the immense zeal of Moses for knowledge and his readiness to endure hardship in its pursuit. The background to this journey is narrated in Sahih Bukhari and Muslim: Moses was once asked who was the most knowledgeable person, and he replied, “I am.” God then revealed to him that there was a servant at the junction of the two seas who had knowledge that Moses did not possess. Moses, with the humility of a great prophet, immediately set out to find him. This story introduces the theme of knowledge, its levels, and the need for patience when encountering things whose wisdom is not immediately apparent. The journey is a masterclass in the adab (etiquette) of seeking knowledge, and it begins with Moses’ sincere resolve and willingness to undertake great effort.
