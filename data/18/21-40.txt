=21=

The long, protective sleep finally gave way to a divinely orchestrated discovery. “That is how We caused them to be discovered” signals that the moment their presence became known was not an accident but part of the grand plan. The purpose was “so that their people might know that Allah’s promise of resurrection is true and that there is no doubt about the Hour.” The people of the city had been arguing about whether the dead would be raised. Witnessing these sleepers wake after centuries was a tangible, irrefutable sign that God can bring the dead back to life. It settled the debate not through philosophical argument but through a miracle standing before their eyes. After the discovery, “the people disputed with each other about the case of the youth after their death.” Once the youths had completed their divine mission and passed away for the final time, the townsfolk differed on how to honour them. Some, likely people of faith, proposed, “Build a structure around them.” This idea was to erect a wall or a monument that would preserve their memory as a sign. They wisely added, “Their Lord knows best about them,” a phrase filled with humility, acknowledging that the true sanctity of the sleepers was known fully only to God.

However, “those who prevailed in the matter” were not the humble believers. They were the influential, the people of power and authority. They declared, “We will surely build a place of worship over them.” This shift from a simple memorial to a masjid was a dangerous deviation. The Prophet ﷺ warned that such practices, turning graves of the righteous into places of worship, lead to veneration of humans rather than God and were a root of shirk among previous nations. The verse gently critiques the prevailing group’s solution, teaching the believers that even well-intentioned honour can cross the limits set by God. For us, it is a caution that reverence for saints must never evolve into worship of them or their relics. The story thus closes with a powerful lesson on the corruptibility of human remembrance when it detaches from the pure worship of the One who alone knows the unseen.

=22=

Having narrated the core of the story, the Quran addresses the inevitable human curiosity about its trivial details. The verse presents the speculations that people would engage in regarding the number of the sleepers. “Some will say, ‘They were three, their dog was the fourth,’ while others will say, ‘They were five, their dog was the sixth,’ only guessing blindly.

” The phrase “guessing blindly” (rajman bil-ghayb) dismisses these claims as conjecture without evidence, akin to throwing stones into the dark. A third opinion is mentioned: “And others will say, ‘They were seven and their dog was the eighth.’” The Quran does not explicitly endorse any of these numbers, though the way the third opinion is presented without the negative qualifier has led many scholars to consider it the most plausible.

However, the verse immediately commands the Prophet to respond in a way that redirects the focus: “My Lord knows best their exact number. Only a few people know as well.” This teaches that while some learned individuals might have access to certain knowledge through previous scriptures, the precise number is ultimately a matter of the unseen known fully only to God. The instruction that follows is direct and practical: “So do not argue about them except with sure knowledge, nor consult any of those who debate about them.” The Prophet and the believers are told not to enter into futile arguments with the People of the Book or others over such details. The only legitimate discussion is that which rests on clear revelation, and even that should not become a source of division. This verse is a profound lesson in epistemic humility. When God has chosen to leave a matter ambiguous, it is because the exact figure adds nothing to one’s faith or salvation. The real miracle is the resurrection sign, not the head count. By forbidding consultation with those who merely debate for sport, the Quran guards the community against losing sight of the spiritual substance of narratives in a sea of speculative disputes.

=23=

This verse begins a crucial lesson in etiquette directly tied to the story of the cave. When the Prophet ﷺ was asked about the sleepers, he had said, “I will answer you tomorrow,” hoping for revelation to come.

However, the revelation was delayed, causing him distress and giving the Quraysh an opportunity to mock him. After fifteen days, the surah was revealed with this corrective. The command “And never say of anything, ‘I will definitely do this tomorrow,’” is absolute. A person should never speak about a future action with unqualified certainty, because the guarantee of life, ability, and circumstances belongs to God alone. Tomorrow is entirely in the unseen; a thousand obstacles can prevent the intended deed. By stating it as a definitive, a person implicitly assumes a power they do not possess. The wording “I will definitely do” (innī fā‘ilun) reflects a firm resolution, but that resolution must be tempered by acknowledging God’s will.

This is not a discouragement from planning or having determination; the Quran itself instructs believers to be prepared and to strive. Rather, it is a spiritual discipline that keeps the heart attached to the Almighty at every moment. It reminds the believer that even the simplest intention—to wake up, to work, to meet someone—is only fulfilled by divine permission. The lesson from the Prophet’s experience is that his momentary omission of the phrase was a teaching moment for the entire Ummah. The Prophet, despite his status, was not exempted from this divine courtesy. The verse immediately ties to the next, which provides the necessary exception to the rule, showing that the solution is not to avoid planning but to always link the plan to the will of God.

=24=

The direct continuation from the previous verse completes the command. The phrase “without adding, ‘if Allah so wills!’” is the exception that must accompany every assertion about the future. “In shā’ Allāh” is not a mere ritual phrase; it is a verbal acknowledgment of God’s absolute sovereignty over time and events. By saying it, the believer hands over the outcome to God’s wisdom, expressing that the planned action will only occur if it aligns with His will. Then the verse mercifully addresses human weakness: “But if you forget, then remember your Lord.” The nature of human beings is to forget, and even the Prophet momentarily forgot. The remedy is to say the phrase the moment one remembers, even if the intended time has passed. Remembering God in this context means filling the gap left by forgetfulness with His remembrance.

Moreover, the person is taught to say, “I trust my Lord will guide me to what is more right than this.” This is a beautiful expression of hope. When a believer forgets to say “in shā’ Allāh” and perhaps misses the opportunity or the outcome differs from what they planned, they should not despair. Instead, they should express confidence that God will guide them to something closer to guidance and truth than what they originally intended. This phrase transforms a simple omission into an opportunity for deeper trust. The entire episode demonstrates how the story of the cave was not just a narrative but a direct source of practical guidance for the Prophet and his community. The connection between the delayed revelation and these verses teaches that every hardship in the mission had a wisdom behind it, and the etiquette taught here has become a hallmark of Muslim speech until the end of time.

=25=

Here the Quran provides one of the clear pieces of information regarding the people of the cave, revealing the duration of their stay. “They had remained in their cave for three hundred years.

” This is the length of their sleep according to the solar calendar. The phrase “adding nine” provides the count according to the lunar calendar, as the difference between three hundred solar years and three hundred lunar years is roughly nine years. By stating both, the Quran speaks precisely to both reckonings, demonstrating its divine origin and removing any potential source of contradiction. The detail is not presented as a point for debate but as a factual statement from the One who possesses complete knowledge. This stands in contrast to the earlier instruction not to argue about their number; here the duration is clarified because it serves a purpose. Three hundred and nine years is a span that stretches far beyond the limits of ordinary human life, making the preservation and awakening of the youths an undeniable sign of power over time and decay. It is long enough that civilizations had changed, their people had died, and their language and currency had become archaic, yet short enough that the cave itself could still be identified. The number also shows the meticulous care with which God preserves the details that truly matter for the lesson of resurrection. The verse leads into the next one where the Prophet is instructed to attribute ultimate knowledge to God, reminding the listener that even while giving the number, the Quran maintains the principle that only God knows the full unseen reality behind every detail. The figure of three hundred and nine years thus functions both as a concrete historical fact and as a springboard for reflecting on the relativity of time and the reality of the afterlife.

=26=

After providing the duration, the Quran immediately instructs the Prophet to declare that ultimate and complete knowledge of the matter rests with God alone. “Allah knows best how long they stayed” is not a contradiction to the previous verse but a nuancing of the epistemology. The three hundred and nine years is the reality that God has revealed and is true, but the exact conditions of their sleep, the precise state of their souls, and all the hidden aspects of their preservation are part of God’s exclusive knowledge. The verse expands this to a universal principle: “With Him alone is the knowledge of the unseen of the heavens and the earth. ” The entire realm of the ghayb, whether the secrets of the cosmos, the future, or the inner realities of hearts, belongs to Allah without any partner. The two attributes “How perfectly He hears and sees!” emphasize that nothing escapes His perception. He hears every whisper and sees every hidden state, including the condition of the sleepers through the ages. Then comes a profound declaration of God’s sole authority and care: “They have no guardian besides Him. ” The people of the cave had no human protector; God was their walī, and the same is true for all creation.

The verse ends with a monumental statement: “and He shares His command with none.” No one, not a prophet, an angel, or any saint, has a share in God’s dominion or decision-making.

This is a direct refutation of any belief that ascribes divine helpers or intercessors who can act independently. It reinforces the pure Tawhid that the surah champions. For the Prophet, this instruction was both a shield against the mockery of the Quraysh and a consolidation of his own reliance on God.

For the believer, it is a reminder that the only true source of knowledge about the unseen is divine revelation, and all other speculations must yield to what God has chosen to disclose.

=27=

With the story of the cave concluded, the address returns directly to the Prophet and, through him, to every believer. The command “Recite what has been revealed to you from the Book of your Lord” is an instruction to hold fast to the Quran, to convey it, to live by it, and to make it the centre of one’s being.

The phrase “from the Book of your Lord” personalizes the relationship; it is a message from the Most High to His servant. The recitation is not a mechanical reading but an act of engagement, contemplation, and submission.

This command was particularly crucial in Mecca where the opposition was intense, and the temptation to compromise or soften the message might arise. The verse immediately provides the reason for this steadfastness: “None can change His Words.” The Words of God are final, protected, and immutable. No human authority, no shifting cultural norms, and no power in the universe can alter what God has decreed and revealed.

This is a source of immense comfort and confidence. The truth the Prophet delivered was not subject to revision by the disbelievers, no matter how much they wished to change it.

The second part of the assurance is deeply personal: “nor can you find any refuge besides Him.” When one stands firm on the truth and faces the resulting hostility, there is no other sanctuary to run to except God. All human allies, fortresses, and strategies are ultimately powerless. True protection lies only with the One who revealed the Book. This verse serves as the thematic anchor for the following instructions about keeping company with the righteous and warning the heedless. It establishes that the believer’s mission is to recite and uphold the unchanging truth, and their sole reliance is on God, who is the only real guardian and protector.

=28=

This verse provides a social and spiritual directive that follows logically from the command to recite the Book. The Prophet is told, “And patiently stick with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure.” The “those” refers to the poor and humble believers who were mocked by the Quraysh elite. The chiefs of Mecca had demanded that the Prophet drive away these “lowly” companions if he wanted the nobles to sit with him. This verse was a decisive rejection of that demand.

The phrase “patiently stick with” (wa-ṣbir nafsaka) implies restraining oneself and enduring difficulty in keeping their company. Their quality is that they invoke God at the beginning and the end of the day, with pure intention, “seeking His pleasure” alone. Their value is not in wealth or social rank but in their sincere devotion. Then the instruction: “Do not let your eyes look beyond them, desiring the luxuries of this worldly life.” The Prophet is told not to divert his gaze away from these sincere believers towards the rich and powerful, hoping to attract them through compromise. The adornment of the world that the disbelievers enjoy should never be the object of longing.

Finally, the warning: “And do not obey those whose hearts We have made heedless of Our remembrance, who follow only their desires and whose state is total loss. ” God had sealed the hearts of certain leaders due to their persistent arrogance, making them heedless. Their lives are governed by whims, and their ultimate end is bankruptcy.

This verse establishes a timeless principle of community: the worth of a person is measured by their faith and devotion, not by their wealth or status. Believers are to prioritize the company of the sincere, even if society considers them insignificant, and to resist the temptation to flatter the powerful for worldly gain.

=29=

Having instructed the Prophet to stick with the believers and not obey the heedless, the Quran now teaches him to declare the truth with uncompromising clarity. “This is the truth from your Lord” anchors the message in divine authority. It is not the Prophet’s opinion but an absolute reality from the Creator. The statement that follows, “Whoever wills let them believe, and whoever wills let them disbelieve,” is not an endorsement of disbelief but a declaration of human responsibility and freedom of choice in light of the clear truth that has been presented. After the warning has been delivered, people are free to choose their path, but they must bear the consequences. The verse immediately details the consequence for those who choose wrong: “Surely We have prepared for the wrongdoers a Fire whose walls will completely surround them.” The imagery is that of being trapped, with no escape. The walls of Hell enclose them from all sides, indicating utter helplessness. Their torment is intensified by the description of the only relief offered: “When they cry for aid, they will be aided with water like molten metal, which will burn their faces.

” The word “muhl” describes a substance like the dregs of oil or molten copper—scalding, thick, and foul. Far from quenching thirst, it destroys the face as it is brought near.

The verse ends with two exclamations: “What a horrible drink! And what a terrible place to rest!” The exclamation form draws the listener into the horror of the scene.

This is the reality that awaits those who treat the truth as a matter of indifference. The stark, vivid description is meant to shake the heart and serve as a powerful deterrent, fully consistent with the Quran’s role as a warner and bringer of glad tidings.

=30=

After depicting the terrifying end of the wrongdoers, the Quran immediately balances the warning with a promise of perfect justice and mercy for the believers. “As for those who believe and do good” combines faith and righteous action, which are inseparable in the Quranic vision of salvation. The verse declares, “We certainly never deny the reward of those who are best in deeds.

” The Arabic construction “innā lā nuḍī‘u” uses an emphatic negation to stress that God will absolutely never let the reward of the doers of good go to waste. Not an atom’s weight of their righteous effort will be lost.

The phrase “those who are best in deeds” echoes the earlier verse about testing which of us is best in deeds. The reward is not merely for the quantity of action but for its quality—the sincerity, the adherence to prophetic guidance, and the excellence in execution. This verse is a profound source of hope. A believer might see the worldly prosperity of heedless people and feel that their own struggles in faith are unrewarded. But God affirms that the accounting is exact and the compensation unfailing. This assurance was critical for the early Muslims in Mecca, who had sacrificed social standing, family ties, and material comfort for their faith. It reminded them that God sees, records, and will recompense every hardship endured for His sake.

The verse also establishes a principle of divine fairness: God does not wrong anyone. The disbelievers earned their punishment through their own choices, and the believers will receive their full reward without reduction. It sets the stage for the detailed description of Paradise in the next verse, where the “fine reward” mentioned earlier in the surah begins to be painted in vivid colours.

=31=

The reward for those who believe and do good is now described in a series of beautiful images. “It is they who will have the Gardens of Eternity,” the phrase “Gardens of Eternity” (jannātu ‘adnin) implies a permanent, unchanging paradise, a dwelling that never decays. The imagery of “rivers flowing under their feet” creates a sense of continuous, effortless abundance. The water is not distant but flowing beneath them, easily accessible, providing coolness and life. Then, the personal adornment of the believers is described: “There they will be adorned with bracelets of gold, and wear green garments of fine silk and rich brocade.” Gold bracelets were a mark of royalty in ancient times, and silk was the fabric of kings, forbidden for men in this world as a test of restraint, but granted freely in the next. The colour green is associated with freshness, life, and tranquility.

The mention of “rich brocade” (istabraq) indicates thick, luxurious silk, emphasizing the tangible, sensory beauty of the reward. Their posture is that of complete peace and honour: “reclining there on canopied couches.” Reclining is the posture of those who are safe, satisfied, and free from worry. The canopied couches provide shade and privacy.

The verse then closes with two exclamations of praise: “What a marvellous reward! And what a fabulous place to rest!

” The word “marvellous” (ni‘ma) and “fabulous” (ḥasunat) invite the reader to reflect on the perfection of this final resting place, which is a rest not of sleep but of uninterrupted bliss. This description of Paradise stands in direct contrast to the horrible drink and terrible resting place of Hell. The structure of the surah continually places these opposites side by side, reinforcing the urgency of making the right choice. The reward is not abstract; it is a real, physical, and eternal honour from the Most Generous.

=32=

The surah now transitions into the second major story, that of the owner of the two gardens, by instructing the Prophet to “Give them an example.” This parable is designed to illustrate the dangers of arrogance, attachment to worldly wealth, and denial of the afterlife. “To the disbelieving one We gave two gardens of grapevines.” The description immediately highlights the extraordinary wealth granted to one of the two men. Gardens of grapevines were highly prized, representing luxury, shade, and delicious fruit. His property was not just a simple plot but two entire gardens, indicating double the bounty. Then, “which We surrounded with palm trees” adds another layer of value; date palms are tall, stately trees that provide protection, wood, and a different kind of sustenance. “And placed various crops in between” shows that his land was intensively cultivated, with grains and other plants filling every available space between the vines and palms, ensuring maximum yield and constant harvest. The detail of the description is important. It shows that the man’s wealth was vast and his property self-sustaining. The source of this wealth is clearly attributed to God: “We gave.” The man did not create these gardens; they were a divine grant, a test in disguise. The story is set in motion without yet naming the second man in detail. The listener is drawn into the scene, picturing a man walking through his magnificent property, his heart swelling with pride. The stage is set for the dialogue that will reveal the spiritual state of the wealthy man and the contrasting wisdom of his companion. The lesson for the Quraysh, who prided themselves on their trade and orchards, is obvious. They were being shown a mirror of their own potential fate if they did not recognize the true Giver of their blessings.

=33=

The description of the gardens continues with further emphasis on their perfection and self-sufficiency. “Each garden yielded all its produce, never falling short.

” The Arabic verb “ātā” conveys that the gardens gave their fruit and crops completely, without any failure, blight, or deficiency. There was no bad season, no failed harvest. This kind of consistent, flawless productivity is every cultivator’s dream.

The phrase “never falling short” indicates that the bounty was always at its peak, a state of perpetual abundance. This detail is crucial because it removes any potential justification the man might have had for his arrogance. His success was not due to his own skill or hard work overcoming natural difficulties; the gardens themselves were miraculously bountiful, a gift from God that required minimal anxiety. Then, “And We caused a river to flow between them” provides the final touch. A river flowing between the two gardens ensured constant irrigation, independence from rainfall, and enhanced beauty. The water was a lifeline, literally and aesthetically, weaving the two gardens together into a seamless paradise. All of these blessings were arranged by God, yet they would become the very source of the man’s spiritual ruin because of his response to them.

The verse teaches that great worldly success is not necessarily a sign of God’s pleasure; it is often a test. The richer and more trouble-free a person’s life becomes, the greater the danger of heedlessness and self-deification. For the early Muslims who owned little, this was a consolation: the splendour of the disbelievers was not to be envied, because it was the material of a severe examination. The stage is now fully set for the wealthy man to enter his garden and reveal the disease in his heart.

=34=

The Quran adds that the man’s assets were not limited to the gardens. “And he had other resources as well.

” The Arabic “thamara” can be understood as other fruits, profits, or forms of wealth such as gold, silver, servants, and livestock. His wealth was diversified and overflowing. This abundance became the fuel for his arrogance. “So he boasted to a poor companion of his.” The companion is commonly understood to be a believing man of modest means. The wealthy man, in the course of normal conversation, turned the interaction into a display of superiority. His words, “I am greater than you in wealth and superior in manpower,” reveal the core of his worldview. He measured worth exclusively by material possessions and social power. Wealth for him was a sign of personal greatness; manpower, meaning his family, servants, and retinue, signified his influence and invincibility. He completely ignored the spiritual reality that the poor companion might be far greater in the sight of God. The sin was not in possessing wealth but in the boastful, contemptuous attitude it fostered. This verse is a vivid depiction of the seduction of dunya (the worldly life). The wealthy man saw his gardens, his resources, and his people, and concluded that he was inherently superior. He forgot that all of it was a trust from God that could be taken away at any moment. For the Prophet’s community, this scene was a warning against looking at the rich with envy, and for the wealthy, it was a caution against the pride that leads to a fall. The dialogue is now underway, and the wealthy man’s following words, which he speaks as he enters his garden, will expose an even deeper layer of spiritual sickness.

=35=

The scene becomes more intimate and charged with spiritual significance. “And he entered his property, while wronging his soul.” The verse does not say he entered with his companion, but rather that he entered, and that his act of entering was a wrong against himself. The wrongdoing (ẓulm) was not a physical action but a state of heart: his pride, his denial of the transience of the world, and his attribution of his wealth to himself rather than to God. “Saying, ‘I do not think this will ever perish.’” The statement reveals a profound delusion. Standing amid the lush, perfectly yielding gardens, with the river flowing before him, he was so mesmerized by the apparent permanence of his riches that he denied the possibility of their extinction. He projected his present comfort into an indefinite future, forgetting that time, disaster, or divine decree could obliterate it all in a moment. This mindset is the essence of the worldly delusion: mistaking the temporary for the eternal. It echoes the earlier verse where Allah said He would reduce the earth’s adornments to barren ground. The man’s error was not merely a lack of intelligence but a moral and spiritual blindness. His heart had become so attached to his property that he could not conceive of life without it. This attitude directly contradicts the Quranic teaching that everything on earth is subject to decay and that only God remains. The verse sets up the dramatic irony of the story; the listener knows that this beautiful garden will soon be reduced to ruins. It is a timeless warning against the false sense of security that wealth can create, a security that prevents a person from preparing for the inevitable Day when all that they owned will be dust.

=36=

The man’s delusion deepens. From denying the destruction of his property, he moves to denying the ultimate reality: “nor do I think the Hour will ever come.” He not only doubts but actively disbelieves in the Resurrection. This reveals the link between materialism and denial of the afterlife. When a person’s heart is entirely filled with the enjoyment of this world, the idea of a divine judgment becomes an uncomfortable intrusion. Denying it is a way to justify the unchecked pursuit of pleasure. He then adds a hypothetical, arrogant statement: “And if in fact I am returned to my Lord, I will definitely get a far better outcome than all this.” His reasoning is corrupt. He assumes that his wealth and ease in this world are a sign of God’s favour, so if there is an afterlife, the same favour should logically continue. He measures the unseen by his limited, material experience.

This is a classic example of a person who views worldly prosperity as evidence of righteousness, a fallacy the Quran repeatedly refutes. God gives the world to those He loves and those He does not, but the Hereafter is only for the pious. The man’s statement is loaded with presumption. He does not say, “I hope for God’s mercy”; he declares with certainty that he will get a better return. This reflects a transactional relationship with God, as if God owed him something. The verse exposes the depths of spiritual ignorance that wealth can breed. It is a direct warning to those who, like the Quraysh, boasted of their riches and assumed that their worldly superiority would translate into eternal favour. The stage is now set for the powerful rebuttal from his believing companion, who will dismantle these illusions with words of faith and clarity.

=37=

The focus shifts to the other man, the believer, who now speaks. “His believing companion replied, while conversing with him.” The dialogue continues as a natural conversation between two people, not a hostile debate. The believer’s approach is instructive; he does not start by attacking the man’s wealth or pride, but goes directly to the foundation of faith. “Do you disbelieve in the One Who created you from dust?

” The question is designed to provoke reflection. The man is asked whether he can truly reject the Creator who fashioned his very being. The stages of creation are laid out: “from dust” refers to the origin of Adam, but also symbolizes the earthly components of the human body. “Then developed you from a sperm-drop” highlights the humble, almost insignificant beginning of every human life. “Then formed you into a man” celebrates the miraculous result: a balanced, intelligent, complete human being. The argument is that the One who initiated this journey from lifeless dust to a fully formed man is fully capable of resurrecting after death. Denying the Hour is illogical for someone who has witnessed the miracle of his own creation. This approach shifts the wealthy man’s gaze from his gardens to his own soul. The believer is not merely defending a doctrine; he is inviting his companion to recognize the signs of God within himself.

This verse teaches an important method of da‘wah: start with what is undeniable, such as the wonder of human creation, and then lead from there to the truths the person is denying. The believer’s speech is filled with a tone of concern and rational persuasion, setting a model for how to address those afflicted with arrogance.

=38=

The believer continues his testimony, shifting from addressing the companion’s disbelief to declaring his own faith. “But as for me: He is Allah, my Lord.

” The use of “but as for me” draws a clear line of distinction. The wealthy man had expressed doubt about the Hour and made arrogant claims, but the believer takes a stand on a completely different foundation. He affirms that the Creator he just described is specifically Allah, and that Allah is “my Lord.” This personal relationship of lordship means he accepts God’s guidance, submits to His commands, and trusts in His wisdom. He then makes the core declaration of Tawhid: “and I will never associate anyone with my Lord in worship.

” The word “never” (lā ushriku) is absolute, a lifelong commitment to pure monotheism. This is a deliberate contrast to the wealthy man’s implicit shirk—associating his own effort and wealth as partners in his success. The believer’s statement is not just a creed but a shield. Because he has made Allah his sole Lord, he is protected from the delusions of wealth. He recognizes that his poor condition does not diminish his Lord’s greatness, and the other man’s rich gardens do not indicate a flaw in God’s justice.

This verse teaches the power of a clear, personal testimony. In the face of a world that measures people by their possessions, the believer redefines honour through faith. His words are calm, firm, and unshaken by the wealth displayed before him.

This is the second time in the surah that a believer makes such a declaration, echoing the words of the youths in the cave who said, “We will never call upon any god besides Him. ” The consistent thread of Tawhid is the sword of the believers in every age, cutting through the glittering illusions of power and status.

=39=

The believer now gently corrects his companion, teaching him the words he should have uttered when he entered his magnificent garden. “If only you had said, upon entering your property, ‘This is what Allah has willed! There is no power except with Allah!’” The phrase “mā shā’ Allāh” is an attribution of all that exists to the will of God. By saying it, a person acknowledges that the beauty and productivity of the garden is not his own doing but the result of God’s will.

The second phrase, “lā quwwata illā billāh,” is a negation of any independent power or strength. It is a confession that the garden’s maintenance, protection, and continued existence depend entirely on God’s power, not human effort. Together, these phrases form a complete antidote to arrogance. The believer is not scolding the wealthy man for his words but expressing a profound wish that he had possessed this spiritual awareness. He then makes a striking admission: “Even though you see me inferior to you in wealth and offspring.” The believer is fully aware of his own worldly status. He does not deny that he has less money and fewer children, two of the primary measures of status in society.

Yet he does not allow this disparity to affect his faith or his sense of self-worth. He understands that value is not measured by these metrics.

This is a powerful lesson in self-confidence rooted in faith. The believer is not bitter about his poverty, nor is he impressed by the other’s riches. He stands on the truth and offers sincere advice.

The verse shows that a believer can acknowledge material inferiority without feeling diminished, because their real treasure lies in their relationship with God. It is this unshakable inner strength that enables him to deliver the warning in the next verse with clarity and courage.

=40=

The believer continues his speech with a statement of hope for himself and a warning for his companion, both anchored in the power of God. “Perhaps my Lord will grant me something better than your garden.

” The word “perhaps” (‘asā) expresses hopeful expectation, not doubt in God’s ability. He trusts that the God who gave his companion the gardens is able to give him, the believer, something even better—whether in this world or, more importantly, in the Hereafter. This shows that he does not covet the specific garden but desires what is with God. His hope is not empty optimism but confidence in divine generosity. Then he delivers the warning: “and send down upon your garden a thunderbolt from the sky, turning it into a barren waste.” A “ḥusbān” is a calamity from heaven, like a destructive hail, fire, or lightning, that can obliterate vegetation completely. The resulting “barren waste” (ṣa‘īdan zalaqan) is a ground so smooth and bare that nothing can grow, a stark contrast to the luxurious gardens. The believer is not cursing his companion out of malice; he is warning him of the possible consequence of his ingratitude and disbelief. He knows that God is capable of removing the blessing as easily as He gave it.

This warning is the culmination of the believer’s sincere counsel. He has reminded the rich man of his creation, declared his own faith, and now points to the reality of divine power over the very garden the man thinks will never perish. The stage is set for the next section, where exactly what the believer warned of comes to pass, and the wealthy man’s arrogant words are answered by the swift decree of God.
