=101=

This verse provides the defining characteristic of the disbelievers for whom Hell will be displayed. They are described as “those who turned a blind eye to My Reminder.

” The Arabic expression “kānat a‘yunuhum fī ghiṭā’in ‘an dhikrī” paints a vivid picture: their eyes were covered with a veil that prevented them from seeing the truth of God’s reminder. The “Reminder” refers to the Quran, the signs in creation, and the guidance brought by the prophets. This was not a case of innocent ignorance; they actively placed a covering over their spiritual sight. They chose not to look, not to reflect, and not to recognize the clear signs that surrounded them.

The second part intensifies their rejection: “and could not stand listening to it.” The Arabic “wa kānū lā yastaṭī‘ūna sam‘an” implies they were unable to bear hearing the revelation. The sound of the Quran, the call to faith, was so disturbing to their ingrained arrogance and lifestyle that they could not endure it. They would plug their ears, make noise to drown it out, or leave the gathering. This describes a willful, aggressive aversion. Together, the blind eye and the deaf ear form a complete rejection of the truth, sealing their fate. The verse follows directly from the display of Hell, answering the unspoken question of why they deserve such a fate. It also echoes the earlier verse about the veils cast over hearts and deafness in ears as a consequence of persistent turning away.

This is a warning to every listener: the doors of guidance are open, but if one repeatedly shuts their eyes and blocks their ears, the ability to perceive truth may be taken away. The verse underscores the importance of approaching the Quran with humility and openness, actively seeking to see and hear what God has revealed, rather than avoiding it out of fear that it will disturb one’s current way of life.

=102=

This verse forcefully refutes the notion of taking created beings as objects of worship or as independent intercessors. “Do the disbelievers think they can simply take My servants as lords instead of Me?” The rhetorical question expresses astonishment and condemnation.

The word “awliyā’” translated as “lords” can also mean patrons, protectors, or allies. The “servants” here include angels, prophets like Jesus, saints, or any righteous beings whom people elevate to divine status. It is the height of irrationality to abandon the worship of the all-powerful Creator and instead turn to servants who themselves are dependent on God. The verse implies that these servants would never consent to such worship; they are themselves devoted to God. This directly connects to the earlier rejection of the claim that Allah has offspring and the story of Satan’s rebellion, where taking him and his descendants as patrons was condemned.

The second part of the verse delivers the consequence: “We have surely prepared Hell as an accommodation for the disbelievers.” The word “nuzul” originally means a lodging or a welcome gift prepared for a guest. Here it is used with bitter irony; the only welcome awaiting those who take false lords is the Fire of Hell, prepared and ready. The verse dismisses any hope that such intermediaries could benefit them. In the authentic hadith about intercession, the Prophet ﷺ affirmed that intercession is only by God’s permission and for those with whom He is pleased; no one can act as an independent lord. This verse reinforces the core Islamic principle of Tawhid al-Uluhiyyah, the oneness of worship. It teaches that any devotion, supplication, or ultimate reliance directed to other than God is not merely a mistake but the very definition of disbelief that leads to Hell, a prepared abode that matches the enormity of the crime.

=103=

This verse introduces one of the most thought-provoking concepts in the Quran with a rhetorical question. The Prophet is commanded to say, “Shall we inform you of who will lose the most deeds?

” The Arabic “al-akhsarīna a‘mālan” refers to those in the greatest state of loss regarding their actions. The question is designed to capture attention and stir curiosity. Every human being naturally works and strives, assuming that their effort will yield some benefit. The notion that there are people whose entire life’s work will result in utter loss is alarming.

The question sets up the detailed answer in the following verse. The use of “shall we inform you” makes the listener lean in. It is an offer of crucial knowledge that can prevent a catastrophe. The theme connects back to the opening of the surah where Allah stated that He made the earth’s adornments as a test to see who is best in deeds. Here the focus shifts to those who fail that test most spectacularly. The loss is not a small deduction but a complete nullification of deeds.

The question also prepares the ground for understanding that outward success and visible productivity are not the criteria; the inner reality of faith and intention determines the value of deeds. For the companions and all believers, this verse prompts a continuous self-assessment: am I among those who will see their efforts turn to dust, or among those whose deeds are heavy on the scales? It is a sobering invitation to re-evaluate the direction of one’s life and the sincerity of one’s heart before the day when deeds are weighed.

=104=

This verse delivers the shocking answer to the question posed in the previous verse. “They are those whose efforts are in vain in this worldly life.

” The word “sa‘y” refers to earnest striving, effort, and hard work. These people are not lazy; they are active, industrious, and perhaps even accomplished by worldly standards.

Yet their entire effort goes to waste (ḍalāl) in the life of this world, meaning it yields no benefit for their Hereafter. The reason is then given with piercing irony: “while they think they are doing good!” Their perception of their own actions is completely inverted. They believe they are performing righteous, beneficial deeds, but in God’s sight, those deeds are worthless. This can apply to several categories. It includes disbelievers who perform humanitarian works but reject faith, because without the foundation of belief in God, deeds lack the soul that gives them eternal value. It also includes hypocrites who perform outward acts of worship for show rather than for God. The Prophet ﷺ warned in a famous hadith that the first people to be judged on the Day of Resurrection will be a martyr, a scholar, and a generous person, all of whom will be dragged to Hell because they performed their deeds to be seen and praised by people, not sincerely for God’s sake. This verse is a profound caution against self-deception. It teaches that a person can be completely deluded about their spiritual state. The remedy is constant self-examination, purifying one’s intentions, and measuring deeds against the revealed guidance rather than personal opinion or social approval.

The verse also offers a lens through which to understand the earlier stories: the owner of the two gardens thought his wealth was his great achievement, but it was his ruin; the disbelievers thought their worldly power was a sign of truth, but it was a deception.

=105=

This verse provides the root cause behind the devastating loss described previously. “It is they who reject the signs of their Lord and their meeting with Him.” The two fatal mistakes are identified: denial of God’s revealed signs and denial of the ultimate encounter with Him in the Hereafter. Rejecting the signs means refusing to accept the evidence of God’s oneness, power, and guidance, whether those signs are in the Quran or in the universe. Denying the meeting with God means disbelieving in the resurrection and judgment. These two rejections work together to strip deeds of all value. Without faith in God and the Last Day, a deed is like a body without a soul; it may appear alive but it is dead in terms of eternal consequence.

The phrase “rendering their deeds void” means their rejection corrupts and nullifies whatever good they might have done. Then comes the imagery of the Day of Judgment: “so We will not give their deeds any weight on Judgment Day.

” The Arabic “nuqīmu lahum yawma al-qiyāmati waznan” means God will not set up any scales to weigh their deeds because there is nothing of substance to weigh. Their deeds are light, insubstantial, like scattered dust.

The image of the scales is central to the Quranic depiction of judgment, where good and bad deeds are weighed with absolute justice. For these people, the scale will be empty of any good that matters. This verse reinforces the absolute necessity of correct belief. It warns that good works, no matter how numerous or impressive, cannot compensate for a lack of faith. The belief must be sound, rooted in acceptance of God’s signs and the reality of the return to Him.

For the believer, this is a call to hold fast to faith and to renew it constantly, ensuring that every action is built upon a foundation that will bear weight on the most critical day.

=106=

The verse pronounces the fitting recompense. “That is their reward: Hell.

” The word “dhālikum” points to the great loss and the lack of weight for their deeds, and sums it up as the punishment of Hell. The term “jazā’uhum” means their recompense, indicating perfect justice. They earned this outcome through their own choices. The two reasons are then explicitly stated: “for their disbelief and mockery of My signs and messengers.” Disbelief (kufr) is their covering up of the truth, and mockery (sukhriyyah) is their active ridicule and belittling of the revelations and the prophets who brought them. The combination of disbelief and mockery shows a deep-seated arrogance and disdain. It is not a passive rejection but an aggressive hostility. Earlier in the surah, verse 56 described how the disbelievers argue in falsehood to discredit the truth and make a mockery of God’s revelations. This verse confirms that such mockery is not a minor offense but a major cause of damnation. The mockery may have been a tool to suppress the truth and silence the believers, but on the Day of Judgment, it will be met with the severest punishment.

The mention of messengers alongside signs emphasizes that rejecting the human carriers of the message is equivalent to rejecting the message itself. The verse serves as a stark warning against taking the signs of God lightly. It also provides a measure of comfort to the believers who were mocked for their faith: the mockers will face the ultimate humiliation, while the believers will be honoured. It completes the description of the wrongdoers’ fate, preparing the heart for the immediate contrast with the reward of the believers in the next verse.

=107=

After the terrifying portrait of the losers, the Quran immediately shifts to the beautiful reward of the faithful, maintaining the surah’s balanced structure. “Indeed, those who believe and do good” repeats the central Quranic pairing of inner faith and outward righteous action. Belief is the foundation, and good deeds are its necessary expression.

The word “al-firdaus” translated as “Paradise” specifically refers to the highest and most elevated part of Jannah. The Prophet ﷺ said, “When you ask Allah, ask Him for al-Firdaus, for it is the middle of Paradise and the highest part of Paradise.

” This promise thus points to the best of the best. The verse states that they will have it “as an accommodation.

” The word “nuzul” is used, which we saw earlier in the context of Hell for the disbelievers. Using the same word for Paradise creates a powerful contrast.

For the disbelievers, Hell is their prepared welcome; for the believers, the Gardens of Firdaus are their prepared welcome. An “accommodation” suggests a place of rest, hospitality, and honour prepared in advance for honoured guests. It conveys the idea that Paradise is not merely a reward but a gift of divine hospitality, where every comfort is arranged. This connects to the earlier description in verse 31 of the Gardens of Eternity, with flowing rivers, gold bracelets, green silk garments, and canopied couches. The believer is not just escaping punishment but being ushered into a state of everlasting honour and delight. This verse is a source of immense hope, reminding the struggling believer that their efforts are seen and their final destination is a place prepared by the Most Generous Host. The contrast with the previous verses reinforces the gravity of the choice facing every human being.

=108=

This verse adds two essential qualities to the reward of Paradise. “Where they will be forever” (khālidīna fīhā) provides the guarantee of eternity. The fear of loss, death, or eviction that accompanies every worldly pleasure is entirely absent. This eternal nature was first mentioned in the surah’s third verse regarding the believers’ reward, and it is repeated here to cement the point. Every moment of joy in this world is tainted by the awareness that it will end. In Paradise, the joy is absolute because it is unending.

The second quality is even more profound: “never desiring anywhere else.” The Arabic “lā yabghūna ‘anhā ḥiwalan” means they will not seek to transfer or be removed from it, nor will they ever desire a change or a substitute. This indicates complete and utter contentment. The human soul in this world is fickle; even in the best situations, it longs for something different, something more. In Paradise, the soul is perfectly satisfied. What God has prepared is exactly and infinitely what the soul desires. There is no boredom, no restlessness, no wish for anything different.

The word “ḥiwal” suggests a turning away or a shift; the inhabitants will never imagine a better state, because no better state exists. This verse answers a deep-seated human fear: what if Paradise eventually becomes monotonous? The Quran assures us that it is a dynamic, ever-renewing, and perfectly satisfying existence. This completion of contentment is a gift from God, the ultimate peace of heart. It connects to the story of the cave youths, who were given peace and provision beyond imagination, and to the believing companion who hoped for something better than the earthly garden.

The final home far surpasses any worldly abode, and its inhabitants will dwell there in eternal, undisturbed bliss.

=109=

This verse unveils the limitless nature of God’s knowledge and speech. The Prophet is instructed to say, “If the ocean were ink for writing the Words of my Lord…” The image is vast and majestic: the entire ocean, an unimaginably large body of water, is turned into ink. All of it is used to write the Words of God—His decrees, His knowledge, His wisdom, His commands, and the revelations that proceed from Him.

The verse then makes a staggering claim: “it would certainly run out before the Words of my Lord were finished. ” The ocean, despite its immensity, would be exhausted while God’s Words would still remain unwritten. And even if another ocean like it were brought as a refill, and then another, the same depletion would occur.

This is a declaration of the infinite nature of divine knowledge. The context of revelation sheds light on this verse. The Jews told the Meccans that they had been given abundant wisdom, citing a statement from their scriptures. This verse was revealed to demonstrate that all the knowledge given to humanity, including the Torah and the Quran, is but a drop from the endless ocean of God’s Words. The verse connects profoundly with the surah’s themes. The cave sleepers’ duration, the hidden wisdom of Al-Khaḍir, the unseen realms known only to God—all are part of these infinite Words. The finite human mind cannot encompass God’s knowledge. This realization fosters humility. It also comforts the believer that God’s power and wisdom are inexhaustible; He never runs out of means to create, provide, and solve problems. The verse echoes the earlier command to say “Allah knows best” and the affirmation that the unseen belongs to God. It is an invitation to trust the all-knowing Lord, whose wisdom far exceeds any human comprehension.

=110=

The surah concludes with a final, comprehensive declaration of the core of the prophetic message. The Prophet is commanded to say, “I am only a man like you.

” This is a profound statement of humility and a rejection of any claim to divinity or superhuman status. The Prophet is a mortal, sharing the same human nature as those he addresses, subject to the same needs and limitations. This counters the exaggeration of prophets into gods, as happened with Jesus, and reinforces that the Prophet is a servant, as stated in the surah’s opening verse.

Yet there is a crucial distinction: “but it has been revealed to me that your God is only One God.” The conjunction “but” (illā) introduces the unique factor—divine revelation. His humanity is shared; his reception of revelation is what sets him apart as a messenger. The content of that revelation is the absolute oneness of God, Tawhid, which is the central theme of the entire surah. Then, the verse delivers the final, practical prescription: “So whoever hopes for the meeting with their Lord, let them do good deeds and associate none in the worship of their Lord.” This sentence encapsulates the path to salvation. “Hopes for the meeting” means believing in the resurrection and desiring God’s reward with a combination of hope, fear, and love. The response to this hope is twofold: perform righteous deeds (‘amal ṣāliḥ) and maintain pure Tawhid by not associating any partner in the worship of the Lord. These two elements—correct action and pure faith—have been the consistent criteria for success throughout the surah, from the people of the cave to the believing companion to the righteous ruler Dhul-Qarnain.

The verse ends with the emphatic prohibition of shirk, closing the surah as it began, with the declaration of praise for God who sent down the Book without crookedness, guiding to a straight path of pure, unadulterated worship and righteous living.
