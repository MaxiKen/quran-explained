=61=

The journey that Moses undertook with his young assistant finally reaches its first milestone. “But when they finally reached the point where the seas met” refers to the exact location God had described, where the two bodies of water converge. Commentators have discussed where this might be—some suggest it is where the Red Sea meets the Indian Ocean, others say it is where the sea of Moses’ time met a river, but the precise geography is not the Quran’s concern. The focus is on what happened there as a sign. “They forgot their salted fish” tells us that they had carried a fish, likely salted and dried, as their travel provision. The forgetting was not a random lapse but part of the divine arrangement to mark the spot where the knowledgeable servant would be found. In a well-known hadith narrated by Ubayy ibn Ka‘b, the Prophet ﷺ explained that the fish was dead and placed in a basket. When they reached the appointed place, the fish came to life, sprang out of the basket, and “made its way into the sea, slipping away wondrously. ” The Arabic “fa-ttakhadha sabīlahu fī al-baḥri ‘ajaban” captures the miraculous nature of the event. The fish’s path into the water became a tunnel or a passage, and the water around it stood still like an arch. For Yusha‘, this was an astonishing sight, but he was distracted and forgot to mention it. The forgetting itself was a test and a means to make them retrace their steps.

This verse teaches that even seemingly small lapses of memory can be part of a larger divine plan to guide a person to a deeper truth. It also shows that God’s signs can appear in the most ordinary things—a fish, a rock, a moment of rest—but recognizing them requires attentiveness. The servant of God was near, and the sign was meant to pinpoint the location, yet human forgetfulness nearly caused them to pass it by. The story subtly reminds the believer that spiritual milestones are often preceded by tests of alertness and that nothing in the journey of seeking knowledge happens without divine orchestration.

=62=

After leaving the junction of the two seas, Moses and Yusha‘ continued walking. “When they had passed further” indicates they travelled beyond the appointed spot without realizing they had already reached it. Human determination, even that of a great prophet, can sometimes overshoot the mark when divine guidance has not yet fully clarified the location. Moses then turned to his assistant and said, “Bring us our meal! We have certainly been exhausted by today’s journey.” The request for food was natural; they had been travelling with great resolve, and the physical demands of the journey had caught up with them.

The word “laqad laqīnā” emphasizes that they had encountered severe fatigue and toil on this particular stage of their travel. This moment of physical weakness and hunger became the occasion for remembering the forgotten fish. It is a profound lesson that our bodily needs, which we often see as distractions from spiritual pursuits, can actually serve as the trigger for remembering divine signs. The hunger of Moses opened the door to the recollection of the miracle.

The verse also highlights the humaneness of the prophets. Moses was not a superhuman being immune to tiredness or hunger. He felt the strain of the journey just as any of us would, yet his quest for knowledge did not stop. He sought to replenish his strength so he could continue. For the seeker of knowledge today, this is a reminder that taking care of one’s physical needs is part of the journey, not a betrayal of it. The request for the meal sets the stage for Yusha‘s response, which will bring the forgotten miracle back to memory and redirect their path precisely to where God intended them to be.

=63=

Yusha‘’s response immediately clarifies the mystery. “He replied, ‘Do you remember when we rested by the rock?’” The rock was a specific landmark near the junction of the two seas. It was at that place, while they paused to rest, that the miracle occurred. Yusha‘ then confesses, “That is when I forgot the fish. ” His forgetting was not mere negligence; it was an oversight that had profound consequences. He then provides a striking theological explanation: “None made me forget to mention this except Satan. ” This statement teaches an important aspect of Islamic belief. Forgetfulness in matters of duty and obedience can be from Satan, who seeks to distract humans from recognizing God’s signs. Yusha‘ does not absolve himself entirely but attributes the cause to Satanic influence, showing an awareness of the spiritual battle behind even small lapses.

However, he also describes the miracle with wonder: “And the fish made its way into the sea miraculously.” The word “‘ajaban” conveys his astonishment. The fish coming to life and taking a path through the sea was a clear, unmistakable sign that they had been at the appointed place. His description confirms that what he witnessed was no ordinary event but a tangible act of divine power. This verse demonstrates the importance of honest reporting in the student-teacher relationship. Yusha‘ could have hidden his forgetfulness out of embarrassment, but he truthfully recounted what happened, including the miracle. His honesty provided Moses with the exact information needed to recognize that this was the sign they were seeking. It also shows that even the closest companions of prophets can be momentarily distracted by Satan, but that does not diminish their overall virtue. The sincere admission of a mistake and the accurate reporting of a sign pave the way for the next phase of the journey.

=64=

Upon hearing Yusha‘s account, Moses immediately recognized the sign. “Moses responded, ‘That is exactly what we were looking for.’” The Arabic “dhālika mā kunnā nabghi” expresses a moment of clarity and confirmation. The miraculous revival of the fish was the precise marker God had provided. There was no hesitation, no doubt. Moses connected the sign with the goal, demonstrating the keen spiritual insight of a prophet. He understood that the God who brought the fish to life would also guide him to the servant of knowledge. The response also shows Moses’ humility. Despite his own immense status as a prophet who spoke directly with God, he eagerly sought a teacher from whom he could learn.

The phrase “what we were looking for” places Moses and his assistant together as joint seekers, showing the collegial nature of the quest. Then they took action: “So they returned, retracing their footsteps.

” The word “fa-irtaddā” means they turned back, carefully following the same path they had just walked. They did not bemoan the wasted steps or complain about the extra effort. The joy of finding the sign made the return journey light. This teaches a profound lesson: when a person realizes they have passed by a place of spiritual benefit or a source of knowledge, they should be willing to go back, even if it means effort and humbling themselves. The world often tells us to always move forward, but the path to divine wisdom sometimes requires a return—a retracing of steps, a revisiting of a moment where God’s sign was present but overlooked. The journey of seeking knowledge is not linear; it includes detours, forgetfulness, and corrections, all of which are part of the training. Their return to the rock leads directly to the encounter that will change Moses’ understanding of knowledge itself.

=65=

They arrived at the rock and found the one they had travelled so far to meet. “There they found a servant of Ours” introduces the figure without naming him, though authentic hadith identify him as Al-Khaḍir. The description “servant of Ours” (‘abdan min ‘ibādinā) is the highest honour; it places him in the category of devoted worshippers of God.

The phrase immediately dispels any idea that this person was an angel or a prophet in the conventional legislative sense, as the majority of scholars hold that he was a righteous servant given special knowledge. The verse then describes two qualities: “to whom We had granted mercy from Us.” The mercy is a direct gift from God’s own presence, implying a special intimacy and divine care. This mercy enveloped him inwardly and outwardly, making him a recipient of God’s compassion and blessings.

The second quality is “and enlightened with knowledge of Our Own. ” The word “‘ilman min ladunnā” is profoundly significant. It is “ladunnī” knowledge, knowledge directly from God, not acquired through study or human instruction. It is an inner illumination regarding the hidden realities of events, the secrets of divine decree, and the wisdom behind occurrences. This type of knowledge is different from the knowledge of law and prophecy that Moses had. Moses was given the Torah, a clear shariah for his people, while Al-Khaḍir was given insight into the unseen layers of events. The combination of mercy and knowledge made him the perfect teacher for the specific lesson Moses needed: that not everything that appears bad is truly evil in God’s plan, and that human intellect, even prophetic intellect, has limits. This verse elevates the station of sacred knowledge and reminds believers that God bestows His gifts upon whom He wills, opening unseen doors of understanding to His sincere servants.

=66=

The encounter begins with Moses displaying exemplary etiquette. “Moses said to him, ‘May I follow you?’” The form is a polite, humble request. Moses, the great prophet to whom God spoke directly, asks for permission to be a follower. He does not demand or assume authority.

This is the first lesson for every student of knowledge: approach the teacher with humility, even if the student holds high worldly or religious status. The phrase “provided that you teach me some of the right guidance you have been taught” clarifies the purpose of the following. Moses is specific: he wants to learn from the “rushd,” the sound guidance and wisdom that Al-Khaḍir has been taught. He acknowledges that the knowledge Al-Khaḍir possesses is a gift from God, not his own invention. Moses uses the word “mimmā ‘ullimta” (from what you have been taught), showing respect for both the teacher and the ultimate source of knowledge. This request also indicates Moses’ intellectual humility. He already possessed the Torah and was the leader of the Israelites, but he recognized that God’s knowledge is vast and that there were areas of understanding he had not been given. The verse is a direct refutation of the arrogance that sometimes afflicts learned people. No matter how much one knows, there is always someone who knows something different, and the path of wisdom is to seek it with an open heart. The seeker is not diminished by following; he is elevated. Moses’ words became a timeless model for the teacher-student relationship, demonstrating that the pursuit of knowledge is an act of worship that requires patience, humility, and a sincere intention to follow the truth wherever it leads.

=67=

Al-Khaḍir’s response is direct and realistic. “He said, ‘You certainly cannot be patient enough with me.’” The Arabic “innaka lan tastaṭī‘a ma‘iya ṣabran” uses the emphatic particle “lan” to indicate that Moses will not be able to endure what he will witness.

This is not a rejection but a statement of fact based on Al-Khaḍir’s divine knowledge. He knows the nature of the events that will unfold and the natural human reaction to apparent injustice, harm, and irrationality. Moses, whose mission was to establish justice, uphold the law, and command good and forbid evil, would inevitably be shocked by actions that seemed to contradict all these principles. The patience required is not ordinary patience in the face of hardship, but a specific endurance in the face of cognitive dissonance—seeing things that make no sense according to conventional morality and law. Al-Khaḍir is essentially preparing Moses for a test of his ability to suspend judgment.

This verse teaches a crucial principle: there are levels of knowledge and wisdom that require an entirely different kind of patience. A person may be patient with physical pain or personal insult, but being patient with intellectual confusion, with seeing an apparently evil act that one cannot yet justify, is far more difficult. The teacher’s role here is to set clear expectations and warn the student of the challenge ahead.

This is a sign of Al-Khaḍir’s own wisdom and mercy; he is not setting Moses up for failure without warning. The verse also implies that the journey ahead will be psychologically and spiritually demanding, preparing the listener for the three shocking events to come. It is a reminder that true learning often involves the painful process of unlearning one’s initial assumptions.

=68=

Al-Khaḍir elaborates on his warning with a rhetorical question. “And how can you be patient with what is beyond your realm of knowledge?

” The phrase “mā lam tuḥiṭ bihi khubran” literally means “that which you have not encompassed in knowledge.” The word “khabar” implies deep, comprehensive understanding. Al-Khaḍir is saying that the events Moses will witness are outside the scope of his prophetic knowledge and his human experience. Moses’ knowledge, vast as it was, was primarily the outer, legal, and revealed knowledge for guiding a community. Al-Khaḍir’s knowledge is the inner knowledge of hidden realities and divine decrees. Without that deeper context, the actions will appear completely wrong.

The question underscores a fundamental epistemological point: a person cannot have full patience with something they do not understand. Impatience arises when the mind cannot make sense of what it sees. If a doctor performs a painful surgery on a child, the child screams and resists because they lack the knowledge that the pain is healing. The parent who understands the surgeon’s intent can remain patient. Moses will be like the child who sees only the surface pain, and Al-Khaḍir will be like the surgeon who sees the cure.

This verse teaches humility about the limits of one’s own knowledge. In life, believers often encounter situations that seem utterly unfair or senseless.

This verse reminds them that they may be in a position similar to Moses, lacking the complete picture that God possesses. The proper response is not to rush to judgment or to lose faith, but to trust that the One who sees the full picture has a wisdom that will eventually be revealed or rewarded.

This is a profound comfort for those going through inexplicable trials, and a caution against condemning what one does not fully understand.

=69=

Moses, eager to begin his learning, responds with a sincere promise. “Moses assured him, ‘You will find me patient, Allah willing.’” His words are full of resolve. He does not say, “I will definitely be patient,” relying solely on his own strength. Instead, he attaches his promise to the will of God: “Allah willing” (in shā’ Allāh).

This is the very etiquette taught earlier in the surah when Allah commanded the Prophet never to say of anything, “I will definitely do it tomorrow,” without adding “if Allah wills.” Moses, the prophet, demonstrates this lesson perfectly. He knows that patience is a virtue granted by God, not merely a product of human willpower. By saying “Allah willing,” he places his trust in God and seeks divine assistance for the challenge ahead. He then adds, “and I will not disobey any of your orders.

” This is a comprehensive commitment to the role of a student. He pledges full compliance with the teacher’s instructions, recognizing that the path of learning requires following, not questioning.

This is the second time in the surah that a believer makes a solemn declaration of intent to follow guidance. The youths in the cave said, “We will never call upon any god besides Him,” and Moses says, “I will not disobey any of your orders.” Both statements reflect a total submission of the self for the sake of truth. Moses’ promise sets the contract between him and Al-Khaḍir.

However, the story will reveal that the reality of witnessing injustice is far more emotionally overwhelming than the abstract idea of being patient. Moses’ sincere intention is clear, and God knows his truthfulness, yet the human element of immediate moral reaction will prove difficult to suppress. This teaches us that good intentions must be accompanied by divine help, and even the best of us can be caught off guard by the intensity of a test.

=70=

Al-Khaḍir accepts Moses’ pledge but lays down a strict condition. “He responded, ‘Then if you follow me, do not question me about anything until I myself clarify it for you.’” The condition is absolute: no questions, no objections, no expressions of disapproval, regardless of what Moses sees.

The phrase “until I myself clarify it for you” (ḥattā uḥditha laka minhu dhikran) places the entire initiative for explanation in the teacher’s hands. Al-Khaḍir retains the right to explain events only at the time and in the manner he chooses. This condition is not harshness; it is the necessary pedagogical framework for the type of knowledge being transmitted. The lesson Moses is about to learn cannot be absorbed if he interrupts with his own moral reasoning after each event. He must first witness the totality of the actions, feel the weight of the perplexity, and then receive the explanation that ties everything together. This condition is also a test of Moses’ sincerity and his earlier promise. True following means trusting the guide, even when the path seems to lead into darkness. For the student of sacred knowledge, this verse teaches the importance of discipline in the learning process. There are stages in understanding where questioning prematurely can block the deeper insight. The teacher knows the sequence of unveiling, and the student must trust that sequence. This does not promote blind following in all matters; rather, it establishes a specific contract for a specific journey. Al-Khaḍir is not asking Moses to accept falsehood, but to delay his judgment until the hidden wisdom is revealed. With the terms set and accepted, the two set out together, and the astonishing events begin to unfold.

=71=

The journey of learning begins. “So they set out” marks the commencement of their travels together. “But after they had boarded a ship, the man made a hole in it.” The incident happens swiftly. They were welcomed aboard a vessel that was presumably carrying innocent people. Al-Khaḍir’s action was shocking: he deliberately damaged the ship by making a hole in its hull. From any conventional perspective, this was an act of sabotage that endangered the lives of everyone on board. Moses, witnessing this, could not contain his reaction. “Moses protested, ‘Have you done this to drown its people? You have certainly done a terrible thing!’” The Arabic “kharqahā li-tughriqa ahlahā” directly links the hole to the potential drowning. Moses’ mind, trained in justice and the protection of life, immediately saw the apparent crime. His exclamation “laqad ji’ta shay’an imran” uses the word “imr,” meaning a grave, horrifying, and monstrous deed. Moses broke his silence, forgetting the condition he had accepted, because the urgency of the situation overwhelmed him. His protest was not out of arrogance but out of sincere concern for the innocent. This first test targeted Moses’ deep-seated sense of justice. It reveals that even a prophet, when faced with what seems to be clear-cut evil, finds it extremely difficult to remain silent. The verse captures the raw human struggle between a commitment to spiritual discipline and the instinctive moral outrage at visible harm. It also sets up the pattern for the next two events, where each apparent transgression will escalate in severity, testing Moses’ patience to its absolute limit.

=72=

Al-Khaḍir’s response is not an explanation but a calm reminder of the contract. “He replied, ‘Did I not say that you cannot have patience with me?’” The question is gentle but firm. It points Moses back to his own limitations, which were forewarned. Al-Khaḍir does not show anger or frustration; he simply reiterates the reality he had stated at the outset.

This is the role of a wise teacher when the student stumbles: not to humiliate or dismiss, but to remind the student of the agreed-upon terms and the challenge they are facing. The reminder serves multiple purposes. It re-establishes the teacher’s authority without harshness. It helps Moses become conscious of his reaction and recognize that his impatience is exactly what was predicted. This self-awareness is the first step toward growth.

The question also implicitly invites Moses to reflect: “If you could not bear this first act, how will you bear what is to come?” The nature of the events will only get more difficult. Al-Khaḍir’s calmness in the face of Moses’ outburst teaches that the bearer of hidden wisdom is not disturbed by the surface judgments of others because they know the deeper reality. For the student of knowledge, this verse is a lesson in receiving correction. When a teacher points out a mistake, the proper response is not defensiveness but acknowledgment. Moses’ next words will demonstrate exactly that. The exchange also deepens the listener’s curiosity: what possible justification could exist for endangering a ship full of people?

The answer will come only at the end of the journey, teaching the believer that some divine actions only make sense when the complete picture is revealed.

=73=

Moses’ reaction to the reminder is a model of humility and accountability. “Moses pleaded, ‘Excuse me for forgetting, and do not be hard on me.’” He does not argue, justify his outburst, or claim that the situation was so extreme that his protest was warranted. Instead, he immediately attributes his lapse to forgetfulness. “Lā tu’ākhidhnī” means “Do not take me to task” or “Do not hold me accountable. ” He admits that he forgot the condition he had sworn to uphold.

The Arabic “nisyān” is the same word used for Yusha‘ forgetting the fish. The parallel is striking: just as Yusha‘ forgot the miracle and was excused, Moses now asks for excusal for his own forgetfulness. This teaches a profound interconnectedness in the story. Both the assistant and the great prophet experienced moments of human weakness, and the proper response was mutual gentleness. Moses then adds, “and do not be hard on me” (wa lā turhiqnī min amrī ‘usran). He is asking Al-Khaḍir not to make the journey overly difficult for him, not to impose a harsh penalty, and to continue to treat him with kindness despite his mistake.

The word “‘usran” implies hardship and constriction; Moses is asking for spaciousness and ease in the teacher-student relationship. This is a beautiful example of how a student should recover after a misstep: acknowledge the error without making excuses, attribute it to human frailty, and ask for continued mercy and guidance. Al-Khaḍir’s acceptance of this plea demonstrates the importance of mercy in teaching. The journey will continue, and Moses will be given another chance. This verse instills hope that mistakes on the path of learning do not mean the end of the road, provided the heart remains sincere and humble.

=74=

The journey continues, and the second test is far more severe than the first. “So they proceeded until they came across a boy, and the man killed him.” Without any preamble or explanation, Al-Khaḍir took the life of a young boy. In the scale of moral atrocities, the deliberate killing of a child is among the most heinous. For Moses, this was beyond comprehension. The verse states his protest in powerful terms: “Have you killed an innocent soul, who killed no one?

” The Arabic “nafsan zakiyyatan” describes a soul that is pure and innocent, completely free of any crime that could justify execution. Moses emphasizes that this boy had not taken another life, so there was no retribution due. His shock is captured in the second exclamation: “You have certainly done a horrible thing!

” The word “nukran” indicates something far more severe than the “imran” he used for the ship incident. It means a deed that is utterly evil, detestable, and unacceptable by every moral standard. This time, Moses’ protest was not about property damage but about a human life. The intensity of his reaction reflects the depth of his commitment to the sanctity of life, which is a core principle of the Mosaic law. From his perspective, the teacher had crossed a red line that no wisdom could justify. The listener, too, is brought to the edge of moral tolerance. How could a merciful servant of God commit such an act?

The question hangs in the air, heavy and unresolved. This verse confronts the believer with the most difficult aspect of the story: the acceptance that God’s wisdom can encompass actions that, on the surface, appear absolutely evil. It forces a deep reflection on the limits of human judgment when it lacks knowledge of the unseen.

=75=

Once again, Al-Khaḍir responds with the same calm reminder, repeating exactly the words he used after the first incident. “He answered, ‘Did I not tell you that you cannot have patience with me?’” There is no immediate explanation, no soothing of Moses’ horror.

The repetition of the identical phrase is itself a lesson. It reinforces the consistency of the condition. Al-Khaḍir is not reacting emotionally to Moses’ outburst; he is simply holding up the mirror of their original agreement. The calmness in the face of such a severe accusation is extraordinary. It indicates that Al-Khaḍir’s knowledge of the hidden reality made him impervious to the surface judgment. He knew that what he had done was, in its ultimate reality, a mercy and a protection, even though it appeared as the most abominable crime.

This repetition also highlights the persistence of human nature. Moses had already stumbled once and been reminded, yet he stumbled again because the test was exponentially harder. The teacher’s patience is remarkable; he does not abandon the student after repeated failure. He simply asks the question again, leaving the door open for Moses to reflect on his own inability to remain silent. The verse subtly teaches that spiritual growth often involves repeated stumbles in the face of escalating trials. The fact that the test became harder after the first failure shows that God was training Moses through these experiences.

For the believer, this verse is a comfort: when we fail a test, God may give us another, more difficult one, not to break us, but to build our reliance on Him and to teach us that true patience is a gift that must be sought anew every moment.

=76=

Moses, now deeply aware of his repeated failure, makes a solemn and final pledge. “Moses replied, ‘If I ever question you about anything after this, then do not keep me in your company.’” He sets a penalty for himself: expulsion from the teacher’s company.

This is a serious self-imposed condition because the entire purpose of the journey was to stay with Al-Khaḍir and learn. He is essentially saying, “If I fail again, I forfeit the privilege of your companionship. ” Then he adds, “for by then I would have given you enough of an excuse.

” The Arabic “balaghta min ladunnī ‘udhran” means that he would have exhausted all valid excuses, and Al-Khaḍir would be fully justified in parting ways with him. Moses acknowledges that two warnings are more than sufficient. A third violation would prove that he cannot abide by the necessary discipline.

This is a moment of profound self-awareness and humility. Moses does not promise that he will definitely be patient; he has learned that his own strength is insufficient. Instead, he sets a boundary for the relationship, recognizing that there must be a limit to the teacher’s forbearance.

This is a mature understanding of the student-teacher dynamic. A student who repeatedly breaks the rules eventually makes the teaching relationship untenable, regardless of the teacher’s mercy. Moses’ words also reflect the Islamic principle of ‘udhr—giving an excuse and acknowledging when one has been given enough chances. The verse sets the stage for the third and final incident. The listener knows that if Moses breaks his silence again, the journey will end. The tension is palpable, and the next event will bring the story to its climax, forcing Moses to choose between his deep-seated moral instincts and the commitment he has just made.

=77=

The final leg of the journey brings them to a town. “So they moved on until they came to the people of a town.” They were tired and hungry after their travels, so they “asked them for food, but the people refused to give them hospitality.

” The Arabic “fa-abau an yuḍayyifūhumā” shows that the townspeople actively refused to host or feed these weary strangers. This was a gross violation of the ancient Near Eastern codes of hospitality, which were sacred duties. Al-Khaḍir and Moses were treated with stinginess and coldness.

Yet, as they walked through this unwelcoming town, “there they found a wall ready to collapse.” The wall was tilted and on the verge of falling, posing a danger. Instead of leaving it or demanding compensation, “the man set it right.” Al-Khaḍir, without being asked and without any payment, undertook the laborious task of rebuilding the wall and making it straight and secure. This act of unsolicited kindness towards a people who had just mistreated them was the final puzzle for Moses. The contrast between the town’s inhospitality and Al-Khaḍir’s generous service was too much. “Moses protested, ‘If you wanted, you could have demanded a fee for this.’” The Arabic “la-ittakhadhta ‘alayhi ajran” suggests that this service should not have been free. They were in need of food and money, the people were undeserving, and at the very least, Al-Khaḍir should have asked for payment. Moses’ protest this time was not about a shocking crime but about a missed practical opportunity. He saw it as a lack of wisdom in dealing with unkind people. This third test was of a different nature: not an apparent evil, but an apparent failure to secure one’s due rights. It tested Moses’ sense of fairness and practical judgment. The tension is now at its peak, because this protest, though milder, triggers the consequence Moses himself had set.

=78=

With Moses’ third question, the condition he himself set is invoked. “He replied, ‘This is the parting of our ways.’” Al-Khaḍir confirms that the companionship has reached its end.

The word “firāq” means separation, and it is delivered with calm finality. There is no anger, no rebuke, just a statement of fact. Moses had said that if he questioned anything after this, Al-Khaḍir would have the right to separate from him, and now that moment has arrived.

However, Al-Khaḍir does not leave Moses in confusion. He immediately adds, “I will explain to you what you could not bear patiently.” The separation is not a punishment; it is the natural conclusion of the teaching phase. The events are complete, and now the time for explanation has come. Al-Khaḍir had promised that he would provide the clarification, and he honours that promise.

The phrase “what you could not bear patiently” (mā lam tastaṭi‘ ‘alayhi ṣabran) reiterates the central theme of the entire encounter. Moses could not endure the sight of these actions because the inner wisdom was hidden from him. The explanation that follows will reveal the mercy, justice, and deep foresight behind each act.

This verse teaches that sometimes in the journey of knowledge, the teacher must part ways with the student when the designed curriculum is complete, and the parting is not a failure but a graduation. The student is now ready to hear the interpretation that makes sense of the painful process. For Moses, the three events formed a transformative education, and the separation was necessary for him to reflect on what he had learned without the immediate pressure of further tests. The listener now prepares for the unveiling of the hidden wisdom, which has been eagerly anticipated through the entire narrative.

=79=

The explanation begins with the first incident. “As for the ship, it belonged to some poor people, working at sea.

” The Arabic “masākīn” emphasizes that the owners were needy, earning their livelihood through hard labour on the water. This immediately changes the moral calculus. Al-Khaḍir’s action was not against the poor but was, in reality, for their protection.

The verse reveals a hidden threat: “for there was a tyrant king ahead of them who seizes every good ship by force.” A tyrannical ruler was in pursuit, confiscating every serviceable vessel to add to his fleet or wealth. If the ship had been sound and intact, it would have been seized, and the poor family would have lost their only means of survival entirely. By making a hole that rendered the ship temporarily defective, Al-Khaḍir made it undesirable to the tyrant, who would bypass a damaged vessel. The small damage was a calculated act of mercy that prevented a total and permanent loss.

The word “kāna warā’ahum” indicates the king was behind them, coming in their direction, so the danger was imminent. This explanation unveils a profound principle: sometimes a partial harm is necessary to avert a complete catastrophe. The surface appearance was an act of destruction; the hidden reality was an act of preservation. For Moses, who had seen the act as a terrible crime, this revelation showed how limited human judgment can be. The lesson for the believer is that when God allows harm or difficulty to touch a person, it may be diverting a far greater disaster that is unseen. The poor sailors never knew the tyrant was coming, but God’s servant, guided by divine knowledge, acted to save them. This teaches trust in God’s hidden care, even when events are painful or perplexing.

=80=

Al-Khaḍir now explains the most shocking act, the killing of the boy. “And as for the boy, his parents were true believers.” The parents are described as mu’minayn, true and faithful believers.

This detail is crucial because it establishes that the act was not against a disbelieving family but was directly connected to preserving the faith of righteous parents. The verse then reveals the hidden knowledge that justified the act: “and we feared that he would pressure them into defiance and disbelief.

” The Arabic “fa-khashīnā” means “we feared” or “we knew with apprehension.” The “we” here is understood by scholars to refer to Al-Khaḍir speaking on behalf of himself and the knowledge God granted him, or it is a style of speech. The fear was not about a possibility but about a known future reality disclosed by God. The boy, if he had lived to adulthood, would have become a source of immense grief, rebellion, and tyranny (ṭughyān) for his parents, and would have caused them to deviate into disbelief (kufr).

The word “yurhiqahumā” means to overwhelm and overburden them with such pressure that their faith would be shaken. This was not a punishment for any sin the boy had committed yet, but a divine intervention to spare the parents a spiritual catastrophe and to spare the boy from accumulating sins that would lead him to Hell. The knowledge Al-Khaḍir possessed was a direct revelation of the boy’s future, and the action was a command from God specific to that case. This explanation highlights that God’s mercy can take forms that the human mind, without that knowledge, can never fathom. It also teaches that the death of a child, while a profound test, can sometimes be a hidden mercy preventing greater tragedies. The verse completes the justification for the most difficult event, leaving Moses and the listener in awe of the vastness of divine wisdom.
