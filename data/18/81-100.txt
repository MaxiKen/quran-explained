=81=

Having explained the reason for the boy’s death, Al-Khaḍir now reveals the divine compensation that followed. “So we hoped that their Lord would give them another, more virtuous and caring in his place.

” The word “hoped” (aradnā) here conveys a confident expectation based on divine knowledge, not a wish that might not come true. The boy who was taken away would be replaced by a child far better in two specific qualities: “zakāh” (purity, virtue, righteousness) and “ruḥman” (mercy, compassion, dutifulness). The replacement child would be more spiritually pure and more tender-hearted towards his parents. Some commentators mention that God granted them a daughter who later married a prophet, or a righteous son who brought them great joy. The exact identity is not as important as the principle: God does not take something away from His believing servants except to replace it with something better, or to avert a greater harm. The parents, who were true believers, would have been devastated by the loss of their child, but they did not know the future that awaited them. Al-Khaḍir’s action, carried out by God’s command, was an act of mercy that encompassed the boy (who died innocent and would enter Paradise), the parents (whose faith was preserved and who would receive a better child), and society (which was spared a future tyrant). This explanation reveals a profound layer of divine generosity. The calamity of losing a child is among the hardest tests for any human being.

Yet this verse teaches that if the believer remains patient and trusting, God may grant a replacement that brings even greater goodness. It also demonstrates that God’s knowledge of the future is absolute, and His decisions are based on a complete picture that we lack. For Moses, this explanation transformed his horror into awe. The lesson for every believer is to hold on to hope even in the darkest losses, trusting that the Lord who took away is also the Lord who gives, and His giving is always better.

=82=

The final explanation concerns the wall that Al-Khaḍir rebuilt without asking for payment. “And as for the wall, it belonged to two orphan boys in the city.” The wall was on the verge of collapse, and if it had fallen, what lay beneath it would have been exposed and lost. “And under the wall was a treasure that belonged to them. ” The treasure (kanz) was a hidden store of wealth—some say gold, silver, or valuable knowledge inscribed on tablets—left for the orphans by their father. The father is described as “a righteous man” (ṣāliḥan). His righteousness was the reason God extended His care to his children even after his death.

This is a powerful demonstration of how the piety of a parent can bring divine protection upon their offspring. The verse continues: “So your Lord willed that these children should come of age and retrieve their treasure.” The wall was rebuilt so that it would remain standing until the boys reached maturity (bulūgh ashuddahumā) and could claim their inheritance safely. Had the wall collapsed, the townspeople, who were inhospitable and likely dishonest, might have taken the treasure. The act of rebuilding was a pure act of mercy, preserving what rightfully belonged to the vulnerable orphans. Then Al-Khaḍir adds a crucial disclaimer: “I did not do it all on my own.

” The Arabic “wa mā fa‘altuhu ‘an amrī” means “I did not do it on my own authority or according to my own judgment.” All three actions were carried out under direct divine command and guidance. This assures Moses that no true servant of God would commit such acts without God’s explicit instruction.

Finally, Al-Khaḍir concludes: “This is the explanation of what you could not bear patiently.” The ta’wīl (interpretation) is now complete. Moses has received the inner meaning of the outward events that had tested his patience to the breaking point.

The story ends with this profound summation, teaching that behind every divine decree or action of God’s righteous servants lies a hidden wisdom that may only become clear when the full picture is unveiled. The believer is left with a renewed sense of humility, trust, and submission to the infinite knowledge of God.

=83=

The surah now transitions to the fourth and final major story, that of Dhul-Qarnain. This story is a direct response to the questions posed by the Quraysh, as mentioned in the introduction to the surah. “They ask you about Dhul-Qarnain.

” The name means “the possessor of the two horns,” and scholars have debated his identity. The Quran does not specify his name or era, and the purpose is not historical identification but the lessons his story imparts. The instruction to the Prophet is clear: “Say, ‘I will relate to you something of his narrative.’” The phrase “something of” (minhu dhikran) indicates that the Quran will give a portion of his story—a meaningful, instructive reminder—not an exhaustive historical account. This mirrors the teaching from the story of the cave, where Allah instructed not to argue about trivial details. The focus is on the wisdom and signs within the narrative. Dhul-Qarnain is presented as a righteous ruler given immense power and knowledge, and his journeys illustrate how he used these gifts in the service of God and justice.

The question of the Quraysh was meant to test the Prophet’s knowledge of hidden histories, and the divine answer came with the truth. By prefacing the story in this way, the Quran sets the expectation that what follows is not mythology or legend but revealed truth that carries guidance for those who reflect. The story will demonstrate the proper use of worldly power, the importance of establishing justice, and the reality of cosmic threats such as Gog and Magog, all culminating in a reminder of the Day of Judgment. The narrative begins with a declaration of God’s role in establishing Dhul-Qarnain’s authority, ensuring that all credit is attributed to the Almighty from the very start.

=84=

The foundation of Dhul-Qarnain’s story is laid with the declaration of divine empowerment. “Surely We established him in the land.

” The Arabic “makkannā lahu” means that God granted him firm authority, stability, and control over vast territories. His power was not self-made but a direct gift from God. This establishment encompassed political might, military strength, and the submission of various peoples. It is a reminder that all earthly kingdoms rise and fall by the will of God, and a righteous ruler recognizes that his authority is a trust, not a personal possession.

The verse then adds, “and gave him the means to all things.” The word “asbāb” (means, ways, resources) is profound. God provided Dhul-Qarnain with the knowledge, tools, and pathways to achieve whatever he needed for his missions. This could include scientific knowledge, military strategy, the ability to travel great distances, and the wisdom to govern diverse populations. He was given access to the causes of success, but he recognized that those causes were created and granted by God, not by his own independent power. This contrasts sharply with the arrogance of the owner of the two gardens earlier in the surah, who saw his wealth as his own doing. Dhul-Qarnain used his means in obedience to God, becoming a model of the grateful and just ruler. The verse sets up the epic journeys that will follow. Having been granted power and the means to accomplish his goals, Dhul-Qarnain embarks on three great expeditions to the west, the east, and a mountainous region. Each journey will reveal a different aspect of his leadership: justice, fairness, and protective service. The believer learns that power and resources are a divine test, and the righteous response is to use them for the benefit of humanity and the establishment of God’s justice.

=85=

The concise statement “So he travelled a course” (fa-atba‘a sababan) opens the first of Dhul-Qarnain’s three expeditions. The phrase literally means “he followed a way” or “he pursued a path.” It indicates a purposeful, determined journey using the means and resources God had given him. The sabab here is the route or the practical means by which he traversed the earth.

This is not a casual wandering but a deliberate mission. As a ruler with great power, he did not sit idle in his palace; he went out to see the state of the world, to administer justice, and to encounter different peoples. The verse is a lesson in the responsibility that comes with power. Leadership is not about luxury but about service and action. Dhul-Qarnain’s journey to the west will bring him to the place where the sun sets, a location where he encounters a people and is faced with a moral decision.

The next verses will elaborate on that encounter. The brevity of this verse serves as a narrative pause, building anticipation. It also underscores that his journeys were facilitated by the means God gave him; he followed the path that God opened for him. In a spiritual sense, every believer is on a journey using the sabab that God has provided—whether it is knowledge, health, or opportunity—and must use those means to reach righteous ends. The verse transitions naturally into the description of his first destination, where the physical signs of the sun’s setting become a backdrop for a profound test of justice and governance.

=86=

Dhul-Qarnain’s first journey brings him to the westernmost point of his travels. “Until he reached the setting point of the sun.” He went as far west as he could, to the edge of the land known to him. “Which appeared to him to be setting in a spring of murky water. ” This is a description from the viewer’s perspective. As the sun descended on the horizon, it looked as if it was sinking into a dark, muddy body of water—likely a vast ocean or a large lake with dark sediment.

This is not a literal claim about the sun’s movement; it is a phenomenological description of what a human observer sees, much like we say “the sun rose over the mountains.” The Quran speaks to human perception while conveying a deeper truth about the limits of Dhul-Qarnain’s reach. At this location, “he found some people.” The text does not specify their nature, but they were a community or nation living near that western shore. Then comes the divine test: “We said, ‘O Dhul-Qarnain! Either punish them or treat them kindly.’” God gave him authority over them and presented him with a choice.

The Arabic “immā an tu‘adhdhiba wa immā an tattakhidha fīhim ḥusnan” means he could either inflict punishment or adopt a course of kindness and goodness towards them. This divine communication could have been through inspiration, a prophet of his time, or a direct revelation. The key point is that his power was not unchecked; he was given a moral framework by God. He had the freedom to choose, but the choice itself was a test. This mirrors the situation of every person in authority: they can use their power for oppression or for benevolence.

The next verse will reveal Dhul-Qarnain’s just response, which balances mercy and accountability in a manner that provides a timeless model for governance.

=87=

Dhul-Qarnain makes his decision, and it reflects a profound balance of worldly justice and eschatological awareness. “He responded, ‘Whoever does wrong will be punished by us.’” The wrongdoers (ẓalamū) among the people he found would face immediate, appropriate punishment from his authority. He does not specify the nature of the wrong, but it can be understood as oppression, corruption, or denial of truth. The punishment is “by us,” meaning his worldly administration will carry out justice.

However, he does not stop there. He adds, “then will be returned to their Lord, Who will punish them with a horrible torment.” Dhul-Qarnain immediately connects his worldly punishment to the ultimate punishment of the Hereafter. This serves two purposes. First, it warns the wrongdoers that even if they escape or outsmart human justice, they cannot escape God. Second, it demonstrates his own humility; he recognizes that his punishment is limited and that the real, absolute justice belongs to God. The “horrible torment” (nukran) is the same word Moses used to describe the killing of the boy, meaning something utterly severe and detestable. Dhul-Qarnain’s justice is not only about maintaining order in this world but also about reminding people of their ultimate accountability.

This is a complete model of leadership: a leader must uphold the law and punish wrongdoing, but must never let people forget that the true judgment lies with God. The worldly punishment is a means of deterrence and reformation, but it pales in comparison to what awaits the unrepentant sinner in the afterlife.

The verse teaches that a just ruler combines firmness with spiritual insight, governing not only bodies but also reminding souls of their eternal destination.

=88=

Dhul-Qarnain now turns to the other side of his decree. “As for those who believe and do good, they will have the finest reward.” He specifies two conditions: faith (īmān) and righteous deeds (‘amal ṣāliḥ). The reward for such people is “al-ḥusnā,” the best and most beautiful recompense. This primarily refers to the reward in the Hereafter, Paradise and God’s pleasure, as he is speaking from a prophetic perspective. He does not claim to be the source of this reward; rather, he acknowledges that God will grant it. Then he adds, “and we will assign them easy commands.

” The Arabic “sa-naqūlu lahu min amrinā yusran” means that his worldly administration will deal with them gently, with light and easy instructions. The believers will not be burdened with harsh taxes, oppressive laws, or difficult demands. They will experience ease and kindness from the state.

This is a remarkable principle of governance: the state should be a source of relief for the righteous, not a source of hardship. The burden of strictness falls on the wrongdoers, while the law-abiding, faithful citizens enjoy liberty and ease. Dhul-Qarnain’s policy reflects the Quranic maxim that “with hardship comes ease” and that God desires ease for His servants. It also indicates that a righteous government recognizes virtue and rewards it by minimizing unnecessary intrusion into the lives of good people. This stands in stark contrast to tyrannies that oppress the righteous and favour the corrupt.

For the believer today, this verse provides a model for what to expect and advocate for in just governance: protection of faith, encouragement of good deeds, and a system where the righteous can live with ease and dignity. It also serves as a personal reminder that those who believe and do good can expect the finest reward from God, no matter how difficult their worldly situation may be.

=89=

With the western expedition concluded and justice established, Dhul-Qarnain sets out again. “Then he travelled a different course” (thumma atba‘a sababan).

The use of “thumma” (then) indicates a sequence in time, and “different course” shows that he now pursued a new direction using the means God had given him. The previous course was westward; this one will take him eastward.

The repetition of the phrase “atba‘a sababan” from verse 85 creates a rhythmic structure in the narrative, tying his journeys together under the same principle: he follows the paths that God has enabled. There is no resting on his laurels after the first achievement. The righteous ruler does not become complacent; he continues his mission to reach other lands and other peoples.

This verse emphasizes sustained effort in the service of God. The means (asbāb) are not exhausted; God’s provision of resources continues as long as the servant continues to strive. The brevity of the verse again serves as a narrative bridge, carrying the listener from the setting sun to the rising sun, from the western horizon to the eastern horizon. It also suggests that the world is vast and full of different communities, each requiring the attention of just leadership. For the individual believer, the lesson is to keep moving forward in the journey of life, using the means God has given for good, and not to stop at a single success. Every new horizon presents a new test and a new opportunity to serve.

=90=

Dhul-Qarnain’s second journey brings him to the opposite end of the known world. “Until he reached the rising point of the sun.

” This is the easternmost limit of his travels, parallel to the westernmost point of the first journey. “He found it rising on a people for whom We had provided no shelter from it. ” The description paints a picture of a people living in an open land with no natural cover, no mountains, no trees, and perhaps no buildings to shield them from the direct, scorching rays of the sun. When the sun rose, it beat down upon them without any barrier. Some commentators suggest they lived in a region near the equator or in a flat desert where they had not constructed dwellings. Others say they were a primitive people who simply endured the sun’s heat. The Quranic focus is not on their ethnicity or exact location but on their condition of vulnerability.

The phrase “We had provided no shelter from it” indicates that God, in His wisdom, had left them without conventional means of protection. This could be a test, or it could be that they lived in such a way that they did not seek shelter. Unlike the first people, who were immediately given a choice of punishment or kindness, the verse here does not record any specific interaction or decree.

The next verse says, “So it was. And We truly had full knowledge of him.” The absence of a detailed judgment might imply that these people were simple and not in need of the same form of governance, or that Dhul-Qarnain’s engagement with them was different.

The image of the exposed people under the blazing sun is a powerful reminder of the diversity of human conditions and the fact that God sees all. Dhul-Qarnain’s journey to them demonstrates the comprehensive reach of his mission, leaving no corner of the world unvisited.

=91=

This brief verse closes the account of the eastern journey with a statement of divine affirmation and comprehensive knowledge. “So it was” (kadhālika) indicates that the matter of those people was as described, and Dhul-Qarnain dealt with them appropriately according to the wisdom and justice he had demonstrated before. The specifics of his interaction are left unmentioned, allowing the focus to remain on the broader lessons.

The second part of the verse, “And We truly had full knowledge of him,” is profoundly significant. The Arabic “qad aḥaṭnā bimā ladayhi khubran” means God encompassed everything concerning Dhul-Qarnain—his actions, his intentions, his resources, and his inner state—with complete, thorough knowledge.

The word “khubr” implies deep, detailed awareness of the hidden and the apparent. This statement serves multiple purposes. It assures the Prophet and the believers that Dhul-Qarnain’s story is accurate and divinely witnessed. It also emphasizes that Dhul-Qarnain’s righteousness and power were constantly under God’s watch. He did not act in secrecy; God knew everything about him.

This is both a commendation and a reminder. For the powerful, it is a warning that God monitors how they use their authority. For the righteous, it is a comfort that their deeds are never lost or forgotten.

The verse also bridges to the third journey. By affirming God’s comprehensive knowledge, it reassures the listener that the upcoming encounter with Gog and Magog, and the construction of the barrier, were all under divine supervision.

The phrase echoes the earlier theme of the surah: God’s knowledge encompasses all things, while human knowledge is limited. Moses could not encompass the wisdom of Al-Khaḍir, but God encompasses all. Dhul-Qarnain’s vast empire is but a small part of God’s all-encompassing knowledge.

=92=

The narrative advances to the final and most famous expedition. “Then he travelled a third course.” The structure is identical to verses 85 and 89, reinforcing the pattern of his journeys. This third course takes him in a direction different from west and east, likely northward, to a region between two mountain barriers.

The repetition of “thumma atba‘a sababan” unifies the three journeys as a continuous, lifelong mission of service. By now, the listener understands that Dhul-Qarnain is a ruler who does not rest; he constantly seeks out new frontiers to establish justice and help those in need.

The third journey will bring him to a people who are linguistically isolated and suffering from the raids of Gog and Magog. This expedition is the climax of his story, where his power and resources are used for the ultimate act of protective public service: building a massive barrier to contain a corrupting force. The lesson here is that true leadership involves going to the farthest reaches, not just the comfortable centres of power. Dhul-Qarnain could have stayed in his palace and sent generals, but he himself travelled, observed, and acted. This hands-on approach is a mark of a sincere servant of God. The three journeys together represent the comprehensive use of means: dealing with diverse peoples, applying justice, showing kindness, and solving large-scale problems. The verse transitions the narrative to the detailed account of the barrier, which will occupy the next several verses, providing a vivid picture of human cooperation and divine assistance in the face of existential threat.

=93=

The third journey reaches its destination. “Until he reached a pass between two mountains.

” The Arabic “bayna as-saddayn” refers to a narrow passage or a valley enclosed by two towering mountain barriers. This geographical feature was strategic; it was the only accessible opening between the two sides. “He found in front of them a people who could hardly understand his language.” The people living there were isolated, and their speech was so different that communication was extremely difficult.

The phrase “lā yakādūna yafqahūna qawlan” indicates they could barely comprehend what was being said to them. Despite this language barrier, they managed to convey their distress.

This detail highlights the universality of Dhul-Qarnain’s mission; not even linguistic obstacles stopped him from understanding their needs. It also shows the diversity of God’s creation, with peoples separated by geography and language. The fact that they could still communicate enough to make their plea demonstrates a shared human experience of suffering and the desire for protection. For a ruler with interpreters and resources, the language barrier was surmountable.

This verse teaches the importance of reaching out to marginalized communities, even when communication is difficult. The isolated people had no one else to turn to, and Dhul-Qarnain did not dismiss them because they spoke a different tongue.

The next verse will reveal their heartfelt plea, giving voice to the fear and desperation of those who live on the edge of chaos. The scene is set for the request that will lead to one of the most monumental construction projects in history.

=94=

The isolated people finally voice their distress. “They pleaded, ‘O Dhul-Qarnain! Surely Gog and Magog are spreading corruption throughout the land.’” Gog and Magog (Ya’jūj and Ma’jūj) are described in Islamic tradition as two wild, destructive tribes or nations. They would pour through the mountain pass and wreak havoc, killing, plundering, and spreading fasād (corruption) across the land.

The word “mufsidūn” is used throughout the Quran to describe those who ruin the peace and order of society. The people were living in constant terror of these raids. Their plea to Dhul-Qarnain is a cry for help from a powerful, just ruler. Then they make a concrete offer: “Should we pay you tribute, provided that you build a wall between us and them? ” The word “kharjan” refers to a payment, a tax or tribute, that they were willing to gather and give him in exchange for his service. They recognized that such a massive undertaking would require resources and labour, and they offered to compensate him. This shows their sincerity and desperation. They were not demanding free aid; they were willing to sacrifice their wealth for security. The offer also reflects a form of social contract: the ruler provides protection, and the people provide resources.

However, Dhul-Qarnain’s response in the next verse will elevate the interaction from a mere transactional arrangement to a mission of higher purpose. His reply will demonstrate that his motivation is not wealth but the fulfillment of his duty to God and to humanity. The presence of Gog and Magog in the narrative also connects to eschatological signs, as their eventual release is one of the major signs of the approaching Hour, a theme the surah will return to.

=95=

Dhul-Qarnain’s reply reveals the purity of his intention. “He responded, ‘What my Lord has provided for me is far better.’” He immediately refuses any payment.

The phrase “mā makkannī fīhi rabbī khayr” means that the power, wealth, and means God had already given him were far superior to any tribute these people could offer. He had no need of their money; he had been enriched by God with something much greater.

This is the response of a true servant who sees worldly wealth as trivial compared to the honour of serving God and helping His creation. His refusal of payment transforms the act of building the barrier from a commercial contract into an act of pure charity and divine service.

Yet he does not simply send them away and work alone. He says, “But assist me with resources, and I will build a barrier between you and them. ” The word “quwwatin” can mean physical strength, manpower, or material resources like tools and labour. He asked them to contribute their effort and whatever local materials they could provide.

This is a profound lesson in community empowerment. While the powerful can provide leadership and overarching resources, the affected community must also participate in their own protection. He did not foster dependency; he invited collaboration. The barrier (radm) he proposed was a solid, massive structure, different from a simple wall. The stage is set for one of the most detailed descriptions of an engineering project in the Quran. The interaction teaches that leaders should serve without greed, that the rich should see their wealth as a trust from God, and that solving large-scale problems requires a partnership between those in authority and the people themselves.

=96=

Dhul-Qarnain now directs the actual construction of the barrier with precise, step-by-step instructions. “Bring me blocks of iron!

” The word “zubar al-ḥadīd” refers to large pieces or ingots of iron. These were the primary structural material. They were brought and placed into the gap between the two mountains. “Then, when he had filled up the gap between the two mountains” describes the first phase: stacking the iron blocks until they reached the level of the mountain sides, completely sealing the pass. This required immense quantities of iron and organized labour.

The phrase “sāwā bayna aṣ-ṣadafayn” indicates he levelled the iron with the sloping sides of the mountains, creating a uniform, solid base. Then he ordered, “Blow!” He commanded the workers to use large bellows to blow air into the fuel, creating a massive fire that heated the iron blocks until they glowed red hot. The iron became like a single fused mass, increasing its strength and durability. Then came the second phase: “Bring me molten copper to pour over it. ” The word “qiṭran” can mean molten copper, brass, or a mixture of metals. This molten metal was poured over the red-hot iron, filling all the gaps and crevices, binding the entire structure together into an impenetrable, smooth-faced barrier. The combination of iron for bulk and copper as a bonding agent created a metallurgical marvel that could not be scaled or breached. This detailed description highlights the knowledge and means God gave Dhul-Qarnain. It was a practical application of physics and engineering, carried out with divine facilitation.

The verse teaches that faith and practical skill go hand in hand. Dhul-Qarnain did not simply pray for protection; he used the material means God provided and executed a sophisticated project, all while acknowledging that the true power came from God.

=97=

The result of the massive construction is stated simply and definitively. “And so the enemies could neither scale nor tunnel through it.” The barrier was completely effective.

The Arabic “mā as-ṭā‘ū” means they were totally unable to climb over it. The height, likely levelled with the mountain peaks and coated with smooth molten metal, offered no handholds or footholds.

The phrase “wa mā as-ṭā‘ū lahu naqban” means they could not make a hole or tunnel through it. The iron core fused with copper was too thick and solid to be pierced by any tools available to Gog and Magog. This state of impenetrability would last until the appointed time decreed by God. The verse highlights the perfection of the work done with God’s guidance. It provided the afflicted people with absolute security, freeing them from the terror they had lived under. The verse is also a testament to the fact that when humans use God-given means with sincerity and skill, the results can be extraordinarily effective.

However, the next verse will immediately temper any pride in this achievement by attributing the barrier’s success to God’s mercy and declaring its ultimate impermanence. This teaches a vital lesson: even the greatest human accomplishments are temporary and depend on God’s will for their continuation. The barrier stood not because of the genius of its engineering alone, but because God willed it to remain. The day will come when God’s promise will cause it to be levelled, reminding humanity that all fortresses and protections of this world are ultimately transient.

=98=

Dhul-Qarnain, standing before the completed barrier, does not take credit. “He declared, ‘This is a mercy from my Lord.’” The Arabic “hādhā raḥmatun min rabbī” attributes the success of the entire project to God’s mercy. It was God who gave him the knowledge, the resources, and the cooperation of the people, and it was God who guided the process to completion.

This is the peak of humility. A lesser ruler would have named the barrier after himself or boasted of his engineering prowess. Dhul-Qarnain sees only God’s mercy in it. Then he adds the prophetic insight: “But when the promise of my Lord comes to pass, He will level it to the ground.” He acknowledges that this barrier, mighty as it is, is not eternal. There is an appointed time in God’s knowledge when it will be destroyed. The promise of God refers to the approach of the Day of Judgment, when the barrier will be flattened, and Gog and Magog will surge forth, as described in Surah Al-Anbiya and in the next verse of this surah.

This statement serves as a reminder of the end of all things. He concludes, “And my Lord’s promise is ever true. ” God’s words and decrees are unerring and will inevitably come to pass. This verse beautifully concludes Dhul-Qarnain’s story with a perfect balance of gratitude, humility, and eschatological awareness. The barrier is a mercy in the present, but the future belongs to God’s ultimate plan.

For the believer, it is a reminder that all human protections—health, wealth, security—are mercies from God, but they will all cease. The only permanent reality is God and His true promise. This directly connects back to the earlier themes of the surah: the fleeting nature of the world and the certainty of the Hereafter.

=99=

The narrative expands from the barrier’s destruction to the cosmic events of the end times. “On that Day, We will let them surge like waves over one another.” When the appointed time comes and the barrier is levelled, Gog and Magog will pour out in a massive, chaotic wave, overwhelming the land.

The word “yamūju” evokes the image of surging, tumultuous waves in a stormy sea, indicating their immense numbers and the disruption they will cause. This is a major sign of the approaching Hour. Then, “Later, the Trumpet will be blown, and We will gather all people together.” The Trumpet (aṣ-ṣūr) is the horn blown by the angel Israfil, signaling the end of all life and the resurrection. The blowing of the Trumpet marks the transition from the worldly chaos to the final gathering. All of humanity, from the first to the last, will be assembled before God for judgment.

The sequence is clear: the release of Gog and Magog is a prelude to the end, and their destruction or the subsequent events lead directly to the universal resurrection. This verse connects Dhul-Qarnain’s historical act of building the barrier to the ultimate future of humanity. It reminds the listener that the story is not just about a past king but about the unfolding of God’s plan through time. The barrier that seemed so permanent was only a temporary mercy, and the real security lies in preparing for the Day when all will be gathered. The verse serves as a powerful transition into the concluding passages of the surah, which will focus on the state of the disbelievers and believers on that Day.

=100=

The gathering leads directly to the presentation of the final destination. “On that Day We will display Hell clearly for the disbelievers.

” The Arabic “‘araḍnā jahannama” means Hell will be brought forth and made plainly visible. It will not be a distant rumor or an abstract concept; it will be a tangible, terrifying reality right before their eyes. The disbelievers will see the Fire they were warned about and denied. This public display is part of the ultimate humiliation and terror. The verse specifies that it is for “the disbelievers” (lil-kāfirīn), those who rejected faith. This sight is a punishment in itself, the overwhelming horror of seeing their eternal abode before entering it. It echoes verse 53 earlier in the surah, where the wicked see the Fire and realize they are bound to fall into it. The connection reinforces the surah’s consistent warning. The display of Hell serves as the ultimate validation of the truth of the messengers’ warnings.

The next verse will provide the reason for their punishment, describing the specific nature of their disbelief and heedlessness. The mention of Hell being displayed clearly (ḍalālan) also indicates that there will be no more veils, no more ambiguity; the truth will stand manifest, and the deniers will face the consequences of their rejection. This verse is a sobering conclusion to the Dhul-Qarnain narrative, transitioning the listener from the stories of worldly power and mercy to the inescapable reality of divine judgment that awaits every soul.
