=1=

The surah opens with a powerful, evocative oath that plunges the listener directly into the heart of a battlefield scene: “By the galloping, panting horses.” The Arabic term “al-ʿādiyāt” refers to horses that run at full speed, their hooves pounding the earth, while “ḍabḥan” describes their distinct, heavy breathing—the panting sound that emerges from their chests as they strain every muscle in the charge. By swearing by these magnificent creatures, Allah draws the attention of the Arabs, for whom the horse was not merely an animal but a symbol of pride, strength, and chivalry. The Quraysh were expert horsemen and traders; they understood the value of a warhorse that would gallop fearlessly into danger. The oath thus uses a familiar image of worldly zeal and dedication to prepare a striking contrast. These horses, in their complete obedience to their riders, plunge into battle without hesitation, giving everything they have. The panting indicates utter exhaustion willingly accepted for the sake of a worldly goal. The surah asks, implicitly: if a mere animal can display such wholehearted effort for its master, how should the human being respond to the Lord who created him? The oath also contains a subtle rebuke: these warhorses, which the Arabs prized so highly, are creatures of Allah, and their breath is sworn by the Creator. The Arabs who worshipped idols or their own prowess are reminded that the might they admire is a created thing pointing to a higher Might. The imagery also sets the emotional tone of urgency and seriousness. The surah is about to deliver a stern lesson on ingratitude and the Day of Reckoning, and it opens with a burst of energy that shakes the listener out of complacency. The scene of galloping, panting horses serves as a metaphor for the human soul’s journey through life: are we exerting ourselves with the same intensity for the sake of our eternal salvation, or are we charging after the fleeting spoils of this world? The oath thus brings the battlefield into the heart, and the verses that follow will reveal that the real struggle is not against human enemies but against one’s own ingratitude and greed.

=2=

The second verse continues the vivid battlefield imagery: “striking sparks of fire with their hoofs.” The Arabic “fa’l-mūriyāti qadḥan” pictures the horses’ iron-shod hooves striking against the hard, rocky ground with such force that sparks fly from the impact.

This detail intensifies the scene of speed and ferocity. The sparks are brief flashes of light in the darkness, a sudden flare that illuminates the charging horses before vanishing.

This image conveys the sheer violence and energy of the raid. In the pre-Islamic context, night raids or dawn attacks were common, and the striking of sparks would be a familiar sound and sight to warriors riding out in the dark.

The verse also captures a sense of urgency: the horses are not trotting gently; they are tearing across the terrain with such power that even the stones protest under their hooves. The sparks can be seen as a symbol of the fleeting nature of worldly glory—a flash of brilliance that fades as quickly as it appears. On a deeper spiritual level, the striking of sparks contrasts with the light of guidance and the fire of divine punishment. The disbelievers kindle their own fires of war, greed, and competition, while the believers seek the light of revelation.

The verse also hints at the effort that humans exert in their worldly pursuits. They will push themselves to the point of creating sparks, metaphorically burning their energy for the sake of wealth, power, and conquest.

Yet, as the surah will shortly reveal, all this effort is often divorced from gratitude to the One who granted the horses and the strength. The surah is building a case: look at the intensity of your worldly striving, the sparks you generate; then look at your spiritual apathy and ingratitude. The contrast is damning.

The verse also serves as a literary masterpiece, using a few words to paint an unforgettable picture that engages all the senses—sound (panting), sight (sparks), and the feel of the pounding hooves.

=3=

The third verse completes the temporal setting of the oath: “launching raids at dawn.” The Arabic “fa’l-mughīrāti ṣubḥan” describes the horses as they carry their riders on a surprise attack in the early morning hours. Dawn is the time when the darkness of night still lingers, but the first light is beginning to appear on the horizon. It was the preferred time for raids in the Arab culture because the enemy would be least alert, the sentries weary, and the cover of darkness would still aid the attackers until the moment of the assault.

This verse adds the dimension of strategy and timing to the portrait of the warhorses. They are not merely running aimlessly; they are part of a deliberate, well-planned operation conducted at the most effective hour.

The mention of dawn also invokes a sense of transition and exposure. Just as the dawn reveals what the night concealed, the coming verses will reveal the hidden reality of human ingratitude and the inevitability of the Day when all secrets are exposed.

The word “ṣubḥan” also connects to the broader Quranic symbolism of dawn as a time of divine mercy, prayer, and awakening. While the disbelievers use the dawn for raids and plunder, the believers use the same hour for the Fajr prayer, for reciting the Qur’an, and for seeking Allah’s pleasure. The implicit contrast is powerful: the same pre-dawn hours that the warhorses spend in aggression and worldly gain, the Prophet and his companions spend in humble prostration. The verse thus uses the familiar cultural image to hold up a mirror to the Quraysh and all who prioritise worldly conquest over spiritual devotion. It forces the listener to ask: when I rise at dawn, what is the purpose that drives me? Is it to launch raids on my fellow human beings, or to launch my soul toward its Lord through prayer and gratitude? The surah’s oath sequence is now complete, and the moral lesson is about to land with full force.

=4=

The fourth verse adds to the scene’s visual intensity: “stirring up clouds of dust.” The Arabic “fa’atharna bihī naqʿan” describes how the galloping horses churn the earth, throwing up a thick, obscuring cloud of dust. In the heat of a raid, the dust becomes so dense that it blankets the field, making visibility difficult and adding to the chaos and terror.

This image reinforces the sheer physical power and the disruptive force of the charge. The dust clouds are a symbol of turmoil, of the upheaval that human greed and aggression cause on the earth.

The verse also subtly hints at the consequences of such worldly pursuits: they stir up confusion, they blind people to the truth, and they leave behind a messy, choking aftermath. On a spiritual level, the dust can be seen as the cloud of heedlessness that covers the heart. Just as the galloping horses obscure the clear air with dust, the love of worldly gains obscures the human soul’s vision of the divine reality. The surah will shortly declare that humanity is extreme in the love of wealth; this verse provides the visual metaphor. The dust raised by the horses is a perfect picture of a life spent chasing material goals—a lot of noise and activity, but in the end, nothing but a cloud that soon settles, leaving the soul empty.

The verse also demonstrates the thoroughness of the Quranic oath: every aspect of the raid—the sound, the sight, the timing, the environmental impact—is captured in a few words. This comprehensive imagery makes the scene unforgettable, and it prepares the heart for the shock of the contrast that follows. The Quraysh, who prided themselves on their cavalry and their ability to stir up dust in battle, are about to be told that their Lord is not impressed by these feats if they are accompanied by ingratitude. The dust they raise will one day settle, and then they will face the One who sees through all clouds.

=5=

The oath reaches its climax with the fifth verse: “and penetrating into the heart of enemy lines!” The Arabic “fa’wasaṭna bihī jamʿan” depicts the ultimate moment of the charge: the horses, with their riders, burst right into the very centre of the opposing gathering, splitting the enemy ranks and creating chaos at the core.

This is the moment of greatest danger and greatest impact, the point where the raid achieves its objective. The word “jamʿan” means a gathered host, a collective, and by penetrating into its heart, the attackers demonstrate total commitment and fearless daring. The oath sequence thus moves from the horses’ panting to the sparks, to the dawn timing, to the dust, and finally to the central penetration. It is a crescendo of action. But then the surah abruptly turns. After this intense, breathless depiction of worldly effort, the listener expects a heroic declaration. Instead, the next verse begins with “Surely humankind is ungrateful to their Lord.” The sudden shift is the rhetorical masterstroke of the surah. The horses, dumb beasts, obey their masters perfectly and plunge into mortal danger. The human being, endowed with intellect and will, fails to obey his Master and plunges into ingratitude.

The verse also carries a deeper metaphorical layer: the human heart is the true “enemy line” that must be penetrated by faith. The lower self and its desires form a gathered host against the soul, and the believer must launch a spiritual raid, galloping with the horses of devotion, striking the sparks of inspiration at the dawn of prayer, and penetrating into the heart of the self’s resistance.

However, the immediate rhetorical purpose is clear: to contrast the dedication of creatures to fleeting worldly goals with the negligence of humans toward their eternal Creator. The oaths are thus a mirror held up to the Arab soul, and to every soul, demanding an honest comparison between the effort we pour into this world and the effort we pour into preparing for the next.

=6=

The surah now delivers the sharp lesson that the oaths have been building toward: “Surely humankind is ungrateful to their Lord.” The word “insān” encompasses all humanity, but the immediate audience is the disbelieving Quraysh who pursue their worldly raids with vigour while denying the favours of their Creator.

The Arabic term “la-kanūd” denotes an intense, habitual ingratitude. It means someone who counts every small misfortune, who forgets the countless blessings, and who is miserly with thanks.

The word originally carries a sense of the earth that yields nothing, a barren soil that refuses to produce. The human heart, when consumed by love of the world, becomes such barren ground, refusing to sprout gratitude. The verse asserts this ingratitude with the emphatic “surely,” indicating that this is a universal tendency unless corrected by faith. The Quraysh, who enjoyed the security of the sacred city, the wealth of the trade caravans, the gift of the Prophet among them, and the marvels of creation around them, responded with denial, mockery, and idolatry. They were the epitome of kanūd.

Yet the verse speaks to every human being. How many blessings have we received today alone—breath, sight, health, a roof, a meal—and how often do we pause to truly thank the Giver? The ingratitude is not just a lack of words; it manifests in using God’s gifts to disobey Him, in spending wealth on forbidden things, in employing the tongue for gossip instead of praise, in waging the horse for raids of plunder instead of raids of righteousness. The verse links back to the oath: the horses display total loyalty, but the human who rides them displays total disloyalty to the Creator of both horse and rider.

This is the tragic irony. The Lord of the panting horses, the Lord of the dawn, the Lord of the dust is the same Lord whom the human rejects. The verse is a divine diagnosis, and the next two verses will amplify and provide evidence of this ingratitude. The listener is left to examine his or her own heart, to see whether they are among the kanūd, and to realise that ingratitude is not a small flaw but the root disease that leads to eternal loss.

=7=

The surah continues its indictment: “and they certainly attest to this.” The Arabic “wa-innahu ʿalā dhālika la-shahīd” can be understood in more than one profound way, and both meanings reinforce the accusation. One interpretation is that the human being is a witness against his own self. Deep within his conscience, he knows his ingratitude. His actions, his complaints, his obsession with worldly gain while neglecting his Lord, all testify to the truth of the accusation. He cannot deny it. Even if he protests with his tongue, his life bears witness. Another interpretation, and one strongly supported by some early exegetes, is that Allah Himself is the witness. The pronoun “he” in “innahu” could refer to God, meaning: “And surely He (Allah) is Witness to that.” Allah testifies to the ingratitude of humankind; He sees every act of denial, every blessing that is received without thanks, every moment of rebellion. A third interpretation is that the very horses sworn by in the opening verses are the witnesses. The galloping, panting horses who obey their riders without question are a living testimony to man’s lack of equivalent obedience to his Maker. The horses do their duty; man does not. All these layers enrich the verse. The overall effect is that the accusation of ingratitude is not an arbitrary divine claim; it is backed by evidence, by testimony, by a witness that cannot be challenged. On the Day of Judgment, as the previous surahs have detailed, the earth itself will speak, the limbs will testify, and the human will stand condemned by his own record. This verse foreshadows that courtroom scene. The human being is placed in a position of undeniable guilt. The purpose, however, is not merely condemnation but awakening. By pointing out that the evidence is already there, the surah invites the human to recognise his condition and to change it before the ultimate witness is presented in the court of the Hereafter. The verse thus deepens the sense of accountability and strips away any excuse the soul might offer for its ingratitude.

=8=

The surah now pinpoints the root cause of the ingratitude: “and they are truly extreme in their love of worldly gains.” The Arabic word “khayr” here does not mean good in the moral sense but refers to wealth, possessions, and material benefits. The human being’s love for this “khayr” is described as “la-shadīd”—intense, extreme, almost violent in its grip on the heart.

This is the diagnosis of the disease: the love of wealth has consumed the heart, leaving no room for the love of God or for gratitude. When the soul becomes fixated on accumulating and hoarding, it becomes blind to the source of all provision. Every blessing is attributed to one’s own effort or to chance, and the thought of sharing with the needy or devoting wealth to righteous causes feels like a painful loss rather than a purification. The Quraysh, with their lucrative trade caravans and their usurious practices, exemplified this obsessive love. They would count their dirhams and dinars with passion, while the orphan and the widow starved at their doorsteps, and while the Prophet called them to worship the One who gave them those riches.

The verse also echoes the warning of Surah Al-‘Adiyat’s companion surah, Al-Humazah, which condemns the one who amasses wealth and counts it over and over. The extreme love of worldly gains is the direct opposite of the sincere devotion and charity demanded in Surah Al-Bayyinah. This love is not a passive affection; it is a driving force that shapes decisions, ruins relationships, and fuels wars—the very wars that the galloping horses at the beginning of the surah were waging.

The verse shows that the brilliant charge of the horses, rather than being a noble thing in itself, is often motivated by the ugly greed of their riders. Thus, the surah connects external violence to internal corruption. The cure for this disease is the remembrance of death and the Day of Judgment, which the final three verses of the surah bring into sharp focus. The verse leaves the reader with a powerful question: what do I love most intensely? Does my heart race for wealth the way those horses raced into battle, and if so, toward what end am I galloping?

=9=

The surah now shifts from diagnosis to warning, asking a rhetorical question that shatters the illusion of worldly permanence: “Do they not know that when the contents of the graves will be spilled out.” The interrogative “Do they not know” is a rebuke, implying that this knowledge is available, that the intellect and the revealed scriptures have made it plain, and yet they live as if they do not know. The “contents of the graves” refers to the bodies of the dead, decomposed and scattered, which have been buried for centuries. The verb “buʿthira” means to be scattered, overturned, and violently expelled. It conjures an image of the graves bursting open and their contents being tossed out, much like the earth threw out its burdens in Surah Al-Zalzalah.

This is the resurrection, the day when all human beings who ever lived will be brought forth from their burial places to stand before their Lord. The verse strikes directly at the heart of the pagan denial. The Quraysh, like many materialists throughout history, found the idea of bodily resurrection absurd. They would ask, “Who will give life to bones when they are decayed?” This verse does not engage in a philosophical debate; it simply announces the event as a certainty, framed with a question that belittles their ignorance. The imagery of the graves spilling out also links back to the earlier verses. Just as the horses stirred up dust in their raid, the resurrection will stir up the dust of the graves. The worldly raids cause temporary chaos for the sake of loot; the divine “raid” of the resurrection causes universal upheaval for the sake of justice. The verse serves as a cold splash of reality on the fever of worldly love. It tells the wealth-obsessed human: the riches you are hoarding will stay behind, and you will come out of your grave empty-handed, with nothing but your deeds.

The question “Do they not know” is an invitation to reflect before it is too late. Knowledge of the resurrection, if truly internalised, would cure the extreme love of worldly gains and replace it with a love of the eternal. The surah is about to reveal what else will be exposed on that Day.

=10=

The exposure escalates from the physical to the deeply personal: “and the secrets of the hearts will be laid bare.” The Arabic “ḥuṣṣila mā fī al-ṣudūr” means that whatever was concealed in the chests—the intentions, the hidden beliefs, the secret plots, the grudges, the hypocrisies, the silent yearnings—will be brought out, examined, and sorted. On that Day, no inner secret will remain hidden. The outward actions will be judged, but also the inward realities that drove them. The sincere faith and the hidden doubt, the charity given for God’s sake and the charity given for praise, the love that was pure and the love that was tainted—all will be distinguished.

This is the ultimate transparency, the end of all masks. In this world, a person can smile at another while harbouring hatred; on that Day, the hatred will be visible, and the person will be held accountable for what the heart contained. This verse explains why the ingratitude and the love of wealth were so dangerous: they are not merely behavioural issues but diseases of the heart. The heart is the vessel that will be examined, its contents spilled out and assessed. The verse connects back to the oaths: the horses’ outward charge was visible to all, but the motivations of the riders were hidden. Allah swears by the visible charge to warn about the Day when the invisible hearts will be charged and exposed.

The word “ḥuṣṣila” also carries the meaning of discrimination and separation; the good and the evil within the heart will be sifted apart, and the person will receive the consequences of each. For the believer, this verse inspires a constant monitoring of the heart. The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught that Allah does not look at your bodies or your forms, but at your hearts. A believer who knows that the secrets of the heart will be laid bare strives to purify those secrets now, through sincere repentance, constant self-examination, and asking Allah to make the inner reality pure. The surah is reaching its final, powerful conclusion, having moved from the panting horses to the panting hearts standing in the divine presence.

=11=

The surah closes with the ultimate assurance of divine knowledge and justice: “surely their Lord is All-Aware of them on that Day.” The emphatic “surely” leaves no room for doubt.

The word “khabīr” denotes the One who is fully aware of the innermost realities, the hidden details, the subtleties that escape even the closest human observer. Allah’s awareness is not a new acquisition on that Day; He has always been All-Aware. The point is that on that Day, this knowledge will become manifest in the form of perfect judgment and recompense. No deed will be forgotten, no secret will be overlooked, and no one will be wronged. The verse brings the surah full circle. It began with the vivid description of horses performing for the eyes of their riders and the world. It ends with the reminder that the Lord sees not only the outward gallop but the inward motivations behind every human action. The rider who launched the dawn raid for plunder and the believer who rose for the dawn prayer—their Lord is All-Aware of both, and each will receive accordingly.

The phrase “their Lord” is particularly significant. It reminds the human being that the One who is All-Aware is not a distant, indifferent observer but the Rabb, the Lord who creates, sustains, and cares. This relationship makes the ingratitude even more grievous. The Lord who was All-Aware of their needs and provided for them, the Lord who was All-Aware of their weakness and supported them, is the very Lord they were ungrateful toward.

Yet, even as the surah ends with this stern warning, there is a hidden door of hope. If the Lord is All-Aware, He is also aware of every sincere repentance, every hidden tear of remorse, every atom of good that was done in secret. The same attribute that threatens the unrepentant sinner comforts the struggling believer. The believer trusts that their unseen struggles, their silent supplications, their forgotten acts of charity are known to the Lord who will never let them be lost. The surah thus leaves the heart in a state of profound awe, calling for a life lived in the constant awareness that the All-Aware is watching, and that the day when graves spill and hearts are exposed is drawing nearer with every galloping heartbeat.
