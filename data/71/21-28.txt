=21=

After centuries of patient, tireless preaching using every possible approach, Noah finally turns to his Lord with a heavy-hearted complaint. The word "Eventually" marks the end of an immense era of forbearance. Noah says, "My Lord!" with the intimacy of a servant who has done his utmost and now places the matter before the only Judge.

The phrase "They have certainly persisted in disobeying me" captures the stubborn refusal of his people. The Arabic verb for "persisted" indicates not a one-time rejection but a continuous, deliberate, entrenched rebellion. They did not merely ignore him; they actively opposed him. Then comes the deeper diagnosis of their problem: "and followed instead those elite whose abundant wealth and children only increase them in loss." The common people were misled by their leaders, the wealthy and powerful chiefs who had a vested interest in maintaining the status quo of idolatry. These elites had abundance of wealth and many children, which in their eyes was a sign of divine favour and superiority. They used their resources to reinforce their authority and suppress Noah's message.

However, Allah reveals the true reality: these very blessings "only increase them in loss." Their wealth is not a blessing leading to gratitude but a tool of oppression that multiplies their sin and ultimate ruin. Their children, instead of being a source of support, become a means of continuing the legacy of disbelief, and on the Day of Judgment they will be witnesses against them.

This is a profound warning that worldly prosperity is not a measure of truth or divine approval; it can be a deceptive test that accelerates a person's destruction if accompanied by arrogance and sin. The verse also mirrors the qualities of the disbelievers mentioned in the previous surah Al-Ma'arij, where the wicked would wish to ransom themselves with their children and clan. Those same children that are a source of pride now become a currency of desperation in the Hereafter. Noah's complaint is not born out of personal hurt but out of sorrow that his people chose the path of loss by following the blind leaders.

=22=

Noah elaborates on the actions of those elite leaders: "and who have devised a tremendous plot." The Arabic word "makr" means a scheme, a cunning plan, a conspiracy. The leaders did not simply disagree with Noah; they actively conspired to undermine his mission, to keep the masses in ignorance, and to entrench idolatry. This "tremendous plot" likely involved several layers. They slandered Noah, calling him a madman, a liar, or a sorcerer. They threatened the weak believers with persecution. They used their wealth to bribe and influence. They exploited the people's emotional attachment to the idols of their ancestors, arguing that abandoning them would be a betrayal of heritage. Some reports mention that they would take their sons to Noah and point him out as a dangerous deceiver, poisoning the next generation against him. The plot was tremendous because it was comprehensive and long-lasting, designed to block every avenue of truth.

The word "tremendous" also indicates that their scheming was not hidden from Allah; He saw its full extent. This verse provides comfort to the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, who faced similar conspiracies from the Quraysh elite such as Abu Jahl, Abu Lahab, and others. They too would plot to kill him, to discredit him, and to prevent people from hearing the Quran. Allah is informing him that this is the ancient pattern: when a messenger comes, the corrupt elite do not simply remain passive; they actively scheme.

However, as the Quran states elsewhere, the plotting of evil does not encompass anything except its own people. Their tremendous plot would ultimately drown with them.

The verse also teaches the believers to be aware that the path of truth often faces organized opposition, and they must rely on Allah, the best of planners.

=23=

The surah now reveals the very words of the tremendous plot: "urging their followers, 'Do not abandon your idols— especially Wadd, Suwa', Yaghuth, Ya'uq, and Nasr.'" This is a chilling glimpse into the strategy of the elite. They did not present intellectual arguments; they appealed to emotional attachment and tradition. The command "Do not abandon your idols" is a rallying cry to preserve the religion of the ancestors. Then they named five specific idols. Ibn Abbas and other early commentators explained that these five names originally belonged to righteous men who lived between Adam and Noah. When those pious men died, Satan inspired the people to make statues of them to remember their devotion. Over generations, the knowledge was lost, and those statues became objects of worship. The names Wadd, Suwa', Yaghuth, Ya'uq, and Nasr were thus the first idols to corrupt pure tawheed, and they eventually spread to different Arab tribes. Wadd was worshipped by the tribe of Kalb, Suwa' by Hudhayl, Yaghuth by Murad, Ya'uq by Hamdan, and Nasr by Himyar. This historical detail shows how shirk begins with seemingly innocent intentions—honouring the righteous—and gradually degenerates into full-blown worship of creation. The elite used these idols as symbols of cultural identity. Abandoning them would mean abandoning one's heritage. This argument is still used today when people are called to return to pure monotheism and leave innovations. Noah's people clung to their idols because they were the idols of their fathers, and the powerful leaders used that sentiment to block the truth. The verse exposes the root of their misguidance: blind following of tradition in the face of clear evidence from a messenger. For the believers, it serves as a warning that even reverence for the righteous, if taken beyond its limits, can lead to the same trap.

=24=

Noah now delivers a sobering judgment on the elite: "Those elite have already led many astray." They are not just misguided themselves; they are the cause of the misguidance of countless others. Their tremendous plot succeeded in keeping the masses in the darkness of shirk. Each soul that followed them bears a share of their sin, and the leaders bear the burden of all those they led astray.

The phrase "already led many astray" is a statement of fact. Noah witnessed the stubbornness and the spread of disbelief generation after generation. Then comes a prayer that some may find surprising: "So O Lord, only allow the wrongdoers to stray farther away." At first glance, this might sound like a curse for increased misguidance.

However, scholars explain that this prayer came after Noah knew, through revelation, that his people would never believe. Allah told him, "None of your people will believe except those who have already believed." When a people reach a point of irreversible rejection, Allah sometimes withdraws His guidance from them, and they are left to wander deeper into their own darkness. Noah's prayer is an expression of that reality. He is essentially saying: since they have chosen misguidance and led others into it, O Lord, let the natural consequence of their arrogance unfold. It is not that Allah would misguide the innocent; rather, the wrongdoers have sealed their own fate, and Noah is asking for that seal to be confirmed so that justice can prevail.

This is similar to the verse in Al-Ma'arij where Allah says to let them indulge until they face their Day. The prayer is a declaration of submission to the divine decree and a recognition that further delay will only produce more victims of their misguidance. It is a difficult but necessary end to an era of mercy that was totally exhausted.

=25=

The surah now succinctly narrates the outcome: "So because of their sins, they were drowned, then admitted into the Fire." The word "So" links the punishment directly to their actions. Their drowning was not an arbitrary act of nature but a direct consequence of their persistent rebellion, their following of the elite, and their worship of idols. The drowning refers to the great Flood. The water gushed from the earth and poured from the sky, overwhelming them completely. Noah and the believers were saved in the Ark, while every disbeliever, including Noah's own son who sought refuge in a mountain, perished. Then comes the shocking transition: "then admitted into the Fire." The drowning was the end of their worldly life, but immediately thereafter they entered the Hellfire in the Barzakh (the intermediate life of the grave) to await the final punishment of the Day of Judgment. Their punishment is continuous. They moved from the overwhelming water to the overwhelming fire.

The phrase "And they found none to help them against Allah" is a devastating conclusion. Their idols—Wadd, Suwa', Yaghuth, Ya'uq, Nasr—could not rescue them. The elites who led them astray were themselves drowning. The wealth and children that they thought were their strength became useless. They had no protector, no intercessor, no shelter. This mirrors the earlier surah's warning that the punishment would be "to be averted by none. " It also connects to the scene where the wicked would wish to ransom themselves with their children and clan, but to no avail. The verse is a stark reminder that when Allah's punishment arrives, all worldly supports collapse. For the believers, this reinforces the importance of relying only on Allah and not being deceived by the power or numbers of the oppressors.

=26=

This verse flashes back to the prayer Noah made before the Flood. The Arabic form indicates this was a pivotal supplication. Noah's words are absolute: "Do not leave a single disbeliever on earth.

" The phrase "a single" (dayyar) means not one, not even a solitary individual. This is a prayer for the complete cleansing of the earth from the filth of shirk.

The word "disbeliever" (kafir) refers to those who, after the clear warning and the prolonged effort, had hardened in their rejection beyond any hope of change. Noah's plea might seem severe, but it came after Allah had already informed him that no more of his people would believe. The flood was decreed. His prayer is therefore an alignment with the divine judgment, not a personal vendetta. It also reflects the severity of the corruption. When a society becomes so pervasively corrupted that it can no longer produce righteousness, its removal is a mercy to future generations. The disbelievers had not only rejected faith but had actively led others astray and plotted against the truth. Their continued existence would only mean the perpetuation of evil. Noah's prayer is therefore a prayer for the protection of the very concept of faith on earth. It highlights that the earth belongs to Allah and should not be a permanent haven for those who wage war against His message. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, was a mercy to the worlds, and his mission extended to all nations; thus, such a prayer was not made for his opponents, as his was a universal and enduring message. But for Noah, the context was different. The lesson for believers is to recognize that there comes a point when evil must be uprooted, and that the survival of faith sometimes requires the removal of its entrenched enemies. The prayer is a sobering demonstration of the ultimate consequence of persistent disbelief.

=27=

Noah provides the reason for his drastic prayer: "For if You spare any of them, they will certainly mislead Your servants, and give birth only to wicked sinners, staunch disbelievers." This is not an assumption; it is based on centuries of observation and divine insight. Noah saw the depth of corruption in his people. Generation after generation, the children inherited the disbelief and stubbornness of their parents. The environment was so saturated with shirk that any surviving disbeliever would simply restart the cycle of misguidance. They would "mislead Your servants" by reviving idol worship, by confusing the believers, and by perpetuating the tremendous plot.

The phrase "give birth only to wicked sinners, staunch disbelievers" indicates that the disbelief had become almost a genetic trait, not biologically but culturally. The children raised in that society would inevitably grow up as staunch disbelievers, excessively sinful and hardened in their rejection.

The Arabic "fajir" (wicked sinner) and "kaffar" (staunch disbeliever) intensify the description. It was a lost society. This prayer is not a blanket statement about all situations; it is specific to Noah's people, whose fate was already sealed by divine decree. It also highlights a principle: sometimes, the eradication of a corrupt element is necessary to allow a new, pure beginning. After the flood, the earth was repopulated through the righteous believers on the Ark. The verse serves as a warning about the power of environment and parenting. If a society is corrupt, its offspring are likely to be corrupt. For the believers, the lesson is to strive to build a righteous society where children are raised upon faith, so that this tragic verdict never applies to them. Noah's words are a lament as much as a prayer, reflecting a heart broken by the sight of one of the worst communities in history.

=28=

The surah concludes with Noah's comprehensive concluding prayer, a beautiful model of prophetic supplication. He begins with himself: "My Lord! Forgive me." Even a mighty prophet who served for 950 years feels his need for divine forgiveness, recognizing that no one is perfect before Allah. Then he says, "my parents." This shows profound filial piety; Noah remembers the parents who raised him, asking Allah to forgive them because they were believers, as supported by other texts. Then "whoever enters my house in faith." The "house" can refer to his physical home, which was a place of safety and worship for the believers, or to the Ark, or even figuratively to his religion. Anyone who enters under his protection as a believer is included in this prayer. Then he extends it universally: "and all believing men and women." This demonstrates that Noah's heart was with the entire community of faith, across all times who would follow his legacy. It mirrors the comprehensive mercy of a prophet. Then comes the contrasting ending: "And increase the wrongdoers only in destruction.

" The word "tabaran" means utter ruin, loss, and perdition. Having prayed for the forgiveness and mercy for the believers, he seals the fate of the hardened wrongdoers with a prayer for their complete annihilation. This prayer is the prophetic echo of the divine decree, a final alignment with Allah's judgment. The balance between mercy and justice is perfect. The surah that began with Noah being sent as a warner to his people before a painful punishment comes to them ends with the fulfilment of that warning in both directions: salvation for the believers and destruction for the disbelievers. It connects to Al-Ma'arij's themes: the faithful who pray, give charity, and fear the punishment will be in Gardens, held in honour, while the mockers and idolaters will face a punishment that cannot be averted. Noah's story is the historical proof of that divine pattern, providing the Prophet Muhammad with comfort, warning the Quraysh, and teaching all believers the power of steadfast faith and the inevitable end of arrogance.
