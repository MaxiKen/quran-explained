=121=

The temptation reached its tragic conclusion. Despite the explicit warning and the clear identification of Satan as an enemy, Adam and his wife both ate from the forbidden tree.

The phrase “So they both ate from the tree” confirms that both were deceived. The immediate consequence was not the promised immortality or kingdom, but a profound loss: “their nakedness was exposed to them. ” Before this, they had been clothed in a state of innocence, but now the garments of Paradise were stripped away, and they became aware of their nakedness. This exposure is both physical and symbolic—it represents the loss of dignity and the cover of divine protection. Their instinctive reaction was to cover themselves, “prompting them to cover themselves with leaves from Paradise.” They frantically began plucking leaves and sticking them together to hide their shame.

This is the origin of human modesty and the awareness of the body as something to be shielded. It also shows that even in their disobedience, they retained an innate sense of modesty. Then the verse states, “So Adam disobeyed his Lord, and so lost his way.

” The Arabic “‘aṣā” (disobeyed) and “ghawā” (lost his way, became misguided) are used. This is not the disobedience of arrogant rebellion but a slip caused by forgetfulness and deception. Adam’s misguidance was temporary; he did not persist in it. The verse does not mention Eve separately, but Adam is addressed as the representative. Importantly, this event is not described as a “fall” carrying original sin upon all humanity, as in some other traditions. In Islam, it is a personal mistake, repented, forgiven, and without inherited stain. The covering with leaves teaches that human beings, when they sin, should immediately seek to correct their state and cover their faults, both physically and spiritually, through repentance. The verse closes the episode of the temptation, setting the stage for the divine mercy that immediately follows, demonstrating that human error does not sever the relationship with Allah when followed by sincere turning back.

=122=

Despite the disobedience, the verse opens with a powerful conjunction: “Then his Lord chose him.” The Arabic “thumma ijtabāhu rabbuhu” indicates that after the sin and the exposure, Allah turned to Adam with a special selection.

The word “ijtabā” means to select, purify, and bring close. This choosing is an act of pure grace; it was not earned by Adam’s deed, because he had just erred. It demonstrates that Allah’s mercy can overtake a sin even before full repentance, preparing the heart to return. Then, “accepted his repentance” (fa-tāba ‘alayhi). Adam and his wife had learned words of supplication from Allah, as mentioned elsewhere: “Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves, and if You do not forgive us and have mercy upon us, we will surely be among the losers.” Allah taught them how to repent, and when they turned back, He accepted their repentance.

The phrase emphasizes that Allah is the One who turns to His servant with forgiveness; human repentance is met with divine acceptance. Finally, “and guided him rightly” (wa-hadā). After forgiveness, Adam was given guidance to navigate the earthly life ahead. He was not left to wander in confusion; prophethood was granted to him.

This sequence—sin, exposure, turning to Allah, forgiveness, and guidance—is the pattern for every human being. There is no sin too great for Allah’s mercy if the servant repents sincerely. The verse directly refutes any notion that Adam’s mistake permanently corrupted human nature. Instead, it shows that the default human relationship with Allah is one of mercy and restoration. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, this was a source of immense hope, and he said, “If you were not to sin, Allah would replace you with a people who would sin and then seek forgiveness so He could forgive them.” The verse beautifully rounds off the story of Adam in this surah by highlighting the triumph of divine mercy over human weakness, a central theme that ties together the stories of Moses, the magicians, and now the father of mankind.

=123=

After the repentance, the divine decree of descent to earth was issued. The command “Descend, both of you, from here” was addressed to Adam and Eve, or to Adam and Iblīs according to some interpretations, but the context here includes Satan as well—“together with Satan as enemies to each other.” The earthly life is established as an arena of enmity between humanity and Satan. This enmity is not a curse but a test; humans are warned that Satan is their sworn foe and will try to lead them astray, just as he did their parents. Then Allah pronounced the core promise that defines the human condition: “Then when guidance comes to you from Me, whoever follows My guidance will neither go astray in this life nor suffer in the next.

” The phrase “guidance from Me” refers to the revelation sent through prophets, scriptures, and the innate human conscience. The promise is comprehensive: those who follow this guidance will be protected from “ghayy” (misguidance, spiritual error) in the world, and from “shaqā’” (wretchedness, misery) in the Hereafter. “Neither go astray nor suffer” is an absolute guarantee of a good life and an excellent eternal outcome.

This is the foundational covenant for all humanity: guidance is provided, and following it leads to safety; rejecting it leads to loss, as the next verse will detail. The descent is not a punishment without remedy but the beginning of a journey in which every soul is equipped with the means to return to Paradise. The phrasing echoes the earlier story of Moses, where following guidance brought salvation and turning away brought destruction. For the Prophet Muhammad, this verse reaffirmed that his message was the latest and most complete form of that “guidance from Me. ” It offers a profound consolation to every human: if you hold fast to the divine guidance in a world of enmity and temptation, you will experience peace here and eternal bliss hereafter, free from the misery that Adam briefly encountered.

=124=

In contrast to the blessed promise, the verse warns of the consequences of rejection. “Whoever turns away from My Reminder” refers to those who deliberately ignore the Quran and the guidance of Allah.

The word “dhikrī” (My Reminder) encompasses all divine revelation. The consequence is twofold: first, “will certainly have a miserable life. ” The Arabic “ma‘īshatan ḍankan” means a life of hardship, constriction, anxiety, and spiritual poverty.

This is not necessarily material poverty; many wealthy disbelievers live in inner turmoil, depression, and meaninglessness. The heart that turns away from Allah is constricted, no matter how much wealth surrounds it. This misery manifests as discontent, fear, and a sense of futility. Second, “then We will raise them up blind on the Day of Judgment.” The blindness here is literal and spiritual. They will be resurrected without sight, unable to see the path to safety or the face of the Prophet interceding, while the believers will have light. This blindness is the direct result of their willful blindness to the truth in this world. The verse aligns with the earlier description of the wicked being blue-faced and gathered in humiliation. The contrast between the “miserable life” and the “goodly life” promised to the righteous believer is a recurring Quranic theme. The Prophet Muhammad explained that the world is a prison for the believer and a paradise for the disbeliever; the believer’s true ease is in the Hereafter, but even in this world, faith provides a spiritual contentment that the disbeliever cannot find. The verse does not claim that every believer will be materially rich, but that the one who turns away from Allah will inevitably experience a constricted heart and a horrifying resurrection.

The mention of “blind” also resonates with the story of the Sâmiri, who was made to cry “Do not touch me!”—a form of social blindness and isolation. This verse serves as a powerful warning, especially when placed after the story of Adam, who repented and was guided, showing that the way out of misery is always open through turning back to the Reminder.

=125=

In the terror of the resurrection, the disbeliever, now blind, will plead with Allah. The cry “My Lord!” is poignant; they recognize His Lordship now, when it is too late.

The question “Why have you raised me up blind, although I used to see?” expresses both bewilderment and a veiled protest. They had physical sight in the world, which they used to enjoy Allah’s blessings and witness His signs, but they refused to see the truth. Now, when sight is most desperately needed to navigate the horrors of judgment, they are deprived of it.

The question implies that they think it unfair, as if they are being punished without reason. They do not yet fully grasp the connection between their worldly behavior and their current state. This verse captures the human tendency to demand justice without recognizing one’s own faults. The blindness is not arbitrary; it is a direct consequence of their own choices.

The question also reveals the pain of the loss: they remember seeing, which makes the blindness more agonizing. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, was asked about how people will be gathered on the Day of Judgment, and he explained that the disbelievers will be gathered blind, deaf, and dumb, while the believers will be with light. The verse sets the stage for the divine response in the next verse, which will provide the clear, logical connection between their worldly neglect of the revelations and their neglected state on that Day. The lesson is that the faculties we are given—sight, hearing, intellect—are tests, and their misuse will lead to their removal or transformation into sources of regret. Every act of ignoring a sign of Allah is, in reality, a step toward one’s own blindness on the Day when sight matters most. The plea “My Lord” also contains a flicker of hope, but for the unrepentant disbeliever, it is a cry that will receive the response of justice.

=126=

The divine response is perfectly just and mirrors the crime. Allah says, “It is so” (kadhālika), affirming that the punishment fits the deed. The explanation follows: “just as Our revelations came to you and you neglected them.

” The Arabic word “nasītahā” comes from the root for forgetting, but here it means willful neglect and abandonment. The revelations—the signs in creation, the prophets, the scriptures—came clearly, but the disbeliever chose to ignore them, to live as if they did not exist. They turned a blind eye to the truth.

Therefore, the response is precise: “so Today you are neglected.” The passive form “tunsā” means you are left, forgotten, disregarded. Just as they treated Allah’s signs as unworthy of attention, they will be treated as unworthy of mercy.

This is not an arbitrary punishment but a direct reflection of their own actions. The irony is profound: they who considered themselves important and self-sufficient will find themselves utterly disregarded.

The verse teaches the principle of just recompense; Allah’s punishment is not a new, separate torment but the inevitable outcome of one’s own choices. It also clarifies that the blindness and neglect are not because Allah forgot them, but because they forgot His revelations. This echoes other Quranic statements: “They forgot Allah, so He forgot them.” The Prophet Muhammad would often emphasize that sins have consequences in this life and the next, and that neglecting the Quran leads to a neglected state. The verse offers a powerful psychological and spiritual insight: what you value and pay attention to shapes your ultimate reality. To neglect the Creator’s words is to court eternal obscurity. The conversation between the disbeliever and Allah reveals the perfect logic of divine justice, leaving no room for complaint.

=127=

After detailing the specific case, Allah generalizes the principle. “This is how We reward whoever transgresses and does not believe in the revelations of their Lord.” The punishment of a miserable life and a blind resurrection is the standard recompense for the combination of transgression (isrāf) and disbelief. Transgression refers to exceeding limits, indulging in sins, and wasting life in heedlessness, while disbelief is rejecting the divine signs. The two often go together; a heart that denies the truth will inevitably transgress moral bounds.

The phrase “revelations of their Lord” emphasizes the personal lordship and mercy that they rejected. Then the verse adds a terrifying dimension: “And the punishment of the Hereafter is far more severe and more lasting.” The miserable life and blind resurrection are only the beginning; the ultimate and unimaginably worse punishment is the Fire. The severity refers to the intensity of the pain, and “more lasting” (abqā) emphasizes eternity. This answers any misconception that the hardship of a disbeliever’s worldly life, or the temporary terror of the gathering, is the full extent of their penalty. It is only a sample of what is to come.

The phrase “more lasting” connects back to the magicians’ declaration to Pharaoh that Allah is “more lasting in punishment. ” It ties the surah’s narratives together, showing a consistent divine law. For the believers, this verse reinforces gratitude for guidance and fear of straying. For the deniers, it is a warning that no temporary worldly pleasure is worth an everlasting punishment. The Prophet was commanded to convey this warning, not to force belief.

The verse also highlights that divine justice operates on a universal scale; no exception is made for anyone who meets these criteria. It closes the section on the consequences of turning away from the Reminder, leading naturally into the next verse which reflects on the historical evidence of such punishment.

=128=

The surah now addresses the Meccan pagans directly, appealing to historical observation. The rhetorical question, “Is it not yet clear to them,” implies that the evidence has been repeatedly presented but they remain heedless.

The phrase “how many peoples We destroyed before them” refers to the civilizations of the past—ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Lot, the Pharaoh and his army, and others—who were annihilated because of their rejection of the prophets. The Meccans traveled on their trade routes and passed by the ruins of these civilizations, such as the stone dwellings of Thamūd in the north and the traces of the people of Lot. The ruins were visible, undeniable testimonies to the destruction that came upon the unjust.

The Arabic “ya‘mashūna fī masākinihim” means they traverse their dwelling places. The fact that they could walk through these ruins and still not take heed was a sign of their spiritual blindness. Then the verse concludes, “Surely in this are signs for people of sound judgment. ” The word “nuhā” again appears, referring to intelligent minds that can restrain themselves from passion and see the deeper meaning. The lesson is clear: if past nations were destroyed for their denial, why should the present deniers feel immune? The Prophet Muhammad was sent as a mercy, but his people were repeating the same arrogance. This verse connects with the earlier story of Pharaoh, whose drowned army is one of the “peoples destroyed. ” It also ties back to the opening themes of the surah, where the stories of the past are recounted as a reminder. The argument is rational: observe history, see the pattern, and use your intellect to avoid the same fate. The verse encourages believers to travel and reflect on the remnants of past civilizations, not as tourists but as contemplators of divine justice, thereby increasing their taqwā.

=129=

This verse addresses a potential question: why are the Meccan deniers not immediately destroyed like the previous peoples? The answer lies in divine wisdom. “Had it not been for a prior decree from your Lord and a term already set” refers to two things. The “prior decree” is the word of Allah that has preceded, which includes His decision to delay the punishment of this Ummah out of mercy, and specifically that the Prophet’s nation would not be annihilated with a general destruction like the earlier ones. The “term already set” (ajalun musamman) is the appointed time for the end of their individual lives or the collective punishment, which might be in the Hereafter or in a specific form. Because of this decree and appointed term, “their instant doom would have been inevitable” — otherwise, their denial was so severe that immediate punishment would have been justified. The verse serves multiple purposes. It comforts the Prophet, who might have yearned for a swift divine punishment against the mockers. It warns the disbelievers that they are only spared by Allah’s forbearance, not because they are right. It also establishes the principle that the Muslim Ummah, unlike some previous nations, would not face a single, overwhelming extermination; instead, its disbelievers might face individual deaths or defeat in battle, but the community as a whole would remain, and the ultimate punishment is deferred to the Hereafter.

This is an aspect of the special mercy granted through the presence of the Prophet. The “prior decree” could also refer to the decree that punishment would only come after clear warning, and the Prophet was still among them. This verse is a direct parallel to other verses where Allah says He would not punish them while the Prophet was in their midst.

The phrase “your Lord” adds a personal touch of reassurance: the Prophet’s Lord is in control, and His decisions are based on deep wisdom. The lesson for believers is to not be impatient for the punishment of the wicked, trusting that Allah’s timing is perfect and His decree is for the best.

=130=

With the explanation that punishment is deferred, the Prophet is given a direct command: “So be patient with what they say.” The patience required is against the insults, mockery, and demands for signs.

The Arabic “fa-ṣbir” is a command of steadfastness, which is the core prophetic virtue. Then, the remedy for bearing their speech is prescribed: continuous glorification of Allah. “And glorify the praises of your Lord” translates “wa-sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbika,” meaning to declare His transcendence while praising Him. The specified times are “before sunrise” (qabla ṭulū‘i ash-shams) and “before sunset” (qabla ghurūbihā), which refer to the Fajr and ‘Aṣr prayers respectively, or more broadly the times of morning and evening remembrance. “And glorify Him in the hours of the night” refers to the night prayers like Tahajjud, and “at both ends of the day” (wa-aṭrāfa an-nahār) could refer to the prayers at the edges of the day—Fajr, Maghrib, and perhaps Ẓuhr and ‘Aṣr together. The purpose is profound: “so that you may be pleased with the reward. ” The Arabic “la‘allaka tarḍā” means that this regimen of worship and remembrance will lead to the satisfaction of the Prophet’s heart, knowing that Allah is pleased with him and that his reward in the Hereafter will be such that he is completely content.

This is a direct response to the distress mentioned at the beginning of the surah: the Quran was not sent to cause distress, and these acts of glorification are the path to spiritual contentment. The Prophet’s contentment is tied to the glorification of his Lord, not to the acceptance of the people. This verse is a powerful prescription for anyone facing opposition and emotional pain: immerse yourself in the worship and praise of Allah, and you will find inner peace and the promise of a reward that will make you forget all hardship.

The mention of specific times emphasizes regularity; the entire day is bracketed by divine remembrance, leaving no room for Satanic whisperings or worldly sorrow to dominate the heart.

=131=

The Prophet is further instructed to disregard the worldly attractions that the disbelievers enjoy. “Do not let your eyes crave” translates “lā tamuddanna ‘aynayka,” literally “do not stretch your eyes,” a vivid expression for longing and envy. The objects of craving are “what We have allowed some of the disbelievers to enjoy,” such as wealth, status, children, and luxuries. These are described as “the fleeting splendour of this worldly life” (zahra al-ḥayāti ad-dunyā).

The word “zahrah” means bloom or flower, emphasizing its temporary, superficial beauty that quickly fades. Then the purpose is revealed: “which We test them with.” Their wealth is not a sign of Allah’s pleasure; it is a test to see if they are grateful or become more arrogant, and it will be a witness against them. This refutes the materialistic view that prosperity indicates divine favour.

The verse then redirects the Prophet’s gaze: “But your Lord’s provision in the Hereafter is far better and more lasting.” The provision (rizq) of Paradise—peace, eternal youth, nearness to Allah—is incomparably superior in quality (khayr) and duration (abqā). This verse consoles the Prophet and all believers who might see the disbelievers flourishing while they themselves endure poverty or hardship. It realigns the heart from temporary glitter to eternal treasure. The command “do not let your eyes crave” is an essential discipline of spiritual focus. It echoes the lesson of Adam: Satan tempted him with a “kingdom that does not fade away,” but what was with Allah was better and lasting, while the tree was a test. The believer must internalize that whatever the disbelievers have is a fleeting test for them, and the believer’s true wealth is the contentment of faith and the promise of the Hereafter. The verse beautifully ties back to the magicians, who renounced Pharaoh’s rewards for the eternal provision of Allah. It is a call to elevate one’s aspirations beyond the material trinkets of this world.

=132=

The Prophet is given a crucial instruction regarding his family and his community. “Bid your people to pray” (wa’mur ahlaka biṣ-ṣalāh) means command your family and your followers to establish the prayer. This emphasizes the central role of prayer in the life of faith. It is the first duty after belief, and the head of the household is responsible for ensuring its observance. The command is followed by “and be diligent in observing it” (waṣṭabir ‘alayhā), which means you yourself must be steadfast and patient in performing it. The Prophet is to lead by example. Then Allah reassures: “We do not ask you to provide.” Provision (rizq) is not the Prophet’s responsibility; he is not required to ensure his family’s sustenance or the economic well-being of his followers at the expense of his spiritual mission. This was a direct comfort, as the Prophet often faced poverty and the burden of his dependents. Allah says, “It is We Who provide for you.” The Lord of all creation takes direct responsibility for the provision of His servants, especially those engaged in His worship.

The verse ends with a powerful principle: “And the ultimate outcome is only for the people of righteousness. ” The Arabic “al-‘āqibatu lit-taqwā” means the good end in this world and the next belongs to those who have taqwā, who are mindful of Allah. This statement shifts the focus from material outcomes to spiritual ones. It echoes the earlier promise that those who believe and do good will have the highest ranks. The verse combines a command to worship with a promise of provision, liberating the believer from the anxiety of sustenance. It also corrects any misconception that the Prophet’s mission was about acquiring worldly power; his mission was to lead people to prayer and righteousness, and the ultimate victory—of the message and the believers—is tied to taqwā, not to wealth or numbers.

This is a timeless lesson for anyone engaged in da‘wah: prioritize prayer and reliance on Allah, and leave the results and provisions to Him.

=133=

The Meccan pagans’ persistent demand for a tangible miracle is addressed. They said, “If only he could bring us a sign from his Lord!” They wanted something spectacular, like the staff of Moses or the she-camel of Ṣāliḥ, instead of the Quran. The verse dismisses this demand by pointing out an already existing and greater sign: the Quran itself. “Have they not already received a confirmation of what is in earlier Scriptures?

” The Arabic “aw lam ta’tihim bayyinatu mā fī aṣ-ṣuḥufi al-ūlā” means that the Quran is a clear proof that confirms the truth contained in the ancient scriptures—the Torah, the Gospel, and others. The Quran narrates stories of previous prophets, aligns with core monotheistic teachings, and provides a clear, consistent message that matches what sincere scholars of those scriptures would recognize.

Furthermore, the Quran’s own miraculous nature—its eloquence, its wisdom, its preservation—is a sufficient sign for those who reflect. The verse implies that demanding additional signs after the Quran is sheer obstinacy. The “confirmation” also points to the fact that the coming of Prophet Muhammad was foretold in earlier scriptures, so his presence and the Quran are themselves the fulfillment of those prophecies. This argument was especially relevant to the People of the Book who might recognize the truth, but the Quraysh also heard from travelers and scholars. The verse is a powerful logical rebuttal: you want a sign, but the sign you have—the Quran—is the ultimate sign, a timeless intellectual and spiritual miracle. It connects with the beginning of the surah, where the Quran is described as a revelation from the Lord of the heavens and the earth. The demand for physical miracles ignores the fact that those who witnessed such miracles in the past, like Pharaoh, still disbelieved. The lesson is that the greatest guidance is the Word of Allah, and those who reject it would not believe even if mountains were moved. The verse challenges the listener to engage with the Quran directly instead of making speculative demands.

=134=

This verse presents a profound divine argument for sending the Prophet. Allah explains that if the Meccans had been annihilated by a divine punishment before the Prophet came, they would have protested on the Day of Judgment. They would have said, “Our Lord! If only You had sent us a messenger, we would have followed Your revelations before being humiliated and put to shame. ” The words “before being humiliated and put to shame” refer to the disgrace of worldly punishment or the shame of the Hereafter. This imaginary argument shows that Allah, in His perfect justice, cuts off all excuses by sending messengers. No people are destroyed without a clear warning. The Quraysh could not claim they had no warner, because the Prophet Muhammad was in their midst, reciting the clear verses. The verse highlights the mercy in the advent of prophethood: it is a preventive grace that removes the pretext of ignorance. The same logic applied to previous nations; every community that was destroyed had received a messenger. For the Meccans to demand further signs after the messenger has come with the clear Quran is, therefore, an act of supreme ingratitude and exposes their insincerity.

The verse also refutes the fatalistic objection that people might be predestined to disbelief; the sending of messengers proves that guidance is offered, and the choice is real. The phrase “before being humiliated” indicates that worldly punishment brings humiliation, and they would have pleaded for a chance. But since the chance is now given in the form of the Prophet, they have no argument left. This verse serves as a direct response to those who might wonder why the Meccans were not immediately punished; they are given respite and the messenger, so that on the Day of Judgment they will have no defense. The Prophet is comforted that his mission is an essential act of justice and mercy, filling a gap that would otherwise have left humanity with a legitimate complaint.

=135=

The surah concludes with a final, powerful challenge from the Prophet to his opponents. The command “Say” is directed to the Prophet, giving him words that embody a confident but patient stance. “Each of us is waiting” — the Prophet and the believers are waiting for Allah’s promise of victory and the ultimate outcome, while the disbelievers are waiting for the destruction of the Prophet and his message, or they are waiting to see what the future holds.

The phrase “so keep waiting” (fa-tarabbaṣū) is a sharp, almost ominous instruction: wait on, for the truth will become manifest. Then the declaration: “You will soon know who is on the Straight Path and is rightly guided.” The “straight path” (aṣ-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm) is the path of Islam, the path of those who follow the guidance. The “rightly guided” (ihtadā) are those who have achieved the goal of guidance.

This is a clear, confident assertion that the truth of the Prophet’s message will soon become undeniable. The waiting referred to could include the worldly outcome—such as the victory at Badr or the conquest of Mecca—or the final judgment in the Hereafter, or both. The tone is neither desperate nor confrontational; it is the calm certainty of a prophet who knows that his Lord’s word is supreme. The verse encapsulates the central conflict of the surah: truth versus falsehood, faith versus tyranny. It echoes the theme that ultimate victory belongs to the believers, as shown in the story of Moses and the magicians, and that the disbelievers’ waiting will end in disappointment. For the Prophet, this was a final reassurance: he did not need to force the outcome; he only needed to deliver the message and wait with patience and confidence. The surah, which began with the comforting words “Ṭâ-Hâ” and “We have not revealed the Quran to you to cause you distress,” ends on a note of ultimate triumph and clarity. The choice is laid before all: wait and see, for the truth will inevitably shine, and those on the straight path will be vindicated.
