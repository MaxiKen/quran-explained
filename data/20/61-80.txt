=61=

Before the contest began, Moses turned to the assembled magicians with a stern warning. He did not view them merely as competitors to be defeated; he cared about their souls. His words “Woe to you” carry deep prophetic concern.

The phrase “Do not fabricate a lie against Allah” addresses the root of their deception. The magicians were about to perform illusions and then attribute such powers to their false gods or to their own genius, which would be a slander against the true Creator. Fabricating a lie against Allah means ascribing to Him what is false, whether by claiming partners for Him, denying His signs, or misrepresenting His message. Moses warned that such a crime would provoke a divine punishment that could “wipe you out,” utterly destroy them. The torment here could be understood as the sudden humiliation they were about to witness, or a greater punishment in this world and the next. He then added a universal principle: “Whoever fabricates lies is bound to fail.

” This is not merely a threat but a statement of a cosmic law. Falsehood, no matter how elaborately constructed, carries within itself the seeds of its own collapse. The magicians were masters of illusion, but Moses spoke with the authority of revelation. His warning was not a lack of confidence; rather, it was a final call to conscience before the undeniable sign would appear. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, similarly warned his opponents that their idols and fabrications would lead them to ruin. This moment also highlights the prophetic ethics: even when facing enemies who sought to humiliate him, Moses gave them a chance to reconsider, demonstrating that the mission is to save, not to destroy. The verse sets the moral stage for the imminent miracle, showing that the real battle is between truth and fabricated lies.

=62=

Moses’s warning had a visible effect on the magicians. Instead of immediately proceeding with confidence, they began to argue among themselves in hushed, private conversations.

The Arabic “fa-tanāza‘ū amrahum baynahum” suggests a heated internal dispute. This indicates that they were not a monolithic block of defiant unbelievers; some may have been shaken by Moses’s tone, sensing that this was not ordinary sorcery. Others might have dismissed the warning and wanted to crush him. The fact that they conversed privately shows that Pharaoh’s side was not as united as it seemed. The warning had planted a seed of doubt. The magicians were the intellectual elite of Egypt, well-versed in the secrets of illusion and sleight of hand. They could recognize that Moses’s confidence did not stem from the typical arrogance of a rival magician. Their dispute likely revolved around whether Moses was really just a magician or something more. This internal division is a common pattern when falsehood confronts truth: the coalition of falsehood begins to crack. The Prophet Muhammad’s opponents also experienced internal conflicts, with some secretly acknowledging the Quran’s beauty while publicly opposing it.

The verse also shows the mercy contained in a well-timed word of warning; Moses’s few words became the catalyst for introspection. Yet, despite the dispute, they did not immediately embrace the truth; they decided to press forward, perhaps out of fear of Pharaoh or hope of reward. The private conversation foreshadows their ultimate surrender; the truth was already working in their hearts before the miracle even occurred. The stage is now set for the public resolution of their inner conflict, which will erupt in a stunning proclamation of faith.

=63=

After their private dispute, the magicians came to a collective conclusion. Their statement, likely addressed to each other to boost morale, reveals the political and cultural framing they adopted. They labeled Moses and Aaron as merely “two magicians,” reducing the prophetic mission to a professional rivalry. The accusation that they “want to drive you out of your land” echoes Pharaoh’s own earlier propaganda. They painted the message of monotheism as a foreign conspiracy aimed at dispossessing the Egyptians. This was a powerful appeal to national pride and fear of displacement. Further, they claimed that Moses sought to “do away with your most cherished traditions.

” The word translated as “traditions” is “ṭarīqatikumu al-muthlā,” meaning your exemplary way of life, religion, and customs. The magicians accused Moses of wanting to dismantle the entire cultural and religious fabric of Egypt, including the worship of their deities and the social order.

This is a typical reaction of those invested in a status quo when confronted with a message that challenges its foundations. They portray reform as destruction and prophets as foreign agents.

The verse reveals that even the magicians, who were about to witness the truth, initially rationalized their resistance by framing the conflict in worldly terms. The same accusations were hurled at the Prophet Muhammad: that he was a poet or magician seeking to divide the community and erase ancestral customs.

Yet, the accusation is hollow; Moses’s demand was not for the Egyptians to lose their land but to free the Israelites and worship the One God. The magicians’ conclusion was a final attempt to steel themselves against the miracle that would shatter their illusions and, ultimately, their own hearts would be the first to be captured by the very tradition they were defending.

=64=

Having framed the conflict as a battle for their way of life, the magicians encourage each other to put on a flawless performance. The phrase “orchestrate your plan” translates “fa-ajmi‘ū kaydakum,” meaning gather all your cunning and stratagems. They were to use every trick, every secret technique, to produce the most impressive display of magic possible. The instruction to “come forward in perfect ranks” emphasizes unity and order, presenting a formidable, disciplined front. They knew that a scattered and disorganized display would weaken its psychological impact. The spectacle was to be a show of force, designed to intimidate Moses and convince the crowd of their superiority. Then they state the high stakes: “Whoever prevails today will certainly be successful.” Victory in this contest, they believed, would determine who held power, prestige, and the allegiance of the people. Their metric of success was entirely worldly—public acclaim, Pharaoh’s favour, and the preservation of their privileged status. They could not yet conceive of a success that lay beyond this world. This reflects the mindset of those who measure everything by immediate, tangible outcomes.

Yet, the verse contains a profound irony: the success they spoke of would indeed be achieved, but not by them. True success would be the imān that the defeated magicians would gain moments later, a success that would outlast Pharaoh’s empire. The call to perfect ranks also shows that falsehood often relies on impressive organization and numbers, while truth may appear small and humble. The Prophet Muhammad’s small band of followers facing the massive Quraysh armies at Badr mirrors this scene. The verse thus captures the pre-battle tension, where the forces of illusion are most confident, just before the truth shatters their ranks.

=65=

With the crowds gathered and the ranks formed, the magicians addressed Moses directly, offering him the choice to begin the contest. Their words “O Moses! Either you cast, or let us be the first to cast” are couched in apparent fairness and professional courtesy. In ancient contests of magic, the order of performance could be a strategic advantage; going first might overwhelm the opponent, while going last could allow a counter-response. By giving Moses the choice, they displayed a certain confidence, perhaps even a touch of condescension, as if to say, “We are so certain of our superiority that we let you decide.” It was also a way to appear magnanimous before the massive festival crowd. Moses, however, would make a choice that surprised them. This moment also underscores the prophetic calm in the face of provocation. Moses did not feel pressured to seize any perceived tactical edge. The magicians, despite their earlier internal dispute, now projected unity and bravado.

Yet, this politeness masked their fundamental misunderstanding of what they were dealing with. They saw the encounter as a duel between magicians, while Moses carried a sign from the Lord of the worlds. The Prophet Muhammad was similarly offered compromises and challenges by the Quraysh, who thought they were negotiating with a mere human claimant. The verse sets up the dramatic sequence: the magicians, with all their ropes and staffs, are about to unleash their greatest illusions, and Moses will respond not with equal trickery, but with divine truth. The choice they gave him became the very means by which their own falsehood would be exposed and swallowed whole.

=66=

Moses’s response was unexpected and strategically brilliant. By telling them to cast first, he allowed the full force of their illusion to be displayed before he presented his sign. This way, there could be no excuse that his miracle merely disrupted their concentration or that they had not had a fair chance. When they cast, a terrifying spectacle unfolded: their many ropes and staffs, through a powerful act of mass illusion, appeared to be living serpents, slithering rapidly across the ground.

The phrase “appeared to him—by their magic—to be slithering” carefully notes that this was a perceptual trick. The objects did not actually transform into living creatures; the magicians used some technique, perhaps involving quicksilver or hidden mechanisms combined with spells, to create the overwhelming impression of a floor filled with snakes. The verse acknowledges that even Moses, the prophet of Allah, was affected by the illusion and perceived the serpents as real. This highlights the sheer mastery of the Egyptian magicians; their craft was so potent that it could momentarily deceive even a prophet’s senses, though not his heart. The detail underscores the human vulnerability of Moses and makes the upcoming divine reassurance all the more necessary. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, also faced the psychological warfare of his enemies, whose words and propaganda were like magic, capable of causing momentary doubt in weak hearts.

The verse teaches that truth does not always appear triumphant at the first glance; falsehood can put on a dazzling show. Yet, the illusion, no matter how convincing, remains hollow. The slithering ropes were a powerful symbol of the deceptive nature of sin and unbelief, which appear lively and formidable but are ultimately lifeless. The scene is now set for the confrontation between the dead illusions and the living miracle.

=67=

Despite being a prophet armed with divine signs, Moses was fully human, and the terrifying sight of the magicians’ illusion filled him with a natural fear. The Arabic “fa-awjasa fī nafsihī khīfatan Mūsā” means he felt a hidden, inner fear. This fear was not a doubt in Allah’s promise, but an instinctive human reaction to a sudden, overwhelming threat. Imagine standing before a sea of writhing serpents, knowing the crowd and the tyrant are watching. Even the strongest heart can tremble. The Quran records this with honesty, neither diminishing the miracle of his prophethood nor hiding his human vulnerability.

This is a powerful lesson: having faith does not mean the absence of fear, but the ability to overcome it through trust in Allah. Moses concealed this fear; he did not let it show on his face or in his actions, preserving his composure before the enemy. That is the mark of true courage—not being fearless, but controlling the fear.

The verse also sets the stage for the immediate divine reassurance. In the most critical moment, when the heart is about to be overwhelmed, Allah’s help arrives. The Prophet Muhammad experienced similar moments, such as when he and Abu Bakr hid in the cave and the pursuers were at the entrance; he reassured his companion, “Do not grieve, Allah is with us. ” The inner fear of Moses was a test, a moment of human weakness that would be met with divine strength, teaching believers that when they feel fear in the path of truth, they are not abandoned. The concealment of fear also demonstrates leadership: showing panic can demoralize followers. Aaron stood beside him, and the Israelites watched from afar; Moses’s self-control maintained their hope until the word of Allah came.

=68=

At that very moment of inner fear, the divine voice spoke directly to Moses’s heart, repeating the earlier reassurance but now with even greater specificity. The command “Do not fear” is an instant balm, coming from the All-Seeing Lord who perceived the concealed fear of verse 67. This illustrates the immediate responsiveness of Allah to the emotional state of His prophets. The promise “It is certainly you who will prevail” uses the emphatic “innaka” and “anta al-a‘lā,” meaning you will be the uppermost, the victorious. There is no condition, no hesitation. The victory was not dependent on Moses’s skill or his own emotional state; it was decreed by Allah. This assurance transformed Moses’s fear into certainty. The promise of victory before any outward sign of it is a recurring pattern in the stories of the prophets. The Prophet Muhammad was similarly assured of ultimate triumph while still a persecuted minority in Mecca.

The word “a‘lā” also carries the connotation of supremacy in truth and dignity, not just a physical win. Moses would not merely defeat the magicians in a spectacle; he would establish the superiority of divine truth over human deception. The reassurance did not deny the reality of the threatening serpents but promised that they would be nullified. This teaches believers that the awareness of Allah’s promise should overpower the perception of danger. The fear was real, but the promise was truer. The verse marks the turning point, the inner shift from anxiety to empowered certainty, which would immediately manifest in his command to his staff. It shows that the source of a believer’s courage is not in themselves but in the living word of their Lord that descends upon the heart.

=69=

The instruction is specific: throw the staff that was in his right hand, the very object that had already turned into a serpent at the sacred valley. The command “it will swallow up what they have made” is a powerful image.

The Arabic “talqaf” means to snatch up and devour rapidly. The staff, once transformed, would not merely match their serpents but consume them entirely, leaving no trace of the illusion. The explanation follows: “what they have made is no more than a magic trick.

” The phrase “ṣana‘ū” implies something fabricated, an artifact of deception. Their serpents were not real life; they were a product of skill and trickery, devoid of substance.

This contrast between divine creation and human fabrication is central. Allah creates with the command “Be,” while magicians merely deceive the eyes. Then the verse pronounces a universal law: “magicians can never succeed wherever they go.

” The word “magician” here represents all who use falsehood to manipulate and deceive for unjust ends. Their apparent victories are temporary; ultimately, they fail because their foundation is illusion.

This statement is both a reassurance for Moses and a judgment on the whole enterprise of sorcery. The magicians themselves would soon testify to this truth. The Prophet Muhammad’s mission similarly confronted the “magic” of eloquence and propaganda with the substantial truth of the Quran, which swallows all false ideologies. The verse beautifully dissects the nature of falsehood: it may look impressive and cause momentary fear, but it has no real existence and cannot withstand the truth when truth arrives. The command to cast also signifies that the believer must act on divine command, and only then will the miracle manifest. Moses had to throw the staff, an act of obedience, and Allah would handle the rest.

=70=

When Moses cast his staff, it transformed into a massive serpent that devoured all the illusions instantly. The effect on the magicians was immediate and overwhelming. They did not simply admit defeat; they fell down in prostration. This physical act of sujūd was the ultimate submission, the very posture of worship they had reserved for Pharaoh or idols. Their prostration was not a polite bow but a collapse in recognition of the unmistakable divine power. They had enough expertise to know that what Moses displayed was not magic; no magician could truly transform a staff into a living creature that devours other objects, nor could any illusion resist their collective craft so completely. Their declaration, “We believe in the Lord of Aaron and Moses,” is profoundly significant. They did not just believe in Moses as a superior magician; they recognized the Lord of the two prophets.

The mention of Aaron alongside Moses emphasizes that both brothers were messengers of the same God. The magicians, who moments ago were the instruments of Pharaoh’s propaganda, now became the first public confessors of faith from the Egyptian elite. Their conversion is one of the most dramatic in the Quran. It shows that true experts, once they see the truth, can recognize it faster than the laypeople blinded by prejudice. The Prophet Muhammad’s staunchest opponents, like Umar ibn al-Khattab, also turned into the greatest champions of Islam when their hearts were opened. The public prostration was an open challenge to Pharaoh’s authority; it was a political as well as spiritual revolution. The verse marks the complete reversal: the plot of Pharaoh became the stage for the triumph of faith, and the magicians, his prized defenders, became the first witnesses to the truth of Moses.

=71=

Pharaoh’s reaction was one of furious arrogance. His first statement, “How dare you believe in him before I give you permission,” reveals his totalitarian mindset. He considered himself the absolute sovereign whose authorization was required even for the innermost act of faith. This exposed the emptiness of his claim to divinity: true God does not need to grant permission for belief, nor can He be defied by a creature’s faith. Then Pharaoh, unable to refute the miracle, reverted to conspiracy theory: “He must be your master who taught you magic.” He accused Moses of being the secret grandmaster of the magicians, implying the whole event was a collusion to overthrow him. This was a desperate attempt to discredit the miracle in the eyes of the crowd. The threat that followed was brutal: cutting off hands and feet on opposite sides (right hand with left foot) and crucifixion on palm trunks. This was an ancient method of torture and public display, designed to strike terror and suppress any dissent. Pharaoh’s question, “You will really see whose punishment is more severe and more lasting,” was a direct challenge to the magicians’ newfound faith, contrasting his temporal punishment with the warning of divine torment they had just accepted. The irony is profound: the punishment that is truly more severe and lasting is the Hellfire that awaits Pharaoh, not the palm trunks he threatened with. The Prophet Muhammad’s opponents similarly tortured early believers like Bilal and the family of Yasir, yet those believers remained steadfast. Pharaoh’s threat shows that when tyranny is exposed, it resorts to violence, because it has no rational argument left. The verse captures the escalating conflict between the kingdom of fear and the kingdom of faith.

=72=

The magicians, now believers, responded with astonishing courage. Their oath “By the One Who created us” shows their immediate internalization of tawḥīd; they swore by the Creator, not by Pharaoh. They declared they would never prefer Pharaoh over the clear proofs that had come to them. The “clear proofs” were the miracle of the staff and the undeniable truth it demonstrated. Their choice was absolute: the truth was worth any torture. Then they challenged Pharaoh with a remarkable statement of spiritual liberation: “So do whatever you want!” This was not despair but a declaration of independence. They recognized that Pharaoh’s power was limited to the fleeting life of this world.

The Arabic “hādhihi al-ḥayāti ad-dunyā” emphasizes the lowliness and temporariness of worldly existence compared to the eternal Hereafter. This is a profound shift in perspective; once the heart is attached to the eternal, the threats of a mortal tyrant lose their terror. The magicians, who hours earlier were seeking Pharaoh’s reward, now dismissed his entire kingdom as insignificant. This transformation is a testament to the power of true faith, which reorders all priorities. The Prophet Muhammad taught, “Be in this world as if you were a stranger or a traveler.” The magicians embodied this instantly. Their words also served as a public dismantling of Pharaoh’s claimed divinity; by restricting his authority to this world, they denied his power over the soul and the afterlife. The verse inspires believers that the worst a tyrant can do is end a worldly life, but the believer’s true life is with Allah. This fearless stance is a high point of the Quranic narrative of courage.

=73=

The magicians then articulated the content of their faith and its motivation. They stated, “Indeed, we have believed in our Lord” — emphasizing their personal commitment. The purpose of their belief was “so He may forgive our sins.” They acknowledged their past of sorcery and opposition to the truth as sins that needed divine pardon. Specifically, they mentioned “that magic you have forced us to practice,” indicating that Pharaoh had coerced them into using sorcery against Moses, though they had initially participated willingly.

The word “forced” (akrahtanā) might reflect the pressure they felt under his rule, or it could mean he compelled them to attempt to defeat Moses. In either case, they sought forgiveness for their involvement. This confession shows that sincere repentance can wash away even a lifetime of falsehood. Then they concluded with a declaration that answered Pharaoh’s earlier challenge: “And Allah is far superior in reward and more lasting in punishment.” Pharaoh had asked whose punishment was more severe and lasting; the magicians now affirmed that Allah’s reward for believers is superior, and His punishment for disbelievers is truly eternal and inescapable. This was a direct theological rebuttal to Pharaoh’s threat. They publicly declared that they feared the punishment of Allah more than the crucifixion, and they desired the reward of Allah more than Pharaoh’s gold. The Prophet Muhammad’s companions similarly weighed the temporary persecutions against the eternal bliss of Paradise. The magicians’ statement is a masterpiece of newborn faith: they acknowledged their sins, sought forgiveness, recognized Allah’s supreme attributes, and defied the tyrant with a calm and reasoned conviction. It shows that true imān brings immediate clarity and fearlessness.

=74=

After reporting the magicians’ bold declaration, the Quran inserts a universal divine decree that frames the entire conflict. The verse speaks of the ultimate outcome for those who come to their Lord as “an evildoer” (mujriman).

The word mujrim refers to one who commits crimes against their own soul through disbelief and rebellion against Allah, like Pharaoh. Such a person will have Hell as their certain destination. The description of Hell is chilling: “where they can neither live nor die.” This means the punishment is a perpetual state of agony, not a death that brings relief nor a life that holds any comfort. The condemned soul will wish for annihilation but will not be granted it.

This is a profound warning that the suffering of the Hereafter is continuous and inescapable, unlike worldly torture which ends with death. The verse serves as a direct commentary on Pharaoh’s threat; his punishment of crucifixion ends in death, but the punishment of Allah for unrepentant evildoers has no such escape. The magicians, by contrast, had chosen to come to their Lord as believers, thus avoiding this horrific fate.

The verse also explains the inner state of the evildoer: they come to their Lord carrying the weight of their crimes, with no intercessor and no repentance. It underscores the urgency of repentance before death. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, this was a core message: the Quraysh were warned that their forefathers who died in idolatry faced a similar doom. The verse seamlessly connects the historical narrative with the eternal reality, reminding listeners that the day of judgment is not a myth but the ultimate context in which all human choices are measured.

=75=

In beautiful contrast, the verse now describes the reward of the righteous. The condition is two-fold: coming to Allah as a believer (mu’minan) and having done righteous deeds (‘amila ṣāliḥan). Faith and deeds are intertwined; both are necessary for salvation. The reward is described as “the highest ranks” (fa-ulā’ika lahumu ad-darajātu al-‘ulā).

The word “darajāt” means steps, levels, or stations of elevation. Paradise is not a flat, uniform experience; it has degrees of honour, nearness to Allah, and bliss, corresponding to the levels of faith and good deeds one achieved in this life. The magicians, by their instant but profound faith and their courageous stand, would be among those attaining lofty ranks. The “highest ranks” are for the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous.

The phrase promises an exalted status far beyond what Pharaoh could offer. In Pharaoh’s court, ranks were determined by proximity to the tyrant and skill in magic; in the divine system, ranks are determined by imān and righteous action. This redefinition of honour was revolutionary for a society built on class and oppression. The magicians understood this and willingly traded the highest worldly rank for the lowest place among the believers, yet they were promised the highest spiritual rank. The verse encapsulates the Quranic principle that true honour belongs to the God-conscious. The Prophet Muhammad often encouraged his followers with the promise of lofty stations in Paradise, a motivation that made the hardships of Mecca bearable. The verse stands as a summary of the two paths, immediately interpreting the magicians’ choice as a sublime success.

=76=

The highest ranks are now described as “the Gardens of Eternity” (jannātu ‘adn). The term ‘adn implies permanent residence, stability, and endless duration.

The image of gardens with rivers flowing beneath them is one of the Quran’s most vivid depictions of Paradise, symbolizing perpetual comfort, beauty, and abundance. The phrase “where they will stay forever” emphasizes the eternal nature of this bliss; no fear of eviction or death. Then the verse identifies the recipients as “those who purify themselves” (man tazakkā). This purification (tazkiyah) encompasses both spiritual cleansing from shirk and sins and the development of virtuous character. It includes repentance, like that of the magicians who purified themselves from their past sorcery, and the continuous effort to remain obedient to Allah. The magicians, in their moment of prostration and confession, had purified themselves in the most dramatic way, and they were rewarded with this eternal promise.

The verse teaches that true success is not the fleeting victory of a contest but the eternal reward of a purified soul. The Prophet Muhammad was sent to purify his followers, and the Quran itself is a means of purification. The contrast with the previous two verses is complete: the evildoer faces a Hell from which there is no release, while the purified believer enjoys an everlasting Garden. This passage, inserted at the climax of the confrontation, elevates the event from a historical episode to a timeless lesson. It assures all believers that any loss suffered in this world for the sake of faith will be compensated with unimaginable, everlasting good. The magicians’ “trade” with Pharaoh—life for Paradise—was the most profitable transaction possible.

=77=

The narrative shifts to the next stage of the story: the Exodus. After years of remaining in Egypt and witnessing the plagues, the final command came. Allah says, “We surely inspired Moses,” indicating that this was a direct divine instruction. The command was to “Leave with My servants at night. ” The timing at night was strategic, allowing the Israelites to depart under cover of darkness and gain a head start before Pharaoh’s forces realized.

The phrase “My servants” is a term of endearment and honour for the oppressed Children of Israel. Then came the miraculous instruction: “strike a dry passage for them across the sea.” Moses was to strike the sea with his staff, and Allah would create a dry path.

The word “ṭarīqan” means a path, and “yabasan” means dry, ensuring that they could walk without sinking or fear. Allah also provided double reassurance: “Have no fear of being overtaken” — Pharaoh’s army would not catch them, and “nor be concerned of drowning” — the sea would not swallow them. This addressed the two obvious fears: the enemy from behind and the water ahead. The command and reassurance mirror the earlier one when Moses was sent to Pharaoh, confirming that Allah’s protection and guidance remain constant. For the Prophet Muhammad, the Hijrah (migration) from Mecca to Medina was a similar moment: a night departure, the fear of pursuit, and the miraculous protection of Allah, including the spider’s web and the cave. The verse encapsulates the pattern of divine deliverance: when the believers have done their part, Allah opens a way where there seems to be none. The stage is set for one of the most spectacular miracles in human history.

=78=

The verse describes the pursuit and its devastating outcome. When Pharaoh learned that the Israelites had fled, he mobilized his army and chased them.

The phrase “fa-atba‘ahum fir‘awnu bi-junūdihi” indicates he followed them with his soldiers, determined to annihilate them. The timing was such that Moses and his people had crossed the dry passage, and Pharaoh’s army entered the sea bed behind them. Then the divine command came, and the waters returned to their normal state. The exclamation “but how overwhelming were the waters that submerged them” translates the Arabic “fa-ghashiyahum mina al-yammi mā ghashiyahum.” The wording is deliberately vague and majestic; it does not give a mundane description but evokes the sheer terror and totality of the drowning. The “yamm” is the deep sea, and what covered them was something only Allah fully knows — a deluge of water that closed over them, leaving no escape. This event was the final seal on Pharaoh’s disbelief and the salvation of the Children of Israel. It demonstrates that the same sea that was a path of salvation for the believers became a grave for the disbelievers. Water, a source of life, became an instrument of destruction by Allah’s command. The verse echoes the earlier assurance: Moses had no need to fear being overtaken or drowning. The destruction of Pharaoh and his army was total; not a single soldier survived. This event is a perpetual sign of Allah’s justice and power. The Prophet Muhammad would later recall this and the fate of other destroyed nations to warn the Quraysh. The verse is brief yet extremely powerful, leaving the reader to contemplate the suddenness with which Allah’s punishment can come when it is finally decreed.

=79=

This verse pronounces the final verdict on Pharaoh’s leadership. “Pharaoh led his people astray” (wa-aḍalla fir‘awnu qawmahu) — he misled them into disbelief, tyranny, and ultimately to their destruction in this world and the next. As the supreme ruler and the one who claimed divinity, he bore the primary responsibility for the spiritual and moral state of his nation. Instead of guiding them to the truth when Moses came with clear signs, he used propaganda, threats, and arrogance to keep them in falsehood.

The phrase “and did not guide them rightly” emphasizes that he failed in the most fundamental duty of a leader. He did not just passively let them stray; he actively led them astray.

This is a warning to all who hold positions of influence: they will be accountable not only for their own souls but for the misguidance they cause in others. The magicians, by contrast, were guided after recognizing the truth, but the masses who blindly followed Pharaoh perished with him.

The verse also serves to absolve the followers of complete innocence; they were led, but they also chose to follow. In the Quranic worldview, leadership and followership are moral choices. The Prophet Muhammad was commanded to be a guide, and he warned that every shepherd is responsible for his flock.

The verse concludes the Egyptian episode with a somber reflection: the mighty civilization that seemed invincible was brought low by the very leader they idolized. The contrast with Moses, who guided his people to safety and the worship of Allah, is stark and instructive.

=80=

The address shifts directly to the Children of Israel, the generation that witnessed the Exodus. Allah reminds them of three immense favours. First, “We saved you from your enemy,” referring to the drowning of Pharaoh and the liberation from slavery. This salvation was a physical, political, and spiritual deliverance. Second, “and made an appointment with you on the right side of Mount Ṭûr.” This refers to the appointment where Allah would speak to Moses and give the Torah, with seventy elders accompanying him. The “right side” of the mount, according to commentators, indicates the blessed, righteous direction or the specific location on the mountain. This appointment was a tremendous honour, a direct communion with the Divine that no other nation had received at that time. Third, “and sent down to you manna and quails.” In the barren desert of Sinai, Allah provided miraculous sustenance: manna, a sweet substance that would appear like dew, and quails, birds that would come in abundance for them to eat. This was provision without toil, a sign of His care.

The verse begins with “O Children of Israel” as a direct call to remember these favours and to live up to the covenant that would follow. It is both a reminder of gratitude and a rebuke for their later complaints and deviations. The favours are listed to show that Allah’s grace upon them was comprehensive—physical rescue, spiritual guidance, and material provision. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, recounting this story was a warning to the Jews of Medina who rejected his message despite the clear signs in their own history. The verse sets the stage for the subsequent events at Mount Ṭûr, where the Torah was given and the incident of the golden calf occurred.
