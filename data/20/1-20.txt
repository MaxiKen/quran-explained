=1=

The surah opens with the disconnected letters Ṭâ-Hâ. These are among the mysterious openings of certain Quranic chapters, whose precise meanings are known fully only to Allah. Many scholars, however, have offered insights. One strong view is that these letters draw attention to the miraculous nature of the Quran, composed of the very same letters of the Arabic language yet inimitable. They serve as a wake-up call for the listener, breaking the silence and demanding reflection. Some companions and early interpreters considered Ṭâ-Hâ to be one of the names of the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, or a form of address meaning "O man" or "O peace," derived from the word "tah" in certain dialects. In that understanding, it is a term of endearment and comfort. This interpretation ties beautifully to the very next verse which assures the Prophet that the Quran was not sent to cause him distress. After the initial period of revelation, there was a pause that made the Prophet anxious, longing for communion. When revelation resumed, the pagans claimed it was a source of hardship for him. Some reports mention that he used to stand in prayer all night until his blessed feet swelled, and his wife Aisha, may Allah be pleased with her, asked why he exhausted himself when his past and future sins were forgiven. He replied, "Should I not be a grateful servant? " Thus, the opening letters and the subsequent verses come as a soothing reassurance: this call is a mercy.

The name Ṭâ-Hâ signals a gentle elevation of the Prophet’s status and a reminder that the revelation is a gift, not a burden. It sets the tone for a surah that will recount how Allah strengthened Moses with words of comfort and guidance in the face of a tyrant, just as He now strengthens the final Messenger. So the surah begins by capturing attention, consoling the heart, and preparing for profound stories and timeless truths.

=2=

This verse directly addresses a misconception and comforts the Messenger, peace be upon him. The revelation did not descend to make him miserable, physically exhausted, or psychologically overwhelmed by the weight of his mission. Rather, the Quran is a source of honour, light, and nearness to Allah. The background includes the Prophet’s intense devotion; he would pray until his feet swelled, and he worried deeply when revelation paused. The Quraysh would mock him, saying his Lord had abandoned him or that the Quran was causing him sorrow. Allah negates all of that.

The word "distress" translates the Arabic "shaqā’," which implies wretchedness, toil, and unhappiness. The Quran was never meant to be a punishment or a cause of complaint. It is a book of guidance that uplifts the soul.

This verse teaches that religious obligations, including prayer and recitation, are not meant to be a crushing weight. The Prophet himself said, "Religion is ease." If someone feels that practicing Islam is causing them unbearable hardship, they may be missing the spirit of the teachings. Allah intended the Quran to be a healing for what is in the hearts, a criterion between right and wrong, and a mercy for the believers.

Thus, the opening declaration dismantles any false belief that divine guidance is a burden. It reminds every believer that the path of faith, though it requires effort, is ultimately a path of peace and liberation from the anxieties of ignorance. The Prophet is assured that his struggle will be eased, and his followers inherit the same assurance: the remembrance of Allah brings tranquility to hearts.

=3=

The Quran’s true purpose is framed as a "reminder" or "tadhkirah." This is profound because it implies that the knowledge of God and moral truths are already engraved in the human soul, but they become clouded by worldly distractions. The reminder awakens that dormant inner awareness. The recipients are specifically "those in awe of Allah," meaning those who possess a conscious fear and reverence of their Lord, known as taqwā. Such people are sensitive to the truth, humble, and eager to receive guidance. They benefit from the Quran because their hearts are not sealed by arrogance or heedlessness. The verse thus clarifies that while the Quran is a universal message, its transformative power is unlocked by a particular attitude: a trembling awareness of standing before Allah. The Prophet is being told that his effort is not for those who stubbornly reject; he is not responsible for forcing them. The reminder will find its home in the God-conscious. This reassures him that his distress is unnecessary when the disbelievers turn away, because his primary audience are the ones whose hearts are alive with fear of their Lord. It also establishes a principle: the Quran is a mercy, but only those with receptive hearts will taste its sweetness. The relationship between the Prophet and the Quran is one of receiving and transmitting this reminder, and in doing so, he himself finds spiritual nourishment. So after negating distress, the verse gives a positive aim, connecting the consolation of the Prophet with the description of the true believers.

=4=

Here Allah identifies the source of the Quran. It is not the Prophet’s own thoughts, nor a poetic inspiration, but a "tanzīl" — a revelation sent down from on high. The One who sends it is described first by His act of creation, specifically creating the earth and the high heavens. This pairing is significant: the earth is our immediate home, low and spread out, while the heavens are elevated, vast, and magnificent. By mentioning both, Allah encompasses all of existence.

The verse reminds the listener that the same Being who brought this immense universe into existence has also taken it upon Himself to provide moral and spiritual guidance. The act of creation is proof of His power, wisdom, and right to command. Why should the One who made the cosmos not also communicate with His creation?

The mention of creation is a recurring Quranic argument for the authenticity of revelation. The One who created the eye and the ear, the stars and the mountains, surely knows what benefits His servants.

Therefore, the Quran’s origin immediately inspires awe and trust. The Prophet is thus anchored in certainty: this message is not his own burden, it comes from the Creator who sustains him and all that exists. The verse forms a seamless link between the themes of comfort and the overwhelming majesty of Allah. By focusing on creation, it prepares the heart for what follows — a fuller description of the Most Compassionate’s attributes and dominion.

=5=

The description continues by introducing the divine name "Ar-Raḥmān," the Most Compassionate, whose mercy encompasses all things. This immediately softens the grandeur of creation with an attribute of tenderness and care. The Quran’s revelation is thus an expression of immense mercy. Then, the verse mentions a foundational creedal truth: Allah is established on the Throne. This refers to His istiwā’, a manner of rising over the Throne that befits His majesty without resemblance to creation. The Throne is the greatest of created things, symbolising Allah’s supreme authority, kingship, and control over the entire universe. Affirming this doctrine is a crucial part of faith. The early generations accepted it without delving into the "how" and without negating the attribute. By combining "Most Compassionate" with the Throne, the Quran paints a picture of a Lord whose mercy and majesty are perfectly joined. He is not a distant, indifferent creator; He is actively governing and caring. For the Prophet, this is a profound reassurance: the One who rules the heavens and the earth with absolute power is also the Most Compassionate who has sent the Quran out of kindness, not to cause distress. This dual attribute should fill the heart with hope and reverence.

The verse also counters the false beliefs of those who thought Allah had abandoned the Prophet; how could the Lord of the Throne, the Most Compassionate, forget His chosen servant? Thus, the description directly supports the earlier negation of distress and reinforces the source of the revelation.

=6=

This verse expands on Allah’s absolute dominion. Everything that exists — in the heavens, on earth, what lies between them like the air and clouds, and even what is beneath the soil — belongs to Him entirely. No creature possesses anything of its own. This comprehensive ownership means that Allah is free from any need, and all creation is dependent on Him. For the Prophet, this is a reminder that his Lord controls every force that might oppose him. Pharaoh’s tyranny and the pagans’ plots are all within a kingdom that belongs to Allah. He can dispose of them as He wills.

The verse also instills humility: the Prophet and believers are themselves part of "whatever is on the earth," so they are His property, under His care and protection. This eliminates any sense of loneliness or helplessness. When Moses later goes to confront Pharaoh, he will rely on the same truth: the earth belongs to Allah, and He can command it to swallow the enemy or part the sea.

The mention of "whatever is in between" highlights that no realm is outside His knowledge and authority, including spiritual and unseen realms. The phrase "whatever is underground" can refer to buried treasures, the dead, seeds, and even the hidden depths. Nothing escapes His grasp. This verse perfectly sets the stage for the story of Moses, demonstrating that the Lord who owns all will protect and guide His messenger.

=7=

Allah’s perfect knowledge is then emphasised. He is aware of every uttered word, whether it is spoken aloud or kept inside the soul. But His knowledge goes deeper: He knows the "secret" — the concealed thoughts and feelings that a person may not express — and "what is even more hidden." This deeper level, referred to in Arabic as "akhfā," is the inner whisperings, the inclinations of the heart that have not yet formed into clear ideas, the subconscious layers that even a person might not fully discern. Such absolute knowledge is exclusive to Allah. For the Prophet, this is both comforting and awe-inspiring. He does not need to articulate every fear or pain; his Lord already knows them. When he faces rejection, ridicule, and danger, Allah sees and hears it all. In the coming story, Moses expresses his fears, and Allah reassures him, "I am with you, hearing and seeing." That same watchful care is for the Prophet Muhammad.

The verse also carries a warning for those who conceal plots; nothing can be hidden from the Divine. It serves as a silent rebuke to the pagans who whispered against the message.

Moreover, in personal devotion, this attribute encourages sincerity, for even the most hidden movements of the heart are laid bare before the Most Compassionate. This knowledge provides the foundation for the intimate relationship between the Lord and His servant, forming the heart of worship and trust.

=8=

The declaration of divine oneness, tawḥīd, is the core message of the Quran. The name "Allah" is the supreme name, encompassing all attributes of perfection.

The phrase "there is no god worthy of worship except Him" negates any form of divinity from anything else and affirms it solely for the One True God. This is the ultimate purpose of revelation and the central call of all prophets. For the Prophet Muhammad, this verse is both a creed and a sword against idolatry. The Meccans believed in Allah as a Creator but associated partners with Him, and this verse shatters that compromise. Then the verse adds a beautiful dimension: "He has the Most Beautiful Names" (al-asmā’ al-ḥusnā). These names describe His attributes — the Merciful, the Powerful, the Wise, the Loving, the Provider, and many more. They are all perfect, free from any defect. The Prophet is taught that he is connected to a Lord whose qualities are of absolute beauty and goodness. In moments of distress, he can call upon these names, like "the Most Compassionate" for comfort, "the All-Hearing" when he feels unheard, "the All-Powerful" when he faces enemies.

The verse ends the opening section on a note of majestic simplicity. It also prepares for the narrative of Moses, who will be commanded to proclaim, "There is no god except Me," and who will invoke these beautiful names in his mission. This systematic presentation of tawḥīd, ownership, knowledge, and beautiful names acts as a spiritual anchor for the Prophet before the lengthy story of past trials and triumphs begins.

=9=

The surah now transitions from universal doctrines to a historical narrative, posing a rhetorical question to the Prophet. This question is not to seek information, for Allah knows best, but to capture attention, create anticipation, and console the Messenger. The story of Moses is the most frequently recounted in the Quran, and here it is presented in rich detail. By asking "Has the story reached you?", the Quran suggests that this is an important, well-known account, but what is coming will add fresh layers and a specific focus — the reassurance and patient perseverance of a prophet against a tyrant. The Prophet Muhammad was going through similar rejection in Mecca, with the Quraysh leaders acting like Pharaoh.

The question implies, "Listen to this story, for it will strengthen your heart and remind you that the path of the prophets is one of trials followed by divine victory." It is as if Allah is gently turning His Prophet’s attention away from the current difficulties to see how His grace operated in the past.

The question format also engages the listener’s mind, making them curious. The Prophet was unlettered and had no access to these historical details except through revelation, so the story itself is a sign of his prophethood.

The mention of Moses right after the beautiful names of Allah links the coming narrative to the attributes just described: the Most Compassionate will show mercy, the Lord of the Throne will control events, the All-Knowing will guide Moses every step of the way. So the question serves as an elegant pivot, infusing the story with theological depth from the start.

=10=

The story begins with Moses traveling with his family in the cold, dark desert after leaving Madyan. He notices a fire burning on the side of Mount Sinai. His words to his family show his human care and leadership. He asks them to stay put, expressing hope that he might bring back a burning torch to provide them warmth and light, or at the very least find someone at the fire who could give them directions.

This is a beautiful depiction of his human predicament — lost, in need, yet hopeful. The Arabic phrase "aw ajidu ‘alā an-nāri hudā" can mean guidance in terms of directions or information. Moses had no idea he was about to encounter the divine. This teaches that Allah’s plans unfold in the most ordinary circumstances. The fire that Moses saw as a source of physical comfort became the place of his prophethood. Allah often brings relief and immense blessings from where one least expects. For the Prophet Muhammad, there is a parallel: his retreat to the cave of Hira for contemplation became the venue for the first revelation.

The story reminds believers not to underestimate small signs; what appears to be a simple fire might be the gateway to a life-transforming meeting with the Lord. The careful detail about Moses speaking to his family also underscores his responsible character, which made him a suitable vessel for the heavy trust of prophethood. The narrative is now set to unfold with sudden, awe-inspiring grandeur.

=11=

As Moses draws near to the fire, the unexpected happens. Instead of finding an ordinary flame, he is addressed directly by a divine call. The verse uses the conjunction "but" to indicate a dramatic shift — he approached seeking a torch, but was met with something far greater. The call comes audibly: "O Moses! " This immediate address by name signals a personal, intimate connection. Allah knew him, chose him, and called him directly. This moment marks the beginning of Moses’s prophethood. The wording suggests that Moses did not see the Speaker; he heard the voice. According to many reports, the fire appeared on a green tree, burning without consuming it, a phenomenon that indicated the presence of the divine light. The call "O Moses" is full of mercy and honour. In the Prophet Muhammad’s life, the first word of revelation was "Read!" — also a direct address. Both moments share the pattern of a sudden divine intrusion into ordinary life, transforming everything.

The verse teaches that when Allah intends to honour a servant, He calls them by name, pulling them out of their ordinary pursuits. For Moses, the fire that was meant to dispel physical darkness became the source of spiritual illumination. The narrative now pauses to let the weight of this call sink in, as the reader waits to hear what will be said next.

=12=

The voice declares its identity with emphasis: "It is truly I. I am your Lord!

" The repetition "innī anā rabbuka" conveys absolute certainty and establishes the personal lordship. The immediate command to remove sandals is a mark of respect and a preparation for entering a holy presence. Taking off sandals was a sign of humility in the presence of kings, and here it signifies the sanctity of the ground. The valley is named Ṭuwa, a blessed place where Allah would speak directly to Moses. Some scholars say Ṭuwa means the valley that has been sanctified twice, or it is the valley traversed at night. By telling Moses to remove his footwear, Allah was teaching him adab — proper etiquette before the Divine. This outward gesture reflects an inner state of reverence and complete submission. For the Prophet Muhammad, this moment contains a subtle echo: during his Night Journey, he removed his sandals before entering the divine presence as well, according to some narrations, though others say he kept them on as a sign of his unique station. Nonetheless, the principle is clear: when approaching Allah, one must shed the attachments of the worldly journey. The instruction also comforted Moses, giving him a tangible action while his mind must have been overwhelmed. The revelation of "I am your Lord" is the greatest possible honour and also the granting of a direct relationship. The scene sets the stage for the heavy mission about to be entrusted to him.

=13=

After establishing His Lordship and the sacredness of the place, Allah announces His selection of Moses. The word "ikhtartuka" — I have chosen you — is a declaration of divine election.

This choice is purely out of Allah’s grace and wisdom, not based on any prior merit that would obligate such a choice, though Moses’s qualities are well known. The election is for prophethood, to bear the message to Pharaoh and the Children of Israel. The command "fa-stami‘ limā yūḥā" — so listen to what is revealed — requires Moses to be completely attentive, physically and spiritually.

This is the moment of receiving the revelation, and it demands total absorption. The instruction to listen before speaking is a crucial lesson for all believers, especially those who carry the call to others. One must first receive fully from Allah before conveying to people. The verse is brief but filled with the weight of authority and love. For the Prophet Muhammad, this mirrors his own experience: he was chosen and commanded to listen to the revelation carefully, as later explicitly mentioned in the surah itself (verse 114). The voice from the fire now gives Moses a series of core commands that constitute the foundation of his mission. The text moves from the establishment of the special relationship to the content of the message, showing that the divine call always comes with a clear set of principles and responsibilities.

=14=

The revelation begins with the same emphatic self-identification as before, now using the supreme name "Allah." This repetition anchors Moses’s entire mission in tawḥīd.

The phrase "There is no god except Me" is the absolute statement of monotheism, the dividing line between truth and falsehood. Immediately, the consequence is commanded: "So worship Me alone. " All acts of devotion, fear, hope, and obedience must be directed to Allah alone, without any partner. Then the specific command to establish prayer is given, tying it directly to "My remembrance." The ultimate purpose of prayer is the remembrance of Allah; it is the most powerful means of keeping the divine reality alive in one’s heart. For Moses, who would face the tyrannical Pharaoh and his claimed divinity, anchoring his heart in prayer was essential. The prayer would be his strength, his refuge, and his constant connection.

The verse also reveals a profound link: the establishment of prayer is not merely a ritual but a direct consequence of recognizing Allah’s sole divinity. It is the practical manifestation of tawḥīd.

The phrase "li-dhikrī" can also mean "for My remembrance" or "so that I remember you," implying a mutual relationship. This first command to Moses contains the essence of the religion that all prophets conveyed. For the Prophet Muhammad, the same command was given, and he found coolness of his eyes in prayer. The verse thus lays down the spiritual constitution for the prophet who is being sent to confront the greatest tyrant.

=15=

After the command for worship, the next revealed truth is the certainty of the Day of Judgment. The Hour refers to the end of the world and the resurrection. It is "sure to come" without any doubt.

The phrase "akādu ukhfīhā" has two famous interpretations: "I almost hide it" meaning it is so hidden that even its nearness is concealed, or "I am about to reveal it" which would mean it is imminent. The majority of commentators, including Ibn Abbas, take the meaning as Allah keeping it hidden from creation. The wisdom behind this concealment is then explained: so that every soul may be rewarded according to what it strives for. If people knew the exact time of judgment, they might delay repentance and good deeds, or their free will would be compromised. The hidden hour maintains a constant state of alertness and sincere effort. For Moses, this doctrine was necessary because he was being sent to a people who had been oppressed and might lose hope in divine justice. The Hour is the ultimate day of recompense, when the oppressed will be vindicated and the oppressors punished. It adds an urgent moral dimension to the mission: the Pharaoh’s worldly power is temporary, and the true lasting reality is the Hereafter. This revelation also connects beautifully with the earlier themes of Allah’s ownership and knowledge. The same Lord who knows the secret and what is more hidden has hidden the Hour in His perfect wisdom.

The verse reminds every believer to live with a sense of accountability, focusing on the quality of their efforts.

=16=

Having affirmed the Hour’s certainty, Allah warns Moses not to be diverted from belief and preparation for it. The warning is against two types of people: those who deny the Hour outright and those who merely follow their lowly desires, living as if there is no judgment.

The word "fa-tardā" means "you will perish" or "you will be ruined." The warning is addressed to Moses directly, and by extension to all who carry the message. Even a prophet is not immune from needing this reminder; it emphasizes the gravity of being distracted from the Hereafter. The disbelievers and the hedonists will try to make the Hereafter seem irrelevant, but the believer must resist that current.

This is incredibly relevant for the Prophet Muhammad in Mecca, where the pagan leaders mocked the concept of resurrection. Allah is telling him through Moses’s story that the denial of the Hereafter is a key obstacle, and the messenger must guard his own heart against its influence. The verse connects desire with disbelief: following one’s whims without divine guidance leads inevitably to rejecting the truth. Moses needed this inner fortification before confronting Pharaoh, whose kingdom was built on the denial of any higher authority. The warning "or you will be doomed" is stark, showing that even the chosen ones are only safe through steadfastness. This instruction immediately precedes the giving of the physical signs, indicating that inner conviction in the unseen must be anchored before outward miracles are granted.

=17=

After the foundational creed and warning, the discourse takes a gentle, dialogical turn. Allah asks Moses a question to which He already knows the answer: "What is that in your right hand?

" The question serves multiple purposes. It calms Moses by engaging him in a normal conversation after the awe-inspiring revelations. It also draws his attention to the ordinary object he held — his staff — preparing him for the miracle about to occur. By asking, Allah allows Moses to describe his staff and his reliance on it, thereby making the subsequent transformation more impactful.

The phrase "in your right hand" specifies the hand, perhaps because the right hand is associated with strength, honour, and possession. This question is a teaching method; it makes the learner reflect on what he already knows before showing him something beyond his knowledge. The entire sequence mirrors how Allah often builds faith through personal engagement. For the Prophet, who himself would be asked "What is that in your right hand?" or similar queries in the context of his own miracles, this is a template of how Allah instructs His messengers.

The question also subtly shows that Allah cares about the details of a prophet’s life, even the staff he uses. It is a beautiful, intimate moment where the Lord of the Throne speaks to Moses as a beloved interlocutor, calming his awe with a gentle query, all while setting the stage for the miraculous.

=18=

Moses answers with a detailed description, much more than a simple "staff." He says, "It is my staff," asserting his ownership and familiarity. Then he lists its functions: he leans on it for support during long walks or while resting; he uses it to beat down leaves and branches to feed his sheep; and he has other various needs for it. This elaboration reveals Moses’s human nature and his shepherd background. Prophets were often shepherds before their mission, learning patience, care, and reliance on simple tools. By enumerating the staff’s practical benefits, Moses shows his attachment to it and how integral it was to his daily life. The staff was a symbol of his worldly occupation. The response is longer than the question required, perhaps stemming from a mix of nervousness and eagerness to speak in that holy presence. Allah’s question was open-ended, and Moses took the opportunity to share. The "other uses" might include defense, reaching things, or leading his flock. This human detail is preserved in the Quran to make the scene relatable. Then, the divine plan will take this utterly ordinary object and turn it into a mighty sign. The lesson is profound: when Allah enters a person’s life, He often uses what they already have, transforming their mundane skills and possessions into tools for His mission. The staff, which Moses leaned on, would soon be leaned on by the whole nation as it parted the sea. The moment is now set for the first miracle.

=19=

The command is simple and abrupt: "Throw it down." There is no explanation, no preparation. Moses is asked to cast away the object of his reliance, the staff that supported him physically.

This is a test of obedience and trust. Often, Allah asks believers to let go of what they depend on in order to show them that true reliance is on Him alone. When Moses threw the staff, he had no idea what would happen, but he obeyed instantly. The command to throw represents the first step of letting go of worldly attachments in the journey of prophethood. The staff was not evil, but it symbolized Moses’s past identity as a shepherd. The new identity required total submission.

The phrase "O Moses" is used again, maintaining the personal connection even in the command. This moment is replicated in some ways in the life of the Prophet Muhammad, when he was asked to leave his home and city, abandoning his familiar surroundings. The courage to obey without hesitation is a hallmark of prophets.

The verse also illustrates that miracles occur at the intersection of divine command and human compliance. Had Moses hesitated or clung to his staff, the sign would not have manifested. The brevity of the verse adds to the dramatic tension. The reader is left in suspense, just as Moses must have been in those split seconds, before the next verse reveals the astonishing result.

=20=

Moses obeyed and threw the staff. The Arabic uses "fa-idhā hiya ḥayyatun tas‘ā" — a particle of suddenness indicating the instantaneous transformation. The staff became a living serpent, rapidly moving as if with a purpose.

The word "ḥayyah" refers to a snake, and some reports describe it as a large, swift serpent that even opened its mouth. The suddenness and vividness emphasize the divine power behind the transformation. A dead piece of wood turned into a living, moving creature in an instant, without any physical means. This miracle was one of the two primary signs given to Moses for Pharaoh. It demonstrated that the Creator can bring life out of inanimate matter, a reminder of the resurrection that Moses had just been told to believe in. For the Prophet Muhammad, this served as a confirmation that the same power would produce the miracles he was given, though his were primarily intellectual and spiritual, culminating in the Quran. The staff-snake also symbolized the swallowing of falsehood, as it would later swallow the magicians’ illusions. The movement "tas‘ā" conveys energy and purpose; it was not a static image but a dynamic, slightly fearsome sight. Moses’s initial reaction, as noted elsewhere, was fear, causing him to turn and flee until Allah called him back. This miracle was the first external proof of his prophethood, and it marked the beginning of his public confrontation with Pharaoh. The narrative has now moved from the intimate call in the sacred valley to the tangible demonstration of power, preparing Moses to return to Egypt with confidence.
