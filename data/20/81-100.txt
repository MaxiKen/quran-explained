=81=

After reminding the Children of Israel of the miraculous manna and quails, Allah now gives them a direct command about those provisions. The phrase “Eat from the good things We have provided for you” is an invitation to enjoy the lawful, pure sustenance that descends as a mercy. The “good things” (ṭayyibāt) are not only physically nourishing but also divinely blessed.

However, this permission comes with a stern warning: “but do not transgress in them. ” Transgression here can mean several things: greed, hoarding beyond immediate need, ingratitude, complaining about the monotony of the food as they later did, or using the provision in disobedience to Allah. Some commentators mention that they were commanded to take only what they needed for each day and not store it for the next, yet some violated this, leading to spoilage. The deeper transgression is spiritual: receiving Allah’s gifts but then turning away from His worship and commands. The consequence is severe: “or My wrath will befall you. ” Divine wrath (ghaḍab) signifies a state of intense displeasure that results in punishment.

The verse then emphatically declares, “And whoever My wrath befalls is certainly doomed.” The Arabic “fa-qad hawā” implies a complete fall into destruction, both in this world and the next. The doomed person loses spiritual elevation and plunges into ruin.

This warning was proven true when some among the Israelites transgressed and faced earthly punishments, but the ultimate doom is the Hereafter. The verse teaches that blessings come with responsibility; ingratitude and disobedience transform a gift into a curse. For the Prophet Muhammad’s community, it is a reminder to enjoy the lawful provisions of Allah with thankfulness and within the boundaries set by Him, avoiding excess and waste. The wrath of Allah is not a light matter; it is the worst loss a soul can suffer. The warning is balanced by the next verse, which opens the door of mercy and repentance, demonstrating that Allah’s justice is always paired with His forgiveness for those who turn back.

=82=

Immediately after the severe warning, Allah declares His immense forgiveness, emphasizing it with “truly” (innī). This verse lays out a clear path for redemption.

The first condition is “whoever repents” (tāba) — genuine turning back from sin, with remorse and a resolve not to return. The second is “believes” (āmana), meaning faith in Allah and the pillars of belief. Repentance without faith is incomplete.

The third is “does good” (‘amila ṣāliḥan), because true faith manifests in righteous deeds. Finally, “then persists on true guidance” (thumma ihtadā) emphasizes steadfastness. It is not enough to have a moment of repentance; one must continue upon the path of guidance until death.

The word “ihtadā” implies actively seeking and adhering to the straight way. This fourfold condition encapsulates the comprehensive nature of salvation. The verse is a direct response to the possibility that some of the Israelites might transgress with the manna and quails, or later with the golden calf; it assures that no matter how grave the sin, the door of forgiveness is open for those who meet these conditions. The story of the calf worshippers would soon follow, and indeed, those among them who repented were forgiven. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, this verse served as a powerful message to the Meccans who had committed idolatry and oppression: if they repented, believed, did good, and remained steadfast, they would find Allah Most Forgiving. It also comforted the believers who might fear past sins. The structure of the verse is profound: it begins with Allah’s attribute of forgiveness, then lists human actions. This shows that divine mercy is the initiator, but it is accessed through sincere human effort.

The phrase “Most Forgiving” (la-ghaffār) is in an intensive form, indicating that Allah forgives repeatedly and abundantly. It is a timeless guarantee of hope for every sinner.

=83=

The scene shifts to Mount Ṭûr, where Moses had been summoned for an appointment of forty nights. In his intense longing to be near his Lord, Moses left his people at the foot of the mountain and ascended ahead of the seventy elders who were also to accompany him. Allah’s question, “Why have you come with such haste ahead of your people, O Moses?” is not a reprimand but a gentle, knowing inquiry.

The question draws attention to Moses’s extraordinary eagerness and love for his Lord. The phrase “such haste” (a‘jalaka) implies that his desire overpowered his usual measured approach. It is as if Allah wanted Moses to articulate the passion that drove him, setting the stage for the answer that would reveal the beauty of his yearning. This also subtly prepares Moses for the news that in his absence, his people had fallen into the worship of the golden calf.

The question itself is a form of intimate conversation, demonstrating that Allah engages with His prophets as close confidants. Some scholars note that the question might contain a mild lesson: a leader should not be so absorbed in personal spiritual ecstasy that he leaves his followers unprepared.

Yet, the primary tone remains one of affectionate curiosity. For the Prophet Muhammad, this reminded him of his own deep longing for revelation and prayer, while also teaching the balance between personal devotion and communal responsibility. The verse captures that Allah is fully aware of His servant’s innermost motivations, and He sometimes asks questions not to seek information but to allow the servant to express himself, to hear his own voice, and to build a deeper relationship.

=84=

Moses’s answer is full of respectful love and concern. He begins by stating, “They are close on my tracks,” meaning his people and the elders are just behind him, approaching the mountain. This reassures Allah, as if to say, “I did not abandon them; they are right behind me.” It shows Moses’s awareness of his leadership responsibility; even in his haste, he ensured they were following. Then he reveals the true motivation: “And I have hastened to You, my Lord, so You will be pleased.

” The Arabic “ḥastu ilayka” conveys a sense of hurried, eager movement towards the beloved. The goal was not merely to fulfill a command but to attain Allah’s pleasure (riḍā).

This statement reveals the heart of a true lover of Allah; the highest aspiration is not personal reward but the smile of the Lord. Moses could have waited for the elders, but his yearning pulled him forward, and he trusted that his hastening would be accepted as a mark of his devotion.

The phrase “my Lord” (rabbi) adds a tone of intimacy. The reply sets an example for every believer: the best reason to race towards good deeds and worship is the hope of pleasing Allah. The Prophet Muhammad would pray, “I ask You for Your pleasure and for Paradise. ” The verse also indirectly addresses the upcoming test; Moses’s hastening for Allah’s pleasure is contrasted with his people’s hastening towards idol worship in his absence. The sincerity of Moses’s answer is so beautiful that Allah does not criticize him but instead reveals the test that has occurred. This teaches that Allah appreciates the pure intentions of His servants, even when the outward consequences seem difficult. Moses’s desire to please his Lord becomes a model for the believers’ spiritual ambition.

=85=

After Moses expressed his loving haste, Allah reveals what happened behind him. The response is not a direct answer to the question but a disclosure of a painful event. “We have indeed tested your people in your absence” — the word “fatannā” means We tried or tested them. The test was the golden calf, a trial to see whether they would remain steadfast in monotheism without their prophet present. The absence of Moses was part of the divine plan to expose the weakness of some hearts. Tests often come during the absence of spiritual leadership, revealing who truly worships the unseen God and who is attached only to a visible guide. Then Allah identifies the instigator: “the Sâmiri has led them astray.” The Sâmiri (often understood as a man from the tribe of Sāmir, or a notable figure among them) was the one who took the people’s gold and fashioned the calf. His name indicates his origin, and he became the primary culprit.

The use of “aḍallahum” means he caused them to go astray, though they themselves were responsible for following him. Allah informed Moses of this before Moses descended, so that he would understand the gravity of the situation and be prepared.

The verse teaches that tests are from Allah, but the actual misguidance is carried out by human agents who choose evil. It also shows that no matter how powerful a prophet is, his followers can falter if they rely on him rather than on Allah. For the Prophet Muhammad, the warning was clear: after his death, the community would face trials, and only those truly connected to Allah would remain steadfast.

The mention of the Sâmiri also introduces the upcoming interrogation and punishment, showing that the agents of misguidance will be held accountable.

=86=

Upon learning of the calf worship, Moses descended from the mountain in a state of extreme emotion. The words “furious and sorrowful” (ghaḍbāna asifā) combine intense anger for the violation of Allah’s rights with deep grief over his people’s spiritual ruin. His reaction shows that true prophetic jealousy is for the honour of Allah, not personal ego. He immediately confronted them with a series of rhetorical questions that cut to the heart. First, “Had your Lord not made you a good promise?” — referring to the promise of the Torah, guidance, and reward. Breaking faith with Allah after such a promise is an act of profound ingratitude. Second, “Has my absence been too long for you?” — a forty-day absence is not a justification for idolatry, and the question exposes their impatience and weak faith. Third, “Or have you wished for wrath from your Lord to befall you, so you broke your promise to me?” — this is a sharp rebuke, suggesting that their actions were so severe it was as if they were actively inviting divine punishment. The promise they broke was the covenant to worship Allah alone, which they had affirmed before Moses left. Moses’s triple question moves from reminding them of Allah’s goodness, to exposing the flimsiness of their excuse, to condemning their recklessness. The anger was not uncontrolled rage but a righteous indignation that sought to awaken their conscience. He then threw down the Tablets of the Torah, according to other verses, not out of disrespect but from the overwhelming shock. The lesson for the Prophet Muhammad was to remain patient with the stubbornness of his own community, for even Moses, who spoke to Allah directly, faced such defiance.

The verse shows that spiritual leadership involves immense emotional pain when followers stray, but the leader must continue to call them back to the truth.

=87=

Faced with Moses’s fury, the people attempted a weak and confused defense. They began by denying full responsibility: “We did not break your promise of our own free will.” They claimed they were not entirely willing participants in the idolatry. Then they explained the circumstances: “but we were made to carry the burden of the people’s golden jewellery. ” This refers to the gold that the Israelites had acquired from the Egyptians before the Exodus. Some reports say they had borrowed it, and when they left, it became a heavy burden of trust and temptation.

The phrase “ḥummilnā awzāran” can also imply they were laden with the weight of sin or the heavy load of that jewelry. They then said, “then we threw it into the fire, and so did the Sâmiri. ” Their narrative was that the Sâmiri told them to cast the gold into a fire to melt it, and he would do the same. The implication is that the Sâmiri orchestrated the whole affair, and they were merely following his lead. This argument is an attempt to shift blame. While the Sâmiri was the instigator, the people willingly gave their gold and participated in the worship of the resulting calf. Their excuse, therefore, holds no real weight. It resembles the excuses of those who follow false leaders and later claim they were merely obeying orders. The Quran exposes the psychology of weak-willed followers: they recognize the gravity of their sin but try to minimize their own agency. For the Prophet Muhammad, this served as a warning about the excuses that would be made by those who blindly follow misguiding leaders.

The verse also highlights the danger of wealth and ornaments when they become objects of temptation; the very gold that was a sign of Allah’s deliverance became the material for an idol when misused.

=88=

The verse describes the fabrication of the idol and the blasphemous claim made by the calf worshippers. The Sâmiri “moulded for them an idol of a calf,” meaning he crafted a lifeless statue from the melted gold.

Yet, this statue produced “a lowing sound,” which made it seem alive. According to many commentators, the sound was caused by a clever mechanical design—perhaps hollow passages that caught the wind—or by some trickery, but it was sufficient to captivate the simple-minded. The calf was not a living animal; it was an inanimate object manipulated to produce an effect. The people, entranced, declared, “This is your god and the god of Moses.” They attributed divinity to a crafted object, and shockingly, they claimed that this calf was the same deity that Moses worshipped. Then they added, “but Moses forgot where it was,” meaning they alleged that Moses had gone to the mountain to seek a god, but had forgotten that the true god was right here among them in the form of the calf.

This is a profound depth of misguidance: not only did they commit shirk, but they also slandered their prophet by suggesting he was forgetful or misguided. The incident shows how quickly the human mind can rationalize idolatry, even constructing a theology around a hand-made idol. It mirrors the psychology of many polytheistic traditions that craft statues and then claim they represent the divine. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a parallel to the Quraysh who worshipped idols and claimed they were intermediaries to Allah.

The verse also illustrates the Sâmiri’s cunning: he used a sound effect to give the idol a semblance of life, exploiting the people’s awe. It is a timeless warning against any charismatic figure who uses spectacle to divert people from the unseen God.

=89=

The Quran immediately demolishes the idol’s pretension with simple, rational arguments. The rhetorical question “Did they not see” appeals to their basic powers of observation and reason. The calf, despite its lowing sound, “did not respond to them.” When they prayed to it, asked for guidance, or called upon it, it remained silent. It could not speak a word of comfort or command; it was utterly unresponsive.

Furthermore, “nor could it protect or benefit them.” It could not ward off any harm—if someone attacked them, the calf could do nothing. It could not bring any benefit—no rain, no provision, no healing. In summary, it was completely impotent. These two criteria—response and power to harm or benefit—are the minimum requirements for any entity that is worshipped. Even the pagan Arabs admitted that their idols could not create or provide; they only claimed they were intercessors.

The verse teaches a fundamental principle of tawḥīd: that which is worshipped must hear and have power, otherwise worship is irrational self-deception. The argument is so clear that even a child can understand it. The fact that the Israelites, who had witnessed the plagues of Egypt and the parting of the sea, could fall for such a powerless object shows that misguidance is not a problem of intelligence but of heedlessness and desire. For the Prophet Muhammad, this verse was a powerful tool against the idolatry of Mecca. The logic is timeless: any false god—whether a statue, a human leader, money, or an ideology—will ultimately prove unresponsive and powerless in the face of the true needs of the soul. The verse thus trains the believers to always apply this test of reason to any object of devotion.

=90=

The Quran now reveals that Aaron was not silent; he had actively opposed the idolatry. His warning began with the affectionate address “O my people,” showing his love and concern. He correctly identified the calf as a test: “You are only being tested by this.” The calf was a trial from Allah to distinguish the sincere from the weak. Tests come in various forms, and a seemingly miraculous object can be a means of trial rather than guidance. Aaron then redirected their focus to the true God: “for indeed your one true Lord is the Most Compassionate.” He used the name “Ar-Raḥmān,” the Most Compassionate, to remind them of Allah’s mercy that should inspire love and trust, not fear of abandonment. He emphasized that this Most Compassionate Lord is the only one deserving worship.

Finally, he commanded, “So follow me and obey my orders.” As the appointed deputy of Moses, Aaron had the authority to lead them in Moses’s absence, and he urged them to obey him in rejecting the calf. His warning was clear, theologically sound, and delivered with gentleness.

However, the people rejected him. This shows that even the most eloquent and correct advice can be ignored by those whose hearts are bent on desire. Aaron found himself in a nearly impossible situation: if he fought them violently, it would cause division and bloodshed; if he remained passive, they would continue in sin. He chose to maintain unity while persistently warning them. His stance was later vindicated by Allah. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a lesson in prophetic patience: sometimes a leader must endure seeing his people err without being able to forcefully stop them, trusting that Allah’s wisdom will unfold.

The verse also highlights the proper response to any trial: recognizing it as a test and holding fast to the Lord of mercy.

=91=

The people’s response to Aaron’s warning was stubborn and conditional. They declared they would not stop worshipping the calf “until Moses returns to us.” This answer reveals several layers of their corruption. First, they implicitly admitted that the calf was not inherently worthy of worship; they tied their devotion to Moses’s absence, suggesting that they saw the calf as a temporary focus or a substitute during the leader’s absence.

Yet, this was a catastrophic error: true worship cannot be directed to a substitute, no matter the circumstances. Second, they placed a condition on their obedience to Aaron, the legitimate deputy. By saying “until Moses returns,” they refused to acknowledge Aaron’s authority and made Moses an idol of sorts—their faith was attached to his person rather than to Allah. They treated Moses’s presence as the criterion for truth, which is a form of shirk in reliance. Third, their statement implied that if Moses approved the calf upon his return, they would be justified; if not, they would then reconsider.

This is the mentality of those who follow falsehood and delay repentance, waiting for a convenient moment or a clearer sign, not realizing that death may come first. The verse exposes the danger of condition-based faith: one must respond to truth immediately, regardless of who delivers it or who is absent. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a warning that some of his followers might waver after his departure, clinging to personalities rather than the Quran and Sunnah. The response of the Israelites also shows the power of groupthink; they collectively reinforced each other’s defiance. The lesson for believers is to obey the truth when it comes, without delay or arbitrary conditions.

=92=

After dealing with his people, Moses turned to his brother Aaron with a sharp rebuke. The phrase “Moses scolded his brother” translates “akhadha bi-ra’s akhīhi,” which literally means he seized his brother by the head, a gesture of intense emotion, likely grabbing his beard and hair in frustration. This physical act was not intended to harm but expressed his grief and anger over the situation, as was customary among some peoples in moments of extreme distress. The words “O Aaron! What prevented you, when you saw them going astray” begin a chain of questioning. Moses assumed that Aaron should have done more—perhaps fought them, abandoned them, or joined Moses on the mountain to report the matter.

The phrase “when you saw them going astray” emphasizes that Aaron was an eyewitness to the idolatry and, in Moses’s view, should have taken stronger measures. This rebuke reflects Moses’s passionate zeal for monotheism and his high expectations of his brother and partner in prophethood. It also highlights the immense pressure on prophets: they feel personally responsible for every soul that strays. The scolding is not a sign of a broken brotherhood but of the intensity of the prophetic mission, where even the smallest perceived shortfall is addressed directly.

However, Moses’s understanding was incomplete because he did not yet know the full extent of Aaron’s efforts or the near-violent situation Aaron had faced. The verse sets up Aaron’s heartfelt defense, which will clarify the wisdom behind his actions. For the Prophet Muhammad, who also had close companions and sometimes needed to correct them, this episode demonstrates that even the noblest of people can be questioned, and that fraternal correction must be met with honest explanation.

=93=

The scolding continues with the completion of Moses’s question. He asked why Aaron did not follow after him to the mountain, implying that Aaron should have abandoned the people to join Moses and inform him immediately of the crisis. Moses saw this as a failure to uphold the command he had given: “Take my place among my people, act righteously, and do not follow the way of the corrupters.” From Moses’s perspective, by staying among the calf worshippers without stopping them by force, Aaron might have appeared to acquiesce.

The phrase “How could you disobey my orders?” is particularly sharp because Moses had left Aaron as his deputy. The question carries the weight of surprise and disappointment; Moses never expected such a situation.

However, Moses’s orders were to guide the people, not necessarily to fight a war against them. The misunderstanding lies in the interpretation of “obedience. ” Aaron’s decision to remain and try gentle persuasion rather than splitting the community or causing bloodshed was a different, but valid, application of the command. This moment highlights the complexity of prophetic leadership: sometimes what seems like inaction is actually a calculated strategy to prevent greater harm. Moses, in his righteous fury, initially judged the outcome rather than the intent.

The verse teaches that even prophets can have differing perspectives on handling crises, and that such misunderstandings are resolved through communication, as the next verses show. For the Prophet Muhammad, who faced similar strategic decisions—whether to confront the Quraysh militarily or bear with patience—this dialogue illustrated the value of listening to the explanation of those who are on the ground. The questioning was not a condemnation but an opportunity for clarity.

=94=

Aaron’s response is a masterpiece of gentle, effective communication under pressure. He begins with a deeply affectionate address: “O son of my mother!” By invoking their mother rather than the father, Aaron appeals to the bond of the same womb, stirring emotions of tenderness and mercy. This was a strategic and emotional plea to calm Moses’s anger. He then requests, “Do not seize me by my beard or the hair of my head.” This shows that Moses, in his fury, had physically grabbed him. Aaron does not rebuke him harshly; he simply asks for release, acknowledging the pain. Then he explains his reasoning: “I really feared that you would say, ‘You have caused division among the Children of Israel, and did not observe my word.’” Aaron’s fear was not of Moses’s physical strength but of a far more painful criticism: that he had split the community. Had he fought the calf worshippers, the result could have been civil war, with brother killing brother. He judged that preserving the unity of the nation, even in error, until Moses returned, was the lesser evil, because Moses could resolve the issue with his authority intact. He also feared Moses would accuse him of not properly “observing” his word, meaning failing in the trust of leadership. So Aaron’s inaction was actually a deep act of responsibility and loyalty to Moses’s ultimate command: to maintain the people’s solidarity. The explanation reveals profound prophetic wisdom: sometimes patience in the face of widespread sin is necessary to prevent greater fragmentation. The Prophet Muhammad would later demonstrate similar restraint at Ḥudaybiyyah, making concessions to preserve peace. The verse is a lesson in resolving conflicts: listen to the other side’s reasoning with an open heart, because what appears to be disobedience may be a higher form of obedience.

=95=

After settling the matter with Aaron, Moses turned his full attention to the root cause of the crisis: the Sâmiri. His question, “What did you think you were doing, O Sâmiri?” is a direct, personal confrontation. The wording “mā khaṭbuka” implies, “What is your matter? What is your explanation for this grievous crime? ” It is a demand for accountability. The Sâmiri is singled out because he was the instigator who molded the calf and led the people into idolatry. Moses’s approach distinguishes between the deceived masses and the deceiver. The masses are to be rebuked and guided, but the instigator of misguidance must answer for his sedition.

This is a principle of justice: those who initiate evil bear a heavier responsibility than those who are led into it. The question gives the Sâmiri a chance to speak, reflecting the divine practice of allowing even the worst criminals to present their case before punishment. It also exposes the Sâmiri’s reasoning, which will reveal the depth of his corruption. The Sâmiri’s answer, recorded in the next verse, shows that his act was not a mistake but a deliberate choice driven by a twisted interpretation of a spiritual experience. The questioning demonstrates that leaders must investigate the causes of a community’s deviation and address the root, not just the symptoms. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a model for dealing with the hypocrites and instigators in Medina, who were questioned and exposed.

The verse also teaches that every soul is answerable for its actions, and no one who leads others astray will escape divine or prophetic scrutiny.

=96=

The Sâmiri’s confession is strange and revealing. He claims, “I saw what they did not see.” According to many commentators, this refers to his witnessing the angel Gabriel on a horse at the time of the crossing of the sea, perhaps when the Israelites were passing through. He perceived something of the spiritual realm that others missed. He then “took a handful of dust from the hoof-prints of the horse of the messenger-angel Gabriel.” He noticed that wherever Gabriel’s horse stepped, life or special effects seemed to spring forth. He gathered some of that dust, keeping it as a powerful token. When the people wanted an idol, he threw this dust onto the molded calf, and by Allah’s permission as a test, the calf emitted a lowing sound.

However, the Sâmiri misused this sign. His statement, “This is what my lower-self tempted me into,” acknowledges that his own nafs (ego) led him to exploit this phenomenon to mislead the people. Instead of using his insight for truth, he used it to craft an idol and elevate his own status. The confession reveals the psychology of a misguided spiritual opportunist: he had access to a genuine glimpse of the unseen, but his corrupt soul twisted it into a tool of shirk. The sin was not in taking the dust but in using it to create a false god and then allowing it to be worshipped. The Sâmiri’s defense, though honest, is not remorseful; it is an explanation of his inner temptation. The verse warns that spiritual experiences and knowledge, when not grounded in pure intention and submission to Allah, can become a means of leading oneself and others astray. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a caution that even among his followers, some might possess knowledge or charisma but use it for self-aggrandizement, like the false prophets who would later appear.

=97=

Moses pronounced a severe, multi-layered punishment upon the Sâmiri. First, he expelled him from the community with the words “Go away then!” (fa-dhhab). This was social excommunication, a banishment from the camp. Second, a lifelong, bizarre condition was imposed: “for the rest of your life you will surely be crying, ‘Do not touch me!’” The Sâmiri was afflicted with a disease or a psychological state that made him terrified of physical contact, and anyone who touched him would suffer a fever, so he would cry out to keep people away. This was a fitting punishment: he who had touched the sacred dust to create a false god would now be untouchable, a living warning. Third, “Then you will certainly have a fate that you cannot escape.” This refers to an appointed punishment in the Hereafter, a divine decree that he cannot flee.

Finally, to demonstrate the utter powerlessness of the idol, Moses said, “Now look at your god to which you have been devoted: we will burn it up, then scatter it in the sea completely. ” The calf was burned, probably to ashes, and those ashes were scattered on the water to show that it could neither defend itself nor benefit its worshippers. The destruction was total, leaving no relic for future idolization. This taught the people that the object of their worship was nothing but metal that could be melted and thrown away. For the Prophet Muhammad, the destruction of the idols around the Ka‘bah after the conquest of Mecca echoed this prophetic act. The punishment of the Sâmiri also illustrates that those who invent lies against Allah and lead people astray will face a combination of worldly disgrace and eternal torment. The lesson is that false gods will always be shattered by the truth.

=98=

After destroying the calf and dealing with the Sâmiri, Moses gathered his people to re-establish the foundational creed. His declaration, “Your only god is Allah, there is no god worthy of worship except Him,” is the essence of tawḥīd. It negates all false deities and affirms the oneness of the true God. This was the very message with which Moses had been sent, and it was now being re-emphasized after the terrible deviation.

The phrase “He encompasses everything in His knowledge” is added here to strengthen their faith. It reminds them that Allah’s knowledge is absolute and all-encompassing; He knows what the calf was, what the Sâmiri did, and what was in their hearts. Nothing escapes His awareness. This attribute is a powerful deterrent against hidden sins and a comfort that the truth will ultimately prevail. By linking tawḥīd with Allah’s vast knowledge, Moses taught that the true God is not just a creator but the All-Knowing who holds the universe and all its secrets. The calf, by contrast, knew nothing.

The verse also signals a new beginning for the Children of Israel; they were called back to the core belief that distinguishes them as a monotheistic community. The lesson was not only for that generation but for all: whenever a community falls into error, the remedy is to return to the pure declaration of Allah’s oneness and the reminder of His comprehensive attributes. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was the ever-relevant call: after every trial or sin, the door to renewal is through the kalimah and the deep understanding of Allah’s knowledge. The verse encapsulates the resolution of the golden calf crisis with the clarity of divine truth, preparing the people for the heavy obligations of the Torah.

=99=

The surah now shifts from narrative to direct address to the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. “This is how We relate to you” points to the detailed, vivid story of Moses that has just been told. Such stories are not mere history; they are “some of the stories of the past” (min anbā’i mā qad sabaq), selected specifically for their relevance and lessons. The purpose is to strengthen the Prophet’s heart, warn the disbelievers, and instruct the believers. Then the verse announces: “And We have certainly granted you a Reminder from Us.” The Reminder (dhikr) is the Quran itself. It is a gift from Allah, full of wisdom, guidance, and remembrance.

The word “dhikr” implies that the Quran reminds humanity of what they already intuitively know—their Lord, their purpose, and their accountability. This verse connects the story of Moses, who received the Torah, to the Prophet Muhammad, who received the Quran. Both were reminders from Allah.

The phrase “from Us” emphasizes the divine origin; it is not a human fabrication. The verse also reassures the Prophet that just as Moses was supported with signs and protected, so is he. The detailed recounting of the past is a proof of prophethood, for an unlettered man could not have known these events except through revelation. The verse serves as a bridge: the story of Moses ends, and the surah will now address the Meccan deniers directly, warning them and calling them to the Reminder. The lesson for all believers is that the Quranic stories are not entertainment; they are mirrors in which we see our own struggles and the timeless patterns of faith and falsehood.

The phrase “This is how We relate” implies that the narrative style is itself a divine favor, making the message impactful and easy to absorb.

=100=

After declaring the Quran as a Reminder, the verse immediately warns of the consequences of rejecting it. “Whoever turns away from it” (man a‘raḍa ‘anhu) includes those who hear the message but deliberately reject it, ignore it, or fail to live by it. The act of turning away is a conscious choice of aversion, a willful distancing from divine guidance. The consequence is stated with emphasis: “will surely bear the burden of sin on the Day of Judgment. ” The Arabic “wizran” means a heavy load or burden. This burden is the accumulation of sins, particularly the sin of disbelief and the sins that follow from lacking guidance. On the Day of Judgment, every person will carry the weight of their own misdeeds, and those who rejected the Quran will carry an especially heavy burden because they turned away from the source of salvation. The burden is not only the original rejection but also the responsibility for leading others astray or for failing to investigate the truth. The imagery of a physical burden pressing down on the back is used elsewhere in the Quran to convey the tangible reality of sin in the afterlife. The verse connects back to the earlier story: the Israelites who turned away from Moses’s guidance bore the burden of the calf worship. Similarly, those who turn away from the Quran will not escape accountability. The Prophet is consoled that he is not responsible for those who reject; they will bear their own burden. This verse serves as a stark opening to a series of descriptions about the Day of Judgment, which follow in the next verses, painting the terrifying scene for those who are heedless. It is a call to take the Reminder seriously, for turning away is not a light matter but a decision that will crush a person under an unimaginable load on the Day when no one can carry the weight of another.
