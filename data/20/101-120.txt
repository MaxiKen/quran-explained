=101=

This verse completes the warning of the previous verse by describing the duration and horror of the burden borne by those who turn away from the Quran. The phrase “suffering its consequences forever” translates the Arabic “khālidīna fīhi,” which indicates an unending state of carrying that weight. The burden of sin, particularly the sin of rejecting divine guidance after it has been clearly presented, is not a temporary discomfort; it becomes a permanent condition in the Hereafter.

The word “khālidīn” is often used for eternal abode, emphasizing that the punishment is unceasing. Then the verse exclaims, “What an evil burden they will carry on Judgment Day!

” The Arabic “wa sā’a lahum yawma al-qiyāmati ḥimlan” is a powerful expression of condemnation. The word “ḥiml” means a physical load carried on the back, which vividly conveys the crushing weight of sin. On the Day of Judgment, when every soul is already overwhelmed with terror, those who turned away will have this additional load of their own rejection and its consequences—the misguidance of others, the missed opportunities for repentance, and the loss of eternal bliss. The exclamation form underscores the pity and horror of their state.

This description links back to the story of the Sâmiri, who bore his own burden and led others astray; those who reject the Quran similarly bear the weight of their own souls and the souls they might have influenced negatively. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, this was a sobering reminder that his mission was to deliver the message, not to force acceptance, and the fate of the rejecters is a tragedy he could warn against but not prevent.

The verse also serves to intensify the fear of the Hereafter in the listener’s heart, encouraging immediate reflection. The burden is not merely a metaphor; it is a spiritual reality that will be experienced. It stands in sharp contrast with the earlier promise that those who believe and do good will have the highest ranks in Gardens of Eternity.

Thus, the Quran continuously juxtaposes the two ultimate ends, urging a choice before the Day when the burden becomes fixed forever.

=102=

The surah now paints a vivid scene from the Day of Judgment. The phrase “the Day the Trumpet will be blown” refers to the ṣūr, the horn blown by the angel Isrāfīl. The blowing of the Trumpet is a momentous event that signals the end of the world and the commencement of the resurrection. The Quran mentions it in several places as a terrifying blast that will stun all creation. Here, the focus is on the gathering of the “wicked” (al-mujrimīn) — those who have committed crimes against their own souls through disbelief and persistent sin. The description “blue-faced” translates “zurqan,” which literally means blue or livid.

This is a colour of extreme fear, suffocation, and thirst. On that Day, the disbelievers will be resurrected in a state of profound terror, their faces pale and bluish from the horror they are witnessing and the deprivation of water. Some scholars say the blueness is the color of extreme thirst, and indeed the wicked will be driven to the place of gathering in a state of severe dehydration, their eyes turning blue from the intensity. This physical manifestation reflects their inner spiritual state: just as their hearts were dark and devoid of the light of faith, their faces lose the color of life and appear ghastly. The verse serves as a powerful warning, especially to the Meccan pagans who denied the resurrection.

The image is designed to shake the listener from heedlessness. The Prophet Muhammad often described the horrors of that Day to instill taqwā. The contrast with the believers is stark: elsewhere the Quran describes the faces of the believers on that Day as radiant, laughing, and rejoicing. The gathering of the wicked “blue-faced” is a scene of humiliation and dread. It also connects with the earlier story: just as Pharaoh and his army were drowned and faced a humiliating end, the deniers of the Quran will be resurrected in a state of disgrace. The verse is a call to reflect on the reality that life’s temporary pleasures will culminate in that terrifying assembly unless one repents and believes.

=103=

In the midst of that terrifying gathering, the wicked will converse in hushed tones, comparing their earthly lives. The word “yatakhāfatūna” means they will whisper, mutter, or speak in low voices to one another, indicating their fear and the overwhelming solemnity of the occasion. Their conversation reveals a shocking realization: the worldly life they were so attached to, and for which they rejected the truth, now seems impossibly short. They estimate that they “stayed no more than ten days. ” This is their perception upon resurrection—the entire span of their earthly existence, which might have been decades, now feels like a brief period of ten days. The number “ten” here is not meant literally but conveys a sense of shortness. Some say they might be referring to the period between death and resurrection, or the entire worldly life. In either case, the duration seems trivial compared to the eternity they now face. This psychological experience is described in other verses as well, such as “On the Day they see it, it will be as if they had not remained except for an evening or its morning.” The discrepancy between their previous valuation of life and the stark reality produces deep regret. Their whispering also indicates a lack of certainty; they are unsure and debate among themselves, but the truth is undeniable. The Prophet Muhammad would emphasize the fleeting nature of this world, saying, “What is the world compared to the Hereafter but like what one of you gets when he dips his finger into the sea; let him see with what it returns. ” The verse exposes the deception of time: a long life seems short when faced with eternal consequences. It calls the listener to prioritize the everlasting over the temporary, a central theme in the surah that contrasts the pleasures of this world with the enduring reward of Allah.

=104=

Allah, in His absolute knowledge, confirms that He is fully aware of all their whisperings. The phrase “We know best what they will say” asserts divine omniscience over the unseen future, including the exact words and the variations in their estimates. Even among the wicked, there will be degrees of perception. The “most reasonable of them” (amthaluhum ṭarīqatan) refers to those with the soundest judgment or the most balanced mind among the disbelievers. They will conclude that the worldly life or the interval of death lasted “no more than a day.” This further shrinking of the perceived time—from ten days to a single day—shows that the more rationally aware they are, the more they recognize the insignificance of the worldly duration. A day is the shortest unit of significant time; they realize that all their years of rebellion were worth less than a single day. This also underscores that even the best estimates of the disbelievers are still miles away from the truth; they can only grasp a fraction of the reality that the world was truly a fleeting illusion. The Quran often uses the dialogue of the people on the Day of Judgment to teach lessons. Here, their own admission becomes a proof against them.

The verse also comforts the Prophet: Allah knows exactly what the deniers say and think, and their disbelief does not harm Him. It is a subtle warning that no matter how much one rationalizes or debates the truth, on that Day, all false justifications will evaporate, and even the most reasonable among them will concede the brevity of their test. This should motivate believers to invest their short time in what pleases Allah, knowing that soon they will look back and see their life as a single day, and they will wish it had been filled with righteousness.

=105=

The surah turns to a specific question that the Meccans might pose, or it simply addresses a common object of human awe: the mountains. The polytheists often marveled at the mountains’ stability and might consider them permanent fixtures. The verse instructs the Prophet on how to respond if they inquire about the fate of these massive formations on the Day of Judgment.

The answer is clear: “My Lord will wipe them out completely. ” The Arabic “yansifuhā rabbī nasfan” uses the verb “nasafa,” which means to scatter, pulverize, and reduce to dust. The mountains, which seem indestructible, will be utterly demolished by the command of Allah.

This is a recurring Quranic image: the mountains will be like carded wool, crushed to powder, and the earth will be leveled. The purpose is to illustrate that nothing, no matter how solid and immense, can withstand the power of Allah when He wills the end of the current order. For the Prophet, this answer serves multiple functions. It refutes any lingering belief that the mountains have any divine permanence; they are mere creatures. It also reinforces the theme of the Hour’s suddenness and totality.

The phrase “My Lord” emphasizes that the Prophet speaks with knowledge received from his Lord, not from himself. This answer also connects with the earlier description of the earth in verse 53: it is Allah Who laid out the earth, and He can easily unmake its elevations. The mountains, often objects of idolatry or symbols of pride, will be reduced to nothingness. This teaches humility: the mightiest physical structures are ephemeral before Allah, so how can human power or wealth boast? The verse prepares for the next description of the earth’s state after the mountains are gone, setting the scene for the assembly of humanity on a perfectly flat plain.

=106=

After the mountains are obliterated, the earth will be left in a state described as “level and bare.” The Arabic “ṣafṣafan” means a smooth, flat plain, while “lā tarā fīhā ‘iwajan wa lā amtan” is elaborated in the next verse, but here “level” indicates a perfectly even surface, free from any rises or dips.

The word “bare” is not explicitly in this verse but is implied by the context of a leveled plain with no vegetation or structures. The earth will become a vast, empty arena for the gathering of all humanity. There will be no hills, no valleys, no buildings, no trees—nothing to hide behind, no familiar landmarks. This physical transformation symbolizes the complete stripping away of the worldly life. All the distinctions and barriers that humans used for shelter, status, or separation will be removed. It is a scene of absolute exposure before Allah. The leveling of the earth also reflects divine justice: on that Day, all people will stand on an equal footing, with no one able to claim superiority due to ancestry, wealth, or geography. The grand mountains that people admired are reduced to dust, leaving a clean slate for the ultimate judgment. The Prophet Muhammad described the Day of Resurrection as the gathering of all creatures on a white, flat ground like a polished disc. The levelness of the earth ensures that the call of the summoner can be heard by all and that no one can escape the sight of Allah.

The image is both awe-inspiring and terrifying; it reminds believers that the structures of this world, no matter how permanent they seem, are destined for annihilation, and only the face of the Lord will remain. Thus, one’s attachment should be to the Eternal, not to the transient landscapes of this life.

=107=

The description of the earth’s transformation is completed with this phrase. “Neither depressions nor elevations” translates “lā tarā fīhā ‘iwajan wa lā amtan.

” The word “‘iwaj” refers to any crookedness, curve, or unevenness, while “amt” means a small hill or bump. Some commentators explain that “amt” includes even the slightest rise or dip, like an anthill. The earth will be so perfectly flat that the eye will detect no variation whatsoever. This absolute flatness has profound implications. In the worldly life, valleys and mountains served as places of refuge, concealment, and strategic advantage. On that Day, there will be no place to hide, no terrain to aid escape. The disbelievers will be completely exposed.

The verse also highlights the flawless execution of Allah’s command; He Who created the intricate topography of the earth will reduce it to a uniform plane with a single will. This flat plain is the stage for the universal gathering, where the entire human race from Adam to the last person will stand. The absence of any elevation or depression also symbolizes the end of all worldly distinctions: no king’s palace, no fortress, no cave. All are equally bare. The scene evokes the plain of Arafat on a grand scale, but stripped of all features. The Meccans, who prided themselves on their mountain-ringed valley, are warned that those very mountains will vanish. The verse encourages detachment from the world’s physical anchors and a focus on the inner state of the heart. In a perfectly flat world, nothing external can distract from the judgment; each soul will be entirely concerned with its own record. The imagery reinforces the surah’s central theme: the fleeting nature of the world and the enduring reality of the Hereafter.

=108=

The scene of the gathering unfolds with absolute divine authority. “All will follow the caller” refers to the summoner, generally understood as the angel Isrāfīl or another appointed angel, who will call humanity to the place of assembly.

The phrase “none will dare to deviate” indicates that no one will have the power or audacity to turn aside, resist, or go in a different direction. The Arabic “lā ‘iwaja lahu” means there will be no crookedness, no ability to deflect from following. The compulsion is not of violence but of overwhelming reality; the truth is so manifest that resistance is impossible. Then the verse describes the atmosphere: “All voices will be hushed before the Most Compassionate.” The mighty attribute “Ar-Raḥmān” appears here, the Most Compassionate, whose mercy is highlighted even in the context of judgment. The hushed voices (khasha‘at al-aṣwāt) denote a state of deep reverence, fear, and submission. The chatter, arguments, and loud protests of the worldly life will cease; only a profound silence will reign. “Only whispers will be heard” translates “illā hamsan,” meaning a faint, subdued sound, perhaps the shuffling of feet or the whispering of hearts. This immense silence illustrates the majesty and awe of the divine presence. It reflects the inner dread and the recognition that the only speech permitted is that which Allah allows. The contrast with the noisy, arrogant gatherings of the disbelievers in this world is sharp. The Prophet Muhammad would frequently emphasize the seriousness of that Day, and this verse draws a vivid picture that stills the heart.

The mention of “the Most Compassionate” in a scene of judgment also carries a promise: even in His wrath, Allah’s mercy is the overarching reality, and those who deserve it will find it. The overall effect is a scene of total surrender, where every soul is focused on its outcome.

=109=

The topic of intercession (shafā‘ah) is addressed. The Meccan pagans believed that their idols and ancestors would intercede for them. The verse shatters that hope. “No intercession will be of any benefit” means that no one can intercede on behalf of another purely on their own. All intercession belongs to Allah, and it operates only under two strict conditions: permission from the Most Compassionate, and the intercessor’s words being agreeable to Him.

The phrase “by those granted permission” indicates that even the greatest prophets and angels must first receive divine leave before speaking. The Prophet Muhammad’s grand intercession on the Day of Judgment is a prime example: he will prostrate and praise Allah with words taught to him, and then Allah will grant him permission to intercede.

The phrase “whose words are agreeable to Him” means that the intercessor’s speech must be pleasing to Allah, which implies that they can only intercede for those with whom Allah is already pleased, or in accordance with His will. This completely nullifies any pagan notion of unregulated intercession.

The verse also connects with the earlier story: Pharaoh claimed no such intercession could save him, and indeed, his magicians’ newfound faith did not permit them to intercede for him. The condition “by the Most Compassionate” emphasizes that even this permission flows from Allah’s mercy. The intercession will only benefit those who had faith, however weak. The idolaters, who worshipped other beings precisely to intercede for them, will find their hopes dashed. The verse offers a balanced doctrine: while intercession exists and is a source of hope for sinful believers, it is entirely under divine control and does not compromise tawḥīd. It also serves as a warning: do not rely on imagined mediators; rather, seek the pleasure of the One Who grants permission. The silence of the previous verse is broken only by the voices that Allah permits, and those voices will speak only what is true and just.

=110=

This verse declares the encompassing knowledge of Allah and the total limitation of human knowledge regarding Him. “He fully knows what is ahead of them and what is behind them” means Allah knows their future and their past, all that is before them of the Hereafter and all that they left behind in the world. Some commentators say “what is ahead of them” refers to the affairs of the Hereafter, and “what is behind them” refers to the worldly life they have passed. Others say it is about what is before the angels and creation. In any case, it signifies absolute, all-encompassing knowledge. No detail, no hidden intention, no future event escapes His awareness.

The verse then states, “but they cannot encompass Him in their knowledge.” This is a fundamental principle of Islamic theology: Allah is beyond complete human comprehension. The finite mind cannot fully grasp the infinite.

The word “yuḥīṭūna” implies encircling, encompassing, or fully comprehending. No matter how much knowledge humans attain, they can never fully know Allah’s essence, His attributes, or the full extent of His plans. This serves as a powerful rebuke to those who demand to understand the “how” of divine actions or who reject the unseen because it does not fit their logic. It also puts human knowledge in its humble place. The verse connects with the previous discussion: only those who are granted permission can intercede, and Allah’s knowledge is so vast that He knows who truly deserves mercy.

The phrase “they cannot encompass Him” also offers comfort to the Prophet: the disbelievers’ arguments and mockery are rooted in ignorance; they cannot grasp the reality of the One they deny. The verse encourages believers to have intellectual humility and to trust the One Whose knowledge is unlimited. The balance between Allah’s complete knowledge of us and our limited knowledge of Him creates a proper relationship of awe and reliance.

=111=

On that Day, every human face will show abasement and submission before Allah. The phrase “all faces will be humbled” translates “‘anatil-wujūhu,” meaning they will be lowered, submissive, and full of humility. There will be no trace of the arrogance that characterized the disbelievers in the world. This humbling is before “the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining” (al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm). These two names are among Allah’s greatest attributes: the Ever-Living who never dies, and the Self-Subsisting who sustains all existence without any need. The contrast is striking: mortal faces, once proud, are now humbled before the only One Who truly possesses life and power.

The verse then singles out a group: “And those burdened with wrongdoing will be in loss.” The “wrongdoing” (ẓulm) here primarily refers to shirk and disbelief, as well as major sins.

The word “khaba” means to fail, to be in manifest loss. This loss is absolute; they lose themselves, their families, their chances of Paradise, and any hope of escape. The punishment is not just physical but an existential failure. The humbling of faces is universal—even believers will experience awe—but for the wrongdoers, it is accompanied by loss, while for the believers, it will be followed by honor and safety, as the next verse will clarify. The verse serves as a severe warning, especially to the Meccan chiefs who strutted with pride. It teaches that the only true dignity is in submitting to the Ever-Living before death, so that on the Day of Judgment, one’s face is not humbled in punishment but radiant with joy.

The mention of al-Qayyūm also reassures that while humans fail, Allah’s sustaining power never wavers, and He will establish justice.

=112=

In beautiful contrast, the verse presents the state of the righteous. The condition is twofold: “does good” (righteous deeds) and “is a believer” (mu’min). Faith and action are inseparable. Such a person will have “no fear of being wronged or denied their reward. ” The Arabic “ẓulman wa lā haḍman” covers two types of injustice: “ẓulm” is any form of oppression or unfair treatment, and “haḍm” means deprivation or reduction of due reward. So the believer is guaranteed that on that Day, they will not be treated unjustly in any way, nor will their good deeds be diminished in the slightest. This assurance is a source of immense comfort. Every human effort, every hidden tear, every sincere act will be accounted for and rewarded fully by the Most Just.

The verse also implies that no intercession or favoritism will wrong the believer; their rights are secure. The use of “mu’minan” (believer) as a condition emphasizes that good deeds only benefit when rooted in imān.

The earlier verse spoke of the burdened wrongdoers being in loss; here, the believers are in complete safety. This binary outcome is the core message of the surah and the Quran. The Prophet Muhammad would often recite such verses to encourage his followers who faced persecution, reminding them that their suffering would not be in vain. The reassurance extends to modern believers: any injustice suffered in this world will be redressed, and no act of goodness will be lost.

The phrase “will have no fear” also addresses the emotional state; while the disbelievers are in terror, the believer will be calm and secure under the shade of the Throne. This is the culmination of the earlier promise in verse 75–76 regarding the highest ranks, offering a glimpse of the peace that awaits the purified. The verse thus ends the Judgment scene on a note of hope and justice.

=113=

After the vivid descriptions of the Hereafter, the surah returns to the Quran itself and its purpose. The phrase “And so We have sent it down as an Arabic Quran” emphasizes that the revelation is in the clear Arabic language, making it understandable to its first audience, the Quraysh.

This is both a mercy and a proof: the message is not in a foreign tongue they could claim not to understand. It is also an honour for the Arabic language, chosen to carry the final revelation.

The verse then mentions that Allah has “varied the warnings in it” (ṣarrafnā fīhi min al-wa‘īd). This means that the Quran presents warnings and threats in diverse ways—through stories, parables, arguments, and direct statements—to penetrate different types of hearts. The stylistic variety is a means to break through heedlessness and intellectual resistance. The purpose is given: “so perhaps they will shun evil or it may cause them to be mindful. ” The verb “yattaqūna” implies attaining taqwā, a state of God-consciousness that leads to avoiding sin. The alternative “or it may cause them to be mindful” translates “yuḥdithu lahum dhikran,” meaning the Quran might create in them a remembrance, an awakening, a new consciousness of the truth.

The use of “perhaps” (la‘alla) on Allah’s part expresses the intended effect, not uncertainty. It is as if to say, “We have done all this so that they might respond. ” The verse encapsulates the Quran’s method: repetition and variation of warnings are not redundancies but pedagogical tools. It also implicitly answers the objection that the Quran contains repeated stories; the variation is deliberate to engender taqwā. For the Prophet, this verse explains the wisdom behind the different styles of the surah, which moved from the story of Moses to the Day of Judgment, all as diversified reminders. It calls upon the listener to engage with the Quran actively, allowing its variations to soften the heart and produce mindfulness.

=114=

This verse begins with glorification: “Exalted is Allah, the True King!” (fa-ta‘āla Allāhu al-Maliku al-Ḥaqq). It declares Allah’s transcendence above all false claims and His true, absolute sovereignty. All earthly kings are transient, but Allah is the King whose rule is eternal and real. This praise sets the tone for the crucial instruction that follows. The Prophet is then given a direct command: “Do not rush to recite the Quran before it is properly conveyed to you.” According to authentic reports, when the angel Gabriel brought revelation, the Prophet would hurriedly move his tongue, trying to memorize it on the spot, fearing he might forget it. He exerted himself greatly out of eagerness. Allah gently instructed him not to hasten; the responsibility of its collection and recitation rested with Allah. The verse parallels Surah Al-Qiyāmah, “Do not move your tongue with it to hasten it. Indeed, upon Us is its collection and its recitation.” Here, the command is coupled with a beautiful prayer: “and pray, ‘My Lord! Increase me in knowledge.’” This is a profound teaching: instead of anxiously rushing to grasp knowledge, one should turn to Allah and ask for an increase in knowledge. Knowledge (ilm) here encompasses the Quran, its meanings, and all beneficial knowledge. The prayer “Rabbi zidnī ‘ilman” is one of the most beloved supplications for students and scholars. It embodies humility, acknowledging that true knowledge is a gift from Allah and that no matter how much one learns, there is always more to seek. The verse connects with the opening of the surah where the Quran was not sent to cause distress; the Prophet is now shown how to receive it with tranquility, trust, and continuous supplication.

For believers, it is a direct instruction: seek knowledge with patience and always ask Allah for increase. It frames the entire pursuit of learning as an act of worship, grounded in reliance on Allah rather than on one’s own intellectual power. The verse beautifully integrates praise, instruction, and supplication into a single transformative moment.

=115=

With this verse, the surah transitions to the story of Adam, the father of humanity, linking the trials of the earlier prophets to the very origin of human existence. Allah says, “We once made a covenant with Adam.” This covenant was the command not to eat from the forbidden tree. It was a direct instruction, a test of obedience. “But he forgot” — the Arabic “fa-nasiya” indicates that Adam did not deliberately disobey out of rebellion, but his memory lapsed, and his resolve weakened. This forgetfulness is part of human nature. Then the verse states, “and We did not find determination in him.

” The word “‘azm” means firm resolve, steadfastness, strength of will. Adam, at that moment, lacked the determination to withstand the temptation. He was not obstinate like Iblīs; he simply faltered.

This statement is not a condemnation but an explanation of his human vulnerability. It shows that even a prophet, created directly by Allah’s hands, was susceptible to forgetting and weakness. This serves to teach all of humanity that we are prone to error, and that perfection belongs to Allah alone.

The verse also implicitly contrasts Adam’s forgetfulness with the stubborn refusal of Iblīs, who willfully disobeyed out of arrogance. Adam’s slip was followed by repentance, while Iblīs’s was followed by defiance.

The mention of the covenant and its breach sets the stage for the subsequent narrative of the prostration of the angels, the refusal of Iblīs, and the temptation. For the Prophet Muhammad, this story was a reminder that his own community would make mistakes, but the door of repentance is open. The lack of determination in Adam is not a permanent flaw; it is a temporary state that was overcome through divine grace, as later verses will describe. The lesson is that human strength is always dependent on Allah, and forgetfulness can only be remedied by His remembrance.

=116=

The story unfolds further. Allah commanded the angels to prostrate before Adam, a prostration of honour and greeting, not of worship.

This command was a test of obedience for the heavenly beings. The angels, being pure and obedient servants of Allah, all prostrated immediately, acknowledging the superiority of Adam’s knowledge which Allah had taught him.

However, Iblīs—who was among the jinn but in the company of the angels due to his devotion—refused. The verse emphasizes “who refused arrogantly” (abā wa-stakbara). His refusal was not out of ignorance but pure arrogance. He considered himself superior, being made of fire, while Adam was made of clay. This arrogance is the first sin in creation, the root of all evil. The prostration of the angels symbolizes the proper human response to divine command, while Iblīs’s refusal symbolizes rebellion. The command itself established Adam as a khalīfah on earth, and the prostration was an acknowledgment of the honour Allah bestowed upon humanity. By recounting this event, the Quran reminds the Children of Adam that their enemy, Satan, declared his enmity from that very moment.

The verse also contrasts with the previous mention of Adam’s forgetfulness: Iblīs did not forget; he actively and consciously rejected. This makes his sin far graver. For the Prophet Muhammad, this narrative served as a warning against the arrogance of the Quraysh, who refused to prostrate to the truth brought by a mere human from among them, just as Iblīs refused to honour Adam. The story illustrates that true nobility lies in obedience to Allah, not in one’s origin or substance. The prostration of the angels also foreshadows the eventual submission of all creation to the final message, while Iblīs’s refusal is the prototype of all disbelief.

=117=

After Iblīs’s refusal and his banishment, Allah turned to Adam and his wife Ḥawwā’ (Eve) with a clear warning. The address “O Adam!” is personal and full of care. The warning identifies Iblīs as “an enemy to you and to your wife,” establishing the permanent enmity between Satan and humanity. This enmity is not merely personal but extends to all descendants. The specific caution is “do not let him drive you both out of Paradise,” indicating that Satan’s goal is to strip them of their blessed abode. The consequence of being driven out is then explained: “for you would then suffer hardship.

” The Arabic “fa-tashqā” means you will toil and experience misery. This hardship includes the difficulties of worldly life—earning a living, hunger, thirst, fatigue—as opposed to the ease of Paradise where all needs were met without effort. The warning is not a threat but a merciful revelation of the consequences. It shows Allah’s desire to protect Adam and his wife from suffering.

The verse also explains why the world is a place of trial and toil: it is the result of that first transgression, yet it is also the arena for redemption. The phrase “to you and to your wife” emphasizes that both are equally targeted; Satan does not discriminate. The warning also implies that the couple had the capacity to resist, which is why they were responsible. This verse serves as a timeless caution for all believers: Satan is an active, cunning enemy who seeks to drive humanity out of the peace of Allah’s obedience into the hardship of sin and heedlessness.

The mention of “your wife” also honors the marital bond and the shared responsibility of protecting each other from the enemy’s plots. The following verses describe the abundant provisions of Paradise that they would lose if they succumbed.

=118=

Allah details the blessings of Paradise that Adam and his wife enjoyed, contrasting them with the hardships of the worldly life they would face if expelled. The first guarantee is that they will never go hungry or unclothed. Hunger is the most basic physical pain, and nudity is exposure and loss of dignity. In Paradise, food was abundant and available without toil, and clothing was provided as a covering of honour. The phrasing “it is guaranteed” (inna laka) emphasizes the certainty of this provision. This provision is a manifestation of Allah’s care. The absence of hunger implies complete satisfaction, and the absence of nakedness implies permanent covering. These two are among the most fundamental human needs, and their fulfillment in Paradise without effort contrasts sharply with the worldly condition where humans must toil for food and clothing. The verse implicitly points to the mercy of Allah, Who did not create humans to suffer but placed them in a place of total provision. The devil’s promise to Adam was of a kingdom that never fades, but what they already had was perfect sustenance and dignity. The warning is thus deepened: why risk guaranteed comfort for a deceptive promise?

The mention of hunger and clothing also has symbolic dimensions: spiritual hunger is the absence of guidance, and spiritual nakedness is the exposure of sins. In Paradise, they were free from both. The lesson for believers is to appreciate the provisions Allah has given and to be wary of Satan’s whispers that make them dissatisfied with pure and lawful blessings, pushing them toward forbidden desires that bring hardship.

=119=

The description of Paradise’s provisions continues with two more guarantees: freedom from thirst and from the sun’s heat. Thirst (ẓamā’) and heat (ḍuḥā) are significant afflictions, especially in the desert context of Arabia. Paradise provided perpetual hydration and a perfectly temperate climate, free from the scorching sun that causes fatigue and discomfort. The shade of Paradise is one of its most mentioned blessings in the Quran. The four guarantees—no hunger, no nakedness, no thirst, no heat—cover the most essential physical needs and comforts. Together, they paint a picture of complete well-being.

This is the state that Adam and his wife would lose by heeding Satan. The contrast with the worldly life is stark: on earth, humans constantly struggle against thirst, sunstroke, and the elements. This serves as a reminder that the worldly life is inherently a place of toil, while the true home of ease is the Hereafter.

The mention of these specific blessings also alludes to the story of Moses and the manna and quails, where the Israelites were provided food and shade in the desert; when they transgressed, they lost some of that ease. For believers, this reinforces the value of contentment with Allah’s lawful provisions and the danger of aspiring to what Satan falsely promises. The heat and thirst also point to the suffering of the disbelievers on the Day of Judgment, who will experience a blazing sun and extreme thirst, as described earlier.

Thus, the choice is between the eternal shade of Paradise and the scorching punishment of Hell. The warning to Adam is a universal warning: do not exchange the guaranteed, pure blessings of obedience for the illusory temptations of sin.

Despite the explicit warning, Satan approached Adam and his wife with a cunning temptation. The verb “waswasa” (whispered) is the origin of the term waswasah, indicating subtle, repeated, and insidious suggestions. Satan did not openly declare his enmity; he presented himself as a sincere advisor. His address “O Adam!” mimics the caring tone of the divine warning, which makes the whisper more seductive. He then made an attractive offer: “Shall I show you the Tree of Immortality and a kingdom that does not fade away?” The tree he referred to was the forbidden tree. He claimed that eating from it would grant them eternal life, making them angels or immortals, and that it would give them a kingdom that never decays or ends. This was a direct appeal to the human desire for permanence and power. The offer was a lie, because Allah had already guaranteed them a blissful life in Paradise. Satan’s strategy was to make them feel that what they had was insufficient and that they could attain something greater by disobeying. He planted the idea that Allah was hiding the tree from them out of a desire to prevent them from becoming superior.

The phrase “kingdom that does not fade away” resonates with the human aversion to decline and loss. Adam and his wife, being free from sin, were susceptible to this deception because they could not imagine a lie. The verse illustrates the archetypal method of temptation: presenting evil as a means to a noble end (immortality, lasting kingdom), exploiting natural human aspirations, and casting doubt on the divine command. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a clear warning against the whisperings that would come to his community from Satan and his allies, who beautify sinful actions and make them seem like paths to success. The verse sets the stage for the fall, which is immediately followed by repentance and divine mercy, demonstrating the full cycle of human error and divine forgiveness.
