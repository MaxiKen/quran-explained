=41=

After recalling the lifelong chain of favours, Allah now declares the purpose behind all that meticulous care: “And I have selected you for My service.” The Arabic verb “iṣṭana‘tuka” comes from the root meaning to manufacture or fashion something with special attention, implying that Moses was personally crafted and chosen for a divine role. This selection is not merely an appointment; it is the culmination of a process in which every event of his life—the river, the palace, the exile—was a stage of preparation. Allah was forming him for prophethood and direct conversation.

The phrase “li-nafsī” means “for Myself,” indicating that Moses was selected for a uniquely intimate relationship with his Lord, one that involved speaking directly without an intermediary. This was a tremendous honour, for which Moses is often called Kalīm Allāh, the one to whom Allah spoke. The verse reinforces that the favours just mentioned were not random acts of kindness but deliberate steps in a plan that led to this sacred election. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, this is a powerful reminder that his own selection as the final messenger was similarly part of an eternal decree. His upbringing as an orphan, his honesty among his people, and his retreat to the cave were all facets of that divine preparation.

The concept of being chosen “for My service” also teaches that the highest honour is not worldly power but being dedicated to Allah. Moses was not chosen to become a king, but to carry the burden of revelation and lead his people to monotheism. The verse transitions beautifully from the comforting recollections of the past into the active command that now follows, giving Moses the full weight of his calling while grounding him in the certainty that he was made for this very moment.

=42=

With the selection confirmed, the command to move into action is issued. “Go forth, you and your brother” pairs them as a team, fulfilling Moses’s earlier request. They are not to go empty-handed but “with My signs”—the staff and the shining hand, and all the subsequent miracles that would manifest. These signs served as both proof and protection. The instruction “never falter in remembering Me” is a profound addition.

The Arabic “wa lā taniyā fī dhikrī” means do not slacken, weaken, or tire in My remembrance. The mission ahead would be exhausting, terrifying, and full of setbacks, but the lifeline was constant dhikr. If they kept their hearts and tongues occupied with the remembrance of Allah, fear would dissipate and resolve would strengthen. The command implies that faltering in remembrance leads to spiritual weakness, which opens the door to failure. For Moses and Aaron, dhikr was the fuel that would sustain them before Pharaoh’s arrogance and the eventual stubbornness of their own people. The verse connects the outward mission with the inward necessity of a living connection with Allah. The signs were external tools, but remembrance was the internal engine. For the Prophet Muhammad, this instruction was echoed when he was told, “And remember your Lord when you forget.” The greatest challenge for anyone engaged in calling to Allah is not the opposition of enemies but the drying up of one’s own spiritual reservoir. The command to go is thus immediately balanced with the command to stay connected.

The pairing of the two brothers in the command also demonstrates that the responsibility of maintaining remembrance is shared; they could remind each other when one grew weak. The verse elegantly merges the physical departure to Egypt with the spiritual journey of the heart.

=43=

The specific destination and target are restated with renewed force. The command “Go, both of you, to Pharaoh” singles out the tyrant himself. They were not to start with the oppressed Israelites or the general public, but to march directly into the centre of power. This underscores the prophetic method of addressing the root of corruption. The reason is given again: “for he has truly transgressed all bounds.

” The repetition of Pharaoh’s transgression from verse 24 reinforces the gravity of his crimes. His tyranny was not merely political; it was a rebellion against Allah’s sovereignty, marked by the claim to divinity, the enslavement of a whole people, and the brutal killing of their infants.

The word “ṭaghā” implies a flood-like overflow, someone who has burst all limits of decency and submission to God. By sending Moses and Aaron directly to such a person, Allah demonstrates that no power on earth is beyond the reach of His message. The prophets are not sent only to the receptive; they must confront the obstinate and give them a clear warning. The fact that they are told “both of you” reassures Moses that Aaron shares the burden fully. They are to stand together before the mightiest king of the age. For the Prophet Muhammad, this verse was a source of strength as he faced the Quraysh leaders who, like Pharaoh, considered themselves lords. The instruction to go directly to the transgressor also teaches that the call to truth must not be diluted by fear of the powerful. Justice begins with addressing the oppressor.

Thus, the command is a declaration of war against falsehood, but a war waged with the weapon of truth and the signs of Allah, not with swords. It sets the stage for the confrontation that follows.

=44=

Despite Pharaoh’s monstrous transgression, Allah instructs Moses and Aaron to adopt a gentle approach. The Arabic “fa-qūlā lahu qawlan layyinan” means “speak to him a gentle word.

” This is one of the most profound lessons in the Quran on the etiquette of da’wah. The softness of speech does not imply weakness or compromise on the message, but rather a wise and compassionate mode of delivery that respects the humanity of even the worst tyrant. The purpose is given: “so perhaps he may be mindful or fearful.

” The word “yatadhakkar” means to receive admonition and remember the truth, while “yakhshā” means to develop a reverential fear of Allah. The goal of the gentle approach is not to flatter Pharaoh but to open his heart. Had they begun with harshness, his arrogance would have instantly erected a barrier. Gentle speech is a means to penetrate pride and awaken the dormant conscience. The verse demonstrates the mercy of Allah, Who desires guidance for all, even a man who said “I am your lord, most high.

” The use of “perhaps” (la‘alla) on Allah’s part indicates hope and possibility, not uncertainty; it teaches believers to remain optimistic in their calling, leaving the outcome to Allah. For the Prophet Muhammad, this verse was a guiding principle when dealing with the Meccan chiefs. He was patient, gentle, and often absorbed their insults with grace, hoping that one day they would believe. The lesson extends to all Muslims: when conveying the truth, the manner matters as much as the content. A gentle word can soften the hardest heart, while harshness can repel even those inclined to accept. The command to Moses and Aaron remains a timeless charter for kind and wise communication, even in the face of extreme evil.

=45=

Despite the divine reassurance and the command to speak gently, Moses and Aaron openly express their human fear. This is not a sign of weak faith but an honest acknowledgment of their vulnerability. They address Allah as “Our Lord,” and confess two specific fears: that Pharaoh might swiftly punish them before they can deliver the full message, or that he might “act tyrannically,” meaning he would escalate in arrogance and transgression, making the mission even more dangerous.

The phrase “an yafruṭa ‘alaynā” can mean that he might hasten to harm them or that his transgression might increase beyond all limits. Their fear is natural; they are two unarmed men walking into the court of a god-king with absolute power of life and death. This plea reveals the full humanity of the prophets, who were not superhuman beings immune to fear. Their courage was not the absence of fear but the strength to proceed despite it. This admission also serves as a form of supplication, indirectly asking for protection and reinforcement.

The verse teaches believers that it is permissible to voice fears to Allah and to seek His help in overcoming them. Suppressing fear can sometimes lead to anxiety, but expressing it in prayer while still obeying the command is the prophetic model. The Prophet Muhammad similarly expressed his fear at the beginning of his mission and was repeatedly comforted. The plea of Moses and Aaron sets the stage for the beautiful divine reassurance that follows immediately, showing that Allah does not rebuke His servants for their fears but answers them with calming promises of His presence.

=46=

The response to their fear is immediate and all-encompassing. Allah says “Lā takhāfā” — “Do not both of you fear.” The dual form addresses both Moses and Aaron, enveloping them equally in the divine protection. The command to not fear is not a reprimand but a strengthening. Then comes the profound reassurance: “Innanī ma‘akumā asma‘u wa arā” — “Indeed I am with you both, I hear and I see.

” This is one of the most comforting statements a believer can ever receive. The divine “withness” (ma‘iyyah) is not a physical presence but a special accompaniment of help, protection, and guidance. Allah hears every word that will be spoken in that confrontation—Pharaoh’s threats, their own replies—and He sees every move. Nothing will happen outside His knowledge and control.

The mention of hearing and seeing specifically addresses their fear of harm; if Allah is listening and watching, then no harm can reach them without His permission, and He will intervene at the right moment. The Prophet Muhammad was given similar reassurance during the Hijrah when he told Abu Bakr in the cave, “Do not grieve; indeed Allah is with us. ” This verse teaches that the awareness of Allah’s presence is the most powerful antidote to fear. When a believer internalizes that the All-Hearing, All-Seeing Lord is with him, the threats of creation diminish. The assurance is given before they even step into Pharaoh’s court, so they can carry that certainty in their hearts.

The verse also illustrates that after the servant expresses vulnerability, Allah responds with more than they asked for—He gives His own company.

=47=

The mission script is now provided. They are to begin by identifying themselves as “messengers from your Lord,” a title that immediately challenges Pharaoh’s claim to be the lord himself.

The phrase “your Lord” reminds Pharaoh that there is a higher authority over him, the very One who created and sustains him. The first demand is the liberation of the Children of Israel: “so let the Children of Israel go with us, and do not oppress them. ” The mission was not just about theological debate; it was about ending systematic injustice. The message of tawḥīd is inseparable from social liberation.

The phrase “do not oppress them” implies that continuing enslavement is an act of oppression that must cease. Then they are to present the proof: “We have come to you with a sign from your Lord. ” This sign would be the staff and the shining hand, tangible evidence of their divine mandate.

Finally, the core spiritual message: “And salvation will be for whoever follows the right guidance.” This is the universal principle—peace and safety in this world and the next are contingent upon accepting and following the guidance that Allah sends.

The word “salām” here carries meanings of peace and safety from punishment. The message is thus complete: acknowledgment of a higher Lord, the demand for freedom, the evidence of prophethood, and the promise of salvation for those who believe. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was the very structure of his call to the Quraysh: worship Allah alone, free people from the slavery of idols, present the Quran as the sign, and promise eternal success for the followers of guidance. The verse distills the entire prophetic mission into a clear, powerful statement.

=48=

The warning that completes the message is now delivered. The phrase “innā qad ūḥiya ilaynā” emphasizes that this is not their personal opinion but a revelation from Allah. The punishment is specified for those who “deny” (kadhdhaba) the truth and “turn away” (tawallā). Denial implies rejecting the message after recognizing it, and turning away means abandoning it in practice and attitude. The warning of punishment balances the earlier promise of salvation; the message is complete with both glad tidings and warnings. This was necessary because Pharaoh and his court were steeped in disbelief and oppression; they needed to know the consequences of persisting in their ways. The punishment mentioned could encompass both worldly destruction and the torment of the Hereafter. Moses and Aaron were instructed to be gentle in speech, but that gentleness did not mean concealing the hard truths. The warning was delivered clearly, without ambiguity, leaving Pharaoh with no excuse. The verse demonstrates the prophetic balance between hope and fear. Presenting only mercy without warning can make people complacent, while only warning without mercy can lead to despair. The message of Moses combined both. For the Prophet Muhammad, the same balance is evident throughout the Quran, where the mercy of Allah is emphasized alongside the severe punishment for those who reject faith. The instruction to Moses and Aaron also shows that warnings should be given before punishment descends, out of Allah’s justice and mercy.

The verse concludes the initial speech that the two brothers were to deliver, setting the stage for Pharaoh’s response.

=49=

After hearing the message, Pharaoh responds not with immediate violence but with a question. His question, however, is not an honest search for truth; it is a mixture of sarcasm, dismissal, and a challenge. By asking “Who then is the Lord of you two, O Moses?” Pharaoh deliberately ignores Aaron, perhaps belittling the partnership or focusing on Moses as the primary speaker. His phrasing “the Lord of you two” insinuates that he does not recognize any such being; he wants Moses to define this deity so he can mock or refute.

The question reveals Pharaoh’s arrogance; he considered himself the lord of Egypt, and now two men from the enslaved race speak of a Lord over him. He asks to know the identity of this alleged competitor.

The question also attempts to trap Moses: if Moses described a physical or local god, Pharaoh could easily deride it; if he described an abstract deity, Pharaoh could dismiss it as irrelevant. This is a common tactic of tyrants when confronted with divine authority—to demand a definition that can be undermined.

The verse shows that those who are invested in their own false lordship find the very concept of a supreme Lord threatening. The question is a pivotal moment in the narrative, setting up Moses’s magnificent reply, which does not fall into the trap but instead points to the universal signs of the Creator. The Prophet Muhammad faced similar questions from the Quraysh, who would ask, “What is your Lord like? ” The Quran taught him to respond by pointing to the signs in creation. The inquiry of Pharaoh, though laced with mockery, became the opportunity for a profound declaration of truth.

=50=

Moses’s answer is a masterpiece of concise theological clarity. Instead of getting drawn into abstract names or debates, he describes Allah by His creative act. “The One Who has given everything its distinctive form” translates “alladhī a‘ṭā kulla shay’in khalqahu.” This means that Allah created each thing with its specific nature, shape, characteristics, and purpose. Every creature, from the smallest particle to the largest celestial body, has been given its proper form in the grand design. Then, after giving form, “He guided it.” This guidance is the innate direction that every creation follows: the fish knows how to swim, the bird how to fly, the celestial bodies how to orbit, the plants how to grow, and the human intellect how to reason. This includes the instinctual guidance (hidāyat al-wujdān) and the guidance through revelation for human beings.

The answer beautifully demonstrates that the Lord’s existence and attributes can be known by reflecting on the world. Moses did not argue from scripture but from the observable facts of creation. Pharaoh, who prided himself on his knowledge of the natural world and his magicians’ manipulation of it, was being told that behind every orderly system is a single Creator.

The answer also implies that just as Allah gave everything its form and guidance, He has now given Pharaoh a message and guidance through Moses; rejecting it means violating one’s own created purpose. The Prophet Muhammad would later give similar answers to the pagans, drawing attention to the creation of the heavens and the earth. The verse stands as a timeless proof of Allah’s lordship, accessible to every person with an open mind.

=51=

Pharaoh, unable to counter the argument from creation, shifts to another line of questioning. He asks about “previous peoples” — the generations of old who did not worship this Lord that Moses proclaims. This question is a rhetorical trap: if Moses says they were misguided and perished, Pharaoh might mock their ancestors. If Moses says they were saved, Pharaoh could ask where their gods are now.

The question also reflects a common skeptical argument: if your Lord is the only true God, what happened to all the nations that came before? Were they all wrong and damned? Pharaoh’s intent is to paint Moses’s message as a novelty, a break from tradition. He wants to portray himself as the guardian of the established order and ancestral ways. This was exactly the argument of the Meccan pagans against the Prophet Muhammad: “We found our fathers upon a religion.” Pharaoh is using the weight of history to dismiss the call to tawḥīd.

The question also carries a veiled threat: those who challenged the established powers in the past met their doom, so Moses should be careful. The verse captures the psychological warfare of the tyrant, who tries to undermine the messenger’s confidence by making him appear ignorant of history or disrespectful of the ancestors. The stage is set for Moses’s response, which masterfully deflects the question away from futile speculation and anchors all knowledge in the divine record, while maintaining the focus on the present call.

=52=

Moses refuses to be drawn into a detailed historical debate that would sidetrack the mission. He responds by assigning the knowledge of the previous peoples entirely to Allah. “That knowledge is with my Lord in a Record” — the Preserved Tablet or the Divine Book of Decrees — meaning Allah knows exactly what those nations did, what they believed, and what their fate was. Moses’s role is not to give a historical lecture but to convey the current message. He then adds a powerful description of Allah: “My Lord neither falters nor forgets.

” The word “yaḍillu” means to err or go astray, and “yansā” means to forget. Unlike human rulers whose knowledge is imperfect and memory fallible, Allah’s knowledge is absolute and eternal. He does not make mistakes, and nothing escapes His record.

This statement indirectly answers Pharaoh’s challenge: whatever those previous peoples did is perfectly recorded, and they have been or will be judged with absolute justice. No error or injustice can occur. The response also implies a warning: Pharaoh’s own deeds are being recorded, and he will be held accountable just as previous tyrants were. The elegance of Moses’s answer lies in its refusal to entertain irrelevant debate while reaffirming Allah’s supreme knowledge. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a model for dealing with those who asked about the fate of past nations or the details of the unseen; the proper reply is to refer knowledge to Allah and keep the focus on the message at hand.

The verse teaches believers that not every question deserves a detailed answer; some are traps, and the best response is to entrust matters to Allah and redirect to His attributes.

=53=

Moses continues his description of the Lord, now returning to the tangible signs of Allah’s power and mercy. After stating that knowledge of the past rests with Allah, he brings the conversation back to the immediate evidence of creation. The verse describes three specific blessings: the earth laid out like a carpet for habitation, pathways carved through mountains and valleys for travel, and rain sent down from the sky that causes a variety of plants to spring forth. “Laid out the earth” points to its stability and suitability for human life. The “pathways” are not just physical roads but also the instinctual guidance by which creatures navigate. Rain is the source of life, and its mention emphasizes the dependency of all life on Allah’s mercy. The “various types of plants” — paired, diverse species — reflect the intricate design.

This description serves multiple purposes: it answers the earlier question about the Lord by making Him known through His works, it demonstrates His benevolence and thus His right to be worshipped, and it silently rebukes Pharaoh, who despite controlling the land, cannot create a single seed or raindrop. The imagery shifts the scene from the court of an arrogant king to the vast, orderly universe, reminding everyone that the true Lord is not a tribal deity but the Sustainer of all. The Prophet Muhammad frequently drew similar attention to the natural world as a sign of Allah’s oneness. The verse integrates theology with ecology, teaching that every natural phenomenon is a sign pointing to the Creator, and that rejecting such clear signs is an act of blindness.

=54=

The direct result of Allah’s provision is stated: humans are to eat of the plants and crops, and their livestock are to graze. The command “eat and graze your cattle” is a simple, universal truth—food for humans and animals all comes from the same divine system of rain and soil. It is an invitation to recognize the Lord’s sustenance and to partake of it with gratitude. Pharaoh, who controlled the grain stores of Egypt and used food as a weapon of control, is being reminded that even his granaries are filled by the same Lord who sends rain.

The phrase “ra‘aw an‘āmakum” literally means “pasture your cattle,” highlighting the pastoral life that was central to the Israelites and many people. Then the verse concludes: “Surely in this are signs for people of sound judgment.

” The Arabic “ulī an-nuhā” refers to those with intellect, insight, and the ability to restrain themselves from falsehood. These signs are obvious, but only those who use their reason without the hindrance of arrogance and desire will perceive them.

The verse draws a clear link between observing nature and attaining spiritual truth. It is a call to contemplation. For Pharaoh and his court, who prided themselves on wisdom and magic, the challenge is thrown: if you truly have sound judgment, recognize the Creator in these clear signs. The meal and the pasture are daily proofs of a compassionate Lord, yet they are often taken for granted. The verse thus elevates the ordinary act of eating into a sign that can lead to faith. It is a recurring Quranic theme that the cycle of rain, plant, and sustenance is among the most accessible and powerful proofs of divine mercy and power.

=55=

This verse encapsulates the entire human story in relation to the earth. “From the earth We created you” refers to the creation of the first human, Adam, from clay, and the ongoing creation of all humans from the nutrients that ultimately derive from the earth. It grounds humanity in humility; our physical origin is dust. “And into it We will return you” points to death and burial, when the body decomposes and rejoins the soil.

This is a reminder of mortality, something Pharaoh with his false claim to divinity desperately sought to deny. “And from it We will bring you back again” is a powerful affirmation of the resurrection. The same earth that receives the dead will one day, by Allah’s command, yield them forth anew for judgment. The three stages—creation, death, and resurrection—mirror the plant cycle described just before: a seed from the earth grows, dies, and is revived. The human being is no different. This verse is strategically placed in the dialogue with Pharaoh, who rejected the concept of the Hereafter. It asserts the inevitability of the Hour that Moses was earlier commanded to believe in. For the Prophet Muhammad, who faced Meccan pagans denying resurrection, this verse and its profound logic were a source of argument. The entire story of life is contained in this single verse, and it is presented as an undeniable reality. The One who originated you from dust can just as easily bring you out again. The verse leaves no room for the arrogance of those who think they can escape their origin and their end. It is both a theological declaration and an existential grounding for every human being.

=56=

After the dialogue, the narrative summarizes the outcome of Moses’s mission to Pharaoh. Allah says, “We certainly showed Pharaoh all of Our signs.” This includes the staff, the shining hand, and the subsequent plagues—the flood, locusts, lice, frogs, and blood—nine clear signs in total. Each sign was a miraculous demonstration of divine power, designed to shake Pharaoh’s arrogance and prove Moses’s truthfulness. Despite this overwhelming cumulative evidence, the response was denial and refusal.

The phrase “fa-kadhdhaba wa abā” uses two verbs: he denied intellectually and refused practically to submit. Denial is the rejection of the truth in the mind, and refusal is the stubborn disobedience of the will. Pharaoh saw the miracles with his own eyes, yet he chose to label them as magic and to reject their implications. This reveals that disbelief is not always due to lack of evidence but often due to arrogance, vested interests, and an unwillingness to surrender one’s power and desires. Pharaoh’s kingdom and god-status were threatened, so he hardened his heart. This verse contains a deep warning: even the clearest signs cannot guide a heart that is determined to reject. The Prophet Muhammad experienced the same pain when the Quraysh saw the miracle of the Quran and yet denied it.

The verse also serves to highlight the justice of Allah; He gave Pharaoh every opportunity, sending Moses with multiple signs and gentle speech, but when the rejection was final, punishment became due. The summary prepares the listener for the unfolding of Pharaoh’s schemes and his ultimate doom.

=57=

Pharaoh’s response to the signs is a masterful piece of political propaganda. Instead of addressing the truth of the miracles, he reframes the entire mission as a political conspiracy. He accuses Moses of trying to “drive us out of our land” — a fear-mongering appeal to nationalism and territorial integrity. By using “us,” he aligns the Egyptian elite and perhaps the common people with himself, portraying Moses as an outsider threatening the homeland. He labels the signs “magic,” thereby dismissing their divine origin and reducing them to a contest of sorcerers. This was a common tactic of tyrants when confronted with prophets: discredit the messenger, attribute his miracles to trickery, and stir up fear of social upheaval. Pharaoh knew that Moses and the Israelites were an enslaved minority; accusing them of a takeover plot would rally the Egyptian populace against them. The irony is that Pharaoh himself was a foreign Hyksos descendant by some accounts, but he now poses as the defender of the land. The verse captures the psychological manipulation employed by those in power to deflect from the truth. The Prophet Muhammad faced the exact same accusations: the Quraysh said he wanted to undermine their ancestral religion, divide their community, and seize power.

The question “Have you come…” is dripping with sarcasm, intended to put Moses on the defensive. Yet Moses does not fall for the bait; he will respond calmly with a challenge.

This verse teaches that falsehood often cloaks itself in the guise of patriotism and cultural preservation, and that the messenger must see through these smokescreens.

=58=

Pharaoh escalates from dismissal to a direct challenge. He claims that Egypt can produce magic equal to Moses’s signs.

The phrase “similar magic” reveals his refusal to accept the miracles as anything beyond sorcery. He is confident in the skills of his own magicians, who were among the most advanced in the ancient world. By proposing a public contest, Pharaoh aims to settle the matter in a way that would humiliate Moses and reassert his own supremacy. The demand for an appointment “that neither of us will fail to keep” and “in a central place” shows his cunning. He wants a neutral, open ground where the maximum number of people can witness the confrontation, believing that a public defeat of Moses would crush the Israelite morale and reestablish his own divine aura. The “central place” would be easily accessible to both the court and the common people. Pharaoh’s strategy was to turn a religious and moral confrontation into a spectacle that he assumed he would win. This reveals his arrogance and his reliance on worldly power and skill. He does not for a moment consider the possibility that Moses’s signs are truly from a Lord greater than him. For Moses, this challenge was an opportunity; a public showdown would make the victory of truth unmistakable. The Prophet Muhammad was similarly challenged by the Quraysh to produce miracles, and while his primary miracle was the Quran, the pattern of opponents demanding displays of power recurs. The verse highlights the desperate attempt of falsehood to compete with truth on its own theatrical terms.

=59=

Moses accepts the challenge with calm confidence and sets the terms for the encounter. He chooses the “Day of the Festival” (yawm az-zīnah), a major Egyptian holiday when people would be off work, dressed in their finest, and gathered in celebration. This was a strategic choice: the festival guaranteed a massive public attendance, ensuring that the showdown would be witnessed by the widest possible audience. The “mid-morning” timing (duḥan) was when the sun was bright and visibility perfect, eliminating any suspicion of trickery done under the cover of darkness or shadows. Moses’s choice demonstrates his complete faith in Allah’s victory; he did not seek a secluded or secret meeting but the most exposed and crowded time. The festival likely honoured Pharaoh or their deities, and Moses’s victory on that very day would symbolically dethrone the false gods in the presence of their own worshippers.

The verse also shows Moses’s wisdom and prudence, ensuring that no excuse could later be made about a rigged or poorly attended event. He gave Pharaoh exactly what he wanted—a public spectacle—but on terms that would magnify the glory of the true God. The Prophet Muhammad’s open proclamation of the message, despite the dangers, mirrors this prophetic confidence.

The verse teaches that when truth confronts falsehood, there is no need for secrecy or dark corners; the light of truth thrives in the full glare of day. The stage is now set for the epic confrontation between the staff of Moses and the sorcery of Egypt.

=60=

After the appointment was set, Pharaoh departed from the negotiation to put his plan into action. The verb “tawallā” means he turned away and withdrew. What followed was a period of intense preparation.

The phrase “fa-jama‘a kaydahu” means he gathered his plot or his deceitful scheme. This involved summoning the most skilled magicians from all corners of Egypt, training them, coordinating their performance, and perhaps preparing props and illusions.

The word “kayd” implies cunning, strategy, and deception. Pharaoh was not taking any chances; despite his arrogance, he mobilized the full resources of his kingdom to ensure Moses’s defeat. Then “thumma atā” — then he came, meaning he returned to the appointed place on the agreed day with his assembled magicians and the public. The verse is concise but paints a vivid picture of the tyrant busily engineering his counterattack. It shows the machinery of falsehood working hard to suppress the truth, while Moses simply relied on what Allah had given him. The contrast is stark: Pharaoh with his massive scheme, and Moses with his staff and his Lord.

The verse also subtly critiques the futility of human plotting against divine will. All of Pharaoh’s careful orchestration would crumble in moments before the serpent of Moses. The Prophet Muhammad’s Meccan opponents similarly plotted and planned, holding meetings in Dar an-Nadwah to decide how to stop him, but their schemes were also shattered.

The verse teaches that while the people of falsehood will always prepare extensively, the outcome is in Allah’s hands. The narrative pauses here, building suspense for the dramatic event that follows.
