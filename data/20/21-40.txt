=21=

After Moses witnessed his staff transform into a slithering serpent, a natural human reaction of fear arose. Allah immediately addressed this fear with two commands: “Take it” and “have no fear.” The order to seize the serpent was itself a test of trust. Moses was being asked to place his hand upon what had just become a terrifying creature, demonstrating that his reliance on Allah must now override his physical instincts. The reassurance “have no fear” acknowledges the human element—fear is not condemned, but it must be overcome by faith. Then comes the promise: “We will return it to its former state.

” The Arabic uses a plural pronoun of majesty, “sa-nu‘īduhā,” emphasizing the divine power behind the transformation. Once Moses obeyed and grasped it, the serpent reverted back into his familiar staff. This immediate restoration showed that miracles operate under Allah’s complete control and serve a temporary purpose rather than permanently altering the natural order. The event teaches that what Allah commands us to take hold of, even if it appears frightening or difficult, is ultimately safe when He has guaranteed its outcome. The staff would now serve a dual purpose: a shepherd’s tool and a divine sign. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, the lesson is profound. He too would be asked to “take” the message with confidence, without fearing the serpents of opposition.

The sequence—throw, fear, take, reassurance, restoration—becomes a pattern for every believer facing daunting tasks. The verse also reassures that whatever we sacrifice or abandon for Allah’s sake can be returned to us in a better and more blessed form.

=22=

The second great sign given to Moses was the radiant hand. The instruction was to insert his hand into his upper garment near the armpit, and when he withdrew it, it would emerge shining white, yet without any disease or blemish. This whiteness was a brilliant, luminous light, not the whiteness of leprosy, which in some other verses is explicitly negated. It was a supernatural glow, a celestial light that testified to prophethood.

The phrase “as another sign” indicates that this was distinct from the staff, a separate miracle to reinforce the message. The location “under your armpit” or “into your bosom” implies a place close to the heart, perhaps symbolizing that the light of guidance springs from a pure, inner source. For Moses, these two signs—one external, in the staff, and one internal, in his own body—equipped him with comprehensive proofs. The shining hand would later be shown to Pharaoh and his court, demonstrating that Allah’s messenger bore marks of divine favour that no magician could replicate. The miracle was instantaneous and reversible; when Moses returned his hand, it reverted to normal. This taught him that the signs were not his own power but a temporary manifestation of Allah’s will. For the Prophet Muhammad, the similar night journey and the splitting of the moon were signs tailored to his context, while here Moses’s signs were suited to a society steeped in illusions of magic. The whiteness of the hand also symbolized purity, truth, and clarity, contrasting with the darkness of idolatry and oppression that filled Egypt.

=23=

The purpose of granting these miracles is articulated clearly: to show Moses “some of Our greatest signs.” The Arabic “min āyātinā al-kubrā” uses the plural “greatest,” indicating that what Moses was given were selections from a vast repertoire of divine wonders. The staff and the shining hand were just the beginning; the plagues, the parting of the sea, and the manna and quails would follow. Each sign was an “āyah,” a pointer to a deeper truth—Allah’s oneness, power, and mercy.

The phrase “so that We may show you” reveals that these signs served a dual purpose: they would convince Pharaoh and his people, but they also served to strengthen Moses’s own faith. Seeing the transformation firsthand grounded him in the certainty that the One Who spoke to him in the valley was truly the Lord of all creation. The greatest signs are not just spectacular displays; they are intimate revelations of the divine attributes. For the Prophet Muhammad, the Quran itself is described as containing the greatest signs, as its verses continuously unveil the wonders of creation and the realities of the unseen. The verse humbles the reader: even a prophet like Moses needed to witness signs to carry his heavy mission. Allah trains His servants gradually, showing them His power in manageable steps.

The mention of “some” suggests that no matter how many signs a person sees, the totality of Allah’s greatness can never be fully comprehended. This prepares Moses for the immense task ahead, assuring him that he is equipped with more than enough evidence to confront the most arrogant tyranny.

=24=

With the signs firmly in hand and his heart fortified, Moses receives the direct command: “Go to Pharaoh.” The mission is now explicit, moving from the private revelation in the sacred valley to public confrontation. Pharaoh is singled out as the primary target because he represents the pinnacle of rebellion against Allah.

The word “ṭaghā” means he exceeded all limits—oppressing the Children of Israel, slaughtering their sons, enslaving them, and most egregiously, claiming divinity for himself by declaring, “I am your lord, most high. ” His transgression was not a minor sin but a monstrous tyranny rooted in arrogance and unbelief. By ordering Moses to go to this specific tyrant, Allah demonstrates that the message of tawḥīd must confront power structures that set themselves up as gods. No matter how entrenched evil appears, the prophet is not allowed to retreat into the comfort of the wilderness. The command is stark and direct, lacking any softening detail, to emphasize the urgency. For Moses, this order must have been overwhelming—he was a fugitive from Egypt, wanted for a killing, and now he was told to return and face the very ruler he had fled.

Yet the command is paired with the earlier signs, providing the tools. The Prophet Muhammad’s own mission similarly involved confronting the Quraysh leadership, the Pharaohs of Arabia.

The verse teaches that the message of Islam does not exist in a vacuum; it must challenge injustice and false authority. The phrase “he has truly transgressed” also serves as a moral verdict before any dialogue, establishing that this is not a negotiation between equals but a call from truth to falsehood.

=25=

Hearing the command, Moses turned immediately to his Lord in supplication. He did not argue or refuse; he asked for the inner and outer resources needed to fulfill the mission. His first request was “Uplift my heart,” or in Arabic “ishraḥ lī ṣadrī.” This phrase means to expand, open, and fill the chest with calm, courage, and clarity. A constricted heart cannot bear heavy responsibilities or face aggressive enemies. Moses knew that the task required a vast inner space that could absorb fear, ridicule, and pressure without breaking. The request acknowledges that such an expansion is a divine gift, not a human achievement. The Prophet Muhammad was granted the same blessing, as mentioned in Surah Ash-Sharḥ: “Did We not expand for you your chest? ” This expansion includes patience, wisdom, and the ability to see the bigger picture. Moses was about to speak truth to power, and he knew his own human limitations—the memory of having killed a man, his speech difficulty, and his long absence. He needed a heart that could contain the light of revelation and withstand the darkness of Pharaoh’s court. The prayer models the proper attitude for anyone taking on a daunting responsibility: begin by asking Allah to prepare the vessel of the heart. Without inner expansion, knowledge and miracles alone are insufficient. The verse beautifully demonstrates the intimacy between Moses and his Lord; immediately after a heavy command, he turned to prayer, showing that all strength comes from Allah.

=26=

After the inward preparation, Moses asks for external facilitation: “Make my task easy.” The task was immense—confronting a tyrant, demanding the release of an enslaved nation, and conveying a message that would uproot an entire system of belief and governance. He did not ask for the task to be removed, only that it be made easy. This teaches that difficulty in a divine mission is guaranteed, but Allah’s facilitation can render the hardest burden light.

The Arabic “yassir lī amrī” encompasses all aspects: ease in delivering the message, ease in being accepted, ease in facing opposition, and ease in achieving the objective. It is a comprehensive prayer for success.

The word “amrī” (my affair, my task) shows that Moses recognized the mission as his own personal responsibility, not merely an abstract command. By asking for ease, he acknowledged that without Allah’s help, the mission would be unbearably heavy. The Prophet Muhammad often recited a similar supplication: “O Allah, I seek refuge in You from worry and grief, from incapacity and laziness…” The request for ease does not imply a life free from struggle, but a state where the heart remains serene and the obstacles are smoothed. The verse aligns with the Quranic principle, “Allah intends for you ease and does not intend for you hardship.” Moses’s prayer thus becomes a template for all believers facing seemingly impossible tasks: ask for the burden to be lifted, not by escaping the duty, but by the infusion of divine assistance that makes the difficult attainable.

=27=

Moses’s third request addresses his known speech difficulty. According to many reports, he had a lisp or a stammer that made his words sometimes unclear, especially when he was anxious. The impediment, translated from “‘uqdatam min lisānī,” literally “a knot from my tongue,” was not removed entirely as a physical defect but was alleviated so that his speech could be understood. The request for removal is specifically connected to the mission: he needed to communicate effectively with Pharaoh, the elite, and the masses. Speech is the primary tool of a messenger, and any barrier to clear communication could hinder the delivery of the message. Moses’s humility is evident; he did not pretend he had no weaknesses. He laid his vulnerability before Allah and asked for it to be healed. This demonstrates that Allah’s prophets, despite their high station, were fully human and reliant on divine grace for their abilities. The Prophet Muhammad, though extremely eloquent, also depended on Allah for the clarity of his message, and the Quran itself is the ultimate miracle of clear Arabic speech. The “knot” may also symbolize more than a physical impediment; it could refer to any psychological or emotional block that arises when facing a fierce enemy. By asking for its removal, Moses sought confidence and fluency. The response to this prayer is mentioned elsewhere: Allah granted that Aaron would assist him, and that Pharaoh’s own arrogance would ultimately be conquered. The lesson for believers is that one should never hesitate to ask Allah to correct personal weaknesses, especially those that affect the service of the faith.

=28=

The purpose of removing the impediment is stated explicitly: “so people may understand my speech.” The goal is not personal comfort but effective conveyance of the divine message. Moses’s prayer is entirely mission-oriented. He wants to be understood so that the truth can penetrate hearts. This reveals the profound prophetic concern: the messenger’s first duty is clear communication. If the message is garbled or misunderstood due to a speech defect, the responsibility of the people might be diminished.

Thus, Moses seeks to remove that barrier. The Arabic “yafqahū qawlī” implies deep comprehension, not merely hearing sounds. He wanted his words to penetrate and be grasped correctly. This shows the importance of eloquence and clarity in Islamic preaching. The Prophet Muhammad was given “jawāmi‘ al-kalim,” concise and comprehensive speech, enabling his message to spread. For Moses, the understanding of his speech was crucial because he would be addressing a proud court skilled in rhetoric and argument.

The verse also underscores that while guidance is from Allah, the medium of clear language is a means that should be sought. The supplication forms part of the comprehensive prayer that includes inner expansion, external ease, and now effective communication. Everything is woven together for one end: fulfilling the mission. It highlights that good communication skills are a blessing to be prayed for, not taken for granted.

The verse continues the sequence of Moses’s humble but strategic requests.

=29=

Moses now asks for a human support. He does not merely rely on the divine signs; he recognizes the value of companionship in a difficult mission. He asks for a “helper” or “wazīr” (literally a minister or someone who shares the burden) from his own family. The choice of family is significant—a relative would share his background, loyalty, and concern. Prophets were often supported by their kin, like the Prophet Muhammad’s uncle Hamza and cousin Ali. The request acknowledges that even the greatest prophets need close allies. The task of confronting Pharaoh was not a solo endeavor; it required a partner who could stand beside him, reinforce his message, and provide emotional support.

The word “ashuddu bihī azrī” in the next verse elaborates on how this helper would strengthen him. By specifying “from my family,” Moses also might have had Aaron in mind already, knowing his brother’s eloquence and righteousness. The prayer teaches that seeking human assistance while relying on Allah is not a sign of weak faith but of wisdom. The Prophet Muhammad’s frequent consultation with his companions and his reliance on Abu Bakr exemplify this principle. The helper would share the task, pray together, and face the enemy together. The request also implies that the mission is larger than one individual; it requires a team.

The verse continues the intimate conversation, where Moses pours out his needs with complete candor, and his Lord listens.

=30=

Moses finally names the specific person he desires as his helper: Aaron, his biological brother. Aaron is described elsewhere as eloquent, more articulate in speech than Moses, which would compensate for Moses’s speech impediment.

The mention “my brother” emphasizes the natural bond of love and trust. Aaron was already a believer and a righteous man. This request was not a random selection; it was divinely inspired, and Allah had already planned to make Aaron a prophet alongside Moses. The request thus aligns perfectly with divine will. Moses’s prayer here is a model of asking for something that is already known to be good and that Allah has implicitly approved. By naming Aaron, Moses shows his deep longing for his brother to share in the honour of prophethood and in the burden of the mission. The relationship between Moses and Aaron is one of the most beautiful brotherhoods in the Quran. The Prophet Muhammad also cherished his bond with his companions, particularly Ali, to whom he said, “You are to me like Aaron was to Moses, except that there is no prophet after me.” The specificity of the request highlights that the person chosen to assist in a sacred cause should be someone of proven character, closeness, and ability. The verse segues naturally into the elaboration of how Aaron would help, which follows next, tying the family bond to the practical strengthening of the mission. The request for Aaron completes Moses’s five-part supplication: expanded heart, eased task, loosened tongue, a helper, and specifically his brother.

=31=

Moses explains the purpose of having Aaron as his helper: “Strengthen me through him.” The Arabic “ushdud bihī azrī” literally means “reinforce my back by him.” The back is a symbol of strength and support; a burden becomes lighter when someone else helps carry it. Moses knew that standing before Pharaoh would require immense courage and resilience, and the presence of Aaron would double his strength. The spiritual and emotional support of a trusted brother can fortify a person against fear and despair. This request also implies a partnership where each complements the other. Aaron would not merely be a passive assistant but an active partner who would lend his own gifts, particularly his eloquence and perhaps his calmer temperament, to the mission. The verse highlights that even a direct recipient of divine revelation does not become self-sufficient; human interconnectedness remains a divine design. The Prophet Muhammad often prayed for his companions and drew strength from their presence, especially in times of persecution. The choice of the word “azrī” (my strength, my back) evokes the image of two pillars supporting a structure together.

For believers today, this underscores the importance of righteous companionship in carrying out good work. Solitary struggles are far more vulnerable; a partner who shares the vision can make the heavy load bearable. Moses’s supplication thus becomes a comprehensive request for both divine and human resources, blending spiritual dependence with practical wisdom.

=32=

The request extends from strength to actual participation: “let him share my task.” The Arabic “wa-ashrik-hu fī amrī” means to make him a partner in my affair, my mission.

This is not merely about assistance but about co-ownership of the prophetic duty. Moses was asking that Aaron become a fellow messenger, which indeed was granted. Sharing the task implies shared authority, shared responsibility, and shared accountability. It lightens the burden mentally, for a partner understands the weight and can make decisions together. The mission of calling Pharaoh to monotheism and liberating the Israelites was too vast for one person alone. The verse reflects the Quranic principle of shura, consultation and collective effort. The Prophet Muhammad consulted his companions on major matters, showing that the principle of sharing tasks is divinely endorsed. The request also ensures continuity and support; if Moses faltered or faced direct threat, Aaron would carry forward the call. By asking for this sharing, Moses demonstrated a lack of ego; he did not want to be the sole hero. He recognized that the goal was the message, not personal glory. The request for a partner in the task is thus an act of supreme selflessness. It also teaches that when one is given a leadership role, delegating and sharing responsibilities with capable, trusted individuals is a prophetic practice. The verse rounds off Moses’s vision of a collaborative prophetic mission, firmly rooted in the love and trust of brotherhood.

=33=

Moses explains the ultimate objective of this partnership: abundant glorification and remembrance of Allah. The partnership was not for worldly power or prestige but for intensified worship. By having a brother and co-messenger, they could together perform dhikr and tasbīḥ on a much greater scale. The two of them could establish prayer, lead the people in worship, and constantly proclaim Allah’s perfection.

The word “nusabbiḥaka kathīrā” means to declare Allah free from any imperfection, frequently and repeatedly. This glorification would be both a personal spiritual practice and a public proclamation that counters Pharaoh’s false claims of divinity. The presence of a partner would enable them to sustain this worship even in the face of relentless hostility. When one grew weary, the other would continue. Together, they could organize worship for the Children of Israel. The request reveals that the ultimate goal of any prophetic mission is the establishment of Allah’s remembrance on earth. The Prophet Muhammad similarly said that he and his followers were sent to worship Allah alone.

The verse teaches that seeking helpers and building teams should be driven by the desire to increase collective worship and service, not by ambition. The glorification of Allah is the pinnacle of all human activity, and Moses’s prayer places it at the center of his mission plan. This shifts the focus from the confrontation with Pharaoh to the positive spiritual aim.

=34=

In close connection with glorification, Moses asks for the ability to remember Allah much. While tasbīḥ (glorification) declares Allah’s transcendence, dhikr (remembrance) encompasses gratitude, praise, and constant awareness.

The repetition “kathīrā” (much) in both requests emphasizes quantity and consistency. The mission required an unwavering connection with Allah, as the challenges would be relentless. Constant remembrance is the fuel that keeps faith alive. Moses and Aaron would need to remember Allah when fear gripped them, when Pharaoh threatened them, and when their own people complained.

The phrase “and remember You much” also hints at the joy found in worship. Remembrance is not a burden but a source of strength. The Prophet Muhammad was commanded, “Remember your Lord much, and glorify Him in the evening and the morning.” Moses’s supplication aligns perfectly with that command.

The pairing of Aaron with Moses would allow them to remind each other and keep the flame of dhikr burning. For believers, the lesson is profound: in any collective effort for good, the group should be bonded by shared remembrance of Allah. This ensures that the endeavor remains spiritually rooted and protected from ego and deviation. The request for abundant remembrance follows logically from the request for a helper, because righteous companionship is one of the greatest aids to consistent worship. The verse thus concludes the specific purposes of the partnership on the highest spiritual note.

=35=

Moses ends his supplication by grounding it in the knowledge of Allah’s continuous oversight. The Arabic “innaka kunta binā baṣīrā” uses the past tense “kunta” to indicate an eternal attribute: “You have always been seeing us.

” This is a declaration of faith and an expression of intimacy. Moses is saying that Allah knows their state, their needs, and their intentions; He sees their weakness and their sincerity.

The word “baṣīr” means All-Seeing, implying not just visual sight but deep, comprehensive knowledge and care. Because Allah is overseeing them, He knows exactly what they require for the mission. The prayer is thus offered with full confidence that it will be heard and answered, not because of Moses’s merit, but because the All-Seeing Lord who watched over him from infancy will continue to do so. The verse echoes the earlier verses of the surah about Allah’s knowledge of the secret and hidden. Moses is essentially saying, “You see the enormity of the task and my inner fears; therefore, grant these requests. ” The phrase also expresses gratitude for past care. Allah had been watching over Moses when he was a baby in the river, and that same watchful Eye would accompany him into Pharaoh’s court. For the Prophet Muhammad, this is a reminder that the Lord who sees everything will not abandon him.

The verse concludes the supplication on a note of tender reliance, perfectly setting the stage for the affirmative divine response that follows immediately.

=36=

The response is immediate, comprehensive, and emphatic. Allah says, “Qad ūtīta su’laka yā Mūsā” — “You have been given your request, O Moses.

” The word “qad” indicates certainty and completion; it is done. Every item Moses asked for is included: the expansion of his chest, the easing of his task, the loosening of his tongue’s knot, and the appointment of Aaron as a partner. Allah answered the prayer in its entirety. This demonstrates the immense mercy of Allah and His responsiveness to His chosen servants. Moses did not have to wait or repeat the request; the answer came instantly in that same sacred encounter. The address “O Moses” is repeated, emphasizing the personal, loving nature of the response. This immediate granting of a comprehensive prayer is a source of immense hope for believers. It teaches that when one prays with sincerity and for goals aligned with Allah’s will, the answer can be swift and complete. The fact that Aaron was made a prophet simultaneously shows that Allah not only granted the helper but elevated him to the highest rank. The response also indirectly comforts the Prophet Muhammad, who would later face his own difficulties: the same Lord who granted Moses’s requests would certainly grant His final Prophet all that he needed. The verse marks the transition from Moses’s private preparation to the public mission, now fully equipped with divine assurance. It serves as a powerful testament to the effectiveness of dua, especially when offered in moments of great need and with an expanded heart.

=37=

With the immediate mission granted, Allah then reminds Moses of His past favours. This serves multiple purposes: it strengthens Moses’s confidence by recounting how Allah protected and guided him from birth, proving that the same Lord who cared for him then will care for him now.

The phrase “laqad manannā ‘alayka marratan ukhrā” uses the word “manna,” which implies a great, undeserved blessing. The “other time” refers to the entire history of Moses’s life before prophethood. The reminder of past favours is a common Quranic technique to inspire gratitude and trust. For Moses, remembering the miraculous way he was saved from slaughter, raised in Pharaoh’s palace, and guided through exile would dissolve any lingering fear. If Allah engineered such complex events to protect him, surely He would not abandon him now. This verse introduces a flashback sequence that will detail several episodes. The past favours include the inspiration to his mother, the casting into the river, the reunion with his mother, the killing of the Egyptian, the sojourn in Midian, and his destined arrival at the sacred valley. Each event is a testament to Allah’s precise planning. For the Prophet Muhammad, hearing this would be a reminder of his own protected childhood and the favours of Allah upon him. The verse beautifully links the present moment of prophethood with a lifelong pattern of divine care, showing that the mission was always part of a larger design.

=38=

The first specific favour recalled is the divine inspiration given to Moses’s mother. The word “awḥaynā” means We inspired, conveying that Allah placed a firm understanding or command into her heart. This was not prophethood in the formal sense, but a direct spiritual communication to a righteous woman in a moment of extreme crisis. Pharaoh had ordered the killing of all newborn Israelite boys, and Moses’s mother feared for her infant son’s life. The inspiration was the solution: to place him in a chest and cast it into the river. The content of the inspiration is given in the next verse. The reminder highlights that Allah’s care for Moses began even before his birth, reaching his mother and guiding her actions. This demonstrates that Allah’s planning encompasses the righteous servants around His prophets, providing them with the intuition and strength to play their roles.

The mention of the mother also honours the often unseen sacrifices of women in the prophetic stories. For the Prophet Muhammad, whose mother Aminah and foster-mother Halimah played crucial roles, the parallel is evident.

The verse teaches that in times of fear and oppression, Allah can open a way out through subtle inspiration. It also illustrates the Quranic theme that Allah’s help can come through the most unexpected means—a terrified mother, a fragile chest, and the currents of a river. The stage is thus set for the detailed account of that miraculous preservation.

=39=

The inspiration given to Moses’s mother is spelled out. She was told to place her baby into a chest—likely an ark of papyrus or wood—and then cast it into the Nile.

This command defied all natural maternal instinct, yet she obeyed. The promise was that the river would carry the chest to the bank, and that Pharaoh himself, the very enemy who sought to kill the male infants, would take him.

The phrase “an enemy of Mine and his” is striking: the one destined to raise Moses in his palace was simultaneously the arch-enemy of Allah and of the child. Yet, in a profound irony, Allah used the enemy to protect and nurture the very person who would later confront him. This illustrates the absolute sovereignty of Allah over all forces, even those of evil. Then Allah adds, “And I blessed you with lovability from Me. ” This means Allah cast a special affection and beauty upon Moses, so that whoever saw him would love him. When Pharaoh’s wife, Asiyah, saw the infant, her heart instantly softened, and she persuaded Pharaoh to spare him. This “lovability” was a divine gift that acted as a shield.

The phrase “li-tuṣna‘a ‘alā ‘aynī” means “so that you would be brought up under My watchful Eye. ” This idiom implies being raised with special care under direct divine supervision. Moses was trained in the finest palace, educated and prepared for leadership, all while Allah’s eye watched over him. For the Prophet Muhammad, the parallel of being orphaned yet protected and nurtured under Allah’s watchful eye is clear.

The verse shows that no enemy can harm whom Allah chooses to protect, and that the most hopeless situations can be transformed into stages of honour.

=40=

The recounting of favours continues with further details. After Pharaoh’s household took Moses, they sought a wet nurse for him, but he refused every woman. His sister, who had followed the chest, came forward and offered to guide them to a suitable family, carefully not revealing her relationship. This led to the reunion with their mother, who then nursed him openly in the palace while being paid a wage. Allah says the purpose was “so that her heart would be put at ease and she would not grieve.” The mother’s emotional wellbeing mattered to Allah; the plan was not only to save Moses but also to comfort his mother. Then the narrative shifts to Moses’s adulthood: he intervened in a fight and accidentally killed an Egyptian. That event caused him fear and sorrow, but Allah says, “We saved you from sorrow. ” He was guided to leave Egypt before the authorities could execute him, and his repentance was accepted.

This is mentioned as a favour, showing that even Moses’s mistakes were wrapped in divine protection and led to his departure to Midian. There, he spent years gaining life experience, marrying, and maturing.

Finally, all those events aligned so that he arrived at Mount Sinai exactly as “pre-destined” (qadaran). The word “qadar” means a precise measure or decree. Every step, from the river to the killing to the sojourn in Midian, was part of a perfect divine timetable. The address “O Moses” at the end ties the entire life story together, affirming that his entire journey was never random but orchestrated for this sacred moment. For the Prophet Muhammad, this is a profound comfort: his own life’s struggles—orphanhood, poverty, persecution—were all a qadar leading to his meeting with Allah.

The verse concludes the flashback with an overwhelming sense of divine planning and loving care, setting the stage for Moses to go forward without fear.
