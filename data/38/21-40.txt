=21=

After showing how David was granted wisdom and sound judgment, the Quran now presents a powerful story that illustrates the testing of that very gift. The verse asks, "Has the story of the two plaintiffs reached you?" This rhetorical question draws the Prophet’s attention and by extension ours to a narrative packed with lessons. The two plaintiffs are described as having "scaled the wall of David’s sanctuary. " The word for sanctuary is miḥrāb, which in David’s context refers to his private chamber of worship, a fortified place where he would seclude himself for devotion. The fact that they climbed over the wall rather than entering through the proper door signals that something unusual and divinely orchestrated was taking place. They were not ordinary litigants; many scholars identify them as angels sent in human form to test David. Their sudden, startling entrance through an unexpected route was the first indication to David that this case carried a deeper purpose. The verse sets the stage for a divine trial, a test not of David’s legal knowledge in a simple sense but of his inner impartiality, his humility, and his swiftness in turning back to Allah when he realised the lesson.

The mention of the story being directed to the Prophet also consoles him, showing that previous prophets underwent trials of judgment and character, yet they emerged beloved to Allah through sincere repentance.

=22=

The verse describes David’s immediate reaction: he was startled, even frightened, by their sudden appearance in his private chamber. His alarm is natural—they had bypassed all guards and barriers, and their manner was intense. They immediately sought to calm him, saying, "Have no fear." Then they explained themselves: "We are merely two in a dispute. " This reassurance lowered the tension while simultaneously framing what was to follow as a judicial matter. Their request is detailed and instructive: "So judge between us with truth—do not go beyond it—and guide us to the right way." They are asking for a verdict based solely on truth, without any deviation or injustice.

The phrase "do not go beyond it" means do not exceed the bounds of fairness even slightly. And "guide us to the right way" means lead us to what is just and upright. This elaborate plea placed David in the full responsibility of his role as a judge. It also subtly foreshadowed the lesson he was about to learn. Even a prophet-judge must remain vigilant against any factor that could cloud pure justice, including the natural human tendency to sympathise with one party before hearing both sides fully. The entire exchange is set up so that David would give a ruling that, while legally correct on the surface, would carry a deeper personal lesson for him.

=23=

One of the two plaintiffs steps forward to present his case. He points to his companion and says, "This is my brother.

" The word "brother" here means a partner or fellow in faith or community, not necessarily a blood sibling, though it evokes the closeness that should preclude oppression. He then lays out the stark inequality: "He has ninety-nine sheep while I have only one. " The imagery is simple and powerful. The one with overwhelming resources is not satisfied; he demands the single sheep owned by the poorer brother.

The phrase "Still he asked me to give it up to him" reveals the greed and lack of contentment. The richer brother’s demand is backed not by need or right but by sheer force of rhetoric: "overwhelming me with his argument. " He used his powerful speech to overpower the weaker party, leaving him with no effective reply. The case appears straightforward: a clear act of injustice by a strong partner against a vulnerable one. David’s instinct as a just ruler would immediately be stirred.

Yet the entire allegory is laden with spiritual meaning. David himself had many wives, and his test would involve him being reminded that even a powerful person can fall into the trap of desiring what belongs to another, or of acting without full impartiality. The story is building to that profound moment of self-realisation.

=24=

David delivered his verdict quickly, based on the apparent facts: "He has definitely wronged you in demanding to add your sheep to his." The ruling was correct given the undisputed account. He then added a general, wise observation: "And certainly many partners wrong each other, except those who believe and do good—but how few are they!

" This statement acknowledged that in business and personal partnerships, injustice is common; only the righteous who are guided by faith treat their partners justly, and they are lamentably few. The moment he pronounced this judgment, the deeper reality struck him. The verse says, "Then David realized that We had tested him." He understood that the two plaintiffs were angels sent by Allah, and that the parable of the ninety-nine sheep and the one sheep was a mirror held up to his own situation. He had been tested in his capacity as a ruler and as a man, and he recognised that he had been swift to judge without perhaps seeking the full picture, or that a subtle desire had crept in. His response was immediate and profound: he asked for his Lord’s forgiveness, fell down in prostration, and turned to Him in repentance. The prostration here is so significant that it became a sunnah, a prostration of recitation for anyone who reads this verse. David’s total humility before Allah is the model for every believer: the recognition of one’s human limitations and the urgent, wholehearted return to Allah.

=25=

The verse brings relief and elevation. Allah says, "So We forgave that for him." The "that" refers to whatever lapse or hastiness in judgment occurred during the incident. The swift and complete forgiveness demonstrates Allah’s mercy towards His beloved servant who repented sincerely.

The verse then immediately transitions to reward: "And he will indeed have a status of closeness to Us and an honourable destination." The phrase "closeness to Us" translates zulfā, an intimate nearness that is a spiritual station of the highest order. Despite the test and the human moment, David’s rank was not diminished; through his repentance, he was raised to greater nearness to his Lord. And the "honourable destination" refers to the beautiful return in the Hereafter, the gardens of bliss and the highest stations of Paradise. This verse is crucial for understanding how Allah views His prophets. Their small lapses, when followed by immediate and deep repentance, become means for drawing even closer to Allah. It also teaches ordinary believers that sin is not the end of one’s spiritual journey if it is followed by genuine tawbah. The shame of the mistake is washed away by the mercy of the Most Forgiving, and the servant’s ultimate standing is determined by that return to Allah, not by the initial stumble.

=26=

After the personal trial and forgiveness, Allah addresses David directly with a set of instructions that form a charter for just leadership. "O David! We have surely made you an authority in the land.

" The term khalīfah (authority, vicegerent) is used, indicating that David was placed as a representative of Allah’s rule on earth. With this appointment comes the paramount duty: "so judge between people with truth. " This is the core function of his authority, to uphold justice based on divine revelation, not on personal whim. The warning that follows is severe: "And do not follow your desires or they will lead you astray from Allah’s Way." Desires, if not checked, distort judgment, create bias, and pull the heart away from the straight path. The lesson from the sheep parable now becomes explicit. The ultimate consequence of being led astray by desire is described with sobering clarity: "Surely those who go astray from Allah’s Way will suffer a severe punishment for neglecting the Day of Reckoning.

" The phrase "neglecting the Day of Reckoning" reveals the root cause: when a person forgets that they will stand before Allah and answer for every judgment, they become careless with truth and justice. This verse is not only for rulers but for anyone in a position of authority, including parents, teachers, and anyone who must adjudicate between people.

=27=

After the story of David and the principles of justice, the Quran returns to confronting the disbelievers’ worldview. It firmly declares, "We have not created the heavens and earth and everything in between without purpose.

" The Arabic bāṭil means falsehood, frivolity, or without any serious objective. This negates the atheistic and materialistic view that the universe is a meaningless, random accident.

The verse then specifically pinpoints the disbelievers’ assumption: "as the disbelievers think." Their behaviour—denying resurrection, rejecting accountability, living for worldly gain—stems from this false belief that creation has no ultimate purpose. Because they think there is no judgment, they act as if their desires are the only law.

The verse then delivers a stern warning: "So woe to the disbelievers because of the Fire! " The word "woe" translates wayl, an expression of severe destruction and grief. The Fire that awaits them is the logical consequence of their wilful denial of the purpose of existence. The connection to David’s instruction is subtle but deep: David was warned to judge with truth and not follow desire because he would face the Day of Reckoning. The disbelievers deny that very Day, and therefore their judgment and their entire life purpose become corrupted, leading to their ruin.

=28=

This verse poses two powerful rhetorical questions that appeal directly to human reason and an innate sense of justice. The creation of the heavens and earth with purpose logically demands that the Creator differentiate between those who fulfill that purpose and those who corrupt it.

The first question asks, "Or should We treat those who believe and do good like those who make mischief throughout the land?" The believers who couple faith with righteous deeds are the ones who align themselves with the purpose of creation. The mufsidūn, the mischief-makers, are those who spread corruption, oppress others, and cause ruin on earth. To treat both groups identically would be a manifest injustice, and Allah is the Most Just.

The second question reinforces this: "Or should We treat the righteous like the wicked?" The righteous are the mutaqūn, those who are constantly conscious of Allah and guard against evil. The wicked are the fujjār, those who burst through moral boundaries without restraint. The structure of the verse implies that it is impossible for divine justice to equate these opposites.

Therefore, there must be a Day of Reckoning where each group receives its due recompense. This verse is a rational proof for the Afterlife. Without it, the moral order of the universe would be incomplete, and the suffering of the innocent and the tyranny of the wicked would remain ultimately unaddressed.

=29=

Having established that the universe is purposeful and that justice requires a final judgment, Allah now explains how this guidance has been given. The Quran is described as "a blessed Book.

" The word mubārak means full of enduring, ever-increasing goodness, spiritual benefit, and divine grace. It is a Book that transforms lives, heals hearts, and provides the criteria for justice that David was commanded to uphold. The primary purpose of its revelation is stated clearly: "so that they may contemplate its verses." Contemplation, or tadabbur, is not a quick, superficial reading. It means to deeply reflect upon the meanings of the verses, to consider their implications, and to allow them to penetrate the heart.

The verse then specifies the intended audience of this contemplation: "and people of reason may be mindful. " The ulū al-albāb are those who possess sound, penetrating intellects. They are the ones who do not let desires cloud their thinking. For them, the Quran is the ultimate source of remembrance and guidance. This verse directly refutes the notion that the Quran is to be recited without understanding. It mandates a thoughtful engagement with the text, connecting reason and revelation. The disbelievers’ problem is not a lack of intelligence but a failure to use their intellects to contemplate the Book that has been sent to them.

=30=

The narrative now shifts seamlessly from David to his son and successor, Solomon. "And We blessed David with Solomon" indicates that Solomon was a tremendous gift from Allah to David, a son who would inherit not just the kingdom but also prophethood and wisdom. The verse then praises Solomon in the highest terms: "what an excellent servant he was!

" The Arabic niʿma al-ʿabd is a divine commendation that carries immense weight. Solomon was a king of unparalleled power and wealth, yet his defining characteristic was his excellence as a servant of Allah. Then the verse adds the very same quality highlighted in David: "Indeed, he constantly turned to Allah.

" The word awwāb, the one repeatedly turning back to God in devotion and repentance, links father and son in the deepest spiritual bond. Despite Solomon’s vast dominion over humans, jinn, animals, and even the wind, his heart remained attached only to his Lord. This introduction sets up the stories of Solomon’s trials and blessings that follow. It also reassures the Prophet Muhammad that the legacy of prophethood is a chain of devoted servants, each tested and each honoured, and that true greatness lies in the quality of servanthood, not in worldly possessions.

=31=

The Quran now transports us to a specific event in Solomon’s life. The command "Remember" calls the Prophet and the believers to reflect on this moment. "When the well-trained, swift horses were paraded before him in the evening.

" The Arabic describes these horses as aṣ-ṣāfināt al-jiyād. Ṣāfināt refers to horses that stand poised on three legs, touching the ground lightly with the tip of the fourth hoof, a stance of disciplined, noble steeds ready for action. Al-Jiyād means swift, excellent racing horses of the finest breed. These were horses prepared for jihad, for striving in the path of Allah. The time mentioned, "in the evening," is al-ʿashīy, the latter part of the day, a time of beauty and reflection. Solomon, as a king and prophet, was reviewing these magnificent creatures. The parade was not a frivolous display; it was a review of strength prepared for the cause of truth.

However, even lawful love and admiration for such blessings can become a test. The detail that this occurred in the evening, which then transitioned towards night, becomes significant for the incident that follows, where his immersion in observing these horses may have distracted him from a particular act of remembrance at its appointed time. The stage is set for a lesson in prioritising the love of Allah above all attachments.

=32=

Solomon’s reaction upon seeing the magnificent horses is recorded in his own words: "I am truly in love with these fine things out of remembrance for Allah." The phrase "I am in love with" translates aḥbabtu ḥubba, expressing a deep affection. But he immediately qualifies that this love is "out of remembrance for Allah," or more precisely, "because of the remembrance of my Lord." This can be understood in two interconnected ways. One is that his love for these horses stemmed from his awareness that they were a blessing from Allah and a means to carry out His commands, especially jihad.

Thus his love was not independent of Allah but rooted in the remembrance of Him. The other understanding is that as he was admiring them, he was continuously remembering Allah in his heart, so his delight was permeated with the consciousness of the Giver.

The verse then notes, "until they went out of sight," meaning he continued watching them as they raced or were led away, disappearing behind the horizon. He spent so much time in this state of delighted observation that the sun set. Some reports indicate that this caused him to miss the time of the afternoon prayer, ʿAṣr, though he did not wilfully neglect it. This becomes the subtle test: even a permissible, God-conscious love, when it causes a delay in a specific obligatory devotion, requires a corrective response from the servant.

=33=

When the horses had vanished from sight, Solomon realised the passage of time and the distraction they had caused. He immediately gave the command, "Bring them back to me!" They were returned to him. Then he began to "rub down their legs and necks. " The Arabic word translated as "rub down" is masḥan, which can mean to pass his hand over them or to strike them with his sword. The classical commentators offer two distinct interpretations, both deeply instructive.

The first is that he stroked their legs and necks out of affection, honouring them for their role in Allah’s cause, and perhaps dedicating them to the service of God by marking them as waqf (endowed) for jihad. The second interpretation is that he used his sword to cut their legs and necks, sacrificing them. Why would a prophet of God do such a thing? Because these horses, which he loved for the sake of Allah, had inadvertently become a veil that distracted him from the remembrance of Allah at its appointed time. By sacrificing them, he demonstrated in the most dramatic way that nothing—no worldly possession, however valuable and however legitimately loved—could be allowed to compete with his devotion to his Lord. Both interpretations highlight his immediate, uncompromising return to Allah. The action, whatever it was, represented his awwāb nature: a swift turning back, removing the attachment that had momentarily occupied his heart over God’s remembrance.

=34=

The Quran now references another trial in Solomon’s life, one that is mentioned briefly and enigmatically. "And indeed, We tested Solomon, placing a deformed body on his throne." The phrase "deformed body" translates jasad. There is a well-known tradition, transmitted from some of the early scholars, about the cause of this test. It is reported that Solomon once swore that all his wives would conceive and bear him a son who would fight in the path of Allah, but he did not say "if Allah wills. " Out of all his wives, only one conceived and she gave birth to a deformed, lifeless body that was placed on his throne. This was a lesson to him about the absolute dependence of all outcomes on the will of Allah, and the importance of always coupling one’s intention with the recognition of divine decree. Other interpretations exist, but the core lesson remains the same: even the mightiest king is completely powerless without Allah’s will.

The verse continues, "then he turned to Allah in repentance. " Solomon immediately recognised the trial for what it was and returned to his Lord in humility. This swift repentance, mirroring his father David’s response, is the mark of the true servant. The test did not break him; it purified him.

The verse teaches that trials for the prophets and the righteous are often precisely in those areas where they are strongest, to remind them that all strength is from Allah alone.

=35=

In the wake of the trial, Solomon turns to Allah with a deeply personal and profound supplication. "My Lord! Forgive me." He begins by seeking forgiveness, acknowledging any shortcoming that may have necessitated the test. Then he makes a unique request: "and grant me an authority that will never be matched by anyone after me. " The word for authority is mulk, meaning a kingdom or dominion. Solomon asked for an exceptional, unprecedented kingdom that would be a sign of his prophethood and a means to manifest Allah’s glory. Importantly, he did not ask for this out of greed or a desire for personal aggrandizement. He asked for it as a divine gift that would remain unmatched, so that its miraculous nature would be clear to all, and so that no one after him would be able to claim an equal status. His request was specifically for something that would not be given to anyone else, which shows that it was for a higher purpose of demonstrating Allah’s power. He ended the supplication with the beautiful words, "You are indeed the Giver of all bounties." He acknowledged that the ultimate source of all gifts is Allah alone, the Wahhāb, the One who gives freely and abundantly without measure. Allah accepted this prayer, and what follows in the next verses is a glimpse of that extraordinary authority.

=36=

The answer to Solomon’s prayer begins to unfold. The first gift mentioned is the subjection of the wind. "So We subjected to him the wind." This was not an ordinary breeze but a wind under his direct command. It blew "gently," meaning softly and obediently, according to his will.

The Arabic rukhāʾan indicates a wind that is easy, pleasant, and not stormy or destructive. It would carry his massive flying carpet or vessel, along with his entourage, to any destination he desired.

The phrase "at his command to wherever he pleased" highlights the precision and completeness of his control. The wind would take him exactly where he wanted, without deviation. Imagine the scene: a prophet-king travelling through the skies with the wind as his subservient mount. This was a manifest miracle that surpassed all ordinary modes of travel. It demonstrated that the One who sent the wind is the One who can make it a servant to His chosen servant. This gift also had a practical purpose: it allowed Solomon to travel great distances to spread the message, to oversee his vast kingdom, and to witness the marvels of Allah’s creation without the hardships of ordinary travel. It was a mercy and a means to facilitate his duties.

=37=

The subjection of the jinn was another extraordinary aspect of Solomon’s dominion. The verse specifies two categories of jinn who served him: builders and divers. The builders constructed whatever he required—temples, palaces, and great structures like the Temple in Jerusalem. Their architectural skills were beyond human capacity, enabling the construction of monumental works that stood as signs of the power Allah had granted His prophet. The divers would plunge into the depths of the seas to retrieve pearls, corals, and other treasures. This pairing is striking: the builders conquered the heights of the earth with their structures, and the divers conquered the depths of the oceans. Together, they represented a comprehensive mastery over the physical world.

Yet all of this was not through Solomon’s own power; it was Allah who made the jinn subservient to him. The Quran elsewhere mentions that the jinn worked for him as he wished, and that if any of them deviated, they would be punished with a blazing fire. This demonstrates that even the rebellious jinn could be controlled by a prophet who ruled by Allah’s command. The lesson is that true authority is a gift from Allah, not a product of human effort alone. Solomon’s kingdom was a sign pointing to the limitless power of the Creator.

=38=

The verse continues with another category of subjection: "and others bound together in chains." This refers to the most rebellious and dangerous devils among the jinn. They were not merely subjugated to work; they were physically restrained in chains and shackles to prevent them from spreading their mischief and evil among people.

This detail reveals that Solomon’s authority extended even to incarcerating malevolent forces that could otherwise wreak havoc. The chains were a manifestation of divine justice executed through His prophet. It was a purification of the land from satanic influence during his reign. For the ordinary believer, this serves as a powerful reminder that Allah can restrain the forces of evil as He wills, and that in the end, all rebellious powers are under His absolute control. The chains also symbolise the binding of Satan and his hosts that will occur in the Hereafter, giving a foretaste of that ultimate victory of good over evil. Solomon’s earthly kingdom thus becomes a miniature model of the complete divine order that will be established on the Day of Judgment.

=39=

After enumerating these immense blessings, Allah speaks directly to Solomon, granting him something even more precious: complete freedom and divine trust. "This is Our gift." The use of the word "gift" (ʿaṭāʾ) indicates that all of this was a pure, unearned bestowal from Allah’s generosity. The instruction that follows is astonishing in its scope: "so give or withhold as you wish, never to be called to account." Solomon was given full discretion over this vast kingdom. He could bestow from its riches upon whomever he pleased, or he could withhold. He was not required to render a strict accounting to Allah for how he distributed this specific gift, because Allah knew that Solomon’s heart was so purified and his wisdom so complete that whatever he decided would be just and in accordance with divine pleasure.

This is a unique honour, a testament to Solomon’s elevated station. It also carries a profound lesson: when a servant reaches the highest level of servanthood, where his will is completely aligned with Allah’s will, he is granted a kind of spiritual liberty. His choices naturally manifest what is pleasing to Allah, so no external accountability is needed for those matters.

This is not a licence for laxity but a recognition of spiritual perfection.

=40=

The section on Solomon concludes with the very same beautiful promise that was given to his father David. "And he will indeed have a status of closeness to Us and an honourable destination." The word zulfā (closeness) reappears, establishing that despite his unprecedented worldly kingdom, Solomon’s ultimate reward is nearness to Allah.

This is the true measure of success, not the wind, the jinn, or the treasures. The "honourable destination" is the bliss of Paradise, a place of beauty and honour that far surpasses any earthly dominion. The verse ties father and son together in their final station, showing that both, after their respective tests and their sincere turning to Allah, attained the highest ranks. It serves as a deeply comforting close to the stories of David and Solomon for the Prophet Muhammad, and for every believer. No matter how great the trial or how magnificent the blessing, the ultimate goal is the same: to be brought near to Allah and to enter the honourable abode.

This promise then stands in stark contrast to the fate of the disbelievers described earlier, who have no such destination awaiting them.
