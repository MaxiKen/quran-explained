=81=

Allah’s reply to Iblīs concludes with a precise time boundary: “until the appointed Day.” This clarifies that the delay granted to Satan is not an open-ended reprieve but a limited term fixed in Allah’s knowledge. The “appointed Day” refers to the moment decreed for the end of Iblīs’s respite. Most scholars of tafsir explain that this is not the Day of Resurrection itself but the time of the first blowing of the Trumpet, when all living beings on earth and in the heavens will swoon and die. Iblīs had asked to be delayed until the Day of their resurrection, but Allah granted him only until that earlier appointed moment, after which he too will taste death and be raised for judgment. This limit is profoundly significant. The entire period of human history, from the descent of Adam to the earth until the blowing of the Trumpet, is the arena of the satanic test, but it is bounded. Iblīs does not have unlimited time to carry out his schemes; there is a fixed terminus known only to Allah. This provides immense comfort to the believer. The enemy, despite his apparent freedom, is on a divine leash. His time is running out with every passing moment.

The phrase “appointed Day” also links back to the surah’s central theme of the Day of Reckoning. The entire cosmos is moving inexorably towards that appointed moment. Just as the earlier verses warned the disbelievers that they are awaiting nothing but a single Blast, so Iblīs is awaiting his appointed end. The delay granted to him is not an act of divine approval but a calculated period that serves a higher purpose: to test humanity and to distinguish the sincere from the hypocrite. The appointed Day will arrive without delay, and on that day, all the arrogant plans of both human and jinn devils will crumble. The verse subtly reminds every listener that their own appointed term of life is also fixed, and the temptation to delay repentance or to follow satanic whispers must be resisted while the door remains open.

=82=

Upon receiving the respite, Iblīs does not express gratitude or show any sign of relenting. Instead, he swears a mighty oath to intensify his war against humanity. “Satan said, ‘By Your Glory! I will certainly mislead them all.’” The oath is staggering in its audacity. He swears by the Glory of Allah, the very attribute of divine majesty and perfection that he is defying. This shows that even in his rebellion, Iblīs cannot deny Allah’s absolute glory; he exploits the sacredness of an oath by Allah to give weight to his evil vow. Swearing by Allah’s Glory is the most serious oath one can take, and Iblīs uses it to pledge his complete dedication to misguidance.

The word “certainly” (la) adds an emphasis of determination. His declared mission is to “mislead them all,” meaning to divert every descendant of Adam from the straight path. He does not aim to mislead only a few; his goal is total destruction.

The phrase “mislead them” translates aghwiyannahum, which implies causing them to fall into error, to stray from the right course, and to be ruined. The method of misleading is described elsewhere in the Quran: he will come to them from before, behind, right, and left, using every avenue of temptation.

However, it is crucial to understand the limits of his power. He can only whisper, beautify falsehood, and invite to evil; he cannot compel anyone. The Quran explicitly says, “There is no authority for him over those who believe and rely upon their Lord.” His oath is a declaration of intent, but it does not grant him control over hearts that sincerely seek refuge in Allah. The verse paints a picture of an enemy utterly consumed by malice, whose very being has become defined by his mission to drag others into his own ruin. The believer is warned never to underestimate this enemy, yet also reminded that his power is no match for sincere faith.

=83=

Even as Iblīs swears his universal campaign of misguidance, he is forced to acknowledge a group that will remain beyond his reach. “Except Your chosen servants among them.

” The word “chosen” translates mukhlaṣīn, which can be read as “those who are purely devoted to You” (mukhliṣīn) or “those whom You have purified and selected” (mukhlaṣīn). Both meanings are intertwined and profound. The chosen servants are those whom Allah has safeguarded, purified from shirk and major sins, and granted sincerity of worship. They are the ones who truly embody the declaration that there is no god but Allah. Iblīs admits that he cannot overcome them. He knows that his whispers cannot penetrate a heart filled with sincere devotion and constant reliance on Allah. This admission is a backhanded testimony to the power of ikhlāṣ, sincerity. The very enemy of humanity confesses that he is helpless against those who belong completely to Allah. In another surah, Iblīs says, “I will sit in ambush for them on Your straight path… then I will come to them from before them, from behind them, from their right and from their left, and You will find most of them ungrateful.” Here, however, he excepts the chosen ones, acknowledging that his ambush will fail against them. This teaches that the fortress against Satan is not intellectual brilliance or worldly power, but a heart purified for Allah alone. The chosen servants are described elsewhere as those who remember Allah often, who pray with humility, who spend from their wealth, and who fear the Day of Judgment. They are the muttaqūn, the righteous, whose honourable destination was described earlier in the surah. Iblīs’s exception is not a concession of mercy on his part; it is a statement of fact forced out of him by the reality of Allah’s protection.

For the believer, this verse is an invitation to strive to be among those chosen, protected servants by purifying their faith and turning wholly to Allah.

=84=

The narrative now reaches its climax with the final, decisive word from the Lord of all worlds. “Allah concluded, ‘The truth is—and I only say the truth—:’.

” The Arabic word qāla (He said) indicates a definitive pronouncement. What follows is not a negotiation or a conditional statement; it is the unalterable divine decree that settles the matter once and for all. Before delivering the content of the decree, Allah inserts a powerful parenthetical statement: “and I only say the truth.” This serves as an absolute affirmation of the veracity and certainty of what is about to be declared. Allah does not need to take an oath by His glory as Iblīs did; He simply states that His word is truth itself. This phrase establishes that everything that follows is irrevocable, bound by divine truthfulness, and will certainly come to pass. It also serves as a contrast to Iblīs’s earlier oath. Iblīs swore by Allah’s glory to mislead, but his oath, however emphatic, is the word of a creature. Allah’s word is the ultimate reality that cannot be altered or prevented.

The repetition of “truth” (ḥaqq) anchors the entire conflict in the divine perspective. The cosmic drama of Iblīs’s rebellion, the test of humanity, and the final judgment all flow from the truth and return to it.

This verse also reassures the Prophet and the believers that despite the apparent strength of falsehood and the success of satanic whispers in misleading many, the ultimate outcome is determined by the truth of Allah’s decree. The truth will prevail, and falsehood will vanish. The stage is now set for the pronouncement of the final judgment, which leaves no ambiguity about the fate of the arrogant and the misguided.

=85=

The divine decree is delivered with terrifying clarity and certainty. “I will surely fill up Hell with you and whoever follows you from among them, all together.

” The word “surely” (la-amlaʾanna) is an emphatic oath, indicating that this is a sworn, unbreakable decree. Hell is depicted as a receptacle that will be filled to capacity.

The first occupants are Iblīs himself and his direct progeny among the jinn. Then the decree extends to “whoever follows you from among them,” meaning those human beings who choose to follow Satan’s path of rebellion, arrogance, and rejection of the truth.

The phrase “all together” (ajmaʿīn) emphasizes that none of the followers will escape; they will be gathered in Hell en masse, just as the angels prostrated all together to Adam. This is the symmetrical justice of the divine decree: those who follow the one who refused to prostrate will share his punishment. The verse is a direct fulfilment of the warning given throughout the surah to the disbelieving Quraysh and to all who tread the path of arrogance. It also answers the earlier rhetorical question: “Or should We treat those who believe and do good like those who make mischief throughout the land?

” The answer is a resounding no. The followers of Satan will fill Hell, and the righteous will fill Paradise. The imagery of “filling” Hell implies that it is vast and that many will enter it. This should cause every heart to tremble and to examine its allegiances. Whose path am I following? The path of the prophets and the righteous, or the path of Iblīs and his followers? The verse does not leave room for neutrality; one either follows divine guidance or is at risk of being swept into the satanic camp.

Yet, it also contains a hidden mercy: the decree is against those who follow Iblīs. The door of repentance remains open for anyone who abandons that following and turns back to Allah. The filling of Hell is a consequence of human choice, not an arbitrary fate, and Allah’s truthfulness ensures that both the promise of Paradise and the threat of Hell are real and certain.

=86=

With the narrative of Iblīs complete, the surah returns to the Prophet’s mission, addressing the specific accusations of the Quraysh. “Say, O Prophet, ‘I do not ask you for any reward for this Quran.’” The Prophet is instructed to publicly declare that he seeks no material compensation, no wealth, no status, and no power in return for delivering the message. This distinguishes him from the false claimants and the worldly leaders. He is not an opportunist exploiting religion for personal gain. His life of poverty, simplicity, and hardship was the living proof of this claim. The disbelievers had accused him of seeking power, but his actions consistently demonstrated the opposite.

The phrase “for this Quran” emphasizes that the revelation itself is a priceless gift that cannot be exchanged for any worldly consideration. Then comes the second part of the disavowal: “nor do I pretend to be someone I am not. ” The Arabic wa mā ana min al-mutakallifīn means he is not one of those who put on airs, who fabricate claims, or who artificially assume a role that does not belong to them. He does not pretend to be an angel, a lord, or a possessor of the treasures of Allah. He does not claim knowledge of the unseen except what is revealed. He is exactly what he declares himself to be: a human messenger, a warner, a servant of Allah. This verse is a powerful lesson in the purity of prophetic character. The messenger of truth has nothing to hide and no hidden agenda. His message is free, and his identity is transparent. This transparency stands in stark opposition to the devious nature of Iblīs, who disguised his arrogance behind a veneer of worship. The believer is called to emulate this sincerity and simplicity, avoiding the burden of pretension and the exploitation of religion for worldly ends.

=87=

The Prophet’s disavowal of reward and pretension is followed by a concise, majestic statement of the Quran’s universal purpose. “It is only a reminder to the whole world.

” The word “reminder” (dhikr) links back to the very opening of the surah, where Allah swore by the Quran full of reminders. The entire Book, with its stories of past prophets, its descriptions of Paradise and Hell, and its account of Iblīs, is a comprehensive reminder of what every soul needs to know: its origin, its purpose, its enemy, its duties, and its final return.

The phrase “to the whole world” translates lil-ʿālamīn, meaning all of humankind and jinn, across all times and places. The message is not confined to the Arabs, or to a particular era, or to a specific class of people. It is universal and eternal. This universal scope answers the Quraysh’s narrow, tribal thinking, where they wondered why the revelation was sent to one of them rather than to a great man from their two cities. The Quran is a reminder to the whole world, and the Prophet’s being from the Arabs is an honour for them, not a reason for rejection.

The word “only” (in huwa illā) restricts the essential nature of the Quran to being a reminder. It is not a book of science, though it contains verses that inspire scientific reflection; it is not a history book, though it contains true history; it is fundamentally a dhikr, a remembrance, an admonition, a wake-up call for the sleeping hearts. The response required from the world is to heed the reminder, to remember what is already inscribed in the primordial human nature, and to align one’s life with that truth. The verse elevates the status of the Quran and simultaneously humbles the Prophet, for he is merely the conveyor of this universal reminder. It is a gift of mercy from the Lord of all worlds to His creation, and the only sensible response is gratitude, acceptance, and a life of remembrance.

=88=

The surah concludes with a powerful, ominous promise that serves as both a warning and a prediction. “And you will certainly know its truth before long.” The “you” here is addressed to the disbelievers who reject the Quran and mock the Prophet. The “its truth” refers to the truthfulness of the Quran’s message, its warnings, and its descriptions of the afterlife.

The phrase “before long” translates baʿda ḥīn, meaning after a period of time, not in the distant future but soon. This prophecy was fulfilled in multiple ways. In the short term, many of the Quraysh witnessed the truth of the Quran when they saw the defeat at Badr and other events that proved the Prophet’s words true. Some of them later embraced Islam and acknowledged that they had been wrong. In the longer term, the truth of the Quran became manifest through the spread of Islam, the conquest of Makkah, and the ultimate triumph of the message. And beyond all worldly events, the full truth will be known on the Day of Resurrection, when every soul will stand before Allah and see with absolute clarity what it had denied. The verse is not merely a threat but an invitation. It tells the disbelievers that their ignorance is temporary, and they have an opportunity to recognise the truth now, while faith can still benefit them. If they wait until the appointed time, the knowing will come, but it will be too late. The surah thus ends as it began, with an emphasis on the Quran as a reminder. It opened with the oath by the Quran full of reminders and the accusation that the disbelievers are in arrogance and opposition. It closes with the assurance that they will come to know the truth of that very reminder. The arc is complete: the message has been delivered, the stories of the prophets have been recounted, the fate of the righteous and the wicked has been detailed, the origin of satanic enmity has been exposed, and the Prophet has been vindicated. What remains is for each soul to choose its path before the time of knowing arrives and the window of repentance closes.
