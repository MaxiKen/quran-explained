=41=

The Quran now moves from the majesty of Solomon’s kingdom to the profound trial of Job, a shift that carries a deep message. The command “And remember Our servant Job” uses the same honoured title, “Our servant,” linking Job to David and Solomon in the chain of beloved worshippers. Job is presented as a model of patience. He cried out to his Lord, but note the nature of his complaint: “Satan has afflicted me with distress and suffering. ” He did not curse his fate or question Allah’s wisdom; he directed his grievance towards Satan, recognizing that the affliction, though decreed by Allah as a test, was unleashed through the enemy. The words “distress” (nusb) and “suffering” (ʿadhāb) together paint a picture of comprehensive hardship—exhaustion of body and soul, loss of wealth, the death of his children, and a prolonged, repulsive disease that made people shun him.

Yet Job’s patience was legendary. Historical traditions tell us that he endured for years without a single word of discontent, and when his wife, in a moment of weakness, suggested he pray for relief, he reminded her that they had enjoyed blessings for far longer and that it was only right to bear trials with gratitude. His cry here is not an objection but a humble, suppressed plea, directed to the only One who could lift the harm. Satan’s role is clearly stated: he cannot afflict without Allah’s permission, and his goal is to drive the servant to despair. Job’s prayer is a masterclass in how to complain to Allah: acknowledge the enemy’s role, but place all hope solely in the Lord’s mercy. This teaches that even prophets are tested, and the greatest test is reserved for the most beloved, to elevate their rank.

The verse also subtly refutes the idea that material loss or sickness is a sign of divine displeasure; rather, it can be the very means of drawing close to Allah.

=42=

The divine response to Job’s patient cry was immediate and miraculous. Allah commanded him: “Stomp your foot.” The earth, which had become a place of isolation and sickness for him, would now become a source of healing. As he struck the ground, a spring burst forth. This was not just any water; it was “a cool and refreshing spring.

” The Arabic bārid (cool) and sharāb (drinking) combine two essential qualities: the water was perfectly cool to soothe his fevered skin, and it was pure and sweet for drinking, restoring his dehydrated body. The command to use it “for washing and drinking” indicates a complete cure: external washing to cleanse the disease that covered his body, and internal drinking to rejuvenate his organs. This moment captures the transformative power of divine mercy. After years of affliction, the remedy was instant, effortless, and located exactly beneath his feet, showing that relief had always been closer than he imagined, withheld only for a wisdom-filled period. The instruction to stomp also implies an active role; Job had to make a movement, however small, to receive the cure, teaching that while healing is from Allah, the servant must engage with the means provided. The coolness of the water is especially significant, as many scholars note that Job’s affliction involved severe, burning inflammation. The cure through water also prefigures the rivers of Paradise that will wash away all pain. The entire scene is one of profound divine tenderness: the patient servant was not forgotten, and at the appointed moment, the relief came in a way that was both physically restorative and spiritually uplifting, a tangible sign of Allah’s care.

=43=

The restoration did not stop at physical healing; it extended to all that Job had lost. “And We gave him back his family, twice as many.” Job had lost his children during the trial, and now Allah blessed him with even more, either by reviving those who had died or by granting him new offspring. Some reports say that the original children were brought back to life and that additional children were born, so that his family literally doubled. This doubling is a pattern of divine generosity: Job’s patience was rewarded with increase, not just compensation.

The verse then explains the purpose behind this restoration: “as a mercy from Us and a lesson for people of reason.” The mercy is directly from Allah, emphasising that it was an unearned gift born of compassion. The lesson is for those with sound intellects, the ulū al-albāb, who reflect on the story and understand its implications. They learn that patience during trials never goes unrewarded, that relief is guaranteed after hardship, and that Allah’s mercy encompasses even the most severely tested. The story of Job becomes a living parable for every believer enduring sickness, poverty, or grief. It fortifies the heart with the conviction that if one remains steadfast without complaint against Allah, the end will be more beautiful than the beginning.

The mention of “twice as many” also connects to the earlier mention of David and Solomon’s gifts, showing that divine generosity knows no bounds when it is met with grateful patience.

=44=

This verse addresses a specific incident during Job’s trial. He had sworn an oath to strike his wife with a certain number of lashes if he recovered, likely because in a moment of extreme weakness she had suggested something inappropriate or had complained about their condition. When Allah restored him, Job was caught between his oath and his love and appreciation for his wife, who had faithfully served him throughout the illness. Allah, in His wisdom and mercy, provided a solution that upheld the oath without causing harm: “Take in your hand a bundle of grass, and strike your wife with it, and do not break your oath. ” A bundle of thin stalks or blades of grass, when struck all at once, counts as a single stroke yet fulfills the letter of an oath for multiple lashes without causing pain. This became an established principle in Islamic law for certain kinds of oaths, demonstrating that Allah desires ease and does not want to burden His servants with impossible situations.

The verse then gives Job a magnificent commendation: “We truly found him patient. What an excellent servant he was! Indeed, he constantly turned to Allah.” The patience was not passive; it was active and constant turning to Allah in every state.

The term awwāb again appears, linking Job to David and Solomon, and showing that the core of all these great servants was their perpetual return to their Lord. Job’s patience is so exemplary that his name became proverbial, and the Prophet Muhammad would often invoke his story to comfort those in distress.

=45=

After the detailed story of Job, the Quran briefly mentions other towering figures to reinforce the theme of remembering Allah’s chosen servants. “And remember Our servants: Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.” The three are mentioned together because they form the patriarchal line of the Israelite prophets. They are described as “the men of strength and insight. ” The Arabic ulī al-aydī wa al-abṣār literally means “possessors of hands and eyes.” “Hands” symbolise strength, capability, and powerful action in carrying out Allah’s commands and spreading the truth. Abraham’s strength was evident in his smashing of the idols and his readiness to sacrifice his son. Isaac and Jacob inherited this strength in upholding the covenant and guiding their families. “Eyes” refers to deep spiritual insight, the ability to see truth clearly amidst falsehood, and to perceive the realities of the unseen. They were not men of brute force alone; their actions were guided by profound wisdom and vision. This combination is the ideal of prophetic leadership: a balance of decisive action and deep contemplation.

The verse reminds the Prophet that these were “Our servants,” a title that elevates their servanthood above all worldly statuses. They were not kings in the conventional sense of the disbelievers’ thinking; they were servants of the Most High, and that was their real glory. Their example stands as a counterweight to the arrogance of the Quraysh chiefs, showing that true strength lies in submission to Allah.

=46=

This verse reveals the unique characteristic for which these great prophets were specially chosen. “We truly chose them for the honour of proclaiming the Hereafter.

” The Arabic akhlaṣnāhum bi-khāliṣatin dhikrā ad-dār means that Allah purified and selected them with an exclusive quality: the constant remembrance of the final abode. Their entire mission was centred on reminding people of the Hereafter, the permanent home that awaits every soul.

This is the “honour” (khāliṣah) that distinguished them. Unlike worldly leaders who focus on accumulating power and riches, these prophets’ hearts were so immersed in the reality of the afterlife that it shaped every word they spoke and every action they took. They lived in this world but their souls were already anchored in the next.

The phrase “proclaiming the Hereafter” also implies that they made the Hereafter the core of their call, warning of its judgment and giving glad tidings of its bliss. This choice by Allah answers the disbelievers who mocked the idea of resurrection; the very prophets they claimed to respect were sent precisely to affirm that truth. For the believers, it is a reminder to orient their lives around the remembrance of the final home, making it the driving force behind their deeds. The Prophet Muhammad is now being shown that he shares this same honoured role with the patriarchs, and his focus on the Hereafter places him in their noble lineage.

=47=

The verse adds another layer of praise, affirming the permanent status of these prophets. “And in Our sight they are truly among the chosen and the finest.

” The phrase “in Our sight” is profoundly intimate; it means that their rank is not determined by human opinion but by Allah’s direct knowledge and pleasure. They are “among the chosen” (al-muṣṭafayn), meaning Allah selected them from among all humanity for the highest mission, and they are “the finest” (al-akhyār), indicating that they are the best in character, conduct, and spiritual purity.

The word akhyār implies a superabundance of goodness, those who are inherently good and whose deeds are excellent. This commendation comes after describing their strength and their focus on the Hereafter, showing that these qualities are what make a person truly chosen and excellent in Allah’s eyes. It demolishes the false standards of the Quraysh, who measured excellence by lineage, wealth, and tribal power. Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob did not seek worldly prestige; they sought Allah’s approval, and that is what made them the finest. The verse serves as a powerful validation of the Prophet Muhammad’s mission, for he followed in their footsteps, and it encourages believers to aspire to be among Allah’s chosen and finest by adopting the same characteristics: strength in faith, deep insight, and a life oriented around the Hereafter.

=48=

The list of honoured prophets continues with three more names. “Also remember Ishmael, Elisha, and Dhul-Kifl.” Ishmael is the firstborn son of Abraham, the ancestor of the Arabs, and the one who helped his father build the Kaaba. His inclusion here ties the Arabian Prophet directly to this blessed lineage. Elisha (al-Yasaʿ) was a prophet sent to the Children of Israel, a successor to Elijah, known for his miracles and steadfastness. Dhul-Kifl is mentioned only twice in the Quran, and scholars offer various identifications, often suggesting he was a righteous prophet or a saintly man known for his commitment to fulfilling a great responsibility.

The name literally means “the one of the fold” or “possessor of a double portion,” and he is praised for his patience and righteousness. The verse concludes, “All are among the best. ” The simple but powerful phrase emphasises that these prophets were not just good; they were among the very best of creation. By commanding the remembrance of these figures, the Quran provides the Prophet with a spiritual support system. In times of rejection and difficulty, recalling the lives of these towering servants—their trials, their patience, their unwavering faith—fills the heart with strength and perspective. The diversity of their stories, from the patriarchs to a figure like Dhul-Kifl, shows that the path of righteousness is walked by many, each with unique tests, but all sharing the common destination of Allah’s pleasure.

=49=

The narrative now reaches a transitional point. “This is all a reminder.

” The phrase encompasses everything that has been mentioned so far: the stories of the prophets, the proofs of monotheism, the warnings to the disbelievers, and the descriptions of blessings and trials. All of it serves as a powerful reminder for those who wish to be reminded. Then the focus shifts to the ultimate outcome, contrasting the reward of the righteous with the punishment of the wicked, which forms the remainder of the surah. “And the righteous will certainly have an honourable destination.” The righteous, the muttaqūn, are those who lived in constant consciousness of Allah, guarding themselves against sin and fulfilling their duties. Their destination is described as “honourable” (ḥusna maʾāb), a beautiful and excellent place of return.

The word maʾāb implies a place one returns to again and again, a permanent home. It is honourable because it is a place of dignity, peace, and divine pleasure, a stark contrast to the humiliation and disgrace awaiting the transgressors. This verse sets the stage for the detailed descriptions of Paradise that follow. It assures the believers that no matter how difficult the journey in this world becomes, the final return is one of honour, prepared by the Most Generous for those who remained true.

=50=

The honourable destination is now named: “the Gardens of Eternity,” ʿAdn, denoting permanence and perpetual residence. Unlike the transient dwellings of the world, these gardens will never wither, and their inhabitants will never leave.

The first striking image of this welcome is that “whose gates will be open for them.” In the world, even the most magnificent palaces have guarded gates that open only for select visitors after scrutiny. But for the righteous, the gates of Paradise are already wide open, ready to receive them with honour. The angels will greet them with peace, and there will be no barriers, no waiting, no fear of rejection. The openness of the gates symbolises complete acceptance and the removal of all obstacles between them and their Lord’s mercy. It also reflects a state of eternal security; the gates never close because there is no danger, no night, and no need for protection.

The image evokes a feeling of joyous arrival: the long journey of faith through a world of trials ends with an entrance that is effortless and welcoming. This is a direct answer to the earlier verses about the disbelievers who arrogantly rejected the call. For them, the gates of Hell will be slammed shut; for the righteous, the gates of Paradise will be flung open, never to close.

=51=

The scene inside the Gardens is painted with a few masterful strokes. “There they will recline.” Reclining is the posture of complete ease, security, and contentment, the opposite of the toil and hardship of earthly life. They will be reclining on sumptuous couches, surrounded by every comfort. From this state of perfect rest, they will “call for abundant fruit and drink.

” The word “calling” (yadʿūn) suggests that they merely need to express a wish or indicate a desire, and it will be immediately fulfilled by servants or brought to them without delay. The “abundant fruit” means fruits of every variety, plentiful and never out of season, without any effort of cultivation or harvest. The “drink” refers to pure, delightful beverages that cause no intoxication or harm. The entire image is one of royal hospitality provided by the Lord of all worlds. There is no cooking, no cleaning, no labour; every desire is met with supernatural ease.

This description is a powerful motivation for the believers enduring scarcity and persecution in Makkah. It tells them that their patience will be rewarded with a life where longing does not exist, because everything one could ever want is available at a word. The contrast with the boiling water and pus that the wicked will taste, mentioned later, could not be sharper. The righteous call for delights; the wicked are forced to swallow torment.

=52=

Among the blessings of Paradise, the Quran mentions the companions of its inhabitants. “And with them will be maidens of modest gaze and equal age.

” The phrase “maidens of modest gaze” translates ḥūr ʿīn: ḥūr refers to those with intensely white eyes and deep black pupils, a mark of extraordinary beauty, and ʿīn describes wide, beautiful eyes. But their physical beauty is paired with a moral quality: “modest gaze” (qaṣirāt aṭ-ṭarf), meaning they gaze only at their spouses with love and fidelity, never looking at anyone else with desire. There is a purity and exclusivity in this relationship that surpasses anything known on earth. “Equal age” (atrāb) means they are all of the same youthful, vibrant age, enjoying one another’s company in perfect harmony. These maidens are not mere objects of desire; they are pure, loving companions created for an eternal, faithful union.

This description countered the pagan Arabs’ notions of sensuality by elevating it to a spiritual, purified form within the context of everlasting joy. It also serves as a comfort for the believing men and women who maintained chastity and fidelity in the world, promising them a reward that is free from the jealousy, betrayal, and imperfection of earthly relationships.

The mention of “with them” indicates that these companions are part of the overall atmosphere of delight, not the sole focus. The entire setting is one of complete, wholesome felicity where every relationship is a source of peace and joy.

=53=

After detailing the blessings, the Quran directly addresses the believers, anchoring these descriptions in certainty. “This is what you are promised for the Day of Reckoning.

” The phrase “Day of Reckoning” (Yawm al-Ḥisāb) is a weighty reminder that all of this is not a fantasy but a guaranteed outcome of a real event. There will be a day when every soul will be called to account, deeds weighed, and judgment pronounced. It is on that day, after the reckoning, that the righteous will enter into this described bliss. The promise is Allah’s word, and Allah never breaks His promise. This verse connects the beautiful imagery back to the surah’s earlier theme: the disbelievers denied the Day of Reckoning and mocked it, but the believers accept it and orient their lives around it. The promise serves as a source of immense hope and patience. When the world inflicts pain or when one must give up immediate pleasures for the sake of Allah, remembering that this is the promised reward for the Day of Reckoning makes every sacrifice worthwhile. It is a transaction where the fleeting is exchanged for the eternal.

The verse also implies a subtle warning: if this is the promise for the Day of Reckoning, what will be the reality of that day for those who denied it? The answer follows immediately in the description of the wicked’s fate.

=54=

The final seal on the description of Paradise is a declaration of its eternal nature. “This is indeed Our provision that will never end.

” The word “provision” (rizq) is deeply significant. In this world, provision is always temporary; the best meal is digested, the most beautiful garden withers, and every joy fades. Human anxiety is often driven by the fear that provision will run out. But Allah states that this provision in Paradise is His provision, and it will never end. It will never diminish, never spoil, never be withdrawn. There is no depletion, no boredom, no limit.

The Arabic mā lahu min nafād means it has absolutely no exhaustion or depletion. This is the ultimate security: not only is the punishment avoided, but the reward is a perpetual, uninterrupted flow of blessings from the Lord of infinite bounty. This verse directly answers the disbelievers’ earlier arrogant question about who possesses the treasuries of mercy.

The answer is Allah, and He opens those treasuries without measure for His righteous servants. It also links back to Solomon’s story, where he was told, “This is Our gift, so give or withhold as you wish.” In Paradise, the gift is even greater: a provision that is eternally available and never withdrawn. The verse brings the description of the righteous’ destination to a close, setting it as an unshakeable hope for every believer.

=55=

A sharp and deliberate contrast is drawn. “That is that.

” The phrase summarizes the preceding description of the reward as settled and decisive. Then the attention turns to the other group: “And the transgressors will certainly have the worst destination. ” The transgressors are the ṭāghūn, those who exceed all bounds in rebellion, arrogance, and sin. They are the opposite of the righteous. Their destination is described as “the worst” (sharra maʾāb), a vile and dreadful place of return.

The use of maʾāb again, paralleling the honourable destination, underscores the symmetry of justice: the righteous return to honour, the wicked return to disgrace. The verse creates a stark cliff in the mind of the listener, preparing for the terrifying description that follows. It mirrors the earlier verse that posed the rhetorical question: should the righteous be treated like the wicked?

The answer is now given in vivid detail. This structure—the beautiful reward first, then the horrifying punishment—is typical of the Quranic method of instilling hope and fear in balanced measure. The believer’s heart should oscillate between longing for Paradise and fear of Hell, both motivating righteous action.

The word “certainly” (la) in the Arabic adds an emphasis of inevitability: just as the righteous will surely enter their bliss, the transgressors will surely enter their doom.

=56=

The worst destination is now explicitly named: “Hell,” Jahannam. The description that follows is brutally direct: “where they will burn.” The verb yaṣlawnahā means they will be thrown into it, roasted in its flames, enduring its intense heat. There is no metaphorical softening; it is a literal, excruciating experience. The fire will envelop them from all sides.

The verse then adds an exclamation of profound disgust and warning: “What an evil place to rest!” The word “rest” translates mihād, which can mean a bed, a resting place, or a cradle. In worldly terms, a person’s bed is a place of comfort and security. But in Hell, the bed will be fire, and the blankets will be fire. The irony is sharp: the transgressors sought their rest and comfort in sin, and now their eternal resting place is the very torment they mocked. The exclamation is not an external comment but a divine verdict meant to pierce the heart of the listener. It echoes the earlier “woe” to the disbelievers. The verse removes any ambiguity: Hell is not a temporary rehabilitation centre; it is an evil resting place, a permanent abode of agony for those who rejected faith and persisted in wrongdoing.

This is the reality that the Prophet was sent to warn against, and the weight of that warning hangs over every verse.

=57=

The punishment is not limited to burning; it includes the vile things they will be forced to consume. “Let them then taste this: boiling water and oozing pus.” The command “taste” is laced with a tone of just recompense; they are being made to experience the fruits of their deeds.

The first item is ḥamīm, boiling water that has reached an extreme temperature. When brought near their faces, it scalds them; when they attempt to drink it, it tears their insides apart. It is the ultimate antithesis of the cool, refreshing spring given to Job.

The second item is ghassāq, translated as “oozing pus.” It refers to the foul discharge that flows from the bodies of the inhabitants of Hell, a mixture of blood, pus, and melted skin. It is described elsewhere as intensely cold or having a nauseating stench, adding layers of agony.

This is what will be offered as drink and food to the transgressors. The contrast with the abundant fruit and pure drink of Paradise is unimaginable. In the world, they indulged in luxurious food and drink, never thanking the Provider; in Hell, their provision is this torment. The verb “taste” indicates a direct, personal experience. They will not merely see it; it will be forced upon them. This verse serves as a severe deterrent, making the listener recoil and flee to the mercy of Allah, seeking the provision that never ends instead.

=58=

The verse intensifies the horror by stating that these two terrible things are not the only ones. “And other torments of the same sort!

” The Arabic means that they will face punishments paired with them, of similar severity and in various forms. Hell contains multiple types of agony: chains, shackles, fire, boiling water, pus, thorny food, bitter fruit, and a scorching wind. All of these are “of the same sort,” meaning they are equally punishing and equally loathsome.

The pairing creates a sense of being overwhelmed; relief never comes because if one torment subsides, another of the same kind takes its place. There is no moment of respite.

The phrase also implies that every sin has a fitting punishment, and the variety of torments corresponds to the variety of their transgressions. This is a manifestation of perfect justice. The verse does not list all the torments, leaving the imagination to grasp that the reality is far worse than words can convey. The believer, upon hearing this, is filled with a sense of urgency to avoid anything that could lead to such a fate. The surah has moved from the patience of Job to the ultimate consequences, driving home that the trials of this world, if borne with faith, lead to eternal ease, while the pleasures of sin lead to eternal suffering.

=59=

The scene now shifts to the interactions among the damned, exposing their mutual hatred. The misleaders are the chiefs and the arrogant leaders who called people to disbelief. When they see their followers being driven into Hell alongside them, they exclaim, “Here is a crowd of followers being thrown in with us.

” The phrase “being thrown in” highlights the violent, humiliating manner of their entry. Instead of showing any remnant of comradeship, the misleaders immediately declare, “They are not welcome, for they too will burn in the Fire.” In the world, these leaders loved the adulation and blind following of the masses, but now that following brings no benefit; it only increases the crowd in the Fire. Their statement that the followers are “not welcome” is a curse of mutual repulsion. There is no honour among the wicked in the Hereafter. The shared suffering does not create solidarity; it creates enmity.

This is a piercing lesson for those who follow others blindly in matters of faith. On the Day of Judgment, the false leaders will disown their followers, and the followers will disown the leaders. The bonds of misguidance will turn into instruments of torment. The verse starkly fulfills the earlier warning that the disbelievers are an enemy force bound for defeat; their unity in sin becomes their joint destruction, with no comfort in numbers.

=60=

The followers, far from accepting the insult, reply with their own curse. “No! You are not welcome!” They return the same expression of rejection, negating any greeting of peace. Then they add the reason for their curse: “You brought this upon us.” They recognize that the misleaders were the primary cause of their doom because they beautified disbelief, misrepresented the truth, and blocked the path of guidance.

This is a moment of painful clarity: all the excuses vanish, and the reality of responsibility is laid bare. The followers admit that they were misled, but their admission does not absolve them; it simply increases their anguish. They end with the same type of exclamation used earlier: “What an evil place for settlement!

” The word qarār, translated as “settlement,” implies a permanent dwelling. Hell is not just a passing punishment; it is their final, fixed abode of misery. The dispute between the two groups will continue, each blaming the other, but the torment will not be lightened.

This verse teaches the critical importance of personal accountability in faith. One cannot rely on one’s family, leaders, or society to bear the burden of misguidance. Each soul must seek the truth for itself, and those who refuse to use their reason, as the surah repeatedly urges, will find no allies in the Fire, only enemies sharing the same fate.
