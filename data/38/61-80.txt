=61=

After the followers have cursed their misleaders and declared them unwelcome, they turn their faces upward with a desperate prayer. They cry out, “Our Lord! Whoever brought this upon us, double their punishment in the Fire.

” The phrase “brought this upon us” refers to the one who first initiated the call to misguidance, the original architect of the disbelief and rebellion that led them all into Hell. The followers recognise that while they themselves were complicit, there is a primary culprit who beautified the sin and led them step by step into destruction. Their request for a double punishment is both an expression of their intense hatred and a profound Quranic principle: the one who leads others astray bears the sin of his own misguidance and a share of the sins of those who followed him, without diminishing the followers’ own burdens in any way. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, said, “Whoever calls to guidance will have a reward similar to the rewards of those who follow him, without that diminishing their rewards in any way. And whoever calls to misguidance will have a burden of sin similar to the burdens of those who follow him, without that diminishing their burdens in any way. ” This verse is the living enactment of that hadith. The followers, now fully aware that their leaders deceived them, do not ask for their own punishment to be lightened; they seek vengeance against the cause of their ruin.

Yet this prayer will not avail them, for each soul is recompensed for what it earned, and the double portion is already decreed for those who combined their own disbelief with the active misleading of others. The verse shows that the social bonds of sin in this world become the chains of mutual enmity in the Hereafter. The friends who encouraged each other to mock the prophets and to deny the resurrection will become the bitterest of enemies, each wishing worse torment upon the other. The lesson for the living is stark: be careful whom you follow, and be even more careful about the influence you exert on others, for leadership in sin is a direct path to multiplied agony.

=62=

The scene deepens as the disbelieving leaders, the tyrants who considered themselves the elite, begin to look around the Fire. They notice something that disturbs them greatly: the absence of certain people. They ask one another, “But why do we not see those we considered to be lowly?

” The word “lowly” translates the Arabic arādhil, a term of contempt that the Quraysh chiefs frequently used for the poor and socially marginalized believers like Bilāl, ʿAmmār, Khabbāb, and Suhayb. In Makkah, the elite regarded themselves as the people of substance and honour, while they dismissed the early Muslims as the weak, the slaves, and the insignificant. They would ask the Prophet to drive these believers away if he wanted the nobles to sit with him. Now, in the blazing Fire, they instinctively expect to see those same lowly believers suffering alongside them. Their arrogance was so deeply ingrained that they assumed the poor believers could not possibly have a better fate. The fact that they do not see them creates a moment of jarring confusion. Some scholars explain that the tyrants are scanning the pits of Hell, searching for the believers among the tortured masses, and when they fail to locate them, a horrifying possibility creeps into their minds. Could it be that those whom they ridiculed are actually in Paradise, enjoying the very delights they themselves were promised if only they had believed? This verse is a divine reversal of worldly standards. The ones the world looked down upon are raised high, and the ones who strutted with pride are plunged low. The absence of the believers from the Fire is a silent but thunderous testimony to the truth of the message that the weak accepted and the strong rejected. It confirms the earlier principle that Allah does not treat the righteous like the wicked.

=63=

The tyrants grapple with two possible explanations for the missing believers. Their first speculation is, “Were we wrong in mocking them in the world?

” This is a glimmer of self-awareness, a crack in their wall of arrogance. They begin to suspect that their entire evaluation was inverted; the people they ridiculed as worthless were in fact the treasured servants of Allah. Their mockery, which they had wielded as a weapon of social exclusion, now returns to haunt them as a source of deep regret.

The word “wrong” could also imply that they were deluded or astray in their assessment. This is a profound Quranic insight: mockery of the righteous is not just a social sin but an intellectual error, a failure to perceive true value.

The second speculation reveals how hard it is for arrogance to fully die, even in Hell. They ask, “Or do our eyes just fail to see them in the Fire?” Perhaps, they think, the believers are somewhere in the deeper, darker pits, and the smoke and the flames obscure their vision. This assumption is a desperate attempt to preserve their old worldview, to avoid the shattering conclusion that the lowly ones are actually in bliss. The fact that both possibilities are presented shows the psychological torment of the damned: they oscillate between a dawning recognition of their error and a stubborn refusal to accept the full truth. It is a state of cognitive dissonance that magnifies their suffering.

The verse teaches that the consequences of worldly arrogance extend into the next life, where the truth becomes undeniable but can no longer benefit the one who denied it. The mockers will learn, beyond all doubt, that the scales of divine judgment bear no resemblance to the social hierarchies of the world.

=64=

Having portrayed the back-and-forth of curses, blame, and bewildered searching among the people of Hell, the Quran now seals this scene with a powerful statement of certainty. “This dispute between the residents of the Fire will certainly come to pass.

” The Arabic emphasizes the word “dispute” (takhaṣum), which implies a vehement, back-and-forth argument. It is not a quiet conversation but a heated altercation in which each party tries to shift blame, express hatred, and seek some form of twisted satisfaction through the condemnation of others.

The phrase “will certainly come to pass” is absolute; there is no doubt or metaphor here. It is a factual event as real as the Fire itself. This verse bridges the detailed descriptions of the afterlife with the overall objective of the surah: to serve as a reminder. The Prophet is being told to convey this scene with conviction, because it is as true as the world they walk upon.

For the disbelievers who mock the idea of a Day of Reckoning, this verse is a direct refutation. Their denial does not make the event any less real. For the believers, it is both a warning and a comfort: a warning against following the arrogant, and a comfort that the mockery they endured from the tyrants will be answered with the perfect justice of that Day.

The verse also prepares the listener for the shift that follows, where the Prophet is instructed to reassert the core of his message in the face of all the denial, anchoring the entire discussion in the worship of the One True God.

=65=

After the graphic portrayal of the afterlife, the Prophet is commanded to declare his mission with absolute clarity. “Say, O Prophet, ‘I am only a warner.’” The word “only” restricts his role and demolishes any false accusations. He is not a magician, a poet, a power-seeker, or someone who fabricates revelation. He is strictly a warner, a nadhīr, sent to alert people to a danger that is approaching and to give them the opportunity to prepare. This echoes the earlier verse where the disbelievers were astonished that a warner came from among themselves. Now the Prophet reaffirms that this is precisely what he is, nothing more and nothing less. Immediately following this self-definition, he delivers the core content of his warning: “And there is no god worthy of worship except Allah—the One, the Supreme.” The declaration of tawḥīd is the foundation upon which the entire message stands. Allah is described with two majestic names: al-Wāḥid, the One, unique and indivisible in His essence, attributes, and right to be worshipped; and al-Qahhār, the Supreme, the Subduer, the One who overpowers all and to whom everything submits.

The pairing is significant. The One God is not a remote, indifferent deity; He is the Supreme Dominant who will hold all creation to account. This directly counters the Meccan idolaters who associated partners with Allah and yet felt secure from His punishment. It also links back to the stories of David, Solomon, Job, and the other prophets, who all called to the worship of this One Supreme God. The verse is a reset, bringing the focus back to the central truth after the terrifying depictions of the Fire, leaving the listener with a clear choice: heed the warner and submit to the One Supreme God, or continue in arrogance and face the consequences.

=66=

The declaration of Allah’s oneness and supremacy is immediately grounded in His absolute ownership and power. “He is the Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between.

” The word “Lord” (Rabb) encompasses the Creator, Owner, Sustainer, and Master. Everything that exists, from the highest heavens to the deepest earth and all that lies in the vast spaces in between, belongs to Him, is sustained by Him, and operates under His command. This echoes the earlier rhetorical challenge posed to the disbelievers: “Or is the kingdom of the heavens and the earth and everything in between theirs?

” The answer is now emphatically given: it belongs to Allah alone. Since He is the Lord of all, He has the sole right to be worshipped and to issue commands. Then the verse couples two more names that balance fear and hope: “the Almighty, Most Forgiving. ” Al-ʿAzīz is the Almighty, the One whose might cannot be resisted, who enforces His will with irresistible power. This name should strike awe into the heart of the rebel, for no one can escape His grasp. But immediately next to it is al-Ghaffār, the Most Forgiving, the One who repeatedly and abundantly forgives the sins of those who turn to Him in repentance. This pairing is a profound mercy. The same Lord who will crush the arrogant tyrants in the Fire is the Lord who eagerly forgives the repentant sinner. No one should despair of His mercy, but no one should take His might lightly. The verse provides the complete framework for a relationship with Allah: a recognition of His absolute ownership, a fear of His irresistible power, and a hope in His boundless forgiveness. The warner’s message is not one of doom alone; it is an invitation to seek the forgiveness of the Almighty before the Day when His might will be manifested in judgment.

=67=

The Prophet is commanded to make a third declaration, this time about the nature of the revelation itself. “Say, ‘This Quran is momentous news.’” The word translated as “momentous” is ʿaẓīm, meaning great, tremendous, weighty. The Quran is described as nabaʾ ʿaẓīm, a piece of news of immense significance. What is this news? It is the news of the resurrection, the judgment, Paradise and Hell, the ultimate triumph of truth and the crushing defeat of falsehood. It is the news that the entire course of human history is moving towards a single, decisive Day of Reckoning. This news is not trivial gossip or ancient legend; it is the most important information a human being can ever receive, because it determines one’s eternal fate. The description of the Quran as “news” is also striking. It is not a work of philosophy or a collection of abstract ideas; it is a report about reality—about Allah, the unseen, the past nations, and the coming future. The Prophet is merely the bearer of this news; his duty is to deliver it faithfully. This framing responds directly to the disbelievers who accused him of fabricating the Quran. How can he fabricate news of such magnitude, details of the unseen that no human could know, and scenes of the afterlife that are described with such precision and consistency?

The word “momentous” also hints at the Quran’s effect: it is a heavy, serious message that shakes the conscience and demands a response. No one can truly hear this news and remain indifferent. One either accepts it with awe and preparation, or one turns away in arrogant dismissal, as the next verse describes.

=68=

The tragedy is that this momentous news, which should command the full attention of every human soul, is being met with deliberate neglect. “From which you pagans are turning away.

” The Arabic verb muʿriḍūn implies a persistent, active avoidance. They are not merely ignorant; the news has reached them through the Prophet’s clear recitation, yet they turn their faces, change the subject, and warn one another not to listen. The surah earlier recorded their leaders going forth and saying, “Carry on, and stand firm in devotion to your gods.” That is the very embodiment of turning away. This turning away is not a passive state; it requires a conscious effort to suppress the natural inclination of the heart to respond to the truth. The pagans busied themselves with distractions, mockery, and arguments precisely to avoid confronting the weight of the Quranic message. The verse contains an implicit warning: turning away from momentous news does not make it disappear. A person who receives a dire warning and ignores it does not neutralize the danger; they merely walk into it unprepared. The pagans’ turning away is a self-inflicted harm.

The verse also offers a subtle psychological insight: the very act of describing the Quran as “momentous news” while observing that they turn away exposes the irrationality of their behaviour. Why would anyone turn away from news of supreme importance about their own eternal destiny unless they were blinded by arrogance or enslaved by desires?

The question hangs in the air, inviting every listener to examine their own response to the Quran.

=69=

The Prophet is instructed to add another crucial point that proves his prophethood. “I had no knowledge of the highest assembly in heaven when they differed concerning Adam.” The “highest assembly” refers to al-malaʾ al-aʿlā, the exalted gathering of the angels. The “differing” concerning Adam refers to the discussion that took place among the angels when Allah announced His intention to place a vicegerent on earth. The angels, knowing the potential of humans to cause bloodshed and corruption, questioned this decision in a spirit of seeking understanding, not objection. Then there was the subsequent command for them to prostrate to Adam, and the refusal of Iblīs. This entire narrative is part of the unseen, events that took place before human history in the heavenly realm. The Prophet Muhammad was an unlettered man living in Makkah, with no access to the scriptures of the Jews and Christians, and no human teacher who could have informed him of these details. By stating that he had no knowledge of this before revelation, he is presenting a clear proof that the Quran is from Allah. The only way he could narrate this story with such accuracy and authority is through divine revelation. This verse prepares the ground for the detailed account of Adam and Iblīs that follows. It is a direct challenge to anyone who doubts the source of the Quran: if the Prophet had no natural means of knowing this, and yet he recites it, the only remaining explanation is that the Knower of the unseen has informed him.

The verse also links the story to the overall theme of the surah: the origin of human enmity with Satan, which explains the root of the arrogance and rejection the Prophet is currently facing.

=70=

The verse concludes the introductory statements before the narrative of Adam. “What is revealed to me is that I am only sent with a clear warning.” The Prophet reiterates that the entirety of the revelation he receives is oriented around a single mission: to serve as a clear warner.

The phrase “clear warning” translates nadhīr mubīn, a warning that leaves no ambiguity, backed by evident proofs and plain language. The knowledge of the highest assembly, the story of the angels and Adam, and all the other unseen news are not given to him for his personal prestige or to satisfy curiosity. They are all part of the warning, designed to awaken the heart and illuminate the reality of the human condition.

The mention of “only” again strips away any pretension: he is not claiming to be an angel, a lord, or a possessor of Allah’s treasuries; he is a man who received a message and must deliver it. This humility is the hallmark of true prophethood.

The verse also connects back to the very beginning of the surah, where the pagans were astonished that a warner came from among them. The Prophet is simply fulfilling that divinely assigned role. The “clear warning” encompasses everything that has come before in the surah: the destruction of past nations, the account of the prophets, the description of Paradise and Hell, and now the story of the primal act of disobedience. All of it serves as a mirror in which every human being can see the consequences of their choices. The stage is now set for the dramatic account of the first act of arrogance against Allah’s command.

=71=

The Quran now transports us back to the moment before human history began, instructing the Prophet to remember and convey this foundational event. “Remember, O Prophet, when your Lord said to the angels.” The address “your Lord” is deeply personal to the Prophet, reminding him of his close connection to the source of this revelation. The audience is the angels, the noble beings of light who ceaselessly obey Allah and surround His throne. The announcement is momentous: “I am going to create a human being from clay.” The original word for “human being” is bashar, which refers to the physical, mortal creature made of tangible material.

The mention of “clay” grounds this new creation in the earth. Unlike the angels who were created from light and the jinn from smokeless fire, the human being’s origin is humble, from the dust and mud of the ground.

Yet this very humility would become the vessel for something extraordinary: a soul breathed directly from Allah, and the capacity for knowledge, free will, and moral choice. The announcement is a declaration of intent, not a consultation. Allah informs the angels of what He will do, and the angels, in another surah, respond with a question born of their desire to understand: “Will You place therein one who will spread corruption and shed blood, while we glorify You with Your praise and sanctify You?” Their question shows their awareness of the potential dark side of a creature with free will. This verse, however, focuses solely on the divine decree to create, highlighting that the creation of humanity is a deliberate, purposeful act of the Almighty. The human being is not an accident, but a chosen creation with a specific role in the cosmic order.

=72=

The creation process is described in two distinct stages. First, Allah says, “So when I have fashioned him.” This refers to the physical formation of Adam’s body from clay, shaped into perfect human form by the direct power of Allah.

The word “fashioned” (sawwaytu) implies a balanced, proportionate, and complete creation, free of any defect. The second stage is the infusion of life: “and had a spirit of My Own creation breathed into him.” The “spirit” is the rūḥ, the soul, the divine breath that transformed the clay figure into a living, conscious being.

The phrase “of My Own creation” indicates that this spirit is a special creation attributed directly to Allah as an honour, though it is not a part of Allah Himself; it is a created entity that belongs to Him in a unique way, being the vehicle of consciousness, intellect, and moral capacity. This breathing of the spirit is the moment of the human being’s distinction. The clay body connects humanity to the earth, but the spirit connects them to the divine command. It is this combination of humble origin and lofty soul that defines the human condition. Then comes the command to the angels: “fall down in prostration to him.” This prostration was not an act of worship, for worship is due only to Allah. It was a prostration of greeting, honour, and acknowledgment of Adam’s superiority in knowledge and his role as vicegerent. The entire heavenly assembly was commanded to bow before this new creation, a profound sign of the honour Allah bestowed upon humanity. This moment represents the first test of obedience in relation to the human being, and it would separate the faithful angels from the arrogant Iblīs.

=73=

The response of the angels was immediate, unanimous, and complete. “So the angels prostrated all together.” The verse emphasises that there was no hesitation, no partial obedience, and no delay.

The word “all together” translates ajmaʿūn, meaning every single one of them, as a unified collective, carried out the command. This is the nature of the angels: they are created to obey. They do not question the wisdom of Allah’s commands once His will is made clear. Their prostration was a magnificent display of humility before the divine decree, even though Adam was made of clay and they were made of light. They did not look at the material origin and deem themselves superior; they looked at the Creator’s command and submitted. This unanimous prostration sets the stage for the shock of the one refusal that follows. It creates a backdrop of perfect obedience against which the rebellion of Iblīs becomes starkly visible. The angels’ action also signified their acceptance of the human being’s elevated status in Allah’s plan, despite their earlier questions about potential corruption. They trusted that Allah’s wisdom in creating Adam was perfect. This verse implicitly teaches the believers the proper response to Allah’s commands: swift, complete, and free from the arrogance that would compare one’s own perceived status to what Allah has ordered. The prostration of the angels is also a prefiguration of the honour that the righteous descendants of Adam will receive in Paradise, where they will be served and honoured by angels.

=74=

In the midst of that unified prostration, one being stood apart. “But not Iblīs.” The exception is stark and solitary. Iblīs, also known as Satan, was a jinn who had, through his intense devotion and worship, been elevated to the company of the angels. He was not an angel by nature—the Quran clarifies elsewhere that he was of the jinn—but he had reached a rank where he was addressed alongside them. When the command came, his inner reality was exposed. The verse describes his crime in two stages: “who acted arrogantly, becoming unfaithful.

” The word “arrogantly” (istakbara) indicates that he considered himself too great to bow before Adam. This was the first sin in creation: pride. He looked at Adam’s origin of clay and compared it to his own origin of fire, and in his heart, a sense of superiority took root. This arrogance then led directly to “becoming unfaithful.

” The Arabic kāna min al-kāfirīn means he became one of those who cover up and reject the truth. Iblīs had knowledge of Allah’s command, yet he willfully disobeyed it. His act was not a slip or a mistake; it was a conscious, prideful rejection that defined his new identity. He moved from being a devout worshipper to being the embodiment of rebellion. This transformation is a timeless warning. Arrogance is the direct path to kufr, disbelief. No matter how much a person has worshipped, if they allow pride to enter their heart and refuse to submit to a clear command of Allah, they can fall from the highest station to the lowest.

The verse also exposes the root of all the rejection faced by the Prophet: it is the same Iblīs-like arrogance that prevents the Quraysh chiefs from bowing to the truth brought by a man from among themselves. The story of Iblīs is the archetype of every subsequent act of rebellion against divine guidance.

=75=

Allah, in His knowledge, already knew the answer, but He questioned Iblīs to make the crime explicit and to establish the just basis for the punishment. The question begins with a direct address: “O Iblīs! What prevented you from prostrating to what I created with My Own Hands?

” The phrase “with My Own Hands” is immensely significant. It denotes special honour and direct attention given to Adam’s creation. While all creation comes into being by Allah’s command “Be,” Adam’s formation is uniquely attributed to Allah’s hands, signifying care, nobility, and a personal involvement that elevates Adam above other creatures. By refusing to prostrate to a being that Allah Himself had so honoured, Iblīs was directly insulting the divine choice.

The question then exposes the inner state of Iblīs with two piercing options: “Did you just become proud? Or have you always been arrogant?

” The first option, “Did you just become proud?” implies a new, emerging arrogance that led to this specific act. The second, “Or have you always been arrogant?” suggests that pride was a deep-seated, permanent trait that was now being manifested. In either case, the arrogance is condemned.

The question is a form of reprimand and a revelation of the absurdity of his stance. Iblīs was not able to claim that the command was unclear or that Adam was unworthy. His refusal had no basis in justice or reason; it was purely the ugly head of pride rising up against the wisdom of the Creator.

This verse teaches that Allah examines not just the act of disobedience but its root cause in the heart. Arrogance is particularly heinous because it implies that a creature knows better than the Creator, or that the creature’s judgment of worth is more valid than Allah’s.

=76=

Iblīs’s response is the classic justification of the arrogant. He does not deny the command; he simply declares his own superiority. “I am better than he is: You created me from fire and him from clay.

” This is the first qiyās, the first act of flawed analogical reasoning in creation. Iblīs compared two elements—fire and clay—and assigned a value hierarchy to them. Fire is upward-leaping, luminous, and energetic; clay is lowly, dark, and passive. Based on this material comparison, he concluded that a creature made of fire must be superior to one made of clay. But his reasoning was corrupt on multiple levels. First, superiority is not determined by raw materials but by piety, obedience, and the honour that Allah bestows. A palace of gold can be a den of evil, while a hut of mud can house a saint. Second, he completely ignored that the clay was fashioned by Allah’s own hands and infused with His spirit, while the fire from which he was made had no such unique honour mentioned. Third, his arrogance blinded him to the fact that Allah had commanded him to prostrate; regardless of his opinion about clay and fire, the command of the Creator nullifies all such comparisons. Iblīs’s logic is the root of all racism, caste systems, and class prejudice. When a person says, “I am better than another because of my skin colour, my lineage, my wealth, or my nationality,” they are echoing the satanic reasoning. The Prophet Muhammad later explicitly demolished this mindset when he said, “O people, verily your Lord is One and your father is one. There is no superiority of an Arab over a non-Arab, nor of a non-Arab over an Arab, nor of a white over a black, nor of a black over a white, except through taqwā. ” Iblīs failed the test precisely because he valued matter over the command of Allah, and in doing so, he became the father of all such error.

=77=

The response of Iblīs sealed his fate. There was no room for further argument or opportunity for repentance. Allah commanded, “Then get out of Paradise.

” The word “get out” (ukhruj) is a decree of expulsion, a forceful ejection from the abode of honour and peace. Iblīs, who had been dwelling in the company of the angels in the celestial realm, was now cast down. Paradise, in this context, refers to the heavenly gardens above, not the Eternal Paradise of the Hereafter, but a realm of divine proximity from which he was now banished. The reason for this expulsion is given immediately: “for you are truly cursed.” The curse is laʿnah, which means being expelled and distanced from the mercy of Allah. It is not merely a word of anger; it is a state of being spiritually outcast, with no hope of redemption.

The word “truly” adds emphasis that this curse is a settled, permanent reality. The arrogance of a single moment led to an eternal loss. This verse demonstrates the decisive nature of divine judgment when the inner reality of a creature is made manifest. Iblīs had enjoyed a long period of worship, yet when his hidden pride was exposed through the test of Adam, all that past worship could not save him. It is a chilling reminder that the end of deeds matters most, and that a hidden disease of the heart, if not rooted out, can undo a lifetime of apparent piety. The expulsion of Iblīs is also the beginning of the cosmic struggle between the forces of satanic pride and the children of Adam, setting the stage for the test of human life on earth.

=78=

The curse is not a temporary state; it is extended and fixed. “And surely upon you is My condemnation until the Day of Judgment.

” The word “condemnation” translates laʿnatī, My curse. The possessive “My” adds a terrifying weight; it is not the curse of a mere creation but the curse of the Almighty Creator, the only One whose curse carries ultimate effect. This condemnation will remain upon Iblīs continuously from that moment until the Day of Judgment, the day when all beings will be resurrected for the final reckoning. This means that for the entire span of human history, Iblīs is an accursed being, operating under divine wrath.

The phrase “Day of Judgment” (Yawm ad-Dīn) ties the story directly to the central theme of the surah. Iblīs’s respite and his mission of misguidance are all contained within this timeframe. On the Day of Judgment, the curse will manifest in its fullest form as he and his followers are cast into the Fire. The verse provides a crucial piece of the cosmic timeline: the curse is operational now, and the final justice will be executed then.

For the believer, this knowledge is both a warning and a source of strength. It is a warning not to follow the accursed one, and it is a source of strength to know that the enemy who whispers in their heart is already under Allah’s curse, his ultimate defeat is certain, and his power is limited to suggestion, not compulsion. The believer fights a cursed enemy, and with Allah’s help, can overcome him.

=79=

Upon hearing his condemnation and expulsion, Iblīs did not ask for forgiveness. Instead, he made a request that revealed his new, malevolent mission. “Satan appealed, ‘My Lord! Then delay my end until the Day of their resurrection.’” The address “My Lord” shows that even in his rebellion, Iblīs acknowledges Allah’s Lordship; he is not an atheist. He knows his Creator and yet chooses defiance. His request for a delay is not a plea for more time to repent, but for more time to exact revenge. He wants his death postponed so that he can live alongside human beings and dedicate himself to their misguidance.

The phrase “the Day of their resurrection” is specific: he wants to live until that great day when all humans are raised from their graves. He knows that after that day, his own punishment will commence fully, but he wants the entire span of human earthly life to be his field of operation. This request shows the depth of his malice. Having been dishonoured because of Adam, he does not direct his complaint solely at Allah but channels his rage towards Adam’s descendants. He wants to prove, in his own twisted way, that the clay creation is unworthy of the honour, by dragging as many of them as possible into his own state of curse. The appeal is granted in part, as the next verse shows, but it is a granting that serves as a test for humanity. The very existence of Satan as a tempter is part of the divine wisdom that allows moral choice to have real weight. Without an enemy whispering towards evil, the choice for good is less manifestly a choice of faith and love for Allah.

=80=

The request is answered, but with a precise qualification. “Allah said, ‘You will be delayed.’” The word “delayed” implies that Iblīs’s life is extended and his death is postponed, but not indefinitely or on his own terms. The respite is granted within limits.

The next verse will specify “until the appointed Day,” which clarifies that he will not live until the Day of Resurrection itself, but until a specific appointed time known to Allah. Most scholars explain that Iblīs will die after the first blowing of the Trumpet, which will cause all living beings on earth and in the heavens to die, thus ending his period of testing humanity. He will then be resurrected along with everyone else to face judgment. So the delay is real, granting him the time he asked for to tempt humanity, but it does not extend to the point where he escapes the very Day of Reckoning he wished to postpone. This limited respite is a manifestation of Allah’s wisdom and a mercy to the believers. It allows the drama of human life to unfold with its full moral dimensions. Every human being will face the whispers of Iblīs, but they will also have the guidance of revelation, the innate disposition towards God, and the assistance of the angels and righteous believers. The delayed enemy becomes the means by which true faith is distinguished from hypocrisy. The verse is a quiet but powerful reassurance: Satan’s time is limited, his power is circumscribed, and the very respite he demanded will ultimately lead to his own multiplied punishment as he bears the burdens of all whom he misled. The believer can therefore face the struggle against satanic whispers with patience and confidence, knowing that the enemy’s days are numbered and his defeat is certain.
