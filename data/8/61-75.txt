=61=

After the command to prepare military strength for deterrence, the surah immediately opens the door to peace. This verse establishes a foundational principle of Islamic foreign relations. “If the enemy is inclined towards peace, make peace with them.

” The word for “inclined” suggests a genuine leaning, a sincere willingness to end hostilities. The command to “make peace” is not a reluctant concession; it is a positive directive. The believers are not allowed to reject a sincere offer of peace because they prefer the potential spoils of war or because they harbour a desire for revenge. Peace is the desired state, and whenever the enemy shows genuine readiness for it, the believers must respond in kind. The condition is that the inclination must be real, not a trick, but the outward posture of the believers is one of openness to reconciliation.

The verse then adds a crucial spiritual dimension: “And put your trust in Allah.” Making peace with a former enemy can be frightening. There is always the risk that the peace offer is a trap, that the enemy will regroup and attack later, or that the believers will be seen as weak. The command to trust in Allah addresses these fears directly. The believers are to take the step towards peace and then leave the outcome to Allah. Their trust should be in Him, not in their own military strength, and not in the sincerity of the enemy. Allah is the one who protects and who knows the hidden intentions. The verse closes with two divine names: “Indeed, He alone is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing. ” He hears all the words of peace and of secret plotting. He knows the true intentions behind the offer. The believers can proceed with confidence because the One who commands peace is the same One who is fully aware of all realities. If the enemy’s inclination is deceitful, Allah knows it and will deal with it. The believers’ duty is to accept the apparent good and rely on Allah for the unseen. This verse balances the earlier commands about firmness and fighting, showing that the goal of Islamic struggle is not perpetual warfare but a just peace that allows the truth to be practised freely.

=62=

This verse addresses the fear that accepting peace might expose the community to treachery. “But if their intention is only to deceive you” acknowledges the possibility that the enemy’s inclination towards peace is a ruse. They might offer peace to buy time, to lower the believers’ guard, or to prepare a surprise attack. The verse tells the believers what to do in such a case: the response is not to reject all peace overtures out of paranoia, but to proceed with the knowledge that “Allah is certainly sufficient for you. ” The sufficiency of Allah means that He is enough as a protector, a planner, and a defender. If the enemy intends deception, their plot cannot succeed because Allah is on the side of the sincere believers. The believers do not need to resort to treachery themselves or to live in constant suspicion that paralyses them. They can accept peace at face value and trust that Allah will handle the deception if it exists.

The verse then reminds them of the evidence for this sufficiency: “He is the One Who has supported you with His help and with the believers.” The divine help refers to the unseen aid of angels, the psychological support of serenity, and the direct intervention that brought about the victory at Badr.

The mention of “the believers” is significant. Allah’s support is channelled not only through miracles but through the community of the faithful. The love, solidarity, and mutual strength of the believing men and women are themselves a manifestation of divine support. This verse connects the theme of peace to the theme of unity. The believers should not be afraid of deception because they have Allah, and they have each other. The unity of the community, which will be celebrated in the next verse, is a fortress against the schemes of the enemy.

The verse teaches a balanced attitude: not naive, not paranoid, but anchored in a deep reliance on the One who has proven His support in the past and who will continue to be sufficient in the future.

=63=

This verse continues the thought of the previous one, explaining the great favour of unity among the believers. “He brought their hearts together” is a direct reference to the transformation that occurred in Medina. Before Islam, the Arab tribes of Aws and Khazraj had been locked in long and bloody feuds. The emigrants from Mecca and the helpers in Medina came from different backgrounds and had no prior bonds. Humanly speaking, such a group could not have become a unified, loving community.

Yet Islam achieved exactly that. The verse states it emphatically: “Had you spent all the riches in the earth, you could not have united their hearts.” No amount of money, no political treaty, no social engineering could have produced the brotherhood that Islam created. Hearts are not united by material means; they are united by a shared faith, a common purpose, and the love of Allah that He places within the believers.

The verse then declares, “But Allah has united them.” This is a pure act of divine grace. The believers are reminded that the unity they enjoy is a gift, not their own accomplishment. They should treasure it and not allow disputes over spoils, tribal pride, or personal ambitions to shatter what Allah has brought together.

The verse ends with “Indeed, He is Almighty, All-Wise.” His might means He can overcome the deepest enmities and hardest hearts. His wisdom means that the specific bonds He created among the companions were perfectly designed for the mission they were to carry. This verse directly addresses the earlier conflict over the spoils. The disagreement threatened to unravel the unity that Allah had miraculously established. The verse calls the believers to step back and recognise the preciousness of their bond, to see it as a divine favour more valuable than any war booty, and to guard it with the same awe with which they received it.

=64=

This verse is a direct and intimate address to the Prophet, bringing him personal reassurance. “O Prophet! Allah is sufficient for you.” The sufficiency of Allah for the Prophet covers every aspect of his mission. Allah is enough as a protector against the plots of his enemies, as a provider for his needs, as a comforter in times of distress, and as the ultimate guarantor of the success of his message. The Prophet had faced rejection from his own tribe, conspiracy to kill him, the loss of his beloved wife and uncle, and the immense burdens of leading a new community under constant threat. This verse tells him that he does not need to look to any other source for support. Allah alone is enough. The reassurance extends to his followers: “and for the believers who follow you.” The believers who sincerely follow the Prophet share in this divine sufficiency. Their connection to the Prophet and their adherence to his path make them the recipients of the same protection and care. This does not mean they will never face hardship, but it means that Allah will be their ultimate guardian and that no harm can befall them beyond what He permits for a wise purpose. The verse echoes the earlier promise that Allah is the protector and helper of the believers. It also serves as a seal on the section that discussed military preparation, peace, and unity. The believers are being told that regardless of the challenges—whether war or peace, unity or discord—their ultimate reliance should be on Allah, who is sufficient. This simple yet profound statement is a source of immense strength. It frees the heart from dependence on any created being and anchors it in the One who never fails.

=65=

The surah returns to the subject of battle with a command to the Prophet. “O Prophet! Motivate the believers to fight.

” The word for motivate carries the sense of urging, inciting, and stirring up the courage of the believers. The Prophet, as their leader, is to inspire them and to remove the fear and hesitation that may linger in their hearts. The encouragement is then supported by a divine promise of extraordinary odds. “If there are twenty steadfast among you, they will overcome two hundred.

” This is a ratio of one to ten. The condition is that the twenty must be steadfast, meaning they have patience, firmness, and discipline. They do not flee or waver. Under that condition, Allah promises that they will prevail against a force ten times their size. The ratio continues: “And if there are one hundred of you, they will overcome one thousand of the disbelievers. ” The reason for this incredible victory is not the superior weaponry or training of the believers; it is “for they are a people who do not comprehend.” The disbelievers lack true understanding. Their comprehension is limited to the material world. They do not grasp the reality of divine power, the purpose of life, or the strength that comes from faith. Because they are spiritually blind, their material advantage becomes useless.

This promise was a tremendous boost to the small Muslim community. It taught them that numbers are not the decisive factor in battle; faith, steadfastness, and divine support are. The verse should not be misunderstood as a mechanical formula that automatically applies regardless of the believers’ state. The condition of steadfastness is central. When believers truly embody patience and reliance on Allah, they can achieve what seems humanly impossible.

=66=

The previous verse promised victory at a ratio of one to ten, but this verse announces a merciful reduction. “Now Allah has lightened your burden, for He knows that there is weakness in you.” Allah’s knowledge of human frailty is perfect. The original command might have been too heavy for the believers to bear consistently. The psychological weight of knowing that they must fight against ten times their number could itself be a cause of anxiety and fear. Allah, in His mercy, lightened the burden and reduced the required ratio to one to two. “So if there are a hundred steadfast among you, they will overcome two hundred. And if there be one thousand, they will overcome two thousand. ” This adjustment makes the command more manageable and demonstrates that Islamic law takes human weakness into account. The addition of the phrase “by Allah’s Will” is crucial. The victory, even with the reduced ratio, is not automatic. It is dependent on Allah’s will. The believers must fight with steadfastness and then place their trust in Allah’s decree. The victory comes from Him, and He grants it according to His wisdom. The verse closes with “And Allah is with the steadfast.” The special divine companionship is the ultimate guarantee. Those who hold their ground, who remain patient under pressure, and who fulfil their duty without complaint have Allah’s presence with them. This verse is a profound example of how Islamic law balances idealism with realism. The ideal of one believer being worth ten disbelievers is spiritually true when the believers are at their peak of faith, but the law accommodates the reality of human weakness and sets a standard that is achievable. The lesson is that Allah does not intend to burden His servants beyond their capacity, and He actively adjusts His expectations based on His knowledge of their condition.

=67=

The surah now addresses a specific incident at Badr: the taking of captives and the acceptance of ransom. After the battle, the Muslims had captured seventy men from the Quraysh. The Prophet consulted his companions about what to do. Some advised executing them, while others, including Abu Bakr, suggested accepting ransom. The Prophet ultimately accepted the ransom, and the captives were released upon payment. This verse issues a divine correction of that decision, not because it was sinful, but because it was not the higher, more fitting choice for that moment. “It is not fit for a prophet that he should take captives until he has thoroughly subdued the land.” The purpose of the early battles was to establish the Islamic state and to break the military power of the Quraysh who were actively persecuting the believers and obstructing the faith. In such a stage of the struggle, the emphasis should have been on decisively weakening the enemy’s fighting force. Taking captives for ransom allows many enemy fighters to return and fight another day.

The verse clarifies that the priority of a prophet, who is establishing the truth from its foundations, should be to completely subdue the hostile forces before turning to the practice of taking prisoners for ransom. The verse then gently reproaches the believers: “You settled with the fleeting gains of this world, while Allah’s aim for you is the Hereafter. ” The believers had been attracted by the ransom money, which provided immediate material benefit. This desire, though natural, reflected a focus on temporary worldly gain. Allah’s aim was to elevate their sights to the everlasting reward of the Hereafter and to the long‑term strategic interest of the faith. The verse closes with “Allah is Almighty, All-Wise. ” His might means that He could have given them worldly gains without compromising their spiritual rank, and His wisdom means that the correction was necessary for their training and growth.

=68=

This verse intensifies the gravity of the matter. “Had it not been for a prior decree from Allah” refers to a divine writ that had already been established, a decree of mercy and forgiveness that preceded the event. Some scholars say this refers to Allah’s foreknowledge that He would later forgive this action, or to the decree that the spoils and ransoms would be made lawful for this community in contrast to previous nations. Others say it refers to the decree that the believers at Badr, due to their participation and sincerity, would be forgiven their lapses. Whatever the exact reference, the message is clear: without this prior decree, a tremendous punishment would have descended upon them.

The phrase “you would have certainly been disciplined with a tremendous punishment” conveys the seriousness of choosing the lesser option. The taking of the ransom, while not forbidden in itself, was a misstep in prioritising the material over the strategic and the spiritual. The threat of a tremendous punishment shows that even good believers can make errors that, absent Allah’s mercy, would have severe consequences. This verse serves as a stark reminder that not everything that is permissible is necessarily the best choice. The believers are called to align their values with Allah’s aims.

The passage also highlights the special status of the Badr participants. Despite this error, they were forgiven and honoured, and their story teaches later generations to constantly check their intentions and to prioritise the Hereafter over the fleeting gains of the world.

=69=

After the correction and the warning, the verse turns to mercy and relief. “Now enjoy what you have taken, for it is lawful and good.” The ransom and the spoils that the believers had taken are declared halal and tayyib—lawful and pure. Allah, having taught them the higher lesson, does not demand that they return the wealth or that they live in guilt over it. He permits them to enjoy it, to use it for themselves and their families. This demonstrates that divine discipline is not meant to break the believer but to educate and then to restore. The command “And be mindful of Allah” follows immediately. The enjoyment of the lawful gains must be accompanied by taqwa, a constant awareness of Allah. This awareness ensures that the wealth does not become a source of arrogance, greed, or forgetfulness of the divine purpose. The believers are to enjoy the provision while keeping their hearts attached to the Provider.

The verse ends with “Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” The two names embrace the entire incident. Allah’s forgiveness covers the error of preferring the ransom, and His mercy is manifest in the fact that He declared the gains lawful and allowed them to be enjoyed. The combination of correction, permission, and forgiveness is a beautiful picture of how Allah deals with His believing servants. He does not humiliate them for their mistakes; He corrects them, forgives them, and then blesses them. This provides a model for how communities should deal with one another: correct with wisdom, forgive with grace, and do not withhold lawful good because of past errors.

=70=

This verse contains a message of extraordinary mercy directed at the very enemies who had fought against the believers. The Prophet is instructed to speak to the captives, who were now in the custody of the Muslims after being defeated and captured. The message begins with a condition: “If Allah finds goodness in your hearts.” The goodness referred to is sincerity, a readiness to accept the truth, and a genuine turning towards faith. The captives included men like al‑Abbas, the Prophet’s uncle, and others who would later embrace Islam. The verse opens a door of hope for them. The promise is twofold. First, “He will give you better than what has been taken from you. ” The captives had to pay ransom to secure their freedom, which meant a loss of wealth. Some might have felt bitter about this. Allah promises that if they have goodness in their hearts and accept Islam, He will compensate them with something far better than the money they paid. This better provision includes the blessings of faith, the spiritual wealth of Paradise, and even worldly provisions that came later when they became part of the Muslim community. Second, “and forgive you.” Their past sins, including their war against the believers, would be wiped clean.

The verse ends with the names “All-Forgiving, Most Merciful,” emphasising that this promise is not a human guarantee but an extension of divine grace. The Prophet is to convey this message with gentleness. It is an invitation, not a threat. This approach aimed at winning hearts, showing that Islam’s goal is guidance, not vengeance. The captives who accepted the message later became some of the staunchest believers.

The verse teaches that even in victory, the believer’s mission is to call people to Allah with hope and mercy.

=71=

The previous verse extended a hand of hope; this verse addresses the darker possibility. “But if their intention is only to betray you O Prophet” refers to those captives who might pretend to accept the message or agree to terms while secretly planning treachery. The verse says that such an intention is not new: “they sought to betray Allah before.” Their previous betrayal was their rejection of Allah’s signs, their plots against the Prophet in Mecca, and their aggression that led to Badr. Their treachery is a pattern, not an isolated act.

The verse then reminds them of the outcome of that previous betrayal: “But He gave you power over them.” Despite their earlier schemes, Allah delivered them into the hands of the believers at Badr.

This is both a warning and an encouragement. It warns the captives that renewed betrayal will meet the same fate, and it encourages the Prophet that Allah, who granted him victory before, will do so again if they break their word.

The verse concludes with “And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.” He knows the hidden intentions of the captives, whether good or evil, and He acts with perfect wisdom. The believers are not required to read hearts; they treat the captives according to their outward behaviour while trusting Allah to handle the unseen. This verse, along with the previous one, presents the dual approach of the Islamic mission: mercy and hope for those inclined to goodness, and a firm warning for those who persist in treachery. The balance is crucial, and it is rooted in the knowledge that ultimate judgment and power belong to Allah alone.

=72=

This verse establishes a crucial social and political framework for the early Muslim community. It defines the bonds of guardianship and mutual responsibility.

The first category mentioned is “those who believed, emigrated, and strived with their wealth and lives in the cause of Allah.” These are the Muhajirun, the emigrants who left Mecca, sacrificing their homes and properties for their faith.

The second category is “those who gave them shelter and help,” the Ansar of Medina. These two groups, the verse declares, “are truly guardians of one another.

” The word for guardians implies a bond of loyalty, mutual support, and inheritance rights. They form the core of the Islamic polity, tied together by faith and the shared experience of sacrifice.

The verse then addresses a third category: “As for those who believed but did not emigrate.” These were Muslims who remained in Mecca or other places under non‑Muslim rule, unable or unwilling to join the community in Medina. The verse states, “you have no obligations to them until they emigrate.

” The word for obligations here specifically refers to the bond of guardianship and inheritance. They are still believers, and the bonds of faith remain, but the political and legal alliance that entitles one to military protection and inheritance is not established until they join the community.

However, an exception is immediately made: “But if they seek your help against persecution in faith, it is your obligation to help them.” If these non‑emigrant believers are being oppressed because of their religion and they call for aid, the Muslim community must respond. The only caveat is “except against people bound with you in a treaty. ” If there is a peace treaty with the oppressing party, the believers must honour that treaty and cannot violate it by attacking them, even to rescue their fellow believers.

The verse ends with “Allah is All-Seeing of what you do,” reminding them that all these complex obligations are under divine watch. This verse laid the foundation for the Islamic law of international relations and the obligations of a Muslim state towards Muslim minorities abroad.

=73=

This verse draws a sharp contrast between the believers and the disbelievers in terms of social solidarity. “As for the disbelievers, they are guardians of one another.” The disbelievers, despite their internal tribal conflicts, form a unified bloc when it comes to opposing the truth. They support each other, protect each other’s interests, and present a common front against Islam.

The verse then addresses the believers: “And unless you believers act likewise, there will be great oppression and corruption in the land.” The command is implicit but clear: the believers must form a parallel, unified guardianship among themselves. If they fail to do so—if they remain divided, if the emigrants and the helpers do not support each other, if they do not build a strong communal bond—then the result will be fitnah. This fitnah is both the persecution of the believers and the general corruption of society. When the righteous do not unite, the wicked dominate, and oppression spreads.

The verse teaches that the unity of the believers is not optional; it is a necessary condition for fulfilling their role as witnesses to humanity and for preventing the spread of injustice. The guardianship among the disbelievers is based on worldly interests and tribal ties. The guardianship among the believers must be based on faith and a shared commitment to divine values. This verse reinforces the earlier calls for unity and against disputation. The believers are being shown that their internal cohesion has cosmic significance. A divided Muslim community cannot stand against the organised forces of falsehood, and the consequence is widespread corruption. The historical context of this verse was the immediate aftermath of Badr, where unity was essential for survival. The principle, however, remains for all times.

=74=

This verse returns to praise the core group that was defined in verse 72, reiterating their status and their reward. “Those who believed, migrated, and struggled in the cause of Allah, and those who gave them shelter and help.” The Muhajirun and the Ansar are mentioned again, their actions carefully listed. Belief is the internal foundation; migration is the physical sacrifice of leaving one’s homeland; struggle with wealth and life is the active commitment to the cause; and providing shelter and help is the generous reception of those who sacrificed. Together, these actions form the complete picture of the early community’s dedication.

The verse then declares: “they are the true believers.” This is the same affirmation given in the opening of the surah to those who possess certain heart qualities and perform prayer and charity. The consistency is deliberate. The true believers of the opening verses, who tremble at Allah’s remembrance and establish prayer, are the same people who migrate, struggle, and support one another. Inward faith and outward sacrifice are two sides of one coin. The reward promised mirrors the reward in verse 4: “They will have forgiveness and an honourable provision.” Forgiveness covers their past sins, and the honourable provision is the generous, dignified sustenance of Paradise.

The repetition of the word “honourable” emphasises that the provision in the Hereafter is free from any shame, toil, or imperfection. This verse serves as an anchor, reminding the community that the distinctions of migration and support are not merely social or political; they are spiritual ranks recognised by Allah. The believers should view each other through this lens of divine honour, not through the lens of tribal lineage or worldly status.

The closing of the surah is approaching, and this verse prepares for the final ruling on inheritance by solidifying the bond among the true believers.

=75=

The surah concludes its legal and social teachings with a final ruling that expands the circle and then clarifies inheritance law. “And those who later believed, migrated, and struggled alongside you, they are also with you.

” This statement opens the door for future generations of believers. The early Muhajirun and Ansar are not an exclusive, closed class. Anyone who comes after them and walks the same path of faith, migration when necessary, and struggle for the cause is counted among them.

The phrase “they are also with you” is a beautiful assurance of inclusion. The later believers share in the brotherhood and the spiritual rank, even if they did not participate in Badr or the early sacrifices. This prevents the development of a spiritual aristocracy that looks down on later converts. Then the verse makes a major legal pronouncement: “But only blood relatives are now entitled to inherit from one another, as ordained by Allah.” In the early period after the migration, the bonds of brotherhood established between the Muhajirun and Ansar included mutual inheritance. An Ansari brother would inherit from his Muhajir counterpart even if they had no blood relation. This verse abrogates that arrangement and returns inheritance to the natural ties of kinship. The brotherhood of faith remains, and its spiritual and social obligations continue, but the legal right to inheritance is reserved for blood relatives.

The phrase “as ordained by Allah” emphasizes that this is a divine decree, not a social convention. The verse ends with “Surely Allah has full knowledge of everything.” He knows the bonds of blood, the bonds of faith, the needs of the community, and the wisdom behind every ruling. This closing statement wraps up the surah by affirming that the detailed commands given—from the spoils of war to the treaties, from the captives to the inheritance—all stem from a single source of all‑encompassing knowledge.
