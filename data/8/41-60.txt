=41=

This verse returns to the subject that opened the surah: the distribution of the spoils of war. The believers had initially disputed over the booty, and they were told that its division is decided by Allah and His Messenger. Now the specific divine ruling is revealed. The command “Know that whatever spoils you take” establishes that this is not a matter of personal preference. The spoils are collectively earned, but the right to dispose of them belongs to Allah.

The verse then lays down the famous ruling on khums: one-fifth of the spoils is set aside for five specified categories. “One-fifth is for Allah and the Messenger” means that this share is to be spent in the cause of Allah and under the authority of the Messenger.

The mention of Allah and the Messenger together indicates that the Prophet, as the leader of the community, would allocate this portion for the public good and the needs of the state. The next category is “his close relatives.” This refers to the relatives of the Prophet from the clans of Banu Hashim and Banu al‑Muttalib. They were given a share because they were not eligible to receive general charity, and this provision ensured their maintenance without compromising the purity of zakah funds. The remaining three categories are “orphans, the poor, and needy travellers. ” These are vulnerable groups who are to be supported from this share. The structure shows that the spoils of war are not simply a reward for the fighters. A significant portion is redirected to the broader social welfare of the community.

The verse then connects the ruling directly to faith: “if you truly believe in Allah and what We revealed to Our servant on that decisive day. ” The “decisive day” is the day of Badr, the day when truth and falsehood were separated. The phrase “what We revealed” can refer to the sending of angels and the divine support. The condition “if you truly believe” challenges the believers to accept this distribution without the grumbling that marked their earlier behaviour. Accepting the khums ruling is presented as a test of genuine faith. The verse closes with “And Allah is Most Capable of everything.” His capability means that He can provide for the fighters from sources other than the spoils, and He can compensate those whose shares might seem reduced.

This statement assures the believers that no one loses by following Allah’s distribution, because the One who ordains it is the One who controls all provision.

=42=

This verse recalls the exact physical positions of the two forces at Badr, emphasising that the encounter was not a human arrangement. The believers were stationed on the side of the valley closer to Medina, while the disbelievers occupied the far side. The trade caravan they had originally hoped to intercept was travelling along a route below them, near the coast. The strategic picture was entirely in Allah’s hands.

The verse then makes a striking observation: “Even if the two armies had made an appointment to meet, both would have certainly missed it.” The disparity in numbers, the different starting points, and the various logistical difficulties were such that, in the ordinary course of events, a battle at that time and place would not have occurred. The Quraysh army had marched out to protect their caravan, and the Muslims had gone out for a different purpose.

Yet the two forces were brought together on the sands of Badr without either side fully intending the exact confrontation that unfolded. The purpose behind this divinely orchestrated meeting is stated: “so Allah may establish what He had destined.” The destiny in question is the separation between truth and falsehood, the victory of the believers, and the defeat of the disbelievers. The verse further explains, “that those who were to perish and those who were to survive might do so after the truth had been made clear to both.

” This is a profound statement about divine justice. No one is destroyed or saved arbitrarily. The battle made the truth manifest. Those who perished in disbelief did so after having witnessed a clear sign, and those who survived in faith did so after their hearts were strengthened by that same sign. The clarity of the truth removes any excuse.

The verse ends with “Surely Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.” He heard the prayers of the believers and the arrogant boasts of the disbelievers; He knew the exact number, positions, and intentions of all parties. The entire arrangement was a product of that perfect hearing and knowledge, ensuring that the outcome was exactly what His wisdom required.

=43=

The scene shifts to a more intimate divine intervention. Before the battle, the Prophet saw a dream in which the enemy army appeared small. Allah directly addresses the Prophet: “when Allah showed them in your dream as few in number.” This was not an ordinary dream but a form of revelation that gave the Prophet reassurance and confidence. The small number of the enemy in the vision meant that the battle was winnable, and the Prophet conveyed this glad tiding to his companions.

The verse then explains the wisdom behind this: “Had He shown them to you as many, you believers would have certainly faltered and disputed in the matter.” The knowledge of the true, large size of the Quraysh army could have disheartened the believers. As the earlier verses of the surah have shown, some were already reluctant and fearful. Seeing the full number in a dream might have led to a loss of courage and renewed arguments about whether to fight. Allah, in His mercy, spared them from that by giving a vision that encouraged rather than alarmed.

The phrase “But Allah spared you from that” highlights divine protection over the hearts of the believers. The dream was a means of that protection.

The verse then grounds this in divine knowledge: “Surely He knows best what is hidden in the heart.” Allah knew the mixture of fear, courage, sincerity, and weakness present in the hearts of the companions. He knew exactly what they could bear and what would cause them to break. The dream was calibrated to their psychological state.

This verse teaches that Allah’s guidance and help are not one‑size‑fits‑all; they are tailored to the condition of His servants. It also shows the importance of leadership in maintaining morale. The Prophet received the vision and shared it, and that vision became a tool for unifying and strengthening the community. The believers learned to trust that Allah would not expose them to a burden they could not carry, a lesson that reverberates through the Quranic narrative.

=44=

The verse moves from the dream before the battle to the actual moment of engagement. “Then when your armies met” refers to the two forces facing each other on the field. At that critical juncture, “Allah made them appear as few in your eyes.” The believers saw the enemy army as smaller than it actually was. This gave them confidence and prevented panic. Simultaneously, “and made you appear as few in theirs.” The disbelievers looked at the Muslim force and underestimated its strength. This had two effects. It made the Quraysh overconfident, leading them to neglect proper strategy and to approach the battle with arrogance rather than caution. It also ensured that they would not be intimidated into fleeing before the battle began, because they thought the Muslims were an easy target.

Thus both sides were given a perception that served the divine plan. The purpose is repeated: “so Allah may establish what He had destined. ” The mutual illusion was a tool for the unfolding of the decree. The believers, heartened but still dependent on Allah, fought bravely. The disbelievers, arrogant and careless, marched into a defeat they could not have imagined.

The verse concludes with the statement: “And to Allah all matters will be returned for judgment. ” This reminds everyone that the events of the battlefield are not the final chapter. Whatever perceptions, victories, and defeats occur in this world, the ultimate judgment rests with Allah. The outcome of Badr was a sign, but the full establishment of justice and the full clarity of each person’s fate will occur on the Day of Return.

This verse also connects to the surah’s ongoing emphasis on divine control. Nothing in the battle—not even what the eyes saw—was left to chance. Allah managed the sensory experience of both armies so that His decree would be carried out precisely as He willed.

=45=

With the lessons of Badr now deeply impressed, the surah issues direct instructions for future engagements. “O believers! When you face an enemy” addresses the community in their capacity as those who may again be called to defend the truth.

The first command is “stand firm. ” Firmness means holding one’s position, not wavering, not entertaining the thought of flight. It is a physical and psychological steadfastness.

This command echoes the earlier prohibition against turning one’s back in battle, but here it is phrased positively. The second command is “and remember Allah often. ” Remembrance of Allah in the heat of battle might seem surprising. The clash of swords and the chaos of war would appear to leave no room for anything beyond physical survival.

Yet the verse makes this remembrance a condition for triumph. Remembrance means keeping the heart connected to Allah, repeating words of His praise and glorification, and maintaining the awareness that He is with them. This remembrance anchors the soul. It prevents fear from overwhelming the mind and stops arrogance from taking root when success comes. The promise attached is “so you may triumph.” Triumph is not guaranteed by weapons and numbers; it is tied directly to steadfastness and the remembrance of Allah. The surah has already demonstrated this at Badr. The believers who stood firm, who prayed, and who were reminded of Allah’s presence were the ones who achieved victory. This verse distills that experience into a lasting principle. It also implies that the spiritual state of the fighters is a critical factor in the outcome. When hearts are filled with the remembrance of Allah, they become channels for divine help. Conversely, when hearts are filled with fear of the enemy or love of spoils, they become vulnerable to defeat. The verse is a concise manual for the believer in any challenging confrontation, whether military or spiritual: hold your ground outwardly, keep your heart busy with Allah inwardly, and success will come from Him.

=46=

The instructions continue with a command that addresses the internal dynamics of the Muslim community. “Obey Allah and His Messenger” is the foundation. Obedience to divine authority ensures that the community is aligned on the right course of action.

The next command is critical: “and do not dispute with one another. ” Dispute here refers to quarrelling, arguing, and division over matters of strategy, leadership, or the distribution of resources. The earlier part of the surah showed the negative consequences of dispute when the companions argued over the spoils and when some hesitated about the march to Badr.

The verse now warns that such dispute has a direct psychological and military cost: “or you would be discouraged and weakened.” The loss of morale and the dissipation of strength are the natural results of internal conflict. When people argue, their hearts become divided, their energy is wasted on each other instead of on the enemy, and their collective resolve crumbles.

The word translated as “discouraged” literally points to the departure of courage and the onset of fear. The word for “weakened” refers to the evaporation of their power and influence.

The verse then commands: “Persevere! ” Patience and endurance are essential. Battles are won not just by initial bravery but by sustained resilience.

The final statement is a promise and a motivation: “Surely Allah is with those who persevere.” The special divine companionship is granted to those who remain patient, obedient, and unified. This verse provides a complete social ethic for the community in times of trial. Unity is not optional; it is a condition for the preservation of strength and the attainment of Allah’s help. The believers are taught to see internal harmony as a form of worship and as a strategic necessity. This lesson reaches far beyond the battlefield. In every collective endeavour, whether it is a community project, a family, or a movement for justice, obedience to rightful authority and the avoidance of internal strife are essential for success.

=47=

This verse draws a sharp contrast between the believers and the Quraysh who marched to Badr. The command “Do not be like those pagans” calls the believers to avoid adopting the attitudes and intentions of their enemies. The pagans “left their homes arrogantly.” They departed from Mecca with boastfulness, music, and a display of pride. They wanted to show their strength, to intimidate the tribes, and to be spoken of with admiration and fear. Their motivation was not justice or defence but vanity and the love of reputation.

The verse adds two more motivations: “only to be seen by people and to hinder others from Allah’s Path.” They were concerned with their image and with putting on a show of power. At the same time, their underlying goal was to obstruct the spread of Islam. They wanted to crush the new faith so that people would remain on the path of idolatry. This combination of arrogance, love of display, and hostility to truth was the exact opposite of the spirit demanded of the believers. The believers were commanded to fight with humility, reliance on Allah, and the intention to establish the truth.

The verse then warns: “And Allah is Fully Aware of what they do.” Their hidden intentions, their secret boasts, and their true objectives are all laid bare before Allah. Nothing of their display escapes His knowledge.

This statement serves as a comfort to the believers and a threat to the arrogant. The believers do not need to worry about the outward show of the enemy, because Allah sees the reality behind it and will deal with it accordingly. The lesson for the believers is to purify their own intentions. They must not fight for fame, for tribal pride, or for material gain. Their only aim should be to please Allah and to uphold His cause. This verse connects back to the opening themes of the surah, where the dispute over the spoils revealed a hint of worldly desire. The cure is to remember the example of the pagans and to flee from any motivation that resembles theirs.

=48=

This verse unveils the spiritual dimension of the Quraysh’s march to Badr. Satan played a direct role in encouraging their evil. “When Satan made their evil deeds appealing to them” means he beautified their idolatry, their aggression, and their confidence. He whispered to their hearts that they were noble and just, that their cause was right, and that their superior numbers guaranteed victory. He then spoke to them, according to some reports appearing in the form of a man from a tribe, and promised, “No one can overcome you today. I am surely by your side.” This false promise of protection and support sealed their arrogance. They marched out feeling invincible. The scene then shifts to the moment of confrontation. “But when the two forces faced off,” when the battle lines were drawn and the believers became visible, Satan’s tune changed drastically. “He cowered” indicates that he retreated in fear. He said, “I have absolutely nothing to do with you. ” This is the classic behaviour of Satan: he entices, he promises, and then he abandons his followers at the moment of truth. He then gave a chilling reason: “I certainly see what you do not see.” According to authentic narrations, Satan saw the angels descending under the command of Jibril. He saw the forces that were invisible to the human eye, the heavenly army arrayed against the disbelievers. His statement “I truly fear Allah, for Allah is severe in punishment” is not a profession of sincere faith. It is a confession born of terrified self‑preservation. Satan knows Allah exists, and he fears for his own safety. This revelation strips away the illusion that Satan is a reliable ally. He will be the first to desert those who follow him when divine punishment draws near.

The verse teaches believers to see through the glamour that sin and falsehood are dressed in. It also warns that relying on anyone or anything other than Allah, including the whispered assurances of Satan, leads inevitably to abandonment and ruin.

=49=

This verse recalls another voice from the time of Badr. The hypocrites of Medina and those with weak or diseased faith looked at the small Muslim army marching out to face the massive Quraysh force. They saw the disparity in numbers and weapons. Their assessment was purely material, and they drew a conclusion: “These believers are deluded by their faith. ” They believed that religious conviction had blinded the Muslims to the physical reality. They mocked the idea that a handful of poorly armed men could defeat a large and well‑equipped army, and they attributed this confidence to a foolish delusion. This attitude is the hallmark of a heart that does not grasp the power of Allah. The hypocrites measured events by human calculations and saw faith as an irrational force that distorts judgment.

The verse then answers this mockery with a profound principle: “But whoever puts their trust in Allah, surely Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.” The believers were not deluded. They had placed their trust in a Lord who is Almighty. His power is not limited by human numbers. The One who created the universe does not struggle to grant victory to a small band of sincere servants.

Furthermore, He is All-Wise. His decision to grant victory at Badr was not a random act of favouritism; it was part of a wise plan to establish Islam and to teach all generations that reliance on Him surpasses all material calculations. The mockery of the hypocrites was based on a narrow, worldly logic that excluded the most important factor: Allah. The verse thus reinforces the central theme of the surah. Trust in Allah is not a psychological crutch; it is the most rational stance a person can take, because it connects one to the ultimate source of power and wisdom. The events at Badr proved the hypocrites wrong and demonstrated that the believers’ faith was not delusion but clear‑sighted recognition of reality as it truly is.

=50=

The surah now transports the listener from the battlefield of Badr to the unseen reality that accompanies death. The phrase “If only you could see” is an expression that creates a vivid image in the mind, drawing attention to a scene of horror that is hidden from ordinary human perception during this life. The scene is the moment the angels extract the souls of the disbelievers. The angels, who are beings of immense power and strict obedience to Allah, are not gentle with those who have spent their lives in defiance. They beat the faces and the backs of the disbelievers. The face represents honour and dignity, and striking it is a form of extreme humiliation. The back represents the body’s strength and the direction of flight, and striking it signifies that there is no escape. This beating is not a random act of cruelty; it is the commencement of the punishment that they had denied and mocked. The angels accompany this physical torment with words: “Taste the torment of burning!” The command “taste” makes the punishment immediate and experiential. They are told to taste what they used to reject. The “torment of burning” refers to the Fire of Hell that awaits them after death and after the Resurrection. This moment at death is the first instalment, the foretaste of the greater torment to come. The verse serves as a powerful deterrent. It reminds the believers that the disbelievers who appeared strong and arrogant on the battlefield are, in reality, utterly vulnerable the moment the angels appear before them. Their swords and their numbers cannot protect them from the angelic grasp. This unseen reality is meant to strengthen the believers’ resolve and to remind them that the true fate of the oppressor is not determined by the outcome of a single battle but by the eternal justice that begins at the moment of death.

=51=

The angels’ address to the disbelievers continues in this verse, explaining the reason for the terrible treatment. “This is the reward for what your hands have done.

” The word “this” points to the beating, the humiliation, and the torment of burning they are experiencing. It is described as a reward, but in the ironic sense: it is the just recompense for their deeds.

The phrase “what your hands have done” roots the punishment in their own actions. The hands are mentioned because they are the primary instruments of human action. The disbelievers used their hands to oppress, to write propaganda against the truth, to strike the believers, and to commit all manner of sins. The punishment is not an arbitrary infliction; it is a direct consequence of their own choices.

The verse then adds a fundamental declaration: “And Allah is never unjust to His creation.” This statement is a cornerstone of Islamic theology. Allah does not wrong anyone, not by an atom’s weight. The punishment He administers is exactly what is deserved, measured by perfect justice. The disbelievers cannot complain that they are being treated unfairly. They were warned repeatedly, they were given time to repent, and they chose to persist in their path. The verse thus shuts down any potential objection that the punishment depicted in the previous verse is cruel or disproportionate. It is not cruelty; it is pure justice executed by the One who is absolutely just. For the believers, this verse provides deep comfort. The injustices they suffer at the hands of the oppressors are not ignored. Every blow, every insult, every act of persecution is recorded, and the One who is never unjust will settle every account. It also serves as a sobering reminder for the believers themselves: if they commit wrong, they too will face the consequences of their deeds, for Allah’s justice applies to all His creation without exception.

=52=

The surah now places the punishment of the Quraysh in the broader context of history. “Their fate is that of the people of Pharaoh and those before them.

” The Arabic word translated as “fate” here refers to a habitual practice or a recurring pattern. The way the disbelievers at Badr were treated is not a new or unique event. It follows the established divine custom in dealing with nations that reject the truth. The people of Pharaoh are the prime example. They had been shown sign after sign through Moses: the staff, the hand, the plagues.

Yet they disbelieved and arrogantly pursued the Children of Israel. Their end was drowning in the sea. Those before them refers to earlier nations like the people of Noah, the Ad, and the Thamud, each of whom rejected the signs brought by their messengers and were subsequently destroyed. The common thread is identified: “they all disbelieved in Allah’s signs.” The signs are the proofs of Allah’s oneness and power, delivered through revelation and through the natural world. Rejection of these signs, when done with full knowledge and stubbornness, invites a decisive seizure.

The phrase “so Allah seized them for their sins” uses a word that conveys a sudden, powerful grip from which there is no escape. The seizure is a direct consequence of their accumulated wrongdoing.

The verse concludes with “Indeed, Allah is All-Powerful, severe in punishment. ” His power means that no force can resist His seizure. His punishment is severe for those who earn it through persistent rebellion. The purpose of this historical parallel is to warn the surviving Quraysh and to console the believers. The believers are not facing an unprecedented evil; the same battle between truth and arrogant falsehood has played out many times, and the outcome has always been the same. The pattern is fixed, and the believers can trust that the God who seized Pharaoh will also deal justly with their oppressors.

=53=

This verse explains the underlying principle that governs the divine pattern just described. “This is because” refers back to the punishment that befell Pharaoh’s people and other nations, as well as the broader concept of the removal of blessings. The principle is stated with beautiful clarity: “Allah would never discontinue His favour to a people until they discontinue their faith.” Favour here means the blessings of security, provision, guidance, and well‑being that Allah bestows upon a community. Allah does not arbitrarily snatch away His blessings. The change always begins from the human side. When a people change what is within themselves—their faith, their gratitude, their obedience, their moral character—then and only then does Allah change their condition. This verse is a profound statement of divine justice and mercy. It shows that Allah’s default posture towards humanity is one of bestowing favour. Punishment and the removal of blessings are a response to a fundamental change that originates in the human heart. The disbelievers of Quraysh had been given the great favour of the Sacred House, honour among the tribes, and abundant provision.

Yet they changed their state by embracing idolatry, persecuting the believers, and rejecting the Prophet. Because they changed their inward condition, Allah changed their outward condition, bringing them to humiliation at Badr.

The verse ends with “Surely Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.” He hears their words of belief or disbelief, and He knows the hidden shifts in their hearts. Nothing escapes His awareness.

For believers, this verse carries both a warning and a hope. The warning is that if a community that once enjoyed Allah’s favour begins to abandon its faith and righteousness, it will lose those blessings. The hope is that if a community finds itself in difficulty, the way back to divine favour is to change what is in its own hearts. The change begins inwardly, and Allah’s response follows.

=54=

This verse reiterates the principle of the previous verse by giving the specific example of Pharaoh’s people in more detail. “That was the case with Pharaoh’s people and those before them.” The pattern is consistent across time. The nature of the crime is repeated: “they all rejected the signs of their Lord. ” The signs are attributed to “their Lord,” which emphasises that the signs came from the very Lord who had created them and sustained them. Their rejection was thus a personal rebellion against the One to whom they owed everything. The response is stated in the first person: “so We destroyed them for their sins.

” The use of “We” underscores the direct divine agency in their destruction. The punishment was not an impersonal law of nature; it was the deliberate act of the Almighty. The specific mode of destruction for Pharaoh’s people is mentioned: “and drowned Pharaoh’s people.” This recalls the climactic scene at the Red Sea, where the sea returned to its flow and overwhelmed Pharaoh and his army as they pursued the Children of Israel.

The final sentence passes a comprehensive judgment: “They were all wrongdoers. ” The word wrongdoers sums up their entire condition. They wronged themselves by disbelieving, they wronged others by oppressing them, and they wronged their Lord by rejecting His signs and claiming divinity for Pharaoh. This verse reinforces the warning to the Quraysh. They were familiar with the story of Moses and Pharaoh, partly through their contact with Jewish communities and partly through the Quranic recitations. By equating their behaviour with that of Pharaoh’s people, the verse tells them that their fate will be the same if they persist. For the believers, the verse is a reminder that the forces of tyranny, no matter how entrenched and powerful, are ultimately destroyed by the One who is the true King. The lesson of history is that wrongdoing carries within itself the seeds of its own destruction.

=55=

This verse returns to the theme of ranking human beings based on their response to truth, which was earlier mentioned in verse 22. There, the worst of beings were described as the wilfully deaf and dumb who do not understand. Here, a specific category is singled out: “those who persist in disbelief, never to have faith.

” The word “persist” indicates an ongoing, continuous state. It is not a temporary doubt or a phase of confusion. It is a settled, stubborn commitment to disbelief that characterises a person’s entire life.

The phrase “never to have faith” is a statement of fact about them. It does not mean they were physically incapable of believing; rather, their persistence has reached a point where, based on Allah’s knowledge, they will never make the choice to believe. Their hearts have become so hardened and their arrogance so entrenched that the door of faith, while theoretically open, is in practice closed because of their own unwavering rejection. To be ranked as the worst of all beings in the sight of Allah is a terrifying designation. It means that in the divine evaluation, such a person is lower than animals, lower than all other creatures, because animals at least fulfil their created purpose by their instincts, whereas these humans were given intellect, free will, and repeated guidance, yet they actively and permanently chose to reject their Creator. This verse is a warning to those who play with faith, who delay their repentance indefinitely, assuming they will always have time. It shows that persistence in disbelief can eventually lead to a state from which there is no return.

The verse also prepares the ground for the following description, which specifically identifies this group as those who violate treaties, adding a social and moral dimension to their corruption.

=56=

The previous verse described the worst of beings in a general way. This verse now identifies them with a specific, concrete group. The “namely” connects the description directly to the people the believers were dealing with. They are those “with whom you O Prophet have entered into treaties, but they violate them every time. ” The reference is to certain Jewish tribes around Medina and possibly some pagan groups who had made agreements of peace and mutual non‑aggression with the Prophet. Despite these solemn covenants, they repeatedly broke their word.

The phrase “every time” indicates a pattern, not an isolated incident. Whenever they entered into a treaty, they would soon find a way to breach it, whether by aiding the enemies of the Muslims, conspiring against the community, or withholding the obligations they had promised. This behaviour demonstrates a complete lack of moral integrity. Trust is the foundation of any society, and these people had made themselves utterly untrustworthy.

The verse adds the chilling detail: “not fearing the consequences.” They did not fear the worldly consequences of their treachery, nor did they have any fear of divine punishment. The absence of fear is not courage; it is a symptom of a dead conscience. A person who does not fear the consequences of breaking a solemn oath is a person who has lost all sense of accountability.

This description reveals why they are the worst of beings. Their disbelief is not a private mental state; it manifests in repeated acts of betrayal that harm the community and undermine the social order.

The verse also provides a historical context for the commands that follow regarding how to deal with such treacherous groups. The believers are being given a clear diagnosis of the enemy they face, so that they are not confused by false shows of peace or tempted to trust those who have proven again and again that their word means nothing.

=57=

This verse gives a directive on how to deal with the treaty‑violators if the situation escalates to armed conflict. “If you ever encounter them in battle” sets the condition. It is not a command to seek out and attack them without cause. The encounter in battle means that when war has become a reality, and the treacherous group stands on the opposing side, then a specific course of action is prescribed. “Make a fearsome example of them” translates a powerful Arabic expression that means to deal with them in a way that scatters and terrifies those behind them. The purpose is stated clearly: “so perhaps those who would follow them may be deterred.” The punishment inflicted on the treaty‑violators is not only a matter of justice; it serves as a strategic deterrent. The enemy’s supporters, allies, or those considering similar treachery in the future should witness the consequences and be restrained from following the same path. This approach aims to prevent further bloodshed by instilling a healthy fear of the cost of betrayal. It is an act of measured severity intended to achieve a greater good: the preservation of peace and the honouring of treaties in the long run. The verse must be understood within its context. It does not advocate indiscriminate brutality. It is a specific response to a specific type of enemy—those who have made a practice of violating solemn agreements and do not fear any consequences. The harshness of the measure is proportionate to the danger they pose to the very fabric of social trust.

The phrase “so perhaps” indicates that the deterrent effect is not guaranteed but hoped for, leaving the outcome to Allah while prescribing the means. This verse also serves as a reminder that leniency towards habitual, unrepentant treachery can lead to greater harm, emboldening other wrongdoers and destabilising the community.

=58=

This verse provides the procedural rule for handling a treaty when there is reason to suspect the other party’s treachery. “If you see signs of betrayal by a people” means that there must be clear indications, not mere suspicion or rumour. The signs could be intelligence reports, observable military preparations, secret communications with the enemy, or other concrete evidence that the other party is about to violate the agreement. In such a situation, the command is to “respond by openly terminating your treaty with them. ” The key word is “openly.” The termination must be declared publicly and unequivocally, so that the other party knows that the state of peace has ended and that they are now on equal footing regarding the possibility of war. It is strictly forbidden to attack them while they are under the impression that the treaty is still in force.

This verse establishes a principle of transparency and honour in international relations. Even when dealing with a treacherous enemy, the believers are not allowed to stoop to deceit. They must act with integrity. The reason is given: “Surely Allah does not like those who betray.” Betrayal is detestable to Allah, regardless of who commits it. If the believers were to attack without prior warning, they would themselves become guilty of the very treachery that they condemn.

This command protects the moral character of the Muslim community. It ensures that their warfare is conducted with a clear conscience and a clean record.

The verse also links back to the earlier command not to betray trusts. The treaty is a trust, and it cannot be set aside in secret. The open termination is a fulfilment of that trust even at the moment of its ending. The lesson is profound: the believers’ standard of justice and honesty is not dependent on the behaviour of their enemies. They answer to Allah, who loves transparency and hates betrayal.

=59=

This verse addresses a psychological temptation that might arise in the believers’ minds. After the commands about treaties and the emphasis on open dealings, a believer might think that the disbelievers who persist in enmity are getting away with their plots. The verse says: “Do not let those disbelievers think they are not within reach.

” The phrase “those disbelievers” refers to the hostile elements that remain opposed to the truth. The believers are warned not to imagine that these enemies have slipped beyond accountability. They may appear to move freely, to plan and conspire, but they are not outside the sphere of divine power and the eventual reach of justice. The instruction is directed at the believers’ own thoughts. It is a correction of a perception that can lead to despair or to a slackening of effort. When wrongdoers seem to prosper and escape consequences, a believer might feel that the struggle is futile. This verse counters that thought by asserting that the disbelievers are, in reality, completely hemmed in by Allah’s power. They have no ultimate escape. The statement “They will have no escape” is a definitive prediction. In this world, they may be defeated militarily or politically, and in the Hereafter, they will certainly be brought to judgment. There is no avenue of flight that can take them beyond Allah’s grip. This verse provides a firm grounding for continued steadfastness. The believers are not chasing an elusive enemy; they are part of a divine plan in which the enemy’s apparent freedom is an illusion. The confidence that the enemy is within reach and will not escape is a source of strength and motivation to prepare and to persevere, which leads naturally into the next verse’s command to prepare military strength.

This verse issues the famous command of military preparedness. “Prepare against them what you can of military power and cavalry.

” The word translated as military power covers all forms of strength that can be mustered—weapons, training, fortifications, strategy, and any means that contribute to a credible defence. The mention of cavalry specifically reflects the military technology of the time, but the principle extends to all forms of force that are effective in deterrence. The objective is clearly stated: “to deter Allah’s enemies and your enemies.” The primary purpose of building strength is not to initiate aggression but to deter it. An enemy who knows that the believers are strong and ready is less likely to attack. This prevents war and preserves peace.

The verse then expands the scope: “as well as other enemies unknown to you but known to Allah.” The believers might not be aware of all the threats that exist. There could be hidden enemies, future adversaries, or plots that have not yet come to light. Preparing strength serves as a deterrent against these unknown threats as well. Allah, in His knowledge, is aware of them, and He instructs the believers to be ready.

The verse concludes with a powerful incentive: “Whatever you spend in the cause of Allah will be paid to you in full and you will not be wronged.” The financial cost of preparation is real, and the believers might hesitate to allocate resources to weapons and training. The promise is that every amount spent sincerely in this path will be reimbursed by Allah in full. The repayment is not merely a return of the same amount; it includes multiplied rewards in this world and the Hereafter. The assurance that they will not be wronged means that no effort or expense, however small, will be overlooked or unrewarded.

This verse establishes the Islamic principle of maintaining a strong deterrent force, not out of love for war but to secure peace and to protect the community’s ability to practice and propagate the faith without oppression. It is a balanced command that marries trust in Allah with the practical duty of preparation.
