=21=

After commanding the believers to obey and not turn away while hearing the call, Allah now warns them against adopting the behaviour of a particular kind of people. The verse says: “Do not be like those who say, ‘We hear,’ but in fact they are not listening.

” The phrase “We hear” on their tongues is a verbal acknowledgment. They use the right words, perhaps to appear compliant or to avoid confrontation.

Yet their claim is hollow because “in fact they are not listening.” The distinction between hearing and listening here is crucial. Hearing can be merely a physical function, the sound reaching the ear. Listening, in the Quranic sense, involves attention, acceptance, and a readiness to act. Those who are described in this verse heard the words of Allah and His Messenger, but they did not truly listen with the intent to obey. Their hearts were absent, their minds closed, and their wills unengaged.

This description applies primarily to the hypocrites and the stubborn disbelievers who heard the Quran but refused to let it change them. Yet the warning is given to the believers, which means that believers can also slip into a state where they hear the commands of Allah without allowing those commands to penetrate their hearts and reshape their actions. The early community had witnessed this phenomenon. There were those who professed faith but, when called to difficult tasks such as battle or financial sacrifice, offered only a superficial “we hear” while harbouring resentment or looking for excuses. The verse is a preventive medicine. It tells the believers that the community must not become a place where divine guidance is acknowledged with the lips but ignored in practice. The rhythm of the surah reinforces this: after the great victory at Badr and the display of divine power, the believers are being trained to maintain a living, responsive connection to revelation. The opposite of the true believers described at the surah’s opening is now becoming clear. True believers have hearts that tremble, faith that increases, and trust that is strong. The ones being warned against have ears that register sound but minds that remain unchanged.

This verse also sets up the following description of the worst creatures, showing the ultimate destination of the path of non‑listening.

=22=

This verse delivers a severe verdict on those who persistently refuse to listen to the truth. “Indeed, the worst of all beings in the sight of Allah” is a designation of extreme gravity. The ranking is not based on lineage, poverty, or social status; it is based on a person’s response to divine guidance.

The phrase “in the sight of Allah” reminds us that this is the ultimate evaluation, unaffected by human opinions. The “deaf and dumb” described here are not physically disabled. They are people whose spiritual faculties have been rendered useless by their own willful rejection. Their ears do not hear the truth with acceptance; their tongues do not speak the truth or seek understanding. The addition of the word “wilfully” in the translation captures the Arabic sense that this deafness and dumbness is a chosen condition, a result of repeatedly turning away.

The final clause “who do not understand” exposes the root of the problem. They lack the faculty of comprehension because they have blocked the channels through which understanding arrives. Reason becomes futile when it is disconnected from the willingness to receive revelation. The verse is not condemning those who are sincerely searching but have not yet understood. It is condemning those who have been given the capacity to hear and reason, the message has been made clear, yet they deliberately shut themselves off.

In the context of the surah, this refers to the Quraysh leaders who heard the Quran’s arguments, saw the signs at Badr, and still refused to submit. Their behaviour was worse than that of animals, because an animal follows its instincts without the ability to ponder divine truth, whereas these humans used their God‑given intellect to devise ways to reject it. The link to the previous verse is direct. The people who say “We hear” but do not listen are now unmasked as the worst of beings. This verse serves as a mirror for believers. It calls them to examine whether their own hearing and speech are spiritually alive or whether they are drifting toward a state of inner deafness. It also frames the coming verses about the hypocrites and the stubborn rejectors, showing why their end is so severe.

=23=

This verse offers a profound glimpse into divine knowledge and human responsibility. It begins, “Had Allah known any goodness in them, He would have certainly made them hear.” The “goodness” here refers to a sincere willingness to accept truth, a kernel of openness buried somewhere in the heart. Allah’s knowledge is absolute and encompasses not only what people do but what they would do under any circumstance. The verse indicates that Allah did not cause them to hear with acceptance because He knew that no such acceptance would ever come from them. There was no unrealized potential for goodness that was overlooked.

This is a challenging statement. It does not mean that Allah arbitrarily withholds guidance from people who genuinely desire it. Rather, it means that their continued rebellion and arrogance had extinguished any trace of receptivity. Allah knew that if He had compelled them to understand or had given them further signs, it would not change their essence.

The second part of the verse reinforces this: “But even if He had made them hear, they would have surely turned away heedlessly.” Even if Allah had miraculously forced understanding into their minds, their willful defiance would have caused them to turn away. They were not victims of insufficient evidence; they were people who had chosen to close themselves to the evidence they already had. The turning away described is not a momentary slip; it is a heedless, stubborn avoidance. This verse balances the doctrine of divine decree with the reality of human choice. It shows that the reason people are not guided is not a deficiency in Allah’s mercy but a hardness in their own hearts that Allah, in His knowledge, has perceived.

This is a warning to the believers not to become like that. They should never assume that merely being in the presence of revelation guarantees guidance. Hearts can harden to the point where even direct divine intervention would not break them. The verse thus calls for a constant prayer for a receptive heart and a fear of the state in which one is abandoned to one’s own heedlessness.

=24=

After describing the tragic condition of those who do not truly listen, the surah turns back to the believers with a warm and urgent call. “O believers! Respond to Allah and His Messenger when he calls you to that which gives you life.” The command to respond is immediate and personal. The call is directed at every individual who claims faith.

The phrase “that which gives you life” is a magnificent summary of the effect of revelation. The teachings of Allah and His Messenger are not a set of burdensome rules that suffocate human nature. They are the very source of life for the heart, the mind, and the community. Just as the body without a soul is dead, the individual and the society without divine guidance are spiritually dead, even if they move and breathe. The call to faith, to prayer, to justice, to struggle, to moral purity—all of it is a call to true life. This reframes obedience. It is not merely a duty; it is the path to becoming fully alive, to experiencing the purpose for which humans were created.

The verse then adds a statement of immense power: “And know that Allah stands between a person and their heart.” This short sentence contains oceans of meaning. Allah is closer to a person than their own heart. He has complete control over the inner thoughts, feelings, and inclinations. A person cannot turn to faith or even have a change of heart without Allah’s permission and help. This knowledge should create a profound sense of dependence. The believer should never rely on their current state of faith as if it were guaranteed. They must constantly turn to Allah to keep their hearts firm and responsive. At the same time, it is a warning: nothing in the heart is hidden from Allah. The secret reluctance, the hidden doubt, the unspoken defiance—all of it is fully known to the One who stands between a person and their heart.

The final clause “and that to Him you will all be gathered” completes the picture. The response to the call that gives life will be evaluated on the Day of Gathering. The entire verse ties together the present call, the inner reality of the heart, and the final accountability, giving the believer every reason to respond with full commitment and sincerity.

=25=

The surah follows up the call to respond with a warning about communal responsibility. “Beware of a trial that will not only affect the wrongdoers among you.

” The word for trial here can mean a calamity, a civil strife, or a divine punishment that descends upon a society. The terrifying aspect of this trial is that when it comes, it does not selectively strike only the individuals who are actively sinning. It can sweep through a community, affecting the righteous and the sinful alike. This does not contradict divine justice, because the righteous who perish in such a trial may find their reward in the Hereafter. But the warning is meant to shake the believers out of complacency. When a community tolerates wrongdoing without attempting to change it, when injustice, corruption, or disobedience become widespread and accepted, the entire society becomes vulnerable to a collective punishment. The silence of the righteous is a form of complicity that removes the protective barrier that divine mercy might otherwise maintain. The verse reflects a key principle of Islamic social ethics: the obligation of enjoining good and forbidding evil is not just a personal virtue; it is a shield that protects the community as a whole. The believers are being taught that they cannot simply look after their own piety and ignore the moral decline around them. Doing so may lead to a situation where they too suffer the consequences of a society that has turned away from Allah.

The second part, “And know that Allah is severe in punishment,” reinforces the seriousness of the warning. The severity of Allah’s punishment is a reality that should never be taken lightly.

This is not meant to drive believers to despair but to keep them vigilant. In the immediate context of the surah, the believers in Medina were a community surrounded by enemies and still containing elements of hypocrisy. The command to respond to the call that gives life is immediately followed by a warning that failure to build a God‑conscious society could invite a collective trial. This verse thus calls for active, communal moral engagement and a deep sense of shared responsibility for the spiritual health of the Muslim society.

=26=

This verse shifts the believers’ gaze backward, calling them to remember their condition before the migration to Medina. “Remember when you had been vastly outnumbered and oppressed in the land.” The address goes especially to the early Muslims in Mecca. They were a tiny minority in a hostile society. They had no political power, no army, and no legal protection. They were oppressed, meaning they were subjected to persecution, boycott, torture, and social exclusion simply because they said, “Our Lord is Allah.

” The phrase “constantly in fear of attacks by your enemy” paints the psychological reality of their daily lives. They woke up each morning not knowing if their homes would be raided or if their fellow believers would be harmed. This state of vulnerability lasted for years. Then the verse recounts the divine intervention: “then He sheltered you.” The shelter refers to Medina, the city that welcomed the Prophet and the emigrants, providing a safe haven after the long years of persecution. “Strengthened you with His help” points directly to the Battle of Badr and the broader establishment of the Muslim community as a protected entity capable of defending itself.

The phrase “and provided you with good things” refers to the spoils of war, the economic opportunities that came after the migration, and the general improvement in their material condition. All these transformations were not their own doing; they were gifts from Allah. The purpose is stated at the end: “so perhaps you would be thankful.” The remembrance of past weakness and divine rescue is intended to cultivate gratitude. When believers recall the fear and helplessness of Mecca and then look at their security and victory at Badr, the natural response should be thankfulness, not argument over the spoils. This verse connects back to the opening theme of the surah. The dispute over the war booty was a sign of forgetfulness. They forgot the terror of Mecca, the shelter of Medina, and the divine hand that changed their condition. The call to remember is a call to return to the heart of the true believer—a heart that is humble, grateful, and fully aware that everything it has comes from its Lord.

=27=

This verse is a direct prohibition addressed to the community, and its placement after the reminder of past blessings is significant. “O believers! Do not betray Allah and the Messenger.” Betrayal of Allah and His Messenger encompasses many things: it can be abandoning the obligations of faith, disclosing secrets of the community to the enemy, failing to support the Prophet in times of difficulty, or knowingly violating clear commands. In the specific context of the surah, some reports indicate that certain individuals had been tempted to give information to the Quraysh or to act in ways that would undermine the Prophet’s plans. But the verse is broad enough to cover any breach of loyalty. Betrayal of Allah and His Messenger is essentially choosing something—wealth, safety, personal relationships—over one’s commitment to the faith when the two conflict.

The second part reads: “nor betray your trusts knowingly. ” Trusts here include all the responsibilities that people have been given: material deposits, secrets, promises, contracts, and positions of authority. The word “knowingly” intensifies the prohibition. Betrayal is even more serious when done with full awareness. A person might fall into error out of ignorance, but to betray a trust while clearly knowing it is wrong indicates a corrupt heart. The verse calls believers to be a people of absolute loyalty and integrity. The society Allah is building is one where trust is foundational. If believers cannot be trusted with spoils, secrets, or promises, how can they be the bearers of the final message to humanity?

This verse also links to the incident of the spoils. The spoils were a trust placed by Allah under the Prophet’s authority. Disputing over them in a way that ignored divine instruction was a form of betrayal of that trust. The verse trains the believers to see all their dealings as part of their covenant with Allah. Honesty in small and large matters alike is a reflection of true faith. To betray a trust, no matter how seemingly insignificant, is to follow a path that diverges sharply from the one walked by the Prophet and his sincere followers.

=28=

This verse explains one of the deepest reasons why betrayal and misbehaviour in matters like the spoils occur. “And know that your wealth and your children are only a test.” Wealth and children are among the greatest loves of the human heart. They are blessings that make life beautiful and worth struggling for.

Yet the verse describes them as a test. This means they are not absolute goods. They are entrusted to a person to see how that person will behave. Will the love of wealth lead them to cheat, to betray trusts, or to withhold what is due to Allah and others? Will the love of children cause them to compromise on their principles, to avoid difficult duties like migration or jihad, or to favour their children unjustly over others?

The word “only” reduces the status of these beloved things. They are temporary means of examination, not the ultimate goals of existence. The believers at Badr had experienced both the temptation of wealth and the pull of family ties. The spoils represented sudden wealth, and the reluctance to fight was partly linked to thoughts of leaving children and dependents without protection. The verse tells them to reframe their perspective. Wealth and children are not to be clung to as the primary measures of success. They are tests that reveal the true state of the heart.

The verse then offers the alternative focus: “and that with Allah is a great reward.” This contrasts the temporary and testing nature of worldly possessions with the permanent and pure reward that Allah has prepared for the righteous. The reward is described as great, meaning it is immense beyond human calculation, and it is with Allah, meaning it is safe, honourable, and eternal. When a believer truly knows this, the test of wealth and children becomes manageable. One can hold wealth loosely, use it in ways that please Allah, and love children without letting that love overshadow obedience to the Creator. The verse thus provides a powerful spiritual logic for the honesty and integrity commanded in the previous verse. Why betray a trust for the sake of wealth that is only a test, when an infinite reward is waiting with Allah?

=29=

After warning about the test of wealth and children, Allah offers a tremendous promise. The condition is stated: “If you are mindful of Allah.” This mindfulness, or taqwa, is a comprehensive state of heart. It means being constantly aware of Allah’s presence, fearing His displeasure, and taking care to obey His commands and avoid what He has forbidden. It is the protective shield that keeps a believer from falling into betrayal and greed. The reward for this mindfulness has three parts. First, “He will grant you a standard to distinguish between right and wrong.” This standard is a light, a discernment, a capacity to see things as they truly are. When a person is mindful of Allah, the fog of confusion lifts. They can distinguish truth from falsehood in matters of belief, and right from wrong in matters of conduct.

In the context of the surah, this means they would see the spoils not as personal prizes but as a divine trust; they would see the enemy’s strength not as a reason for fear but as a test of reliance on Allah. This standard is both knowledge and insight granted by Allah. Second, “He will absolve you of your sins.

” The word for absolving suggests the covering and removal of faults. Mindfulness of Allah leads to forgiveness of past mistakes. The anxiety over sins is replaced by the peace of knowing that Allah has wiped the slate clean. Third, “and forgive you.” This adds emphasis and completeness to the promise of divine pardon. The verse closes with “And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty.” His bounty is not limited; He gives beyond what anyone deserves. The promise of a standard of discernment, expiation, and forgiveness is a manifestation of that boundless generosity. The verse calls believers to shift their ambition. Instead of chasing the limited spoils, they should chase the infinite bounty that comes through mindfulness.

This promise also connects to the earlier description of the true believers, whose hearts tremble at Allah’s remembrance. That trembling is the seed of mindfulness, and from that seed grows the standard of discernment that guides one safely through the tests of life.

=30=

The surah now turns to a specific historical event to illustrate the divine protection that mindfulness brings and the futility of the disbelievers’ schemes. The verse addresses the Prophet: “And remember when the disbelievers conspired to capture, kill, or exile you.” This refers to the meeting of the Quraysh leaders in Dar an‑Nadwah, their assembly hall in Mecca. The situation had become critical for them after the migration of the Prophet and many believers to Medina. They feared the growing strength of the new community. So they gathered to decide the fate of the Messenger. Some proposed imprisonment, some proposed execution, and others proposed banishment.

Finally, they resolved to kill him in a way that would scatter the blame among many tribes so that his clan could not exact revenge. This was a carefully plotted, seemingly foolproof conspiracy. The verse answers: “They planned, but Allah also planned.

” The word “planned” here applied to Allah does not carry any connotation of deception in a negative sense. It refers to His perfect counter‑arrangement of events in a way that completely nullified their scheme. While they were plotting in darkness, Allah was already arranging the Prophet’s safe escape. The angel Jibril informed the Prophet of the plot and instructed him to leave Mecca that very night. Ali bravely slept in the Prophet’s bed to deceive the assassins, while the Prophet slipped away unharmed, eventually reaching Medina. The verse declares: “And Allah is the best of planners. ” No human plan can outsmart the divine plan. The best of human strategies, when they go against the will of Allah, become the very means of their own failure. This verse connects to the immediate context of Badr. The same Quraysh that conspired in Mecca were the ones who marched to Badr and met their defeat. Their plot against the Prophet was the beginning of a chain of events that led directly to their humiliation. The verse reassures the believers that the forces arrayed against them are never beyond Allah’s control. The same divine planning that protected the Prophet in the dark of night was at work on the sands of Badr and remains at work for the faithful in every age.

=31=

This verse exposes the arrogant dismissal that the disbelievers used as their defence against the Quran. “Whenever Our revelations are recited to them” indicates a repeated behaviour. They heard the Quran not once but many times, yet their reaction was not one of contemplation. Instead, they issued a challenge wrapped in mockery. They said, “We have already heard it.” This was meant to trivialise the revelation, as if to say, “There is nothing new or impressive here; we are already familiar with it and are unimpressed.” Then came the boastful claim: “If we wanted, we could have easily produced something similar.” They asserted that the Quran was merely human speech, and that they, masters of Arabic eloquence, could replicate it if they bothered to do so. This was a false claim born of arrogance, because the Quran had already thrown down a challenge for anyone to produce even a single surah like it, and no one had been able to meet it. Their failure to do so was not for lack of literary skill; it was because the Quran was not a human composition.

The final dismissal is the harshest: “This Quran is nothing but ancient fables!” They called the stories of past nations, the warnings of the Day of Judgment, and the profound moral teachings “fables”—old tales retold with no relevance. This was an attempt to reduce divine revelation to the level of bedtime stories. The verse captures the psychology of willful disbelief. When a person does not want to submit, they must find a way to devalue the source of the message. Claiming prior familiarity, challenging human production, and mocking as myths are all tactics to avoid confronting the truth.

This verse also sets up the shocking prayer that follows in the next verse. When argument fails, the stubborn disbeliever sometimes turns to a defiant challenge thrown at heaven itself, which is exactly what the Quraysh did.

=32=

This verse records one of the most startling moments of defiance in the Meccan period. The Quraysh, instead of humbling themselves, turned to a prayer that was essentially a dare. They said, “O Allah! If this is indeed the truth from You, then rain down stones upon us from the sky or overcome us with a painful punishment. ” This prayer is often attributed to Abu Jahl or other leaders, but the Quran presents it as the collective attitude of the entrenched disbelievers. It is the language of ultimate arrogance. They called upon Allah—the very God they associated partners with—and asked Him to destroy them if Muhammad’s message was true. On the surface, it might appear as if they were open to the possibility of the truth. In reality, it was a sarcastic dismissal. They were so confident that the message was false that they were willing to invite punishment as a rhetorical device. They assumed that nothing would happen, and their continued safety would prove their point. This prayer reveals a deep misunderstanding of divine wisdom. Allah does not respond to insolent dares on the arrogant person’s schedule. His punishment comes according to His own will and timing. It also shows that people can reach a level of hardness where they would rather be destroyed than admit they are wrong. The verse mentions two specific requests: stones from the sky, reminiscent of the destruction of previous nations like the people of Lot, and a painful punishment in general. Both requests were ultimately granted, but not in the way or time they expected. The defeat at Badr was a painful punishment for them, and for those who died in disbelief, a far greater punishment awaited in the Hereafter. The prayer stands as a dark monument to the human capacity for self‑destructive pride, and the following verse explains why Allah did not immediately answer their dare.

=33=

This verse gives two reasons why the destruction the Quraysh had recklessly invited was not sent down upon them immediately. The first reason is: “But Allah would never punish them while you O Prophet were in their midst.” The presence of the Prophet among a people is a mercy and a protection. Even though he was in Mecca during the early years of his mission, his presence meant that the community still had the direct opportunity to hear the truth from the chosen messenger. Sending a devastating punishment while the Prophet was still living among them would have been contrary to the divine pattern, because the messenger is a means of guidance, not a harbinger of destruction while actively calling his own people. This honour given to the Prophet reflects his status and the mercy that Allah extended to his nation.

The second reason is: “Nor would He ever punish them if they prayed for forgiveness.” This is a profound declaration of divine mercy. Even the most arrogant disbelievers, if they were to turn sincerely to Allah and seek His forgiveness, would find the punishment averted. The door of repentance is always open. The act of seeking forgiveness, istighfar, is a powerful shield against calamity. This shows that Allah is not eager to punish. His desire is that people return to Him, cleanse themselves of their sins, and attain His mercy.

The verse also implies that while there were some among the Meccans who disbelieved and mocked, there were others who would later become believers, and even the disbelievers might have descendants who would accept faith. Had a sweeping punishment been sent, it would have cut off that future good. The principle is timeless: the presence of righteous people and the sincere seeking of forgiveness can avert collective punishments. This verse should inspire believers to value the company of the pious and to make istighfar a constant practice, knowing that it is a means of protecting themselves and their communities from the trials they might otherwise deserve.

=34=

This verse continues the theme of punishment by presenting a reason that justifies it. The question “And why should Allah not punish them?” is rhetorical. The disbelievers of Quraysh had committed a crime that made them fully deserving of divine chastisement. That crime was hindering the believers from the Sacred Mosque. The Masjid al‑Haram in Mecca was a place meant for the worship of the One God, a sanctuary where all people could come for pilgrimage. The Quraysh, however, had turned it into a centre of idol worship and had physically prevented the Prophet and his companions from performing the pilgrimage and from praying there. They not only committed idolatry but also obstructed those who wanted to worship Allah alone. This obstruction was a direct violation of the sanctity of the House that they claimed to serve.

The verse adds that they were “claiming to be its rightful guardians.” They took pride in their role as the custodians of the Kaaba, considering it a source of honour and authority.

Yet their behaviour contradicted their claim. True guardianship of a sacred house of Allah belongs to those who are mindful of Allah, not to those who fill it with idols and bar the sincere believers from entry.

The phrase “None has the right to guardianship except those mindful of Allah” establishes the divine standard. It is not lineage, tribal prestige, or historical custom that grants the right to serve the Sacred Mosque. It is piety and God‑consciousness. The verse closes with “but most pagans do not know. ” Their ignorance is not a lack of intelligence but a lack of spiritual insight. They were trapped in a tradition that confused worldly authority with religious legitimacy.

This verse also connects back to Badr: the battle was fought against the very people who had expelled the believers from the Sacred Mosque and continued to block access to it. Their defeat was therefore a deserved punishment that restored the balance of justice.

=35=

This verse delivers a scathing description of the worship practices of the Quraysh at the Kaaba. “Their prayer at the Sacred House was nothing but whistling and clapping.

” The word translated as prayer here can also be understood as worship or devotion. The Quraysh had turned the holiest place on earth into a scene of noise and empty ritual. Instead of humble prayer, sincere prostration, and the remembrance of Allah, their devotions consisted of whistling through their fingers and clapping their hands. Some reports indicate that they would circle the Kaaba naked, making these sounds, believing that this constituted worship. The verse strips their practice of any spiritual legitimacy and declares it utterly void. This was a profound corruption of the purpose of the Sacred House, which had been built by Abraham and Ishmael for the pure worship of the One God. The contrast between the prayer of the true believers—which is described earlier in the surah as an act of those who establish prayer with reverence—and the nonsense of the pagans is stark.

The verse then issues a direct rebuke: “So taste the punishment for your disbelief.” The defeat at Badr, the death of their leaders, and the humiliation they suffered were a taste of the consequences of their denial and their corruption of the religion of Abraham. The command “taste” brings the punishment into the realm of direct, personal experience. It is not a theoretical warning; it is an immediate reality they are called to feel. This verse serves as a warning that ritual without truth, and worship corrupted by idolatry and innovation, are not merely ineffective; they invite divine displeasure. The sacredness of a place is tied to the truth of the worship conducted there. When a people abandon the truth and replace it with their own invented practices, they lose the right to guardianship and expose themselves to punishment. The believers are thus taught to value the content of worship over mere outward association with holy sites.

=36=

This verse shifts the focus to the economic dimension of the struggle. The Quraysh had spent enormous amounts of money to equip the army at Badr, to wage propaganda against the Prophet, and to support all efforts that would block people from embracing Islam. “Surely the disbelievers spend their wealth to hinder others from the Path of Allah.” Their spending was not for charity or a good cause; it was explicitly aimed at obstructing the way to Allah. They funded poets to satirise the Prophet, they financed military expeditions, and they used their wealth to intimidate those who might consider accepting Islam. The verse predicts the outcome of such spending: “They will continue to spend to the point of regret.” They will pour their resources into fighting the truth, and the result will be profound loss and sorrow. Their wealth will be drained, and they will have nothing to show for it except failure and remorse.

The next phrase “Then they will be defeated” was fulfilled at Badr and in the subsequent battles. Their military might, funded by their wealth, collapsed before the believers.

The final clause “and the disbelievers will be driven into Hell” extends the picture to the Hereafter. The wealth they spent in this world will not only fail to save them; it will become a witness against them, and they will be herded into the Fire. The verse is a powerful assurance for believers who might feel overwhelmed by the financial might of those who oppose the truth. No amount of spending can defeat the will of Allah. The money of the disbelievers will become a source of regret for them, both in this life, when they see their efforts crumble, and in the next, when they face the ultimate consequence. This teaches believers to invest their own wealth in the path of Allah with confidence, knowing that the opposing financial power is ultimately doomed to failure.

=37=

This verse explains the divine purpose behind the events just described. “So Allah may separate the evil from the good.” The battle of Badr, the spending of the disbelievers, and their eventual defeat all serve a larger function in the moral order of the universe. The struggle between faith and disbelief is a process of sorting and distinguishing. The evil ones are those who persist in their rejection, enmity, and corruption. The good ones are the believers who respond to Allah’s call, sacrifice their wealth and lives, and remain steadfast. This separation occurs in this world through tests and conflicts, and it will be made fully manifest in the Hereafter.

The verse then gives a vivid depiction of the fate of the evil ones: “He will pile up the evil ones all together and then cast them into Hell. ” The image is one of accumulation and disposal. The evil doers from different times and places will be gathered, heaped together like worthless material, and then thrown into the Fire. There is a sense of contempt in the image—they are not honoured individuals but a collective mass of rejected wickedness.

The verse concludes with the definitive judgment: “They are the true losers. ” In the opening of the surah, the believers were told that they would have elevated ranks and an honourable provision if they were true believers. Now the other side is revealed. The true losers are not those who lose a battle or lose some wealth in this world. The true losers are those who lose themselves and their families in the Fire, who have wasted their lives and their resources on enmity to the truth and have nothing to show for it but eternal regret. This separation of the evil from the good is also a comfort to the believers. The presence of hypocrites and the confusion between sincere and insincere elements within the community can be troubling. The verse assures them that Allah will, in His wisdom and at the proper time, separate the two groups and render to each what they deserve.

=38=

This verse is a direct instruction to the Prophet to communicate a message of mercy and warning to the disbelievers. “Tell the disbelievers that if they desist, their past will be forgiven.

” The word “desist” means to stop their disbelief, their warfare against the believers, and their obstruction of the path of Allah. It is a call to repentance and reform. The promise attached is astonishing: their entire past, with all its sins, enmity, and crimes, will be forgiven. Islam wipes away what came before it. This offer shows the immense breadth of divine mercy. No matter how long a person has fought against the truth, the door is open if they sincerely cease and turn to Allah.

The verse then presents the alternative: “But if they persist, then they have an example in those destroyed before them.” Persistence in their ways will place them in the category of the previous nations who rejected their messengers—the people of Noah, the Ad, the Thamud, the people of Lot, and Pharaoh. Those nations were given chances, but when they persisted, they were destroyed by various forms of divine punishment. The Quraysh were familiar with these stories, either through oral tradition or from the Quranic recitations. The reminder serves as both a historical warning and a rational argument: the law that governed the downfall of past nations is still in effect. The verse is a perfect blend of encouragement and warning. The door of forgiveness is held wide open with one hand, while the other hand points to the ruins of the obstinate.

In the context of the surah, this verse is a continuation of the reasoning with the Meccans after Badr. They had seen a taste of punishment, and now they are being given a clear choice. This offer of forgiveness for the past if they desist also ties back to the earlier theme that Allah does not punish those who seek forgiveness.

=39=

This verse gives a clear command with a defined objective. “Fight against them until there is no more persecution.

” The word for persecution here is fitnah, which encompasses religious persecution, oppression, and the attempt to force believers away from their faith. The fighting is not an end in itself; it is a means to remove the obstacle of persecution so that people are free to choose and practise their religion without coercion. The goal is stated next: “and devotion will be entirely to Allah.

” This is the ultimate purpose. Religion, worship, and obedience should be directed solely to Allah, not to idols, not to human tyrants, and not to cultural traditions that rival divine authority. When persecution ends and people are free, the truth of Islam can be presented and accepted or rejected based on conviction, not fear.

The verse then repeats the condition: “But if they desist, then surely Allah is All-Seeing of what they do.” If the enemy stops their aggression and persecution, the believers must also stop fighting. The command to fight is conditional, not absolute. The reference to Allah being All‑Seeing implies that if they merely pretend to desist while plotting treachery, Allah is fully aware and will hold them accountable. The believers are not required to read hearts; they judge by outward actions and leave the hidden intentions to Allah.

This verse establishes a balanced Islamic ethic of war: it is permissible to fight against religious persecution, but the moment the oppression ceases and the freedom to worship Allah is secured, hostility must end. The verse is often misunderstood as a command for perpetual warfare. In reality, it tightly binds the fighting to the cause of ending fitnah and securing the freedom of devotion to Allah. It connects to the previous verse’s offer of forgiveness and warns that desisting brings safety while continued aggression brings consequences.

This verse closes the unit with a powerful reassurance for the believers. “And if they do not comply” refers to the disbelievers’ refusal to desist from persecution and aggression. If they continue their hostilities, the believers should not lose heart. The verse instructs them: “then know that Allah is your Protector. ” The word for Protector, Mawla, carries meanings of a guardian, a patron, and a supporter. It is a relationship of protection and loyalty. The believers are not alone in the fight. They have a Protector who is infinitely stronger than any human force.

The verse then extols this Protector: “What an excellent Protector, and what an excellent Helper!” This exclamation is meant to fill the heart with confidence and peace. If Allah is the Protector, there is no need to fear the numbers, wealth, or plots of the enemy. He is the best guardian one can have, and He is the best source of help. This directly addresses the emotional state of the believers who had earlier felt as if they were being driven to death. The verse tells them that with such a Protector, the fear of the enemy should dissolve. The qualities of Allah as Mawla and as Helper encapsulate all the divine attributes that were demonstrated at Badr. His protection covered them when they were few and weak; His help came through angels, rain, and courage.

The verse also balances the conditional command of the previous verse. Fighting is not pleasant, and believers may naturally feel apprehensive. But the knowledge that Allah is their Protector and Helper transforms the command into a source of strength. They fight not with reliance on their swords but with reliance on the One who controls all outcomes. This verse is a fitting conclusion to this section, linking back to the core theme that victory comes only from Allah, and that the believers’ ultimate relationship is with a Protector who will never abandon them.
