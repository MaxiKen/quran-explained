=21=

After establishing the absolute ownership and constant worship of all beings in the heavens and the earth, the surah now confronts the pagans with a simple, devastating question: have they taken gods from the earth who can raise the dead? The phrase “from the earth” is significant because it points to the lowly origin of their idols, fashioned from stone, wood, or metal dug out of the ground. These gods are earthbound, created by human hands, utterly powerless. The challenge “who can raise the dead?” strikes at the heart of the pagan worldview. The Quraysh did not believe in resurrection; they considered it a myth.

Yet, if their gods were truly divine, they should possess the power over life and death, the most fundamental divine attribute. The question is rhetorical: the answer is obviously that none of them can. The idols are lifeless, and they cannot grant life to anyone. The verse forces the listener to think about the logical requirement of divinity: a true God must be able to originate life and restore it.

This is the core argument for the oneness of Allah. If the idols cannot raise the dead, they cannot judge, reward, or punish; they are worthless.

The question also prepares the mind for the later discussions in the surah about resurrection. It highlights the absurdity of worshipping objects that are themselves dug from the ground and will one day be buried again, alongside those who worshipped them. The only One worthy of worship is the One who will raise all of humanity from their graves on the Day of Judgment. This verse is a call to reason, asking the pagans to assess their gods by a clear, rational standard of divine capability.

=22=

This verse presents one of the Quran’s most powerful rational arguments for monotheism, known as the argument from mutual hindrance or cosmic order. The logic is precise: if there were multiple gods in the heavens or the earth, each possessing independent will and authority, the universe would fall into chaos and corruption. Rival wills would clash; one might want the sun to rise while another wanted night to continue, leading to the collapse of the orderly system we observe. The perfect, harmonious functioning of the cosmos is a testament to a single, uncontested Will. The verse declares that both realms would have become corrupted, meaning utterly spoiled and ruined. This refutes any polytheistic notion of a pantheon managing different departments of the universe. The conclusion “So Glorified is Allah, Lord of the Throne, far above what they claim” exalts Allah above all such false attributions. The Throne represents His supreme authority over all creation.

The mention of the Throne, a symbol of majestic dominion, reinforces that He alone governs everything. This argument resonated deeply with the Meccans, who appreciated the order of the seasons and the stars due to their trade journeys. The verse appeals to that shared observation: the world is not chaotic, therefore its Governor must be One. The lesson for believers is that the unity of Allah is not just a dogma but an observable truth reflected in the harmony of nature. It also teaches that wherever there are multiple ultimate authorities, corruption inevitably follows, a principle that applies even in human governance. The grandeur of the verse leads the heart to declare Allah’s transcendence over the limitations of polytheistic imagination.

=23=

This verse establishes the absolute sovereignty of Allah and the accountability of human beings. “He cannot be questioned about what He does” means that His actions are not subject to the scrutiny or judgment of any creature. Allah does what He wills, and His wisdom is so complete that there is no room for anyone to demand an explanation.

This is not an arbitrary assertion; it flows from His perfect knowledge, justice, and mercy. A human judge can be questioned and his rulings appealed because he is fallible. But the One whose decisions stem from infinite wisdom and whose actions are inherently good and just is beyond question.

In contrast, “they will all be questioned” — every human being will be brought to account for their deeds. This double declaration brings immense comfort and solemn warning. It comforts the believers that the injustices they suffer are under Allah’s supreme wisdom, and He will not be questioned about why He allowed them, though He will hold the oppressors accountable. It warns the transgressors that while they now question the Prophet and demand signs, one day they will be the ones interrogated.

The verse also answers those who ask why certain divine commands are given; the proper stance is submission, recognizing our limited intellect compared to divine wisdom. However, this does not discourage asking for understanding; it simply defines the proper relationship of humble submission. The verse powerfully shifts the perspective: instead of humanity putting Allah in the dock, it is humanity that will stand in the dock.

This teaching nurtures a profound sense of humility and responsibility before the Divine.

=24=

The questioning continues, challenging the polytheists to provide evidence for their worship of other gods. The command to the Prophet is clear: demand proof. Religion is not a matter of blind following; it must be built on clear evidence. The Prophet is told to say, “Show me your proof. ” This is a rational challenge, demonstrating that Islam does not fear scrutiny. Then the verse immediately presents the proof for monotheism: “Here is the Quran, the Reminder for those with me; along with earlier Scriptures, the Reminder for those before me.” The Quran, and the Torah, Psalms, and Gospel in their original forms, all testify to the absolute oneness of Allah. The consistent message of all prophets is the same: there is no god but Allah. So the proof is historical and scriptural. If the pagans claim divine sanction for their idols, let them produce any scripture or revelation that commands it. They cannot, because no true revelation ever sanctioned polytheism.

The phrase “But most of them do not know the truth, so they turn away” explains their rejection: it is not based on a careful examination of evidence but on ignorance and a stubborn desire to avoid the truth. This verse is a masterclass in how to engage with false beliefs: ask for evidence, and present the clear evidence of revelation. It also underscores the unity of the divine message, a powerful link between the Muslim community and the earlier communities of faith. The believers hold the Reminder, and the earlier scriptures, although corrupted in their present form, originally contained the same Reminder.

This is a call to the People of the Book to recognize Islam and to the pagans to realize that their practices have no scriptural basis.

=25=

This verse declares the universal and unchanging core of all prophetic messages. From the first messenger to the last, the fundamental call has always been the same: pure monotheism.

The phrase “We never sent a messenger before you” emphasizes that this is not an innovation brought by Prophet Muhammad but the eternal truth. The revealed message is summarized in the twin declaration: there is no god worthy of worship except Allah, and therefore worship must be directed to Him alone.

The word “worship” includes not only ritual acts like prayer and sacrifice but also ultimate love, fear, hope, and obedience. The verse refutes any notion that different religions have fundamentally different goals or that idolatry was ever permitted. All prophets—Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and others—taught their people to abandon false gods and submit to the one true God. The historical deviations into polytheism and trinitarianism were later corruptions.

This is a deeply reassuring verse for believers: the path they are on is the same path walked by all the prophets. It also silences the Meccan pagans who claimed that their idol worship was an inherited tradition. The verse tells them that no messenger ever sanctioned such a tradition. It places the Prophet Muhammad within a grand brotherhood of monotheistic preachers, strengthening his resolve. The lesson for all humanity is that salvation lies in the pure worship of the one Creator, a message that remains as urgent today as it was in any age. It strips away all theological complexities and returns to the simple, pristine truth that resonates with the natural disposition of every human soul.

=26=

The surah now addresses a specific and grievous false claim: the assertion that Allah has offspring. Some Arab pagans claimed the angels were daughters of God, while Christians spoke of Jesus as the Son of God, and certain Jewish groups had similar notions about Ezra. The verse uses the divine name “The Most Compassionate” (Ar-Rahman) to highlight the absurdity of such a claim; the One whose mercy encompasses all is far above needing a child. The immediate response is “Glory be to Him!” — a declaration of His perfection and transcendence.

The verse then corrects the misconception: those they call offspring, specifically the angels, are nothing more than honoured servants of Allah. They are not divine relatives; they are creations, elevated in rank but still subservient. This correction serves multiple purposes. It refutes the pagan Arab belief, it challenges the Christian doctrine of divine sonship, and it places the angels in their proper theological context as noble servants, not objects of worship.

The word “honoured” indicates their high status, but it is the status of a servant, not a child. This teaching liberates the human mind from the myth of a divine family, which was often used to justify idol worship by claiming that these lesser deities could intercede.

The verse begins a passage that describes the true nature of the angels, their complete submission and their fear of Allah, utterly dismantling the idea that they could be independent divine beings.

=27=

Continuing the description of the angels, this verse emphasizes their absolute obedience. They do not speak until He has spoken, meaning they never initiate an action or utterance without divine permission.

This is a direct contrast to the human perception of powerful beings who act independently. The angels, despite their immense power and high rank, are utterly bound by the command of Allah. They do not intercede on their own, they do not offer unsolicited advice, and they do not alter the decree. This point is crucial for dismantling pagan worship; if the angels themselves cannot speak or act without Allah’s leave, how could they possibly answer prayers or provide help to those who worship them?

The second part, “only acting at His command,” reinforces the first: their entire activity is reactive to divine will. This also serves as a model for believers. The noblest creatures are those who submit their will perfectly to Allah. The angels’ state of constant readiness and obedience is the ideal that the believers strive for when they say “we hear and we obey. ” The verse also prepares for the following clarification that the angels intercede only with Allah’s permission, not as an independent right.

In the context of the surah, this is a further proof of pure monotheism; the entire heavenly realm operates under a single, unchallenged command. The description of the angels in these verses serves as a mirror for humanity, showing what true servitude looks like and exposing the foolishness of elevating any servant, no matter how exalted, to the status of the divine.

=28=

This verse continues to outline the relationship between Allah and the angels, emphasizing divine knowledge and control. “He fully knows what is ahead of them and what is behind them” signifies that Allah’s knowledge encompasses every aspect of the angels’ existence: their future, their past, their deeds, and their inner states. Nothing about them is hidden.

Therefore, they are not autonomous beings who might surprise Allah or act outside His knowledge. The verse then states a crucial theological principle regarding intercession: “They do not intercede except for whom He approves.” The pagan Arabs believed their idols and angels would intercede for them with Allah, pleading on their behalf. This verse refutes that idea by declaring that any intercession by the angels is strictly conditional on Allah’s approval. They do not intercede out of their own love for someone or because of offerings made to them; they intercede only for those whom Allah is already pleased with. This nullifies the core of pagan hope in intercession.

The final clause, “and they tremble in awe of Him,” portrays the angels’ profound reverence. Despite their nearness and honor, they are overwhelmed by fear of Allah’s majesty. This trembling indicates that they fully grasp His greatness and their own insignificance, far from the proud independence that the pagans attributed to their gods. The verse thus teaches believers to rely directly on Allah’s mercy and to seek His approval directly, because even the angels can only help those whom Allah has already chosen to help.

=29=

This verse concludes the passage about the angels with a severe warning, applicable to any exalted being. Even if an angel, despite their nearness, were to claim divinity and say “I am a god besides Him,” the punishment would be swift and certain: Hell. This hypothetical scenario serves to underscore the absolute oneness of Allah. No rank, no nearness, no past obedience exempts any creature from the consequences of the greatest sin: associating partners with Allah. The angels are presented as the ultimate test case; if the most honored servants would be cast into Hell for such a claim, then what about mere human beings who make such claims or worship others alongside Allah?

The verse also implicitly refutes the Christian belief in the divinity of Jesus and the pagan worship of angels. It declares that any creature who crosses the line into claiming godhood will be dealt with as a wrongdoer.

The phrase “This is how We reward the wrongdoers” makes clear that the punishment is not arbitrary; it is the just recompense for the ultimate wrongdoing. This verse provides a powerful moral lesson: humility and acknowledgment of one’s servant status are fundamental to righteousness. The moment a creation, no matter how great, oversteps its bounds and asserts independence or divinity, it brings upon itself utter ruin. It reinforces the theme that all honor belongs to Allah, and any honour granted to a servant is a test, not a license to be worshipped. The believers are reminded to be steadfast in directing all worship, in all its forms, to the One who alone deserves it.

=30=

The surah now shifts to the signs in the universe that testify to Allah’s power and oneness. The verse calls the disbelievers to reflect on the origin of the cosmos.

The phrase “the heavens and earth were once one mass then We split them apart” describes a primordial unity followed by a separation that formed the structured universe. This has often been noted by commentators as harmonizing with the modern concept of the Big Bang, but the Quran’s primary purpose is not science; it is to demonstrate the creative power that brought the cosmos into being from nothing, which is the same power that can resurrect the dead.

The second sign is “We created from water every living thing.” Water is the essential component of all known life. This observation was as evident to the desert Arab as it is to the modern biologist, though the Quran states it as a fact pointing to a Creator.

The question “Will they not then believe?” is a direct appeal to the mind. The signs are so clear that denial is unreasonable. The verse links the macrocosm (the universe) with the microcosm (living organisms), both demonstrating a single intelligent design.

This is a powerful argument against atheism and polytheism, for the unity and coherence of creation point to a single Creator. The lesson is that reflection on the world around us should naturally lead to faith.

The verse also provides a perfect example of how the Quran uses observable phenomena to guide to spiritual truths, making it a timeless reminder that science and faith, when properly understood, are not in conflict but are complementary paths to recognizing the Creator.

=31=

Continuing the signs, the verse moves from the cosmic to the terrestrial. Mountains are described as having been placed firmly upon the earth to prevent it from shaking with its inhabitants. The Quran uses the metaphor of pegs or stabilizers for mountains, suggesting that they play a role in the geological stability of the earth’s crust, a notion that resonates with modern understanding of isostasy and tectonic plates, though the simple meaning is that mountains make the earth stable and habitable rather than a constantly quaking surface.

This is a sign of Allah’s care for humanity, making the world a secure dwelling. The second part mentions the “broad pathways” made in the earth, referring to mountain passes, valleys, and natural routes that allow people to travel and find their way. For the Meccans, who embarked on long trade journeys through mountainous terrain, this was a direct and personal sign of divine facilitation. Without such pathways, travel and commerce would be extremely difficult. The verse invites gratitude and reflection: the earth is not a chaotic or hostile place but has been carefully prepared for human life and civilization. This provision is from the same Creator who split the heavens and the earth. Together, these physical signs form a coherent picture of a world designed with purpose, which logically points to a Designer who will also bring about a Day of Judgment to complete the purpose of creation. The practical lesson is to travel the earth with a conscious eye, seeing in its features the generosity and wisdom of Allah, and to use those pathways to spread goodness and truth.

=32=

The verse ascends to the sky, describing it as a “well-protected canopy.” The sky, or the atmosphere and the heavens beyond, is a protective ceiling over the earth. It shields life from harmful cosmic radiation, from the vacuum of space, and from many celestial hazards.

The word “canopy” conveys the image of a sheltering roof. This protective function is a sign of Allah’s mercy, as without it, life on earth would be impossible.

The verse then laments that despite this clear sign, the disbelievers still turn away. They look at the sky every day, experience its benefit, but refuse to see the Giver behind the gift. Their turning away is not due to lack of evidence but to a willful aversion.

The phrase “its signs” refers to the signs present within the sky: the sun, the moon, the stars, the orderly movement of celestial bodies, and the protective nature itself. The verse connects the physical sign with the spiritual blindness of the disbelievers, creating a poignant contrast.

For believers, this verse is an invitation to look up at the sky with renewed faith and gratitude, recognizing it as more than just a physical phenomenon; it is an ayah, a sign pointing to the Creator. It also warns against the danger of taking the sky for granted and missing the message it carries. The same sky that protects can also become a means of destruction when the heavens are rolled up on the Day of Judgment, a theme that the surah will later address. The sky, then, is both a present mercy and a reminder of the ultimate return.

=33=

This verse continues the cosmic signs by focusing on the most immediate and observable phenomena: the alternation of day and night and the celestial bodies. Allah is the one who created the day and the night, bringing them in a precise, regular cycle that governs all life. The day is for activity and seeking provision; the night is for rest and tranquility. This alternation is not haphazard but is a deliberate, merciful design.

The verse then mentions the sun and the moon, describing each as “travelling in an orbit.” The Arabic word “falak” denotes a rounded course or orbit.

This description, revealed in the 7th century, accurately conveys the motion of celestial bodies in set paths. The sun orbits the center of the galaxy, and the moon orbits the earth, all in precise, predictable trajectories. This orderliness speaks of a Lawgiver who established the physical laws. For the Meccans, the sun and moon were objects of awe and sometimes worship; the Quran demystifies them by describing them as created entities moving in assigned orbits, entirely under Allah’s command. This undermines any worship of celestial bodies and redirects awe toward their Maker. The verse is a powerful call to Tawheed: the One who creates and sustains these great lights is the only One worthy of worship. It also invites reflection on the precision of the universe, which science has only increasingly uncovered. The harmony of day and night and the motion of the sun and moon are signs that the Creator is All-Wise and that He has set a term for everything, just as He has set a term for human life. This naturally transitions to the next verse about mortality.

=34=

This verse directly addresses the Prophet Muhammad and, through him, all humanity, about the reality of death. The pagan opponents often waited for the Prophet to die, thinking his message would then fade away. They also found the idea of resurrection strange. The verse declares a universal law: no human being before Muhammad was granted immortality. Every prophet, every king, every mighty person eventually died. So the question “if you die, will they live forever?” is a powerful rhetorical blow. It confronts the pagans with their own mortality. They may rejoice at the thought of the Prophet’s death, but they too will die. The message he brought is not dependent on his physical permanence; it is the truth from the Eternal God.

This verse also comforted the Prophet, reminding him that death is the natural end for all mortals and that his role was to deliver the message, not to live forever. The verse implicitly refutes any tendency to worship the Prophet or to consider him divine, as his mortality is shared by all. The lesson for believers is to detach their faith from personalities and attach it to the eternal truth. When a righteous leader or scholar passes away, the community should not despair, because the source of the message is the Ever-Living.

The verse also serves as a memento mori, a reminder of death that should inspire immediate preparation for the afterlife. It prepares the heart for the next verse, which universalizes the statement of mortality to every soul.

=35=

This verse is one of the most profound declarations about human existence in the Quran. “Every soul will taste death” is absolute and all-encompassing. No one, no matter how powerful, rich, or pious, can escape this cup.

The word “taste” suggests that death is an experience that everyone will fully undergo, passing through the gate from this world to the next. The second sentence, “And We test you with good and evil as a trial,” explains the nature of this life. Life is not a random series of events but a carefully designed test. The “good” includes health, wealth, success, and all blessings, which test whether a person will be grateful and use them righteously. The “evil” includes poverty, illness, loss, and hardship, which test patience and faith. Neither condition is a sign of divine pleasure or wrath in itself; both are tools of trial.

The phrase “then to Us you will all be returned” reveals the destination. Life’s test ends with a return to Allah for judgment. This verse encapsulates the entire Islamic worldview: life, death, trial, and return. It is a call to live consciously, always mindful that every situation is a test.

The verse also provides immense comfort. For the one suffering, it reminds that the trial is temporary and will be compensated. For the one enjoying ease, it warns that the blessing is a trust to be accounted for. This explains why the Prophet and the believers face hardship while the disbelievers may enjoy luxury; it is all part of the trial, and the ultimate outcome will be determined in the Hereafter. The verse thus serves as a spiritual compass for navigating all of life’s circumstances.

=36=

The surah returns to describing the contemporary reaction of the Meccan disbelievers to the Prophet. They do not meet him with respect or serious debate; they only make fun of him. The mockery is captured in their words: “Is this the one who speaks ill of your gods?” They would point him out to one another, ridiculing the man who dared to criticize their cherished idols.

The phrase “speaks ill of your gods” reveals their primary irritation: the Prophet’s uncompromising monotheism threatened their ancestral religion and their social and economic structure built around idol worship. The verse then exposes a deep inconsistency in their attitude. They scoff at the Prophet for challenging their gods, yet they themselves disbelieve at the mention of the Most Compassionate. When they hear the name Ar-Rahman, the uniquely merciful God, they react with rejection and aversion.

In fact, according to historical reports, the Quraysh even disliked the name “Ar-Rahman” and preferred the names of their idols. This contrast shows that their anger is not really about protecting sacred things; it is about clinging to their own false deities while rejecting the true God whose mercy encompasses all. Their mockery is thus a symptom of a deeper spiritual illness. This verse comforted the Prophet, showing him that the mockery was rooted in their own blindness, not in any deficiency in his message.

For believers, it is a lesson that being ridiculed for speaking the truth is a prophetic legacy, and one must endure it with patience, knowing that the rejecters are only harming themselves by shutting their ears to the name of the Most Compassionate.

=37=

This verse diagnoses a fundamental human trait: haste. Humankind is made of haste, meaning impatience and a desire for immediate results are woven into human nature. The pagans, out of this haste, demanded that the punishment they were warned about be brought immediately. They would say, “When is this promise, if you are truthful? ” The verse addresses this directly. Allah declares, “I will soon show you My signs,” meaning the signs of His power and the truth of the message will become manifest in due time, whether in this world through victory or punishment, or in the Hereafter. The command “so do not ask Me to hasten them” is a rebuke. The timing of divine signs is based on absolute wisdom, not human impatience. Rushing divine decree is a sign of a lack of trust and understanding.

The verse also contains a profound psychological insight. Haste leads people to demand proof on their own terms, and when it doesn’t come immediately, they reject the message altogether. But divine wisdom often unfolds gradually, allowing time for hearts to repent and for the test to complete its course. The believers are taught to be patient and to trust that Allah’s timing is perfect. The history of the prophets is filled with examples where victory or punishment came after a long period of waiting, and when it came, it was all the more decisive. The verse thus counsels a calm, long-term view of the struggle between truth and falsehood, rooted in the knowledge that Allah’s signs will definitely appear, but according to His schedule, not ours.

=38=

The verse now quotes the exact question the disbelievers would pose to the Prophet and his followers. Their question, “When will this threat come to pass if what you say is true?” is a challenge wrapped in sarcasm. They did not believe in the Day of Judgment or the punishment; they considered it a fairy tale. By asking for a specific date, they sought to embarrass the believers and prove that the warning was empty. This demand for a precise timetable is a classic tactic of those who reject unseen realities. They treat the delay as proof of falsehood, not realizing that the delay itself is a mercy.

The verse presents the question without an immediate answer in this same verse, allowing the following verses to address it in depth. The question reflects the materialist mindset that only values the immediate and tangible. It also reveals a deep misunderstanding of the nature of divine threats. Allah’s warnings are not like human threats that lose credibility if not carried out swiftly. The delay is part of the divine plan, allowing people to repent and making the eventual punishment, if it comes, an undeniable manifestation of justice. For the believers, hearing this question posed so openly in the Quran validated their own experience of being heckled by the pagans. It reminded them that they were not alone in facing such taunts; the scripture itself recorded and answered them. The verse captures the tension of the Meccan period and sets up the powerful and frightening response in the next two verses.

=39=

This verse begins the response to the previous question by painting a terrifying scene of the Hereafter. It expresses a wish or a lament: “If only the disbelievers knew…” This is not merely a wish for their knowledge in this world, but an expression of how their certainty will come too late. The time in question is the Day of Judgment, when they will face the Fire of Hell. The description “they will not be able to keep the Fire off their faces or backs” is graphic and all-encompassing. The face and back represent the front and rear; the punishment will surround them completely, and they will be powerless to ward it off even for a moment. The face, the most honored part of the body, will be exposed to the flames, and there will be no shield.

The mention of “backs” suggests that the fire will pursue them from every direction, making escape impossible. The final clause, “nor will they be helped,” extinguishes any hope of rescue. No idol, no ancestor, no intercessor will come to their aid. The verse answers the sarcastic question “When will this threat come to pass?” by asserting that it will come at a time when all mockery will vanish and be replaced by overwhelming terror and helplessness. The lesson is that the delay of punishment should not be mistaken for its cancellation. The verse is a powerful appeal to the imagination, urging the listener to picture that scene now and take protective action through faith and righteousness, rather than waiting until the moment when protection is impossible.

=40=

This verse reinforces the sudden and inescapable nature of the Hour. “In fact” corrects any assumption that the Day of Judgment will come with predictable warning signs that allow for last-minute repentance. “The Hour will take them by surprise, leaving them stunned.” The suddenness is a key feature of the end of the world. People will be going about their daily business, and suddenly the trumpet will be blown, and they will be struck with overwhelming shock, unable to think or act.

The word “stunned” conveys a state of paralysis, a complete loss of composure. The verse then emphasizes two negations: “they will not be able to avert it, nor will it be delayed from them.” There is absolutely no escaping the Hour once its time has come. No amount of running, hiding, or calling on false gods will turn it away. It cannot be pushed back even for a moment, so that someone might quickly believe or make amends. This finality is crucial. The disbelievers’ demand for the threat to be hastened is shown to be foolish, because when it arrives, it will be so sudden and final that their past mockery will become an endless source of regret.

The verse also serves as a comfort to the believers, who were weary of the taunts; it assures them that the justice they long for is certain and will come at its appointed time, unstoppable and undeniable. The overall effect of this passage is to shift the listener’s concern from the “when” to the “how,” urging immediate preparation for a day that gives no second chances.
