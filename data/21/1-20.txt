=1=

The opening of this surah strikes with urgency. The phrase “the time of people’s judgment has drawn near” refers to the Day of Resurrection and the final accounting before Allah. It is described as near because whatever is coming is certainly close from the perspective of divine decree, and human life is fleeting. Despite this nearness, the verse laments that people are heedlessly turning away. This heedlessness is not a lack of information; it is a deliberate indifference, a turning aside from the reminders all around them. The Meccan polytheists to whom the Prophet was sent were deeply engrossed in their trade, their idols, and their social status. When the Prophet warned them of a coming judgment, they shrugged it off as remote or impossible. The Quran uses this tone of urgency to shake the listener awake. This verse echoes the opening of Surah An-Nahl and Surah Al-Qamar, where the nearness of the Hour is also proclaimed. The wisdom behind emphasizing nearness is to remove any sense that one can postpone repentance indefinitely. A person who believes that judgment is distant will delay preparation; one who feels it is near will hasten to correct his course.

The concept of “people” here is universal, encompassing all of humanity, because every soul will stand before its Lord. The heedlessness described is a disease of the heart: outwardly people see the signs, but inwardly they refuse to let the truth penetrate their consciousness. The lesson is that believers must not fall into this pattern. While living in the world, one must remain aware that the ultimate judgment is ever-approaching. The verse thus sets the stage for the entire surah, which presents evidence, stories of past nations, and the certainty of resurrection, all aimed at curing this heedlessness and calling people to wakefulness before it is too late.

=2=

This verse describes the disbelievers’ reaction to the Quran. “Whatever new reminder comes to them from their Lord” refers to the successive revelations of verses and surahs that the Prophet Muhammad recited to them. Each new portion of the Quran was a fresh reminder, a renewed call to believe in one God, to fear the Day of Judgment, and to live righteously.

Yet their response was not reflection but mockery. They “only listen to it jokingly,” meaning they approached the divine words as entertainment, making fun of the message and its messenger. In the gatherings of Quraysh, they would nudge one another and laugh, treating the profound truths as if they were nothing more than amusing tales. This attitude is a defense mechanism against the power of the truth. When a person’s heart is attached to worldly pleasures and false beliefs, sincere engagement with revelation becomes threatening; mockery is a way to dismiss without thinking. It is also a sign of arrogance, because it implies that the listener considers himself above the reminder. The verse highlights a tragic reality: the very words meant to give them life and guidance were turned into material for jest. For the believers, this verse serves as a warning: never allow familiarity with the Quran to breed a casual, joking attitude toward its verses. Every reminder from Allah should be received with attention and humility. It also comforted the Prophet, letting him know that the mockery he faced was not new; it was a pattern of those who reject faith. So the verse lays open the psychology of rejection, showing that the barrier is not intellectual but attitudinal, rooted in a heart that prefers amusement over contemplation.

=3=

The first part of the verse, “with their hearts totally distracted,” uncovers the inner condition that feeds their mockery. Their laughter is not innocent; it comes from hearts that are distracted from their true purpose. The heart, in Quranic language, is the center of understanding and decision. When it becomes distracted by worldly ambitions, grudges, and vain pursuits, it cannot focus on the truth even when it hears it clearly.

The verse then reveals their secret conversations. The evildoers among Quraysh would confer in hushed tones, away from the Prophet and the believers. They said to one another, “Is this one not human like yourselves?” This was their chief objection: a messenger should not be a mere man. They expected an angel or a supernatural being. Their statement “Would you fall for this witchcraft, even though you can clearly see?” shows they accused the Quran of being sorcery that captivated minds, making rational people accept something absurd. They appealed to common sense, as if the truth were obvious falsehood. This secret counsel reveals the conspiratorial nature of the opposition; they sought to reinforce one another’s rejection and prevent individuals from seriously considering the message. The verse exposes the tactic of attacking the messenger’s humanity, a theme that will be answered in the following verses by affirming that all messengers were human. The lesson is that when hearts are distracted, the mind finds rationalizations to reject even the clearest signs. It teaches believers to guard their hearts against the distractions that lead to such tragic misjudgments.

=4=

In response to their secret accusations and mockery, the Prophet is instructed to turn to Allah. He declares that his Lord fully knows every word spoken in the heavens and the earth.

This statement accomplishes several things at once. First, it reassures the Prophet that none of their plots or whispers are hidden from Allah; He hears them all and will hold them to account. Second, it is a subtle warning to the disbelievers that their private conversations are not really private; the One who revealed the Quran hears them. Third, it reframes the entire dispute. Their argument was that a mere human could not be a true messenger, but the Prophet redirects attention to the divine source: if Allah, the All-Hearing and All-Knowing, has chosen him and hears all, then the truth of his message is not dependent on his human nature. The two divine names at the end, “All-Hearing, All-Knowing,” are perfectly suited. Allah hears every utterance, so He heard their secret speech, and He knows their hidden intentions, so their inner distraction is not concealed from Him.

This verse teaches believers a profound lesson: when confronted with mockery and secret plots, do not become consumed by them or try to refute every whisper. Instead, entrust the matter to Allah, who is fully aware. It also builds the Quranic theme that Allah’s knowledge encompasses all things, a source of comfort for the oppressed and a terror for the oppressors who think they can hide their schemes.

=5=

The disbelievers’ position is presented as inconsistent and desperate. First, they call the Quran “a set of confused dreams,” suggesting it is the rambling of a man who sees jumbled visions in sleep. Then, they change their accusation: “No, he has fabricated it,” claiming he deliberately invented it. Then again, “No, he must be a poet,” accusing him of being a talented wordsmith whose words enchant but are not divine truth. This wavering between contradictory labels shows their confusion and refusal to settle on a reasoned critique. They are throwing out any accusation that might stick, a sign that they have no solid basis for rejection. Their demand follows: “So let him bring us a tangible sign like those prophets sent before.” They wanted a visible, physical miracle, something like the staff of Moses or the she-camel of Salih. In their minds, a book of recited verses was insufficient. They failed to see that the Quran itself is the greatest and most enduring sign. The verse captures a classic pattern of disbelief: when people are determined not to believe, they set conditions and move the goalposts endlessly. If a sign were sent, they would likely find another excuse. The Prophet’s response to such demands is not recorded in this verse, but the surah will later emphasize that the destruction of past nations came after they rejected clear signs, and yet their belief at the point of punishment was not accepted. This teaches that signs are given according to divine wisdom, not human demands, and that the Quran’s internal miracle is sufficient for those who sincerely reflect.

=6=

This verse provides a historical argument against the demand for a tangible sign. Allah points out that every society destroyed for its disbelief was given signs, but those signs did not produce faith. The nations of Noah, Hud, Salih, Lot, and others all received clear miracles tailored to their peoples, yet they rejected them and were annihilated.

The question “Will these pagans then believe?” is rhetorical; the implication is that even if they were given the specific sign they demand, their hearts are no different from those of the destroyed nations. The verse serves as a warning to the Quraysh that demanding signs while entertaining mockery and secret counsel is a path to destruction, not to guidance. It also teaches a deep principle about the nature of faith. Miracles can compel outward acknowledgment, but they rarely produce the heartfelt, voluntary submission that Allah values. The real obstacle is not lack of evidence but pride, distraction, and love of the status quo. The Quran frequently emphasizes that the natural world, human conscience, and the revealed Book are all-sufficient signs for those who truly want guidance. By linking the Meccans to the destroyed societies, the verse is both a threat and an invitation: a threat that they are following the same doomed pattern, and an invitation to break that pattern by believing without waiting for a catastrophic sign. It also comforts the Prophet by showing that the rejection he faces is not a reflection on his message but a repetition of an age-old refusal.

=7=

Now the surah directly addresses the objection raised in verse 3, that a messenger should not be a mere human. Allah states a universal principle: every messenger sent before Muhammad was a man, not an angel. They ate, walked in the markets, married, and lived human lives. The only distinction was that they received divine inspiration. By affirming this, the Quran dismantles the argument that a human cannot be a messenger. The polytheists are then told, “If you do not know this already, then ask those who have knowledge of the Scriptures.” This refers to the Jews and Christians among the People of the Book, who knew that their own prophets—Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and others—were human beings. The Quraysh had commercial and social contact with Jewish communities in Arabia, and they respected the knowledge of the Scriptures. The verse urges them to verify this fact if they doubt it.

This is a powerful logical move: it appeals to an authority the Meccans themselves recognized, showing that their own objection was baseless even by the standards of existing religious traditions. It also establishes an important Quranic method: using the witness of previous scriptures to confirm the truth.

For believers, this verse reinforces the beauty of the prophetic model. A human messenger can be imitated; an angel could not be. The fact that the prophets were human means their lives are practical examples for us.

The verse also hints that the real issue is not the messenger’s humanity but the disbelievers’ arrogance; they think themselves too great to follow someone like them, not realizing that divine choice elevates a man above all worldly rank.

=8=

Continuing the argument of the previous verse, Allah emphasizes the complete humanity of the messengers. They were not given bodies that could dispense with food, meaning they experienced hunger, thirst, and the need for sustenance. Nor were they immortal; they lived their appointed terms and faced death. This further dismantles any expectation that a prophet should be a supernatural being.

The mention of food is significant because eating is a fundamentally human need that underscores dependency on the Creator. By highlighting that messengers ate food, the Quran is pointing to their humility and their oneness with the people they were sent to guide. Had they been angels who needed no food, they would have been distant from human experience.

This verse also serves as a rebuttal to those who later exaggerated the status of prophets, such as Christians who elevated Jesus to divinity. A being who eats cannot be divine, for the divine is self-sufficient. The verse’s mention of mortality is equally important. The Prophet Muhammad is reminded, and through him all believers, that every human will die. This anticipates later verses in the surah that address the Prophet’s own mortality. The fact that messengers died means their mission was not about their personal permanence but about the eternal message they left behind.

Thus, the verse contains a lesson about humility, the nature of prophethood, and the proper focus on the message rather than the messenger’s person.

=9=

This verse shifts from the humanity of the messengers to the divine action that accompanied them. Allah states that He fulfilled His promise to those messengers: He saved them and the believers who followed them, and He destroyed the transgressors who rejected them. This historical pattern is a core theme in the Quran. The promise was always that the messengers and their followers would ultimately be delivered, while those who persisted in disbelief and oppression would face destruction. By mentioning this, Allah is comforting the Prophet Muhammad, assuring him that the same pattern will hold. The Meccans who mock and plot should not think they are beyond this law.

The phrase “along with whoever We willed” indicates that divine salvation is selective, based on faith and righteousness, not on tribal or familial ties. It also emphasizes that deliverance is by Allah’s will, not by the messenger’s power. This verse bridges the general principle about messengers with the specific historical examples that will soon follow in the surah. It sets up an expectation: just as Allah was true to His word with previous nations, He will be true with this one.

For believers, it is a source of immense hope. When oppression seems overwhelming and the truth appears weak, they can recall that the arc of history, under divine control, bends towards the salvation of the righteous and the downfall of the obstinate transgressors.

The verse also serves as a warning to those who hear the message but choose to mock; they place themselves on the wrong side of an unbreakable divine promise.

=10=

The addressee here is the entire community, or specifically the Quraysh. Allah declares that He has revealed a Book in which there is their glory, their honor, and their remembrance. This verse is a direct appeal to the Meccans to recognize the immense value of what has been sent to them. The Quran, revealed in their own language through one of their own, is not a source of shame or a cause for division; it is the very thing that could elevate them among the nations.

The word “glory” translates “dhikr,” which also means reminder and renown. So the Book is simultaneously a reminder of their duties and a source of lasting honor if they accept it. The rhetorical question, “Will you not then understand?” is a call to think deeply. It suggests that their rejection is born of a failure to truly grasp the gift they have been given.

Historically, the Quraysh took great pride in their lineage and their custodianship of the Kaaba. The verse implies that the Quran is a far greater source of honor than any of those worldly distinctions. This appeal to honor is a wise pedagogical approach; instead of only threatening, the Quran offers a positive motivation.

The verse also connects to the earlier mention of the destroyed societies; those societies lost their chance, but the Quraysh still have the opportunity to embrace the Book and attain glory in this life and the next. The failure to understand is a recurring Quranic theme, pointing to a deliberate closing of the mind.

Thus, the verse is a passionate invitation to reconsider, to see the Quran not as confused dreams or poetry, but as the very thing that could give their lives meaning and their community true greatness.

=11=

The surah now begins to illustrate the pattern mentioned in verse 9 with a vivid call to reflect on history. The command “Imagine” or literally “And how many...” invites the listener to mentally survey the ruined civilizations of the past. Allah destroyed many societies of wrongdoers, meaning those who persisted in oppression, injustice, and rejection of the truth. The destruction was total, but Allah then raised up other people after them. This teaches that no nation is indispensable; if one generation fails its moral test, others will inherit the earth. The Quraysh passed by the ruins of the ‘Ad, Thamud, and the people of Lot during their trade journeys. The verse taps into that direct experience; they could see the physical evidence of past destructions. The aim is to instill a sense of vulnerability. The Meccans may feel secure in their wealth and power, but the same earth that cradles their cities once cradled those who are now only a memory. By mentioning that other people were raised up, the verse also hints at a divine plan of continuous replacement, which is both a comfort to the oppressed (new, better communities will come) and a threat to the oppressors (they can be easily replaced). The lesson for believers is to always remember that the rise and fall of civilizations is in Allah’s hands, governed by moral laws. No amount of material advancement can protect a society that becomes a “society of wrongdoers. ” The verse also sets the stage for the more detailed description of the panic that seizes the wrongdoers when the punishment arrives, which follows in the next verses.

=12=

The narrative zooms in on the moment destruction approaches a wrongdoing community. “When the wrongdoers sensed Our torment” describes that terrifying instant when the signs of imminent divine punishment become undeniable. Their immediate reaction is not repentance, but flight. They start to run away from their cities, as if physical escape could outrun divine decree.

This is a deeply human picture: even the most arrogant oppressors, when confronted with overwhelming catastrophe, instinctively flee. The verse uses this image to expose the futility of trying to evade Allah’s judgment. Their cities, which were the seats of their power, luxury, and sinful enjoyment, suddenly become places of terror that they desperately want to leave behind.

Historically, this could refer to various destroyed nations. Some scholars link it to the people of Lot, or to other nations upon whom a sudden storm or earthquake descended. The point is universal. It also shows the psychology of the wrongdoer: they recognize the punishment as coming from Allah, but instead of turning to Him in humility, they attempt to escape it. Their flight is a continuation of their rebellion, not a genuine turning back. This verse serves as a warning that when Allah’s punishment arrives, there is no refuge. It also comforts believers that the apparent power of tyrants is an illusion; the same people who strut arrogantly will one day flee like frightened animals, unable to save themselves. The vividness of the imagery is meant to awaken the heedless hearts mentioned in verse 1, painting a scene they should dread and seek to avoid through faith and righteous deeds.

=13=

As the wrongdoers flee, they are met with a sarcastic divine command, either from angels or as a statement of their inevitable fate. They are told not to run away, but to return to their luxuries and their homes.

The word “luxuries” translates a term that conveys opulence, ease, and the material blessings in which they took pride. The command to return is not a genuine offer of safety but a bitter irony. They are being reminded of what they are losing and of the life they valued more than the truth.

The phrase “so you may be questioned about your fate” implies that they will be held to account for how they used those blessings. Some commentators interpret the questioning as a literal interrogation about their deeds, while others see it as a rhetorical statement meaning “you will face what you deserve.” In either case, the verse underscores that their luxuries and homes, which they thought were signs of divine favor or their own cleverness, are actually a test, and now the test is over. The scene is stark: the very things they ran from their cities to save their lives are now the things to which they are mockingly told to return, because there is no escape anyway. This verse is a profound meditation on the attachment to worldly comforts. Those comforts can blind people to their spiritual state. When the moment of reckoning comes, these comforts provide no refuge and become part of the punishment through regret. The lesson is to hold worldly blessings lightly, enjoy them with gratitude, but never let them become the reason to reject truth, for they are fleeting and will be a witness against those who misused them.

=14=

At last, the wrongdoers confess. When all escape routes are cut off and punishment is upon them, they cry out “Woe to us!” — an expression of profound grief, regret, and despair. Their admission, “We have surely been wrongdoers,” is a clear acknowledgment of their guilt. It is a belated recognition of what the messengers had been telling them all along. This confession is significant because it demonstrates that the truth was never hidden from them; they had always known, deep down, that they were on the wrong path. But now the confession is of no avail. The door of repentance has closed with the arrival of the decisive punishment. This pattern is repeated throughout the Quran: Pharaoh’s confession as he drowned, the cries of other nations as destruction enveloped them.

The verse teaches a crucial lesson about the window of opportunity. Sincere repentance is accepted as long as the sun has not risen from the west and the death rattle has not reached the throat. Once the punishment is witnessed and certain, faith born of compulsion is not true faith.

The verse also contains a warning for those who procrastinate in turning to Allah, thinking they will repent later. Later may not come, or it may come at a time when repentance is no longer accepted.

For believers, hearing this cry of “Woe to us!” should motivate them to examine their own lives now, to admit their wrongdoings while they still have time to correct them. The confession, though futile for those who utter it, serves as a testimony against them and a lesson for all who hear the Quran.

=15=

This verse completes the picture of destruction with a terrifying image. The wrongdoers’ cry of “Woe to us!” was not a single utterance; it became their constant refrain, their only remaining speech, as the punishment raged around them. They kept repeating their cry of regret, but it did not halt the divine judgment.

The phrase “until We mowed them down” uses the imagery of harvesting. Just as a farmer cuts down crops with a sickle, leaving them lying flat and lifeless on the ground, so Allah’s punishment cut them down.

The word “lifeless” emphasizes that they were left utterly still, extinguished, a people who once swaggered with pride now reduced to silent corpses. The image is stark and designed to strike fear into the heart of anyone who hears it. There is a clear cause-and-effect logic: their persistent wrongdoings and mockery of the messengers led to persistent regret and then total annihilation.

The verse also conveys the finality of divine judgment when it arrives; there is no reprieve, no second chance. The repeated cries highlight the depth of their anguish, yet the heavens were deaf to them because they had closed their own ears to the cries of the messengers during their lifetimes. The lesson here is about the justice of Allah. He does not wrong anyone; people wrong themselves, and their own tongues will testify against them. This conclusion of the scene of destruction serves as a powerful bridge to the next part of the surah, which will refute the idea that creation is purposeless and will establish the truth of resurrection and judgment. The vividness of the destruction imagery is meant to shake the listener from the heedlessness mentioned in the very first verse.

=16=

Having warned of the destruction of wrongdoers, the surah now turns to a fundamental argument for the reality of judgment. It declares that Allah did not create the massive, intricate universe “for sport.” The heavens, the earth, and all that exists between them were not brought into being as a meaningless game or a passing amusement.

This is a direct response to those who think that life ends with death and that there is no ultimate purpose or accountability. Creation, with its order, beauty, and complexity, points to a Creator with serious purpose.

The word “sport” translates “la‘ib,” which conveys play, amusement, something done without serious intent. To suggest that the universe is the product of divine sport is to insult the wisdom of the Creator. The verse argues from the nature of creation to the nature of its end. Because creation is purposeful, there must be a final reckoning where the purposes of moral choice are realized. The heavens and the earth are not a stage for empty entertainment; they are a testing ground.

This verse also refutes the materialist worldview that sees the cosmos as a random accident without inherent meaning. The Quran consistently appeals to the natural world as a sign, and here it uses the very grandeur of the universe to argue that the Creator does not engage in frivolity. The lesson is that human life, as part of this creation, is likewise purposeful. We were not created for sport, so our lives should not be lived in vain pursuits. This verse sets the foundation for the next verses, which will elaborate on what divine purpose is not and what it is.

=17=

This verse continues the argument of the previous one by posing a hypothetical scenario. If Allah had wanted to take amusement, He would not have needed to create the universe to do so. He could have found it “in Our presence,” meaning within the divine realm, without the elaborate process of creation, revelation, and judgment. The statement implies that Allah is entirely self-sufficient; any notion of amusement is inherently unworthy of His majesty. The hypothetical is framed to show the absurdity of thinking that creation is a divine pastime.

The phrase “if that had been Our Will” further emphasizes that Allah does not will such frivolity. The verse is also a subtle rebuttal of those who believed that angels were daughters of God or that God had a family, ideas that are explicitly refuted later in the surah. It anticipates the statement that all beings in the heavens and the earth belong to Allah and worship Him. By asserting that amusement was not the purpose and that any supposed amusement would not require this grand creation, the verse reinforces the concept that the universe is created in truth and for a serious purpose. The logical flow is impeccable: if you claim creation is a game, you imply a need or a whim in the Creator, but the Creator is above such things. The lesson for believers is to align their own understanding of life with this divine purpose. If Allah does not create for amusement, then our lives should not be lived for amusement either. The world is a place of trial and meaningful action, not a playground. This prepares the mind for the next verse, which describes what Allah actually does with truth and falsehood.

=18=

Instead of amusement, the real divine action is described in powerful combat imagery: Allah hurls the truth against falsehood. The truth, meaning divine revelation, sound belief, and righteous reality, is like a solid projectile. Falsehood, with all its illusions and baseless claims, is the target. The result is that falsehood is crushed, its structure shattered, and it quickly vanishes. This metaphor explains the dynamic of history and the cosmic struggle between right and wrong. While falsehood may temporarily appear strong and widespread, a single strike of truth from Allah causes it to crumble, just as a glass idol shatters when struck by a hammer.

The phrase “And woe be to you for what you claim” is a direct warning to the polytheists who make false claims about Allah, including the ideas that He has offspring or partners, or that creation is purposeless. Their claims are part of the falsehood that will be destroyed. The verse offers immense hope to the believers: the truth they hold may seem weak in the face of worldly powers, but it carries within it the force of divine reality. When Allah wills, He hurls it, and falsehood cannot stand. This also explains why the Prophet and his followers should not be disheartened by mockery; the battle is not theirs but Allah’s, and the outcome is certain. On a personal level, every believer can apply this verse by “hurling” the truth of the Quran against the false ideas that whisper in their own hearts, shattering doubts and vain desires through firm faith.

=19=

This verse transitions from the destruction of falsehood to the universal reality of divine ownership and worship. Everything in the heavens and the earth belongs to Allah; this ownership is absolute. Among those who belong to Him are the angels, described here as “those nearest to Him.” Despite their elevated status and closeness to the divine throne, they are not too proud to worship Allah. Unlike the arrogant polytheists who disdained following a human messenger and refused to bow to the Creator, the angels, who are far superior in power and purity, continuously serve Allah without any sense of pride or fatigue.

The phrase “nor do they tire” is crucial. It shows that worship is not a burden they wish to escape; their devotion is constant and joyful.

This is a sharp contrast to the Meccan elite, who considered it beneath their dignity to worship one God and who were weary of the call to prayer and devotion. The verse implicitly asks: if the noblest creatures serve Allah tirelessly, who are you to refuse? It also refutes any idea that angels are partners or offspring of God, as they are themselves servants.

This description of angelic worship prepares the way for the next verse, which emphasizes their continuous glorification. For the believer, this verse provides a model of humble servitude. The noblest beings are the most humble before their Lord. Pride is the attribute of the disbeliever, while humility and tireless devotion mark those truly close to Allah. The verse invites us to examine our own worship: do we perform it out of love and reverence, without weariness?

=20=

Completing the description of the angels, this verse emphasizes the constancy of their glorification. They glorify Allah day and night, meaning perpetually, without interruption.

The phrase “never wavering” translates a word that indicates no fatigue, no slackening, no loss of energy. Their glorification is perfect and continuous. This glorification is both their primary activity and their natural state of being. The verse functions on multiple levels. First, it glorifies Allah by describing the worship of the most honored beings. Second, it serves as a model for believers, who are called to remember Allah often, standing, sitting, and lying on their sides. While human beings cannot match the angels’ ceaseless glorification due to the need for sleep and daily duties, the ideal is to fill one’s life with the remembrance of Allah so that it becomes a constant thread. Third, it contrasts the angels’ unwavering devotion with the disbelievers’ heedlessness and the believers’ occasional lapses. The angels’ glorification is not only verbal; it is an expression of their submission to Allah’s command. The verse thus forms a beautiful conclusion to this passage, which began with the heedlessness of humans turning away from judgment, passed through the arguments for purpose and the smashing of falsehood, and arrives at the picture of universal, tireless worship. It reminds us that the entire cosmos, except for defiant humans and jinn, is in a state of submission and glorification. The only ones out of tune with this cosmic symphony are those who refuse to worship their Lord. This verse inspires a sense of awe and a desire to join that great company of worshippers, however imperfectly.
