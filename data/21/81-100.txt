=81=

After mentioning David, the surah highlights the extraordinary gifts bestowed upon his son Solomon. The winds were made subservient to him, described here as “raging winds” that nonetheless obeyed his command.

The term “raging” conveys their powerful, swift nature, yet they were tamed by Allah for Solomon’s use. They blew “by his command to the land We had showered with blessings. ” This blessed land is generally understood to be greater Syria and Palestine, the same land to which Abraham had migrated and which was filled with divine barakah. Solomon’s ability to direct the wind meant he could travel vast distances in an incredibly short time, facilitating his governance, trade, and communication across his expansive kingdom. The wind, a natural force of immense power, became a docile servant, demonstrating Allah’s absolute control over creation and the honor given to a grateful prophet.

The verse concludes with “It is We Who know everything. ” This reminds Solomon and all who hear the story that this miraculous power was not Solomon’s own but a gift from the All-Knowing, who perfectly understood how to grant him such a dominion and who also knows how the gift is being used. It serves as a subtle caution against pride and a reminder of accountability.

For believers, this verse illustrates the principle of taskhir: the subjection of the natural world to humanity in general, and to righteous servants in particular. While we do not command the wind literally, we are entrusted with harnessing nature’s forces, and this trust must be exercised with the consciousness that the One who subjected them knows everything we do.

The verse also evokes the transitory nature of worldly power; Solomon’s wind-borne journeys carried him across a blessed land, pointing to the ultimate journey toward the Hereafter, the land of eternal blessing.

=82=

In addition to the winds, Allah subjected a faction of the jinn to Solomon’s service. The verse specifies that they “dived for him,” retrieving pearls, corals, and other treasures from the depths of the sea. They also “performed other duties,” which included building lofty chambers, statues, and great basins as mentioned in Surah Saba. The jinn are beings created from smokeless fire, normally invisible to humans and possessing abilities beyond human capacity. Their subjection to a human prophet was a unique miracle granted to Solomon, emphasizing his unmatched dominion. This service was not oppressive or demonic; it was a disciplined labor under divine command.

The verse emphasizes, “It is We Who kept them in check.” The jinn, being powerful and potentially rebellious, were only held in this service by Allah’s direct control. This prevented them from harming Solomon or escaping their duties. The statement underscores that no creature, no matter how powerful or cunning, operates outside Allah’s authority. For the Prophet Muhammad, who was offered a similar dominion over the jinn and the mountains but chose instead to remain a humble servant, this verse provided context. It showed that such miracles were given to previous prophets, but the final prophet’s greatest miracle is the Quran.

The verse also serves as a warning against seeking help from jinn through forbidden means, a practice common among the Arabs. The jinn are not independent gods or helpers; they are creatures under Allah’s control, and any service they render is only by His permission. The righteous engage only with what Allah has made lawful. Solomon’s story thus demystifies the jinn and places them firmly within the framework of divine sovereignty.

=83=

The surah now turns to Prophet Job (Ayyub), whose story is one of the most profound examples of patience and trust in divine mercy. The command “remember” invites reflection on his ordeal. Job was a wealthy, healthy, and righteous man blessed with a large family. Then he was tested with the loss of his wealth, the death of his children, and a severe, prolonged illness that left him physically ravaged. His society shunned him, and only his faithful wife remained by his side. After years of suffering, he turned to Allah with this beautiful prayer. The prayer is remarkable for what it does not say. He does not demand a cure, does not complain about injustice, does not ask why he was singled out. He simply states his condition: “I have been touched with adversity.

” The word “touched” minimizes the suffering in his own expression, showing profound restraint. Then he addresses Allah with the most exquisite courtesy: “You are the Most Merciful of the merciful.” By beginning with a description of Allah’s mercy rather than his own pain, he places his hope in the one attribute that can transform his situation. He does not even explicitly ask for healing; the mention of Allah’s mercy in the context of his suffering is itself the most eloquent supplication. This prayer teaches believers the etiquettes of dua: acknowledging one’s state without complaining, and magnifying Allah’s attributes with hope. It is a model for those enduring chronic illness, loss, or hardship. The focus is not on the severity of the test but on the boundlessness of the tester’s mercy, trusting that the Most Merciful will respond in the most merciful way.

=84=

The divine response to Job’s prayer was immediate and generous. “We answered his prayer” affirms the direct connection between his humble, trust-filled supplication and the relief that followed.

The phrase “removed his adversity” means his illness was completely cured. According to exegetical reports, Job was instructed to strike the ground with his foot, from which a cool spring gushed forth; he bathed and drank, and all his afflictions vanished. Then, “gave him back his family, twice as many.” This indicates that the children who had died were restored in the form of new children, and the number was doubled, a gesture of overflowing divine generosity. Some scholars suggest that the deceased children themselves were resurrected and additional children were granted. In either case, the blessing exceeded what was lost. The verse states the purpose of this restoration: “as a mercy from Us and a lesson for the devoted worshippers.” The mercy was for Job personally, a tangible embrace from the Most Merciful. The lesson extends to all devoted worshippers across time. Job’s story teaches that patience in the face of suffering is not in vain, that Allah’s mercy is always present even when veiled by pain, and that relief follows hardship with divine precision. The “devoted worshippers” are those who learn from Job that the essence of worship is not only to serve in times of ease but to maintain devotion in times of trial, knowing that the One they worship is the Healer and the Restorer. This story deeply comforted the Prophet Muhammad during the long years of Meccan persecution, reassuring him that the testing of a prophet is a prelude to immense divine mercy and victory.

=85=

The surah briefly mentions three more prophets, grouping them together under the quality of steadfastness. Ishmael (Isma‘il) was the elder son of Abraham, the forefather of the Arabs, and a prophet who settled in Mecca and helped build the Kaaba. His life was marked by absolute submission, most famously when he willingly lay down for sacrifice at Allah’s command. Enoch (Idris) is praised elsewhere in the Quran as a man of truth and a prophet who was raised to a lofty station. His patience and dedication to calling his people are legendary, and he is considered one of the earliest prophets after Adam. Dhul-Kifl, whose name means “the one of the fold” or “the one of the pledge,” is identified by many scholars as a righteous prophet who took a solemn pledge to judge his people with justice and to spend his nights in worship and his days in fasting, fulfilling his covenant perfectly. Others identify him with Ezekiel or a different righteous figure. The verse singles out their shared quality: “They were all steadfast. ” Patience and perseverance are the backbone of prophethood. Each of these men faced daunting challenges, hostile audiences, or severe personal trials, yet they remained unwavering in their faith and mission. By mentioning them, the surah reinforces the universal theme that the path of truth requires sabr. The Prophet Muhammad is implicitly told that he is in the company of these giants of patience. For the believers, the verse is a reminder to honor all the prophets equally, to study their lives, and to extract the elixir of patience from their stories. Steadfastness is not passive endurance but active, consistent commitment to God regardless of circumstances.

=86=

The verse delivers the ultimate verdict on Ishmael, Enoch, and Dhul-Kifl. After their lives of steadfastness, the final destination was entrance into Allah’s mercy. This phrase “admitted them into Our mercy” is a beautiful metaphor for Paradise and the divine good pleasure. It echoes the same phrase used for Lot earlier in verse 75, creating a thread of continuity in the surah’s prophetic biographies. The mercy here is not a temporary relief but an eternal state of bliss and divine nearness. The reason for this admittance is given: “for they were truly of the righteous.

” The word “truly” (inna) emphasizes the depth and authenticity of their righteousness. It was not a superficial label but a reality that permeated their entire being, from their beliefs to their actions, from their public lives to their private moments. Righteousness (salah) is the key that unlocks divine mercy.

The verse teaches that prophethood and divine favor are not based on lineage or worldly status but on genuine righteousness. The Quraysh, who prided themselves on descent from Ishmael, are implicitly reminded that the honor of Ishmael is rooted in his righteousness and steadfastness, not just his bloodline, and they must emulate his faith to claim any true connection to him. For the believers, this verse provides a clear roadmap: strive for authentic righteousness in your character and deeds, and the outcome, by Allah’s mercy, will be admittance into an eternal abode of mercy.

The sequence also reassures the Prophet that those who are steadfast in the face of rejection, as these prophets were, are never forgotten and their end is secure with Allah.

=87=

The narrative now focuses on Jonah (Yunus), referred to as “the Man of the Whale” (Dhu’n-Nun). His story is a powerful lesson in prophetic fallibility, repentance, and divine mercy. Jonah was sent to the people of Nineveh, who rejected his call. Frustrated and angry, he “stormed off” from his city without waiting for Allah’s explicit command. This act was a lapse in prophetic patience, though not a major sin. He thought “We would not restrain him,” meaning he assumed that his departure would not result in immediate divine constriction or punishment for him. He underestimated the accountability that comes with the prophetic mission. Allah’s power, however, does restrict and test. He boarded a ship, was thrown overboard, and was swallowed by a massive whale. In the “veils of darkness” — the darkness of the whale’s belly, the deep sea, and the night — he cried out the famous supplication. This prayer is a masterpiece of monotheistic devotion. He begins with the negation of all false gods and the affirmation of Allah’s absolute oneness: “There is no god worthy of worship except You. ” Then he glorifies Allah, declaring Him far above any injustice: “Glory be to You!” Only then does he confess his own fault: “I have certainly done wrong.” The order is significant: he places Allah’s transcendence and perfection first, then his own error. This supplication, known as the prayer of Dhun-Nun, is so powerful that the Prophet Muhammad said that no Muslim who supplicates with it for anything will have it except it is granted.

The verse teaches that even a prophet can experience a moment of haste, but what distinguishes the righteous is their immediate return to Allah, acknowledging His glory and their own need, especially in the depths of darkness.

=88=

The divine response was immediate. “We answered his prayer” is a testament to the power of sincere repentance and the glorification of Allah. No matter how dark the situation — literally in the belly of a whale at the bottom of the sea — the call of a sincere heart reaches Allah and is answered. “Rescued him from anguish” refers to both the physical deliverance from the whale and the spiritual anguish of his separation from his mission and his error. The whale ejected him onto a shore, where a gourd plant grew to protect and nourish him, and he was restored to health and returned to his people.

The verse then expands this individual salvation into a universal principle: “And so do We save the true believers.” The deliverance of Jonah is a prototype of how Allah rescues the believers from their own mistakes, from the consequences of their sins, and from the oppressive situations they find themselves in.

The phrase “true believers” indicates that this divine rescue is contingent upon genuine faith. The conditions are the same as those demonstrated by Jonah: sincere recognition of Allah’s oneness, glorification of Him, humble admission of one’s own wrongdoing, and turning to Him in supplication with a heart full of hope. This verse is a source of immense hope for every believer who has erred. It promises that no matter how far one has strayed or how deep the “darkness” is, the path back to light is open through the words of tawheed and istighfar. It also comforts those who are unjustly oppressed, assuring them that the same Lord who rescued Jonah from the whale’s belly can rescue them from the grip of the oppressor, as long as they turn to Him sincerely.

=89=

The surah now turns to Zachariah (Zakariyya), the righteous prophet from the lineage of Aaron and the guardian of Mary. Zachariah and his wife had reached extreme old age without having a child, a source of deep personal sorrow and a concern for the continuation of his prophetic mission. His cry to his Lord is introduced with the same “remember” that links all these prophetic stories, emphasizing their relevance to the Prophet Muhammad. Zachariah’s prayer is tender and respectful. He begins with “My Lord,” expressing intimate, personal devotion. His request, “Do not leave me childless,” reveals a profound human longing, not merely for an heir to his property but for a righteous successor who would carry on the legacy of prophethood and divine service in the temple.

Yet, immediately after stating his need, he adds a statement of complete submission: “though You are the Best of Successors.” This phrase is deeply moving. Zachariah is saying that even if Allah were to leave him without a child, he acknowledges that Allah is the ultimate inheritor of everything; all things return to Him, and He needs no heir. His own personal desire is subordinated to his recognition of Allah’s perfection. This teaches the balance in supplication: one should present one’s needs with urgency and hope, but always with the underlying acknowledgment that Allah’s wisdom is supreme, and His decree is better than our plans. The verse highlights the etiquettes of dua in the lives of the prophets: a blend of frank expression of need, intimate address, and glorification of the Lord.

For those who suffer from childlessness or any unfulfilled desire, this is a model of how to turn to Allah with hope while finding peace in His perfect dispossession.

=90=

The divine response to Zachariah’s heartfelt prayer was swift and miraculous. “We answered his prayer” indicates that his cry was heard and accepted.

The answer took the form of granting him a son, John (Yahya), a name chosen by Allah Himself, as mentioned in another surah. The miracle involved not only Zachariah’s old age but also the healing of his wife’s barrenness, as the verse says “and made his wife fertile. ” This double miracle — curing both male and female infertility — signified the absolute power of Allah over what humans deem biologically impossible.

The verse then shifts from the miracle itself to the spiritual character of Zachariah, his wife, and John. “Indeed, they used to race in doing good,” meaning they were not passive recipients of divine favor; they actively competed with one another in righteous deeds, seizing every opportunity for charity, prayer, and service. “And call upon Us with hope and fear” describes their state of worship: a perfect balance between hoping for Allah’s mercy and fearing His punishment, never becoming complacent or despairing. “Totally humbling themselves before Us” refers to khushu‘, a profound state of inner and outer humility, devotion, and stillness before Allah.

This description is a window into the household that produced John, the forerunner of Jesus. It reveals that divine miracles are granted in an atmosphere of sincere, balanced worship. The lesson for believers is that while we may ask for specific worldly blessings, the greatest goal is to cultivate a home environment characterized by racing in good deeds, balanced hope and fear, and deep humility before the Creator. When a household is built on these qualities, it becomes a fitting receptacle for divine grace.

=91=

The surah now mentions the Virgin Mary and the miraculous birth of Jesus. The phrase “the one who guarded her chastity” is a beautiful, honoring description of Mary (Maryam). Her guarding of chastity was not limited to physical virginity; it encompassed complete moral purity, modesty, and devotion. She is the highest example of feminine piety in the Quran. The verse says, “so We breathed into her through Our angel, Gabriel.” This refers to the immaculate conception, where the angel Gabriel appeared to Mary in the form of a man and blew into her garment or into her, by which Allah’s creative command “Be” took effect, and she conceived Jesus without a human father. This breath was not a paternal contribution; it was the means by which the divine miracle was initiated, a direct act of creation. The result was “making her and her son a sign for all peoples. ” Mary, a woman who conceived without a man, and Jesus, a prophet who spoke in the cradle and performed miracles, together constitute a sign that testifies to Allah’s power over nature and His ability to create as He wills. The sign is “for all peoples,” transcending the Children of Israel, indicating the universal significance of this miracle. This verse is a powerful refutation of both the pagan Arab denial of miraculous birth and the later Christian deification of Jesus. It affirms the miracle while keeping Jesus firmly within the category of a created being and a prophet, born of a chaste servant. The Prophet Muhammad is reminded of this profound sign, reinforcing that his own miraculous Quran is part of a long chain of divine signs meant for all humanity.

=92=

After the panoramic survey of so many prophets, the surah now addresses them collectively, and through them, all of humanity. The address “O prophets” (or “O messengers,” according to variant readings) is remarkable. It implies that in the divine communication, all prophets are addressed as a single community with a unified mission. The core message is stated: “Indeed, this religion of yours is only one. ” This is the essence of the entire chapter and indeed the entire Quran. From Noah to Abraham, to Moses, David, Solomon, Job, Jonah, Zachariah, Jesus, and finally Muhammad, the religion is fundamentally the same. It is the religion of Islam in its broadest sense: complete submission to the one true God. The differences in legal codes, rituals, and languages are superficial branches; the trunk is pure monotheism and moral guidance. Then comes the direct divine command: “and I am your Lord, so worship Me alone.” The logical link is clear: since there is only one religion, there is only one Lord, and therefore worship must be directed exclusively to Him. This verse is a powerful declaration against all forms of religious division and polytheism. For the Prophet Muhammad, it confirmed that he was not bringing a new, separate religion but was restoring and completing the same religion preached by all his brothers. For the Quraysh, it was a direct challenge: if they honored Abraham, they must follow his monotheistic religion. For the believers, this verse fosters a profound sense of solidarity with all genuine followers of the prophets throughout history. The unity of religion is a source of strength, and the exclusive worship of Allah is the foundation of human dignity and freedom from servitude to created things.

=93=

Despite the clear divine mandate of religious unity, the verse laments the historical reality: “Yet the people have divided it into sects.” The “people” refers to the followers of the prophets, the communities that came after them. After the passing of their messenger and the passage of time, they fragmented their one religion into numerous sects, each claiming exclusivity and often accusing others of misguidance. This division is a human innovation, a deviation from the pristine unity commanded by Allah. The causes of division are many: jealousy, love of power, blind following, misinterpretation of scripture, and the influence of cultural prejudices. The Quran and the Prophet Muhammad frequently warned against this sectarianism, urging Muslims to hold fast to the rope of Allah and not be divided.

The verse then closes with a sobering reminder: “But to Us they will all return.” This return to Allah is for judgment. Every sect, every group, every individual who divided the religion will stand before the One Lord and be questioned. The truth will be made clear, and those who divided for worldly or egotistical reasons will face the consequences of their actions.

This statement is both a comfort and a warning. It comforts the true believers who uphold the one religion and are grieved by the divisions, assuring them that ultimate justice rests with Allah. It warns those who cause and perpetuate divisions without sincere scholarly basis, reminding them that they will answer to the One who commanded unity. The verse calls for a conscious effort to minimize division by returning to the original sources, the Quran and the authentic Sunnah, and by maintaining a spirit of brotherhood despite legitimate differences of scholarly interpretation.

=94=

After the warning about sectarian division, the verse opens a door of hope and clarifies the standard of acceptance with Allah. The standard is twofold: belief and good deeds. “Whoever does good” refers to righteous actions, whether prayer, charity, justice, or kindness. But action alone is not sufficient; the person must also be “a believer. ” Faith is the soul of the deed; without faith, good deeds are like a body without life. This combination of iman and amal salih is the consistent Quranic condition for salvation. The verse promises that such a person “will never be denied the reward for their striving.

” The word “never” is emphatic, a divine guarantee. No effort, however small, will be lost or unappreciated.

This is a direct rebuttal to any notion that salvation is restricted to a particular tribe, sect, or label. The field is open: whoever meets the universal criteria of sincere faith and righteous action, regardless of their historical or social context, will find their reward secure with Allah. The last phrase, “for We are recording it all,” reinforces the guarantee with the certainty of divine record-keeping. Every prayer, every act of charity, every moment of patience, every struggle against temptation is meticulously inscribed by the angels who never miss a thing. This awareness is intended to motivate the believer to fill their record with good, especially in the face of those who divide the religion. Instead of engaging in sectarian disputes, the verse directs the believer’s energy toward personal faith and righteous action, leaving the judgment to the All-Knowing Recorder. It fosters a hopeful, action-oriented spirituality grounded in the absolute justice and mercy of Allah.

=95=

This verse transitions to a discussion of the end times and the finality of divine punishment for those destroyed nations. The statement “It is impossible for a society which We have destroyed to ever rise again” refers to the people whom Allah annihilated due to their disbelief and iniquity — the people of Noah, ‘Ad, Thamud, Lot, and others. Their destruction was total, and they will not return to this world for a second chance at life. This refutes the pagan Arab notion that after death, there is nothing, or that souls might reincarnate or return. It also emphasizes the finality of Allah’s judgment in this world for those who have reached a point of no return. The apparent contradiction with the concept of resurrection is resolved by noting that “rise again” here means returning to the worldly life, not the Hereafter. They will indeed be resurrected on the Day of Judgment for accounting, but they will not come back to the earthly existence to make amends. The verse serves as a stern warning to the Quraysh, telling them that the destroyed nations they pass by in their travels are gone forever, and if they persist, they too could face a destruction from which there is no return to worldly power. It also carries a deep philosophical point about the precious, irreplaceable nature of the current life as the only arena for earning one’s eternal fate. There is no second chance after death or after collective destruction. This urgency is meant to shake the heedless from their complacency, as mentioned in the opening verses. The verse forms a crucial link to the next verses, which describe the release of Gog and Magog as a sign of the approaching Hour and the finality of the disbelievers’ doom.

=96=

The surah introduces the apocalyptic figure of Gog and Magog (Ya’juj and Ma’juj). Their story is mentioned in detail in Surah Al-Kahf, where a righteous king, Dhul-Qarnayn, built a massive iron barrier to contain their destructive raids. The verse here says that the destroyed nations will not rise again “until Gog and Magog have broken loose from the barrier.

” The word “until” connects the previous verse to the events of the end times. The release of Gog and Magog is one of the major signs of the approaching Hour. They will break through the barrier that held them for centuries and will “swarm down from every hill.

” This description paints a terrifying picture of an overwhelming, unstoppable horde pouring forth from all directions, spreading chaos and corruption across the earth. They are a vast number of people known for their ferocity and destruction. Their release signals the near end of the world as we know it. The verse serves a dual purpose. First, it is a proof of the Quran’s knowledge of the unseen, giving a specific future event that will occur. Second, it is a warning to all humanity that the current order of the world is not permanent; a time will come when divine restraint will be lifted, and immense tribulations will sweep the earth. This ties directly to the heedlessness mentioned in the first verses; people are turning away, but the forces that will overwhelm them are as real and certain as the dawn.

The verse teaches believers to be prepared for the great trials of the end times by strengthening their faith and holding fast to righteousness before the great upheavals make such preparation impossible.

=97=

The release of Gog and Magog directly precedes the fulfillment of the True Promise, which is the Day of Judgment. They are the vanguard of the final upheaval. When the Hour is established, the scene shifts dramatically to the disbelievers.

The word “behold!” (fa-idha) indicates a sudden, shocking change of reality. Their state is one of staring in horror, eyes wide with terror, fixed on the unimaginable scene before them. Their cry echoes the cry of the destroyed nations in earlier verses: “Oh, woe to us! We have truly been heedless of this.

In fact, we have been wrongdoers. ” This is the ultimate confession, but it is far too late. The phrase “heedless of this” directly recalls the opening verse of the surah, “yet they are heedlessly turning away.” The circle is complete. The very heedlessness they indulged in during their worldly life becomes the source of their eternal regret. They admit to two things: their heedlessness of the warning and their status as wrongdoers. The admission is clear and unambiguous, stripping away all the excuses, mockery, and arguments they used to dismiss the messengers.

The repetition of “woe to us” throughout the surah (verses 14, 46, and now 97) creates a haunting refrain, a warning that persistently calls out to the listener not to become the one who utters these words when it is too late. The verse is a masterful piece of narrative symmetry, bringing the themes of warning, heedlessness, and belated confession to a climax, urging immediate repentance before the day when the cry will bring no relief.

=98=

The surah now delivers a crushing verdict upon the disbelievers and their false gods. The address is direct: “Certainly you disbelievers and whatever you worship instead of Allah will be the fuel of Hell.

” The word “fuel” (hasab) originally means the kindling or firewood thrown into a furnace to keep it burning. Both the worshippers and the worshipped — the idols of stone or wood, and even the false deities among the jinn or humans who accepted worship — will be cast into the Fire as its fuel.

This is the ultimate humiliation for the idols: they, who were set up as divine, are reduced to mere firewood. The phrase “You are all bound to enter it” leaves no room for escape. The polytheists believed that their idols might intercede for them or that they themselves were too honored to be punished. This verse extinguishes that hope completely. The verse echoes the scene of Abraham smashing the idols; just as the idols could not defend themselves then, they cannot defend themselves or their worshippers from Hell.

This is a powerful psychological attack on the foundation of shirk. It teaches the absolute principle of Tawheed: anything worshipped besides Allah is not only powerless to help but will itself become a part of the punishment. The verse is harsh but just. It aims to save its hearers by presenting the horrific end with stark clarity.

For the believer, it reinforces the duty to call people away from all forms of false worship, knowing the catastrophic eternal consequences. The imagery of “fuel” also reminds us that the Fire of Hell is a real, consuming entity, and those who enter it will be consumed in a cycle of perpetual punishment, as the next verses will describe.

=99=

The logic of monotheism is presented with devastating simplicity. “Had those idols been true gods, they would not have entered it.” A true god would be self-sufficient, powerful, and honored. The fact that these idols will be cast into the Fire alongside their worshippers is the ultimate proof that they are not divine. If they possessed any real power or deserved any worship, they would have been able to save themselves from such an end. Their entry into Hell is the definitive empirical refutation of their divinity. This addresses the pagans’ last shred of hope that their gods might somehow be real and able to rescue them. On the Day of Judgment, the falsehood will be exposed not through argument but through observable reality.

The verse then states, “And they will be there forever.” This eternity applies to the idols, their worshippers, and the false deities. For human and jinn worshippers, the punishment for shirk and disbelief, if they die without repentance, is eternal abode in Hell, as established in the Quran.

The mention of the idols’ eternal presence there emphasizes their permanence as symbols of the eternal nature of the punishment; they are not burned up and annihilated but remain as part of the torment. Some scholars clarify that lifeless idols will be given a form of life so that they can feel the pain and so that their worshippers can see their humiliation. The verse is a final, crushing blow to any remaining pagan sentiment among the Quraysh and a clear call to the absolute worship of the one God who is far above entering any fire, the Ever-Living, the Self-Sustaining.

=100=

The surah closes the description of the people of Hell with a vivid sensory image. “In it they will groan” conveys the continuous, involuntary sound of extreme agony and distress.

The word translated as “groan” (zafir) refers to a deep, heavy exhale, the kind emitted from the depths of a tormented chest. This is not a calm existence; it is an unceasing state of suffering where relief is not even a distant hope.

The second phrase, “and will not be able to hear,” has layers of meaning. It may mean that their own groaning and the roaring of the Fire are so loud that they cannot hear anything else, including any possible call of mercy or reprieve. It may also mean that they are deaf to anything that could bring them comfort; their auditory senses are filled entirely with the sounds of punishment. Some commentators add that they will not hear anything from the outside world, completely cut off from any source of joy or communication. The combination of continuous groaning and inability to hear paints a picture of absolute isolation within torment. There is no companionship in misery, only a cacophony of shared suffering that drowns out everything else.

This is the final destination of the heedlessness and mockery described at the beginning of the surah. The contrast with the people of Paradise, described in the very next verse, is stark: the people of Paradise will not even hear the faintest sound of Hell, and they will have whatever their souls desire. The surah thus masterfully uses vivid, sensory contrasts to motivate the listener to seek one destination and flee from the other. The verse is a sobering conclusion to the passage of warning, driving home the urgency of the opening call.
