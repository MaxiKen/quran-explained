=101=

After the terrifying description of the groaning inhabitants of the Fire, the surah immediately turns to the contrasting fate of the righteous. The verse opens with a statement of absolute assurance: “Surely those for whom We have destined the finest reward.

” The word “destined” translates “sabaqat” which conveys a preceding decree from Allah, a preordained good that has already been written for them in the Preserved Tablet. This destiny is based on Allah’s eternal knowledge of their sincere faith and righteous deeds. The “finest reward” (al-husna) is understood by all commentators to be Paradise and the beatific vision of Allah’s countenance, the ultimate good that eclipses all other blessings.

The verse then declares that these people “will be kept far away from Hell.” The phrase indicates a complete spatial and experiential separation. They will not merely be at a distance; they will be actively kept away, protected by divine grace.

The concept of being “far away” is both physical and psychological. Their hearts will be entirely free from the fear and horror of the Fire. This mirrors the Quranic promise that the righteous will not hear the faintest sound of it, as the next verse elaborates. The verse provides a profound sense of security for the believer. The destiny of the finest reward is not an arbitrary selection; it is earned through a life of faith, and it culminates in a salvation that removes every trace of anxiety. For the Prophet’s companions who endured relentless persecution in Mecca, this verse was a balm, assuring them that their present suffering was a prelude to an eternal position of safety and honor. It also implicitly calls the heedless to reflect: while they mock, there are those for whom the finest reward has already been decreed, and they will be shielded from the catastrophe they ridicule.

=102=

The separation of the righteous from Hell is described with extraordinary sensory precision. “Not even hearing the slightest hissing from it” means that the sense of hearing, which is often the first to perceive danger, will receive absolutely no signal of the Fire’s presence.

The word “hissing” translates “hasis,” which refers to a low, faint sound, like the sizzle of a flame or the whisper of movement. The people of Paradise will not be subjected to even this minimal auditory reminder of the torment. This complete sensory isolation indicates a perfect peace of mind; their environment is so thoroughly shielded that the very existence of Hell, though real, will not intrude upon their consciousness in any distressing way.

The verse then reveals the positive side of their state: “And they will delight forever in what their souls desire.” This is the ultimate fulfillment. Paradise is not merely a place of generic pleasures; it is a realm where the deepest, most personal and purified desires of the soul are continuously satisfied. Whatever a righteous soul yearns for—be it reunion with loved ones, knowledge, beauty, tranquility, or the vision of the Lord—will be granted.

The word “forever” (khalidun) seals the deal with permanence, eliminating the fear of loss that mars even the greatest worldly pleasures. The verse draws a stark contrast with the people of Hell, who groan in agony and cannot hear. The believers, in contrast, enjoy a silence broken only by peace and the fulfillment of every heartfelt longing.

This teaching has always inspired Muslims to orient their desires toward what is permissible and noble, knowing that purified desires will be eternally fulfilled, while base desires, if not repented from, lead to a place where not even the faintest sound of relief is heard.

=103=

The surah now mentions a specific event of the Day of Judgment: the “Supreme Horror” (al-faza‘ al-akbar). This refers to the overwhelming terror that will seize all creation when the Trumpet is blown, the heavens are split, and the universe collapses. It is a panic so immense that every nursing mother will forget her child and every pregnant female will drop her burden, as described in other surahs.

Yet, the verse promises that this supreme terror “will not disturb them. ” The righteous will be in a state of such tranquility that the cosmic upheaval around them will not cause them fear or grief. Their hearts are secure in the knowledge of their Lord’s pleasure.

This is a special divine gift: immunity from the greatest psychological shockwave that will ever occur. Instead of terror, they are met with a completely different experience: “the angels will greet them. ” The angels, descending from every gate, will approach them with peace and salutations, conveying congratulations and welcome. The greeting “This is your Day, which you have been promised” transforms the Day of Judgment from a court of dread into a day of celebration for them. The promise they heard from their prophets in the world—that a day of eternal reward awaits the believers—is now visibly, tangibly realized. The verse thus provides a beautiful frame: the very same Day that is the source of ultimate horror for the disbelievers is a day of joyous reunion and honor for the believers. This reinforces the Quran’s message that the objective reality of the Hereafter is filtered through the state of one’s own soul. For the believers, the instruction is to live in such a way that the thought of that Day brings not dread but anticipation, by believing in the promise and preparing for it with righteous deeds.

=104=

This verse describes one of the most dramatic physical transformations of the universe on the Day of Judgment. “We will roll up the heavens like a scroll of writings” is a powerful visual simile. The vast, seemingly infinite expanse of the sky, with its stars and galaxies, will be folded up as effortlessly as a scribe rolls up a finished parchment.

The mention of a “scroll of writings” (sijill) evokes the image of a document that has served its purpose and is now closed and put away. The heavens, which were spread out as a sign and a canopy, will be deconstructed with ease by their Creator. This demonstrates that the physical world is not eternal; it is a contingent creation that will be terminated when its term ends.

The verse then draws a logical parallel to refute the disbelievers’ skepticism about resurrection: “Just as We produced the first creation, so shall We reproduce it.” The initial act of creating everything from nothing is, by logical necessity, a greater or at least equally great act of power compared to recreating it.

Therefore, the One who did the first can certainly do the second. This argument, repeated throughout the Quran, appeals to human reason.

The verse concludes with emphatic assurance: “That is a promise binding on Us. We truly uphold Our promises! ” Allah binds Himself by His own word, not because of any external compulsion, but as an expression of His absolute truthfulness and mercy.

The phrase “binding on Us” conveys an unshakeable certainty. The believers are called to internalize this promise so deeply that no doubt can touch their hearts.

The verse also connects beautifully to the earlier mention of the books and records; just as the scrolls of deeds will be laid open, the scroll of the universe will be rolled up, and a new creation will unfold.

=105=

This verse reveals a profound divine decree that was inscribed in the scriptures given to earlier prophets. The phrase “following the heavenly Record” refers to the Preserved Tablet (al-Lawh al-Mahfuz), the original divine register from which all sacred scriptures descend. From that heavenly source, Allah decreed and wrote down in the revealed Scriptures—such as the Torah, the Psalms, and the Quran itself—the promise: “My righteous servants shall inherit the land.” This exact phrase is found in the Psalms (Zabur) of David, specifically Psalm 37:29: “The righteous shall inherit the land and dwell upon it forever. ” The Quran confirms and universalizes this promise. The “land” here can be understood in two complementary ways. First, it refers to the earth in this world; the righteous, though often persecuted, will ultimately be granted authority and succession, as the Muslim community later experienced. Second, and more profoundly, it refers to the “land” of Paradise, the eternal inheritance of the believers. In the Hadith, the Prophet explained that the “land” is the land of Paradise. This interpretation aligns with the immediate context of the Hereafter. The verse thus bridges the two worlds: the righteous will inherit both the moral and physical successorship on earth and the everlasting gardens of the Hereafter. For the persecuted believers in Mecca, this was a powerful promise. They were a small, landless, and oppressed group, yet the ancient scriptures had prophesied their ultimate inheritance. This verse elevates the discourse, showing that the Quran is in harmony with the earlier revelations, fulfilling their promises. It underscores that righteousness, not wealth or power, is the true key to inheritance, a principle that stands in stark contrast to the values of the Quraysh.

=106=

After the grand cosmic promises, the verse returns to the immediate value of the Quran itself. It declares, “Surely this Quran is sufficient as a reminder.

” The word “sufficient” (balagh) also carries the meaning of a clear proclamation, a message that reaches its intended audience completely. The Quran, in its entirety, contains all the reminders, warnings, laws, stories, and arguments that a human being needs to achieve guidance and salvation. It does not require supplementation by any other source of ultimate truth.

The phrase “for those devoted to worship” defines the receptive audience. The Quran’s sufficiency is actualized specifically for people who are already oriented toward worshipping Allah, who are engaged in devotion and are seeking the truth with sincerity. For such hearts, the Quran is a complete and self-sufficient guide. It answers their deepest questions, heals their spiritual ailments, and directs them to the straight path. This verse is a testament to the comprehensiveness of the Quran. It also subtly addresses the pagans’ demand for different kinds of signs; the answer is that the Book itself is the ultimate, sufficient sign. The Prophet is thus reminded that he does not need to worry about those who turn away; the reminder is fully present and effective for its intended recipients. For the believers, this verse instills a deep reliance on the Quran as the primary source of guidance. It encourages a lifelong engagement with its verses, understanding that every era, every problem, and every spiritual need can find its answer therein. The condition is to be among the “devoted to worship,” those who approach the Book not as a literary curiosity but as a life-giving message from their Lord, read with humility and a yearning for guidance.

=107=

This is one of the most beautiful and comprehensive descriptions of the Prophet Muhammad’s mission. The verse addresses him directly with a statement of profound compassion. “We have sent you only as a mercy” uses the particle “illā” which indicates exclusivity. His primary, overarching quality and purpose is mercy. Everything about his mission—his teachings, his example, his intercession, the legislation he brought—is a manifestation of divine mercy.

The phrase “for the whole world” (lil-‘ālamīn) expands this mercy to encompass all of creation: humans, jinn, animals, the environment, and even the angels. His mercy is not confined to the believers alone; even the disbelievers benefit from the mercy of the Prophet’s presence. Because of him, the immediate and total destruction that wiped out previous nations was withheld from his rejecters. His mercy extended to captives, enemies, children, and the natural world. A famous hadith illustrates that when the Prophet was asked to curse the polytheists, he replied, “I was not sent as a curser, but I was sent as a mercy.” Even in the conquest of Mecca, those who had persecuted him for years were granted amnesty. His mercy is also seen in the balanced and compassionate law he brought, which provides ease, forgiveness, and gradual reform. The verse refutes any notion that the Prophet was sent as a harsh warner only. The warning is itself a mercy, alerting people to danger. The believers are thus called to embody this mercy in their own behavior, to be merciful to all of Allah’s creation, following the example of the one sent as a mercy to the worlds. This verse provides the ultimate context for all the warnings in the surah: even the descriptions of Hell are a merciful call to avoid it.

=108=

The Prophet is instructed to boil down his entire message to its absolute core and present it with crystal clarity. “Say” is a command to openly declare the essence of revelation. “What has been revealed to me is this” narrows the focus, preparing the listener for the fundamental truth that underlies everything else. That truth is the purest form of monotheism: “Your God is only One God. ” The word “only” (innamā) confines divinity exclusively to Allah. There is no partner, no offspring, no associate.

This is the same message brought by every prophet, the foundation of all faith. The verse then follows this declaration with the logical and emotional conclusion: “Will you then submit? ” The Arabic word used is “tuslimūn,” which directly links to Islam—submission to the one God.

The question is an invitation, a challenge, and a plea all at once. It appeals to the innate human disposition, which recognizes the truth of monotheism when it is presented without distortion. After all the lengthy arguments, the stories of the prophets, the descriptions of the universe, and the warnings of the Day of Judgment, the matter is brought to a single point: one God, so will you submit? This simplicity is the hallmark of the Islamic message.

The verse teaches that the invitation to Islam should be direct and focused on Tawheed. It also implies that those who persist in polytheism after this clear declaration are not just mistaken but are consciously refusing to submit to a truth they can understand. For the believers, it is a reminder to keep their own submission pure, constantly renewing their commitment to the One God, and to use this clear, unambiguous language when calling others to the faith.

=109=

The surah now addresses the scenario of rejection. After the clear call to submit to the One God, some will still turn away. The Prophet is instructed on what to say. First, he affirms that the warning has been delivered fairly and openly: “I have warned you all equally. ” The word “equally” (ʿalā sawā’) indicates that the message has been given to everyone without discrimination, without hidden knowledge for a select few, and with complete transparency. No one can claim they were not informed or that the warning was unclear.

The second part of the verse is a profound statement of prophetic humility and the limits of human knowledge: “I do not know if what you are threatened with is near or far.” The Prophet is commanded to explicitly disclaim any knowledge of the timing of the punishment. The pagans often demanded to know when the threatened torment would occur, seeking to disprove him if it was delayed. Here, the Prophet is told to openly acknowledge that the knowledge of the unseen, specifically the timing of the Hour and the punishment, belongs exclusively to Allah. This admission is a powerful refutation of any claim to divinity and a clear boundary between the Creator and the servant. It also adds urgency to the warning: since no one knows if the punishment is near, it is foolish to delay repentance.

The verse teaches believers to be content with the limits of their knowledge, to deliver the message without distortion, and to leave the timing of outcomes to Allah. The fact that the punishment might be far is not a comfort; it is a test, as the next verses will explain.

=110=

This verse reinforces the previous point by emphasizing the comprehensive knowledge of Allah, who knows both the public and the private. The pagans’ open mockery, their public challenges, and their verbal attacks are all known to Allah. But more importantly, their hidden whispers, their secret conspiracies, and the inner doubts and arrogance they conceal in their hearts are equally known. Nothing escapes His knowledge.

This is a direct response to the secret conversations mentioned earlier in the surah (verse 3), where the evildoers whispered among themselves. It reminds them that while the Prophet may not know the hidden timing of the punishment, and may not be privy to their secret talks by his own power, his Lord knows everything. Their hidden schemes are laid bare before the divine gaze, and they will be held accountable for not only their open words but also their concealed intentions. The verse serves both as a comfort to the Prophet and a warning to the disbelievers. The Prophet is comforted because he does not need to uncover their secrets; Allah, who hears all, will deal with them justly. The disbelievers are warned that their internal state is fully exposed, so pretending to be open-minded while hiding deep-seated rejection is futile.

For believers, this verse cultivates a profound sense of muraqabah, the awareness that Allah knows every thought that crosses the mind, every feeling in the heart. It encourages sincerity (ikhlas) and a deep cleansing of the inner self, knowing that one’s hidden state is as transparent to Allah as one’s outward speech. The verse seamlessly links to the next, which addresses the possible reasons for the delay of punishment.

=111=

The Prophet is instructed to continue his declaration of limited knowledge, this time speculating on the possible wisdom behind the delay of punishment. He says, “I do not know if this delay is possibly a test for you.” The “delay” refers to the fact that the promised punishment has not yet arrived. Rather than being a sign of the message’s falsehood, as the pagans assumed, the delay could be a profound test from Allah. The test is to see how they will use the extra time granted to them: will they persist in disbelief and mockery, deepening their guilt, or will they reflect and repent? The delay, therefore, is an extension of the trial of life.

The second possibility mentioned is “and an enjoyment for a while.” The delay also serves as a period of temporary enjoyment, allowing them to take pleasure in their worldly lives. This enjoyment is not a reward but a respite, a provision that will eventually run out, making the sudden arrival of the punishment all the more devastating.

The phrase “for a while” underscores the temporary nature of this worldly life, no matter how long the delay lasts. This verse teaches an essential theological principle: the delay of punishment is not evidence of divine indifference or approval, nor is it a weakness of the messenger. It is a part of the divine plan, a period of either testing or temporary provision. The Prophet Muhammad’s open admission that he does not know the precise reason for the delay in a specific case is a model of intellectual humility. It demonstrates that even a prophet does not claim to know the details of Allah’s decree except what has been revealed to him. This closes the door on any who might later claim a leader has full knowledge of the unseen. The believers are taught to always see time, whether of ease or hardship, as a test and an opportunity that will soon end.

=112=

The surah concludes with a powerful supplication that the Prophet is instructed to make, and which serves as the final word on the entire dispute. The phrase “In the end, the Prophet said” signals the closing argument, the ultimate appeal to the highest court. The prayer is “My Lord! Judge between us in truth. ” This is a request for a decisive, truthful judgment from Allah that will settle the conflict between the believers and the disbelievers once and for all. It is not a prayer for personal revenge but for the establishment of truth and justice.

The word “in truth” (bil-ḥaqq) is pivotal; the Prophet seeks a verdict based on absolute reality, not on whims or appearances. The supplication acknowledges that ultimate judgment belongs to Allah alone. Then the prayer concludes with a beautiful invocation: “And our Lord is the Most Compassionate, Whose help is sought against what you claim.” The Prophet turns to the attribute of supreme mercy, even as he seeks judgment. This indicates that even in the face of false accusations and mockery, the heart of the believer rests in the boundless compassion of Allah.

The phrase “against what you claim” refers to all the false allegations made by the pagans: that the Quran is poetry or sorcery, that Allah has partners, that there is no resurrection. The Prophet seeks help from the Most Compassionate against the harm and falsehood of these claims. The surah thus ends with a perfect balance: a call for truthful judgment and a refuge in divine compassion. It is a model for the believers in every age when faced with falsehood and slander: turn to Allah, ask for a judgment that will vindicate the truth, and seek shelter in His mercy, leaving the outcome to Him. The surah, which began with the heedlessness of people as their judgment draws near, ends with the Prophet entrusting the entire matter to the Judge, the Most Compassionate.
