=21=

After challenging the disbelievers to identify any force that could help them besides the Most Compassionate, the surah now narrows the focus to the most basic and tangible of needs: provision, or rizq. The verse asks, “Or who is it that will provide for you if He withholds His provision?” This rhetorical question forces the Quraysh to confront their utter dependency on Allah for every morsel of food, every drop of water, and every breath of life. Provision in the Quran encompasses not just food but all forms of sustenance—rain, crops, livestock, health, and even spiritual nourishment. The Meccans, who relied heavily on their trade caravans and the produce of their land, understood all too well that a season of drought or a failed caravan could spell disaster.

The question reminds them that the ultimate source of their sustenance is not their own efforts, their idols, or their trading partners, but Allah alone. If He were to withhold His provision, absolutely no one else could supply it. No idol, no intercessor, no powerful tribe could bring rain or grow a single blade of grass.

The verse then diagnoses their persistent state: “In fact, they persist in arrogance and aversion.” Despite these clear signs and logical arguments, they do not humble themselves.

The word “lajjū” means they stubbornly persist, and “ʿutuwwin” indicates a haughty, tyrannical refusal to accept the truth. “Nufūr” means a vehement turning away, like a wild animal that bolts at the sight of something it fears. Their arrogance prevents them from acknowledging their dependence; they would rather attribute their provision to their own gods or their own cleverness. This verse is a powerful remedy for the modern disease of self-reliance that makes one forget the Provider. It calls the believer to deeply internalize that every salary, every meal, every blessing is a direct provision from Allah, and its absence can occur in a heartbeat.

The verse also connects to the earlier mention of the earth being smoothed out for provision; the same Lord who provided can withhold, and there is no power that can prevent His decision. The arrogance that denies this truth is the very same arrogance that led to the destruction of previous nations.

=22=

The surah now presents a vivid metaphor that contrasts the disbeliever and the believer in the most striking visual terms. The rhetorical question “Who is rightly guided?” sets up a comparison between two kinds of people.

The first is “the one who crawls facedown” (man yamshī mukibban ʿalā wajhihi). This image is of a person who is bent over, dragging himself on his face, unable to see where he is going, constantly stumbling, and unable to move forward with purpose or stability. This represents the disbeliever, who, having rejected divine guidance, is spiritually groping in the darkness. His worldview is flawed, his path is crooked, and despite all his worldly efforts, he is heading towards destruction.

The second is “the one who walks upright on the Straight Path” (sawiyyan ʿalā ṣirāṭin mustaqīm). This person stands tall, walks with balance and clarity, and sees the path ahead clearly.

This is the believer, guided by the revelation of Allah, walking on the clear, straight path of Islam. His journey has a destination, his steps are confident, and his heart is at peace. The metaphor is profound because it shows that guidance is not just a matter of choosing different destinations; it fundamentally alters the way one navigates life itself. The disbeliever, even if he achieves worldly success, is spiritually deformed, crawling in ignorance and misguidance. The believer, even if poor and simple, walks with the dignity and uprightness of divine guidance. The reference to the “Straight Path” echoes the opening of Al-Fatihah, the prayer recited daily, reminding the believer that the Straight Path is a tangible reality of upright walking, not just an abstract concept.

This verse also serves as a reply to the Quraysh who mocked the Prophet’s followers as being lowly and misguided. The truth is the exact opposite: it is the arrogant disbelievers who are crawling in the filth of their idolatry, while the believers are walking upright towards Paradise. The verse invites every listener to assess their own stance: am I crawling through life, guided only by desires and guesses, or am I walking upright on a clear path revealed by the All-Knowing?

=23=

The Prophet, peace be upon him, is instructed to direct the attention of the disbelievers to the very origins of their existence and the faculties they use every day. “He is the One Who brought you into being” (ansha’akum) refers to the initial creation of human beings from nothing, and the ongoing development of each individual from a drop of fluid to a fully formed human.

The use of “ansha’a” implies not just creating but nurturing, raising, and developing. Then three specific faculties are singled out: hearing, sight, and intellect (al-af’idah, literally the hearts or cores of understanding). The order is significant. A newborn first hears before it sees, and its intellect develops gradually. These three faculties are the primary tools for acquiring knowledge. Hearing allows us to receive revelation, advice, and warnings; sight allows us to observe the signs in the universe; and the intellect allows us to process, reflect, and draw conclusions. The entire test of life, as mentioned in verse 2, depends on the correct use of these gifts.

The verse then ends with a stinging indictment: “Yet you hardly give any thanks.” Despite being given these precious faculties that enable existence, knowledge, and salvation, the disbelievers do not use them for their intended purpose: to recognize and worship the Creator. Instead, they use their hearing to mock the truth, their sight to admire idols, and their intellect to plot against the Prophet. Their ingratitude is profound.

For the believer, this verse is a call to a moment of deep contemplation. Each sound we hear, each sight we see, each thought we think is a loan from Allah, a trust that must be used in gratitude. The true thanks for the faculty of hearing is to listen to the Quran; for sight, to see the signs of Allah; for the intellect, to reason one’s way to iman.

The verse also serves as a reminder of the Day of Judgment when these very faculties will testify against those who misused them. It ties back to the earlier verse where the people of Hell will regret not having listened and reasoned.

Thus, verse 23 is a powerful reminder that the tools for guidance are already installed within us; the tragedy is that they are so seldom used to give thanks to their Giver.

=24=

The Prophet is commanded to deliver another statement highlighting Allah’s power and the inevitability of the resurrection. The first part, “He is the One Who has dispersed you all over the earth,” points to the incredible spread and diversification of humanity. From a single pair, Adam and Eve, Allah multiplied and scattered human beings across every continent, island, and climate. This dispersion resulted in diverse languages, colours, and cultures, which the Quran itself notes as a sign of Allah’s power (30:22).

The word “dhara’akum” carries the meaning of multiplying and spreading like seeds scattered on the ground. This is a miracle that the pagan Arabs took for granted: the presence of different tribes and peoples in far-flung lands was a direct result of divine decree and creative power.

The second part, “and to Him you will all be gathered,” turns this observation of the beginning into a proof of the end. The One who had the power to scatter humanity from a single origin to every corner of the earth is certainly capable of reversing that process, gathering every single human being back together for judgment. There is no scattered atom, no decomposed bone, no lost identity that is beyond His ability to reconstitute. For the Meccan idolaters who denied the physical resurrection, this verse was a direct refutation. It uses their own daily observation of human diversity to argue for the logical conclusion: He who originated and dispersed can easily gather. This also serves as a warning: your dispersion on earth is temporary; do not become so attached to your particular piece of land or your worldly travels that you forget you are heading towards a single, inescapable gathering. For the believers, the verse instills a sense of universal brotherhood: all humans originate from one Creator and are heading to one destination. It also imparts a profound sense of accountability. The fact that we will all be gathered, regardless of where we die, means that no one escapes the final appointment. The verse seamlessly links the power of Allah in creation with the justice of Allah in resurrection, urging immediate preparation for that gathering.

=25=

Despite the overwhelming signs and logical arguments presented in the surah, the disbelievers persist in their mockery. This verse records one of their favorite challenges: they would ask the believers, and especially the Prophet, to specify the exact timing of the promised Day of Judgment.

The question “When will this threat come to pass?” was not a sincere inquiry; it was a sarcastic and defiant taunt. By demanding a precise date, they thought they could expose the Prophet as a false warner if he could not provide one, or they could continue in their disbelief because the punishment seemed far off.

The phrase “if what you say is true” drips with condescension and doubt. They thought that the delay in punishment was proof that it would never come. This reflects a deep misunderstanding of divine wisdom. Allah does not operate on human schedules; His timing is perfect and based on His knowledge. The delay is actually a mercy, giving them time to repent. The believers in Mecca, who were a persecuted minority, faced this taunt regularly. The verse captures the psychological warfare of the disbelievers, adding to the distress of the faithful. The response, which follows in the next verse, is a powerful lesson in humility before divine knowledge.

The verse also serves as a timeless portrait of the skeptic’s mindset. Even today, many demand to see scientific proof of God or a sign of the Hour, using the lack of an immediate, tangible answer to justify their rejection.

The Quran addresses this attitude by redirecting the focus from the “when” to the “what”: instead of obsessing over the timing, one should focus on preparing for the event, which is as certain as the creation itself. The inclusion of this question in the surah validates the feeling of the believers who are mocked for their faith, and it prepares them with a divinely crafted answer that shifts the burden of proof and warning back onto the skeptics.

=26=

This verse is the direct and perfect response to the disbelievers’ taunt. The Prophet is instructed to reply that the knowledge of the Hour’s timing is exclusively with Allah. He does not possess that knowledge, nor does any angel or prophet.

This is a fundamental tenet of Islamic creed: the keys of the unseen are with Allah alone. The response dismantles the skeptics’ false assumption that the prophet must know everything or that his inability to specify a date discredits his message. The role of the Messenger is then clarified with beautiful simplicity: “I am only sent with a clear warning.

” The word “nadhīr” (warner) perfectly encapsulates his mission. He is not a fortune-teller or an oracle who predicts future dates. He is a warner who conveys the message of the coming judgment with absolute clarity and undeniable proofs.

The phrase “clear warning” indicates that the evidence for the truth of his warning is manifest and sufficient; the disbelievers have no excuse for ignoring it. This verse also provides immense comfort to the Prophet and the believers. The Prophet is liberated from the burden of answering a question that is not his to answer, and the believers are taught the limits of human knowledge and the importance of focusing on the warning itself, not on the timing. It reorients the conversation from speculative curiosity to urgent moral and spiritual preparation. The fact that the timing is hidden is itself part of the test, keeping humanity in a state of watchfulness. If the exact date were known, the psychology of faith would be completely different.

The verse also connects back to the opening of the surah, where Allah is described as the One in Whose Hands rests all authority, and He is Most Capable of everything. The authority over the Hour is part of that absolute dominion. The believer’s response to any question about the end of the world should be rooted in this verse: the knowledge is with Allah, and my duty is to heed the clear warning and prepare accordingly.

=27=

Having addressed the question of “when,” the surah now paints the scene of “when” it actually happens. The verse describes the moment the disbelievers see the promised punishment approaching—whether at the time of death, the minor signs, or the major events of the Day of Judgment.

The phrase “drawing near” (zulfatan) indicates that it is no longer a distant, mocked threat; it is imminent and visible. The immediate physical reaction is that “the faces of the disbelievers will become gloomy. ” The word “sī’at” means they become ugly, darkened, and twisted with fear, grief, and humiliation. The light and arrogance that once shone on their faces are completely extinguished, replaced by a palpable expression of terror and despair.

This is the reversal of their worldly state, where they mocked the believers and walked with pride. Then comes the verbal humiliation: “and it will be said to them, ‘This is what you claimed would never come.’” The voice could be that of the angels, or a general declaration from the divine presence. It is a direct echo of their own mocking words, now used to rub salt into their wounds. The punishment they denied so vehemently is now their inescapable reality.

The phrase “what you claimed” (mā kuntum bihi tamtarūn) refers to their previous state of doubt and denial. This moment is the ultimate vindication of the Prophet’s warning and the ultimate regret of the disbelievers. For the believers, this verse is both a reassurance and a warning. It reassures them that the mockery they endure will one day be answered with undeniable truth, and the mockers will be shamed. It also warns them to never allow themselves to become like those who doubt the coming judgment. The vivid description of the gloomy faces serves as a deterrent, motivating the heart to stay firmly on the straight path so that on that Day, one’s face will be radiant with the light of faith, not darkened with the gloom of denial.

=28=

The surah returns to a rhetorical challenge, instructing the Prophet to present the disbelievers with a thought experiment that exposes their hopelessness. He is to ask them to consider two possible outcomes for himself and his followers: death or mercy. “Whether Allah causes me and those with me to die” refers to the possibility that the Prophet and the believers might be killed or die naturally before seeing victory or the punishment of their enemies. “Or shows us mercy” refers to the possibility that Allah spares them, grants them victory, or allows them to witness the downfall of disbelief. The critical question then is: in either scenario, “who will save the disbelievers from a painful punishment?” If the believers die, their death does not benefit the disbelievers one bit; the punishment of the Hereafter awaits them regardless. If the believers are shown mercy and victory, the disbelievers face both worldly defeat and the torment of the next life. So the disbelievers are trapped in a lose-lose situation. Their mockery and their hopes that the Prophet’s death would end his message are futile. This verse was a direct rebuttal to the Meccan pagans who wished for the Prophet’s death, thinking his message would die with him and they would be spared. The verse tells them: even if he died, your problem remains, because the punishment is from Allah, not from Muhammad. The Prophet’s death or survival is irrelevant to your inevitable doom if you persist in disbelief.

The verse also serves as a powerful statement of tawakkul (trust) for the believers. Their fate is in Allah’s hands; whether they live or die, they are under His mercy. The disbelievers, however, have no such safety net. This challenge deepens the psychological defeat of the skeptics, leaving them with no logical or emotional escape from the reality of the coming punishment. It turns their own wish for the Prophet’s death into a rhetorical trap, showing that such a wish, if granted, brings them no advantage whatsoever. The verse is a masterpiece of rational argumentation embedded in a deeply spiritual warning.

=29=

The Prophet is then commanded to make a simple, powerful declaration of the believers’ creed, in stark contrast to the confusion and delusion of the disbelievers. “He is the Most Compassionate” (Ar-Raḥmān) is the name that encapsulates Allah’s all-encompassing mercy, the very mercy that the disbelievers deny and attribute to their false gods. The believers declare that they believe solely in this Most Compassionate Lord, and they place their exclusive trust in Him alone.

The phrase “in Him alone we believe, and in Him alone we trust” uses the verb “tawakkul,” which implies complete reliance after taking all necessary means. This is the essence of Islamic faith: a combination of deep inner conviction and outward reliance that frees the heart from fear of any created being. The declaration is made without arrogance, but with calm certainty.

The verse then ends with a prophetic warning: “You will soon know who is clearly astray.” This is not a vindictive statement but a solemn prophecy. The disbelievers, who had accused the Prophet and his followers of being astray, will come to see with absolute clarity who was truly on the wrong path. The “soon” can refer to the Day of Judgment, which is near in the scale of eternity, or to the immediate military and moral defeats the Quraysh were about to face. This verse echoes the structure of the end of Surah Al-Kafirun, where the Prophet says, “For you is your religion, and for me is my religion,” leaving the final judgment to Allah. It is a masterful closing to the series of rhetorical questions. It shifts from argument to declaration, from challenge to proclamation. The believer, reciting this verse, is reminded to root his identity in Ar-Raḥmān, to refresh his faith and trust, and to remain steadfast in the face of mockery, knowing that the ultimate truth of who was astray will be revealed in the most undeniable manner. The verse is a fortress of peace for the heart, anchoring it in the mercy and protection of the Most Compassionate.

=30=

The surah concludes with a final, brilliant rhetorical question that brings the argument back to the most basic, everyday necessity: water. The Prophet is instructed to ask the disbelievers to consider their water source—the wells, springs, and rivers they relied on for life. The Meccans, living in the arid valley, depended on the well of Zamzam and other aquifers. Allah tells them to imagine if that water were to “sink into the earth,” a phenomenon known as “ghawr,” where the water table suddenly drops and the water disappears deep underground, utterly inaccessible.

The question: “then who else could bring you flowing water?” No human power, no idol, no scientific technology can create water or bring it back if Allah causes it to vanish.

The word “maʿīn” describes fresh, flowing, easily accessible water. The loss of such water would mean certain death.

The question exposes the complete vulnerability and powerlessness of humanity before Allah. It ties back to the opening of the surah, where Allah is described as the One in Whose Hands rests all authority. The water they drink is under His direct command. This final verse serves as a profound seal to the surah’s message. It began with the creation of life and death, and it ends with the life-sustaining element of water, which is itself a testimony to resurrection. The One who brings forth water from the dead earth can bring forth the dead from their graves. The verse is also a powerful reminder to every human being that the most mundane blessings we take for granted—like a glass of clean water—are miracles of divine provision. If Allah withheld this single blessing, no one could replace it. The disbelievers are left with no answer, their arrogance shattered by a simple question about the water they drink every day.

For the believer, this verse instills a deep sense of gratitude and fear. Every sip of water becomes an act of worship, a recognition of the Most Compassionate’s continuous mercy. The surah thus closes with a question that resonates in the conscience, urging all who hear it to recognize the complete dependence of creation on the Creator, and to submit to the One who holds every drop of water, and every soul, in His blessed hands.
