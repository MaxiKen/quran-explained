=21=

This verse completes the diagnosis of the human spiritual condition that began in the previous verse. After stating that the deniers love this fleeting world, Allah declares that they “neglect the Hereafter.

” The Arabic word for neglect here carries a sense of leaving something behind deliberately, of abandoning it and turning away from it. The two verses together paint a complete picture: the heart is attached to what is immediate and visible, and consequently it is detached from what is to come. The love of the worldly life and the neglect of the Hereafter are two sides of the same coin. One cannot fill the heart with love for this transient world and still have room for the concern about the eternal life. The neglect of the Hereafter manifests as a failure to prepare for it, a lack of reflection upon its realities, an absence of fear and hope regarding its outcomes, and ultimately a life lived as if death and judgment will never arrive. The verse explains the root cause of the disbelief, the mockery, and the denial of resurrection described earlier in this surah and the previous ones. The disbelievers are not primarily in doubt because of a lack of intellectual evidence. Rather, their hearts are so immersed in the pleasures and pursuits of this world that they have pushed the thought of the Hereafter to the margins of their consciousness. It is a psychological and spiritual neglect, not an intellectual one.

The pairing of love and neglect is profoundly instructive. Human nature abhors a vacuum. If the love of the eternal does not occupy the heart, the love of the temporal will flood in and take over. The verse is a call to examine one’s own priorities. Does the weekly prayer, the daily remembrance, the preparation for death, take second place to the endless chase for money, status, and entertainment? The neglect of the Hereafter is not a passive oversight; it is an active choice repeated daily. The surah is building toward the contrasting images of faces that will be bright and faces that will be gloomy, which are the direct results of where the heart placed its love.

=22=

Having described the two opposing attitudes in the world, the surah now unveils the results in the Hereafter. “On that Day some faces will be bright.

” The Arabic “wujuhun yawma’idhin nadirah” refers to faces that are radiant, glowing with light, freshness, and beauty. The word “nadirah” implies a lush, blooming quality, like a flourishing garden. It is the face of someone who is profoundly happy, at peace, and honored. The brightness is not merely a physical glow; it is an outward manifestation of inner purity, faith, and the good news of salvation. In the world, these faces may have been tired from night prayers, dusty from poverty, or marked with the traces of tears shed in fear of Allah. But on that Day, they will shine like the full moon. This brightness is a reward for their love of the Hereafter over the fleeting world. They neglected the temporary pleasures and sought the eternal, so Allah grants them an eternal radiance.

The verse also serves as a powerful visual contrast. The listener is invited to imagine the scene: a vast plain of resurrection, and among the multitudes, some faces are illuminated with a light that sets them apart. This light is described elsewhere as the light of faith that will walk before them and on their right, as they cross the Bridge over Hell. The brightness is also a sign of recognition and honor; the angels and the prophets will know them by this light. For the believers who hear this verse, it is a source of immense hope. It tells them that every hardship endured for the sake of Allah, every night spent in prayer, every act of charity done in secret, is accumulating as light for that Day. The verse calls the believer to live in such a way that his face will be among the bright ones, not through cosmetics or worldly beauty, but through the inner light of iman.

=23=

This verse is one of the greatest and most beautiful promises in the entire Quran, and it explains the ultimate source of the brightness on those faces: “looking at their Lord.” The Arabic “ila rabbiha nadhirah” means they will gaze upon their Lord.

The word “nadhirah” comes from “nazar,” meaning to look, and the construction implies a continuous, pleasant, expectant gaze. The believers in Paradise will be granted the supreme honor of seeing Allah, the Exalted.

This is the beatific vision, the greatest reward of Paradise, surpassing all other pleasures. The Prophet, peace be upon him, confirmed this in authentic hadith, stating that the believers will see their Lord as clearly as they see the full moon on a cloudless night, without any doubt or difficulty. This vision will be a source of such overwhelming joy and delight that all the other pleasures of the Gardens will pale in comparison. When the believers gaze upon the Face of their Creator, they will forget every hardship they ever endured. The verse refutes those who claim that Allah cannot be seen. The wording is clear: the faces are looking at their Lord. The preposition “ila” (toward) indicates direction. The early Muslims, the Ahl al-Sunnah, firmly believed in the reality of this vision without asking how it occurs or imagining any physical resemblance to creation.

The verse also provides a profound contrast with the previous warnings. The deniers neglected the Hereafter and loved the world; now, in the Hereafter, they will be veiled from seeing Allah, which is the greatest deprivation. The believers, who loved Allah and yearned to meet Him, are granted the vision.

The word “nadhirah” also carries a sense of freshness and radiance, which ties back to the brightness in the previous verse. The light on their faces is a reflection of the Light they are beholding. The faces are bright because they are looking at their Lord. The verse is an invitation to live for that moment. Every act of worship, every restraint from sin, is a step closer to the vision of the Most Merciful. The heart that truly believes in this promise will not be overly attached to the glitter of this world, for it awaits a far greater sight.

=24=

The surah now presents the stark opposite. “And other faces will be gloomy.

” The Arabic “wa wujuhun yawma’idhin basirah” means faces that are dark, scowling, frowning, and grim. The word “basirah” comes from “busur,” which describes a face that is contracted, harsh, and expressing intense distress. These are the faces of the deniers, the ones who loved the fleeting world and neglected the Hereafter. Their inner state of terror, regret, and despair is now printed on their features. There is no light, no hope, no beauty. The gloom is the external manifestation of the darkness of disbelief and sin that filled their hearts. Just as the believers’ faces are bright with the reflection of the divine vision, the disbelievers’ faces are dark with the anticipation of punishment and the veil from Allah. The verse creates a powerful binary. On that Day, there will be no neutral faces. Every face will be either bright or gloomy. The dividing line is faith and deeds. The gloom is not merely a temporary emotion; it is a permanent mark of their wretched state. In the world, they might have walked with pride and smiled with arrogance, but on that Day, their faces will betray their true condition. The verse is a warning to those who wear a mask of happiness in this world while their hearts are empty of faith. The mask will fall, and the reality will appear.

For the believer, this verse instills a healthy fear and a deep gratitude. It is a reminder to seek the radiance of faith and to avoid the deeds that cause the face to darken.

The mention of gloomy faces immediately leads to the description of what they are anticipating, which is the subject of the next verse. The contrast is complete: brightness and vision for one group, gloom and impending doom for the other.

=25=

The surah now reveals what is causing the gloom on those faces: they are “anticipating something devastating to befall them.” The Arabic “tadhunnu an yuf‘ala biha faqirah” means they are certain, they expect with dread, that a catastrophic, spine-breaking punishment is about to be inflicted upon them.

The word “faqirah” comes from “faqr,” meaning to break the vertebrae of the back. It describes a calamity so severe that it snaps the spine. The faces are not just sad; they are frozen in the horrified expectation of an imminent, crushing disaster. They know, with absolute certainty, that they are about to be thrown into the Fire, and the sheer terror of that approaching doom is already written on their faces.

This is the state of the criminal who has been sentenced and is awaiting the executioner. Every second is an eternity of dread.

The word “tadhunnu” here means certainty, not mere suspicion, because on that Day, all doubts are removed. They see the angels of punishment, they see Hell, and they know their destination. The anticipation itself is a punishment. The contrast with the believers is profound. The believers are looking at their Lord with radiant faces, in a state of peaceful bliss. The disbelievers are looking at the approaching punishment with gloomy faces, in a state of spine-breaking fear. The verse encapsulates the essence of the Day of Judgment: it is the day of final separation. The one who lives in this world fearing the punishment of the Hereafter, as described in Al-Ma‘arij, is saved from this terror. The one who lived secure and mocking is now shattered by fear. The “devastating” thing is exactly what the prophets warned about, what the Quran described, what they denied and laughed at. Now it is no longer a distant threat; it is a present, overwhelming reality.

The verse ends the general description of the Day and leads into the specific moment of death, where the transition to this gloomy state begins. It is a call to take the warning seriously before the moment of anticipation becomes the moment of infliction.

=26=

The surah now shifts to a terrifyingly intimate scene: the moment of death for a denier. “But no! Beware of the day when the soul reaches the collar bone as it leaves.

” The Arabic “kalla idha balaghat al-taraqee” uses “kalla” to jolt the listener out of any remaining heedlessness. The soul, at the time of death, rises up through the body and reaches the “taraqee,” which are the bones of the upper chest, the collarbones.

This is the final stage of the soul’s extraction. The imagery is graphic and visceral. The person is on his deathbed, and the soul is being pulled out. The family stands around helplessly. The “collar bone” signifies the point of no return; the soul is at the exit door of the body. The verse does not say “when a person dies,” it zooms in on that precise, agonizing moment.

This is the moment that every human being will experience. For the denier, this moment is filled with terror because he knows what awaits him. The angels of death come, and he sees his destination. The surah is inviting the listener to imagine that moment now, while there is still time to prepare.

The phrase “as it leaves” clarifies that the soul is in the process of departing. This is the moment when the worldly life is definitively over, and the Hereafter is about to begin. All the wealth, the children, the status that the denier loved are now completely useless. They cannot stop the soul from reaching the collarbone. The verse is a powerful meditation on mortality. It reminds the believer that every soul will one day reach that point. The difference is what the soul carries with it—faith or disbelief, good deeds or sins. The denier who loved the fleeting world will face this moment with horror, while the believer who longed for the meeting with his Lord will face it with peace and longing. The verse is the beginning of a dramatic narrative of death, leading to the shroud and the final march to judgment.

=27=

As the soul reaches the collarbone and the person is in the throes of death, the verse describes the desperate reaction of those around him. “And it will be said, ‘Is there any healer who can save this life?’” The Arabic “wa qeela man raq” means someone asks, “Who is a raqi?

” The word “raqi” comes from “ruqyah,” which is incantation, healing by spiritual means, or a skilled physician who can administer a cure. The people around the dying person are in a state of panic. They are looking left and right for anyone who can perform a miracle, who can recite something, who can do anything to stop the soul from departing. Some commentators say it is the angels who say this, meaning they ask, “Who can ascend with this soul and take it up?” But the predominant interpretation is that it is the grieving relatives and friends, crying out in desperation. They seek a healer, a savior, but there is none. The angel of death does not wait. The appointed time, which was delayed and denied, has now arrived.

The question “Is there any healer?” echoes the helplessness of human power in the face of divine decree. The best doctors, the most powerful medicines, the most recited spells cannot push back the soul once it has reached the collarbone. The scene exposes the futility of all worldly attachments. The denier who relied on his wealth, his tribe, his influence, now lies dying, and none of those can offer a “raqi” who can save him.

The verse also carries a subtle irony. The disbelievers used to mock the Prophet and seek refuge in their idols and their magicians. Now, at the moment of truth, they are asking for a healer, but the only true Healer is Allah, whom they rejected. The call for a healer goes unanswered. This verse is a stark reminder that death is the one appointment no one can cancel or reschedule. The believer is taught to prepare the “ruqyah” of faith and good deeds in advance, so that at the moment of death, instead of panic, there are angels with glad tidings.

=28=

At that same moment, the veil is lifted from the dying person’s own consciousness. “And the dying person realizes it is their time to depart.

” The Arabic “wa dhanna annahu al-firaq” means he is certain that this is the separation. The word “dhanna” here indicates conviction, not guesswork. The dying denier now knows with absolute certainty that he is leaving the world. The “firaq” is the parting from his wealth, his family, his body, and his earthly life. This realization is the culmination of his worldly experience. For his entire life, he denied the Hereafter and lived as if he would never die. Now, the truth slaps him in the face. The separation is final. He is crossing a border from which there is no return.

The phrase “it is their time to depart” captures the profound loneliness of death. No companion can come along. The person is separated from everything he loved and hoarded. The verse exposes the psychological state of the denier in that moment. It is not a peaceful acceptance but a horrified recognition. The certainty that he mocked has now become his own immediate reality. The “firaq” is also the separation from the false hopes he harbored. He may have thought that his idols would intercede, or that death was the end, or that he would somehow escape. All those illusions are now shattered. He realizes he is departing to face the consequences of his deeds. This verse is a powerful tool for self-reflection. It asks the listener to imagine that very moment of realization. What will it feel like to know that your time is up, that the chapter of worldly action is closed forever, and that you are now entering the chapter of eternal consequence? The believer uses this reflection to stay vigilant and to hate the idea of being unprepared when the realization dawns.

The surah continues to describe the final steps of the departure.

=29=

The final physical act of the worldly life is described with a stark, grim image: “and then their feet are tied together in a shroud.” The Arabic “wal-taffat al-saqu bil-saq” literally means “the shin is wrapped around the shin” or “one leg is joined to the other.

” This is a description of the moment when the corpse is prepared for burial. The legs are straightened and wrapped together in the shroud. The body that once walked arrogantly, that rushed toward the Prophet in mockery, that kicked and strutted with pride, is now a motionless, bound object being readied for the grave. Some commentators also interpret this phrase as the joining of the last agony of this world with the first agony of the Hereafter, meaning that one suffering is wrapped upon another, with no respite. Others say it refers to the two legs of the soul and the body being separated but joined in the experience of death. The most common and powerful interpretation, however, remains the binding of the feet in the burial shroud. It is the ultimate humiliation for the arrogant denier. The man who had abundant wealth, who wore fine clothes, who enjoyed a smoothed path in life, is now reduced to a wrapped corpse, his feet tied.

This is the inevitable end of every human being, believer and disbeliever alike, but the context here specifically highlights it as a stage in the transition to punishment for the denier. The verse strips away all the glamour of the worldly life and shows its final scene. It is a profound reminder of human frailty. The believer accepts this end with dignity, knowing that the grave is a garden of Paradise or a pit of Hell depending on one’s deeds. The denier, who never prepared, faces it with nothing but the terror of what follows. The binding of the feet also signifies the complete helplessness. The legs that could have walked to the mosque, walked to help the poor, walked to visit the sick, are now bound. The opportunity for voluntary movement is over.

=30=

The journey of the denier continues from the deathbed and the shroud to the final destination. “On that day they will be driven to your Lord alone.

” The Arabic “ila rabbika yawma’idhin al-masaq” means the driving, the herding, is toward your Lord. The word “masaq” implies being driven like cattle, forcefully and without choice. The angels will drive the soul, and later the resurrected body, toward the judgment of Allah. There is no escape route, no detour, no hiding place.

The phrase “your Lord” is addressed to the Prophet, but it also applies to every human being—each person will be driven to his Lord. The mention of “Lord” here is significant because it reminds the listener that the destination is not an impersonal court but the presence of the Creator, the Sustainer, the One who was worshiped or rejected. The deniers will stand before the very Lord they turned away from.

The word “alone” indicates that there will be no helpers, no intercessors to divert the path. The driving is direct and singular in its goal. The verse echoes the earlier statements that the wicked will have no refuge and no escape. The shroud and the binding of the feet are the prelude to this forceful march. In the world, the denier walked freely, choosing his own paths of misguidance. Now, he is a prisoner being transferred to the supreme court. The verse is a solemn conclusion to the scene of death. It answers the rhetorical question asked earlier by the deniers: “When is this Day of Judgment?” It tells them that the Day begins at death, with the driving to the Lord.

For the believer, this verse is a reminder that the journey to Allah is inevitable. The difference is that the believer comes to his Lord willingly, through a life of worship, and his meeting is a joyous reunion. The denier is dragged there against his will, and his meeting is a horrifying confrontation. The surah has now taken the listener from the moment of death to the threshold of judgment.

=31=

The surah now provides a summary of the denier’s life that led to such a horrific end. “This denier neither believed nor prayed.

” The Arabic “fa la saddaqa wa la salla” links back to the previous verses with a particle of consequence. So, this is the person who ended up being driven to his Lord. His first crime is that he did not “saddaqa”—he did not accept the truth, he did not confirm the message, he did not believe.

This is the root sin, the rejection of faith itself. The second crime, mentioned alongside and immediately after, is that he did not “salla”—he did not pray.

The pairing of belief and prayer is foundational. Belief is the inner conviction, and prayer is the outer manifestation of that conviction. The one who does not pray has given evidence that his belief is either absent or fatally weak. As in the confession of the damned in the previous surah, the neglect of prayer is the first specific deed mentioned. It is the line that separates the Muslim from the non-Muslim in practice. The denier’s failure to pray is not a small oversight; it is a conscious refusal to bow to his Creator. The verse uses the past tense, looking back at the life of this individual from the perspective of the Hereafter. His life is now being summarized in these two damning negations: he did not believe, and he did not pray. Everything else that he did—his wealth, his status, his family—is irrelevant. These are the only two things that mattered in the end. The verse is a chilling warning to anyone who claims to have faith but neglects the prayers. It calls for an urgent examination of one’s own consistency. The denier’s path to Hell began with these two omissions. The surah will further elaborate on his character, but the foundation of his ruin is now clearly laid bare.

=32=

The description of the denier’s life continues with his general attitude: “but persisted in denial and turned away.” The Arabic “wa lakin kadh-dhaba wa tawalla” shows that he did not merely disbelieve passively; he actively called the truth a lie.

The word “kadh-dhaba” implies a vigorous, repeated denial. He accused the Prophet of falsehood, he belied the Quran, he rejected the Day of Judgment. This was his verbal and intellectual stance. Then “tawalla” means he turned his back, he walked away physically and spiritually. He did not just disagree; he withdrew, he boycotted, he showed aversion.

This is exactly the behavior described in the previous surahs: the arrogant turning away, the frowning and scowling, the flight from the reminder. The combination of “persisted in denial” and “turned away” captures the complete posture of the disbeliever. His mind is set against the truth, and his body follows. There is no openness, no willingness to consider.

The verse teaches that disbelief is not merely a lack of information; it is an active state of rejection and aversion. The denier is not a seeker who couldn’t find the truth; he is a refuser who saw the truth and ran from it. This attitude is the opposite of the believer, who says, “We hear and we obey,” and who turns toward Allah in repentance and worship.

The verse also connects to the earlier description of the people of Hell, who admitted they indulged in falsehood along with others. This denier is a typical example. The surah is building a case, showing that the terrible end at death and the gloomy faces on the Day of Judgment are the direct result of a life lived in denial and turning away. It is a warning that the direction one turns in this life determines the direction one is driven in the next.

=33=

The portrait of the denier concludes with a vivid description of his worldly demeanor: “then went to their own people, walking boastfully.” The Arabic “thumma dhahaba ila ahlihi yatamatta” means after rejecting the truth and turning away, he went back to his family, his tribe, his companions, strutting and swaggering with pride.

The word “yatamatta” comes from “matya,” which relates to a camel’s gait. It describes a person walking with an affected, arrogant, self-satisfied swagger, swinging his shoulders, taking pride in his rejection. He is not ashamed of his disbelief; he flaunts it. He returns to his people as if he has achieved a victory, having dismissed the Prophet’s message. His people are like-minded deniers, and they reinforce his arrogance. The scene is a powerful contrast to the believer who hears the truth, humbles himself, and walks away with a bowed head, pondering. The denier walks as if he owns the earth, forgetting that the same feet will one day be bound in a shroud. The verse exposes the psychology of the arrogant elite. Their public swagger is a performance to maintain status and to intimidate the believers. They want everyone to see that they are unfazed by the warning. But this boastful walk is a delusion. The surah has already shown where that walk ends: at the collarbone, then in the shroud, then being driven to the Lord. The contrast between the boastful walk in the world and the humiliating march to judgment is devastating.

The verse also reminds the believers not to be impressed by the swagger of the disbelievers. Their arrogance is a symptom of their disease, not a sign of strength. The real strength is in the humility of the one who bows to his Creator.

The phrase “to their own people” highlights the importance of social environment. The denier surrounds himself with those who applaud his disbelief, creating an echo chamber that seals his fate. The believer is called to choose his company wisely, surrounding himself with those who encourage humility and faith, not arrogance and rejection.

=34=

After narrating the life and death of the denier, the surah turns to directly address him with a thunderous threat. “Woe to you, and more woe!

” The Arabic “awla laka fa-awla” is a phrase of severe threat and imminent destruction. It literally means “nearer to you, and nearer,” implying that a great evil is closing in, that destruction is drawing closer and closer. Some commentators explain it as “Woe to you, then woe to you,” a repeated curse. Others say it means “Hell is nearer to you, and nearer.

” The repetition intensifies the threat. The denier who walked boastfully is now being told that the very thing he mocked is creeping toward him. Every step of his boastful walk is a step closer to the abyss.

The phrase is directed at the individual denier, but it encompasses all who share his characteristics. It is a chilling moment in the surah. The tone shifts from narrative to direct, confrontational address. The listener is made to feel that these words are aimed at him if he recognizes the traits of the denier in himself.

The repetition “awla fa-awla” creates a sense of an echo, as if the threat is bouncing off the walls of the denier’s consciousness. It is a divine warning that leaves no room for ambiguity. The verse is a verbal strike, a slap of reality to wake up the heedless. The woe is not just a temporary misfortune; it is the eternal woe of Hellfire.

The word encompasses all the torments described earlier: the scorching fire, the shackles, the choking food, the gloomy face, the spine-breaking punishment. All of that is “awla”—it is right there, approaching fast. The believer reads this verse with a sense of gratitude that he is not among those to whom it is addressed, and with a renewed fear that he must never become like them. The verse is a sharp, unforgettable warning.

=35=

The surah amplifies the threat even further with a second, intensified repetition. “Again, woe to you, and even more woe!

” The Arabic “thumma awla laka fa-awla” uses “thumma” to indicate a successive, piled-on woe. It is not just one woe; it is woe upon woe upon woe.

The first threat was terrible, but the second is even more emphatic. This repetition is rare in the Quran and signifies the exceptional severity of the case. The denier described in these verses—the one who neither believed nor prayed, who belied and turned away, who walked boastfully—has reached a depth of wretchedness that merits this quadruple warning. The rhetorical effect is overwhelming. The listener feels the weight of divine anger pressing down. The verse leaves no room for the denier to dismiss the warning as a generic statement. It is personal, repeated, and escalating. Some scholars have noted that this is one of the most severe threats in the Quran, directed at a specific type of individual: the arrogant, wealthy, prayer-abandoning denier.

The repetition also serves as a mercy, because it is a warning before the punishment. Even now, after this thunderous threat, the door of repentance is still open for the living. The verse is a final alarm bell. If a person hears “woe to you” repeated four times and still does not change his ways, what else will move him? The “woe” is a prediction, not a wish. It is the statement of a fact in the divine knowledge.

For the believer, this verse is a source of profound reflection. It makes him cling to prayer and humility, lest he ever develop the slightest trace of the denier’s arrogance. The surah has now completed its direct warning to the individual denier, and it will shift to a broader argument for the resurrection.

=36=

The surah now transitions from the specific threat to a universal, philosophical argument for the resurrection. “Do people think they will be left without purpose?

” The Arabic “ayahsabu al-insanu an yutraka sudan” asks a rhetorical question that strikes at the core of human arrogance. “Sudan” means aimless, neglected, left to wander without any accountability, without any command or prohibition, without any ultimate judgment. It describes a state of being utterly free of responsibility, like a camel left to graze with no shepherd.

The question implies that such a thought is absurd and insulting to the Creator. Would Allah, who created the heavens and the earth with such precision, who formed the human being in stages, simply abandon His creation to eat, drink, and die like animals? The human being is “al-insan,” the species known for its intellect and moral capacity. To think that such a creature would be left without purpose is a failure of basic reasoning. The verse challenges the materialist worldview that sees life as a meaningless accident. It asserts that the very existence of human consciousness, morality, and the longing for justice point to a higher purpose and an eventual judgment. The disbelievers’ denial of the resurrection is rooted in this false assumption: that they are their own masters, left to do as they please without consequence. The surah refutes this by appealing to the wisdom of Allah. A creator who designs a magnificent machine does not leave it to rust without a manual or a purpose. Every prophet was sent to inform humanity that they are not “sudan,” that they have a purpose, and that they will be held accountable. The verse is a powerful call to reflect on the meaning of life. It tells the believer to live with purpose and to prepare for the meeting with the One who gave that purpose.

The next verses will provide the biological evidence for this purpose by reminding the human being of his humble origin.

=37=

The surah now presents the first proof of resurrection: the very origin of human life. “Were they not once a sperm-drop emitted?

” The Arabic “alam yaku nutfatan min maniyyin yumna” reminds the human being of his most humiliating stage. The “nutfah” is a tiny drop of fluid, a microscopic entity. “Maniyyin” is the seminal fluid that is “yumna”—emitted, ejaculated, poured forth. The human being who now walks boastfully, who denies the resurrection, who thinks he is a great and independent being, was once that insignificant drop.

This is a fact that no one can deny. The verse uses it as a devastating argument against arrogance. How can a creature whose origin is so lowly and base question the power of the One who created him from that drop? If Allah could bring a fully formed, intelligent, seeing, hearing human being from a fluid that is almost nothing, then bringing him back to life after death is an easier task in comparison. The logic is irrefutable.

The word “yumna” meaning emitted also emphasizes the passive, involuntary nature of the beginning. The human being did not choose to be created; he was the result of a biological process designed and executed by Allah. The verse strips away all pretensions of self-made grandeur. Every person, regardless of wealth or status, shares this same humble origin. The denier who looks down on others should remember that he began as a despised drop. The argument from creation is used throughout the Quran. It appeals to the rational mind. The one who can create from nothing can certainly recreate.

The verse also connects to the earlier warning: the boastful walker, the prayer-abandoning denier, was once just a sperm-drop. How did he become so arrogant? The reminder is a mercy to bring him back to humility and to acceptance of the truth.

=38=

The verse continues the stages of human creation, adding detail and wonder. “Then they became a clinging clot of blood, then He developed and perfected their form.

” The Arabic “thumma kana ‘alaqatan fa-khalaqa fa-sawwa” takes the listener on a journey. After the sperm-drop, the next stage is the “‘alaqah,” a leech-like, clinging clot that attaches to the uterine wall.

This is a stage of utter dependence and primitive structure. Then “fa-khalaqa” means He created, shaped, and formed the being. He gave it proportions, organs, limbs. Then “fa-sawwa” means He perfected, balanced, and fashioned the form in the best shape. The process moves from a single cell to a complex organism with systems, senses, and a unique face and fingerprints. The verbs are all in the third person singular, referring to Allah alone. No one else intervened. This gradual, meticulous development is a proof of the Creator’s wisdom, knowledge, and power. The verse marvels at the human creation as a sign. Every human being passes through these stages in the womb, completely unaware, utterly vulnerable. The same power that orchestrated this symphony of development can surely orchestrate the resurrection.

In fact, the resurrection is described in the Quran as an easier feat than the original creation. The mention of “perfected their form” is also a subtle answer to the question of whether the exact same body will be resurrected. The One who perfected the form the first time knows every detail and can perfectly reconstruct it, even the very fingertips, as mentioned earlier. The verse inspires awe and gratitude in the believer. He looks at himself and sees the trace of the divine Artist. The denier should feel the weight of this evidence and realize how irrational his denial is. The journey from a sperm-drop to a perfected human is a daily miracle that is ignored. The surah forces the listener to stop and think.

=39=

The final detail of the creation argument is the emergence of gender. “Producing from it both sexes, male and female.

” The Arabic “fa-ja‘ala minhu al-zawjayn al-dhakara wa al-untha” means from that originally single, undifferentiated drop, Allah made the two paired types: the male and the female. This is a profound biological truth. The sperm-drop does not contain a fully formed miniature human; it is the combination of the male and female gametes, and the determination of the sex is part of the divine creative process. The verse highlights the wonder of reproduction. From a single, homogeneous fluid, two vastly different beings can emerge, each with distinct reproductive organs, physiological characteristics, and psychological dispositions. The male and female are “zawjayn,” a pair, designed to complement each other and continue the species.

This is another sign of the Creator’s design. The verse refutes those who attribute creation to nature or chance. The consistent production of male and female, the precise balance of sexes in the population, is a sign of intelligent purpose, not randomness.

The mention of “from it” (minhu) referring to the sperm-drop, emphasizes the humble origin. The mighty human male, with all his strength, and the beautiful human female, with all her grace, both came from the same base fluid. There is no room for gender-based arrogance. Both are equally the creation of Allah from a lowly origin.

The verse also serves as a bridge to the final argument: if Allah can do all this, can He not bring the dead back to life? The creation of the sexes is one of the great signs that are repeatedly mentioned in the Quran. It is a call to reflect on the obvious, daily miracle of birth. Every newborn child is a proof that the Creator is not incapable of resurrection. The denier is left with no intellectual escape. The evidence is in his own body, in his own origin, in the very fact that he exists as a male or female.

The surah concludes its argument with a powerful, rhetorical question that is the climax of the entire discourse. “Is such a Creator unable to bring the dead back to life?

” The Arabic “alaysa dhalika bi-qadirin ‘ala an yuhyiya al-mawta” literally means “Is not that (Creator) powerful over giving life to the dead?” The demonstrative pronoun “dhalika” (that) refers to the Creator who has just been described: the One who created from a sperm-drop, who developed the clinging clot, who perfected the form, who made the two sexes.

The question appeals directly to the intellect and the innate human sense of logic. If someone can build a complex machine from raw materials, no one would doubt that he can repair it if it breaks. If Allah can create a human being from a drop of fluid, how can anyone doubt His ability to recreate that human being after death?

The word “qadir” indicates complete, unrestricted power. The question is rhetorical, expecting only the answer “Of course He is able.” The verse does not end with a statement but with a question to engage the mind actively. The listener is forced to answer silently, and in doing so, he affirms the very truth he might have been denying.

This is the Quranic method of persuasion at its finest. The surah, which began with the oath by the Day of Judgment and the self-reproaching soul, now ends with the logical conclusion that the Day is not only possible but inevitable, given the power of the Creator.

The question also carries a moral weight. If the Creator is able to resurrect, then He will resurrect, and the accountability that the deniers mocked is a certainty. The verse is a call to prepare. The one who believes in the resurrection will live a life that reflects that belief. The one who denies it is living in a dangerous delusion. The surah’s final note is one of hope and warning, leaving the listener with the clear message: the Creator who gave you life once will certainly give it again, so live accordingly.
