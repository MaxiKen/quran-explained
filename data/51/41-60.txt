=41=

After the account of Pharaoh’s drowning, the surah moves to yet another example of a destroyed nation, the people of ‘Âd. They were an ancient Arab tribe who lived after the time of Noah, in a region of towering pillars and impressive architecture. The verse states that in their story there was another lesson, an ayah, a sign for those willing to reflect. The lesson centres on a devastating wind that Allah sent against them.

The Arabic word ‘aqeem, translated as devastating, literally means barren or sterile. This wind was called barren because it carried no rain, no pollination, and no benefit whatsoever. It was a wind stripped of all mercy, a pure instrument of destruction. The people of ‘Âd were known for their immense physical strength and their tall, imposing buildings. They took pride in their might and openly boasted that no one was stronger than them. Allah responded to their arrogance by sending a wind that made their strength utterly meaningless. For seven nights and eight days, the wind howled against them relentlessly, as detailed in Surah Al-Haqqah. It was not a gust that passed quickly but a sustained, unbroken assault that wore them down completely. The lesson here operates on several levels. First, it demonstrates that no human power, no matter how formidable, can stand against a force of nature that Allah commands. Second, the wind, which is usually a sign of mercy bringing rain clouds, was transformed into a sign of wrath. This shows that the very elements that sustain life can, by Allah’s command, become agents of death. Third, the barren wind echoes the earlier themes of the surah, where the winds that scatter dust and the clouds loaded with rain were sworn by as signs of divine administration. The same winds that carry rain to revive the dead earth can be sent barren to punish an unrepentant people. For the Quraysh, the ruins of ‘Âd were known through oral tradition and visible remnants, serving as a cautionary tale. The verse invites the listener to connect the outward destruction with the inner sickness of arrogance and to realise that the promise of a greater judgment is entirely credible given these historical precedents.

=42=

This verse intensifies the description of the wind’s destructive power, stating that it left nothing in its path without turning it into something utterly ruined and decayed, like ashes or crumbling dust. The Arabic word "ramim" refers to something that has completely disintegrated, such as decayed bones or withered remains.

The image is stark and absolute. The mighty civilization of ‘Âd, with its tall pillars and sturdy buildings, was reduced to rubble so thoroughly that nothing remained intact. The wind did not merely knock down structures; it pulverised them. This comprehensive destruction underlines the totality of divine punishment when it arrives. There is no partial sparing, no corner that escapes. The wind came upon everything they had built, every shelter they trusted, and every body they thought was invincible, and it left them as scattered fragments.

This description connects back to the very first verse of the surah, which swore by the winds scattering dust. The winds that scatter dust normally carry particles across the earth in a benign manner. Here, the wind scattered the people of ‘Âd themselves, reducing their bodies and their works to scattered remnants. The lesson for the one reflecting on resurrection is profound. Just as the wind reduced the mighty ‘Âd to scattered particles, Allah’s knowledge encompasses every scattered particle of every human being. If He can track the dust of a destroyed civilization, He can certainly reassemble the dust of every human body for the Day of Judgment.

The verse also serves as a meditation on the fragility of human achievement. Empires, monuments, and physical strength seem permanent to those who build them, but they are entirely dependent on Allah’s protection. When that protection is withdrawn, the mightiest structures become as fragile as dry leaves in a storm. The disbelievers of Mecca, who trusted in their own strength and lineage, are being warned that what happened to ‘Âd is not a myth but a very real possibility.

=43=

The sequence continues with the people of Thamud, another ancient Arabian tribe that succeeded ‘Âd. They were the people to whom Prophet Salih was sent. They were known for carving elaborate homes out of the mountains and for their skill in construction and agriculture. In their story was another lesson, another sign for those who consider. The specific detail highlighted here is the message given to them: they were told to enjoy themselves only for a short while.

This statement came after they had killed the miraculous she-camel that Allah had sent as a sign, in defiance of Salih’s warnings. They were given a respite of three days before the punishment would descend. During those three days, they were told to enjoy whatever worldly pleasures they could, because their time was now limited to a short count.

The phrase captures the deceptive nature of worldly enjoyment for those who are under the shadow of impending punishment. They continued their lives as normal, perhaps feasting and laughing, but every moment of their enjoyment was borrowed time ticking down toward a terrifying end. The lesson is that the pleasures of this world are not a sign of Allah’s approval when they are accompanied by disobedience and denial. Sometimes a respite is given, but it is not a cancellation of the sentence; it is a brief opportunity that only increases the regret if not used for repentance. For the Quraysh, who were enjoying their commercial success and tribal dominance while mocking the Prophet, this was a chilling parallel. Their own period of enjoyment was also limited.

The verse reminds every reader that the time in this world, whether long or short, is essentially a brief enjoyment. The real life is the Hereafter. To be told to enjoy oneself for a short while is one of the most terrifying things one can hear, because it means the opportunity for change has passed and only the final moments of comfort remain before the blow falls.

=44=

This verse explains why the respite ended in destruction. Despite the warning of the three days, the people of Thamud persisted in their defiance. They did not use the opportunity to repent or to reconsider their treatment of Prophet Salih. They remained entrenched in their arrogance and rebellion against the commands of their Lord.

The phrase "persisted in defying" translates the Arabic "ataw," which conveys a sense of prideful rebellion, a refusal to submit to authority that one knows is legitimate. Their hearts were hardened, and they chose defiance over humility. The consequence came in the form of a mighty blast, a thunderous sound or shriek that descended upon them.

The Arabic word "saihah" can refer to a loud cry, a blast, or a piercing sound, and it is often associated with the punishment of Thamud in other surahs. The punishment arrived while they were looking on, meaning they were awake and witnessing the beginning of their own destruction, fully conscious and unable to do anything to avert it. They saw it coming and were frozen in terror.

This detail of them looking on amplifies the horror of the scene. The punishment was not something that happened in their sleep, without awareness. They experienced the full psychological terror of seeing the approaching doom while being completely powerless. This contrasts with the earlier description of the righteous who will be joyfully receiving what their Lord grants them. The deniers mock the warning now, but on that day their eyes will be wide open, and their mockery will be replaced with sheer terror. The verse is a vivid reminder that the delay of punishment should not be mistaken for its cancellation, and that when it arrives, it can do so with a suddenness that allows no escape.

=45=

This verse brings the story of Thamud to its final, pitiful conclusion. After the mighty blast struck them, they were not able to rise up.

The Arabic phrasing indicates that they could not even stand on their feet, let alone escape or mount a defence. They fell where they were, and no movement was possible. The mighty people who had carved mountains and built secure dwellings were reduced to a state of complete physical helplessness.

The verse then adds that they were not helped. No ally came to their rescue. No god besides Allah responded to their cries. No military power, no tribal confederation, no wealth, and no stratagem could deflect the punishment or offer them any relief. They had lived in defiance of the One True God, and in their moment of greatest need, there was none to help them except the God they had rejected, and His help had turned to justice. This dual negation of their ability to rise and their receiving of help serves as a complete statement of the hopelessness of the situation of those upon whom divine punishment falls. The lesson ties back to the central message of the surah. The deniers in Mecca relied on their social structures, their trade alliances, and their idols for support. This verse tells them plainly that none of that will avail them on the Day of Judgment or if a punishment descends in this world. The only support that matters is the support of Allah, and that support is only for those who submit to Him. For the believers, this verse offers a strange comfort. It assures them that the oppressors who seem so strong and well-supported today will one day be completely unable to rise and will find no helper. The ultimate balance of justice is certain, even if it is delayed.

=46=

The surah now reaches back to the very first example of a nation destroyed for their rejection of a messenger, the people of Noah. They were destroyed earlier, before ‘Âd, before Thamud, before Pharaoh, and before Lot.

The sequence is going backward in time to show that this pattern is as old as humanity itself. Noah called his people for nine hundred and fifty years, inviting them to worship Allah alone and to abandon their idols. Only a small number believed. The rest persisted in their rebellion, mocking Noah and threatening him with violence.

Finally, the command came, and the flood engulfed them all while Noah and the believers were saved in the ark. The verse describes them as truly a rebellious people, using the word "fasiqin," which refers to those who have stepped outside the bounds of obedience, whose hearts are corrupt and whose behaviour is defiant.

This description encapsulates the root cause of their destruction. It was not that they lacked intelligence or resources. They were rebellious, and rebellion against the Creator is the fundamental sin from which all other evils branch. By placing the people of Noah at this point in the sequence, the surah rounds out the gallery of destroyed nations. Each story illustrates a different form of punishment: Lot’s people by stones of baked clay, Pharaoh by drowning, ‘Âd by a barren wind, Thamud by a blast, and now Noah’s people by a flood. The diversity of the punishments demonstrates that Allah’s arsenal is not limited. He can strike from the sky, from the sea, from the wind, from the earth. No environment is safe if His protection is withdrawn. For the Quraysh, who lived in a valley and felt secure in their city, the mention of the flood was a reminder that walls and geography provide no safety against divine wrath.

The verse also serves as a precursor to the cosmic signs that follow, moving from the stories of destruction to the grandeur of creation.

=47=

After the series of historical examples, the surah turns the attention back to the cosmic signs of Allah’s power. The declaration "We built the universe with great might" establishes that the entire cosmos is a constructed edifice, not a self-existent or accidental phenomenon.

The word "built" implies deliberate design, planning, and execution. The universe is not a chaotic explosion but a structured creation brought into existence by the hands of divine power.

The phrase "with great might" translates "bi aydin," which can also mean with hands, but the classical understanding points to strength, power, and capability. The universe is vast beyond human comprehension, containing billions of galaxies and trillions of stars, and its construction required a might that is utterly beyond the created order. Then follows a statement that has captivated believers and scholars alike: "We are certainly expanding it.

" The Arabic word "lamusi’un" can mean to expand, to make vast, or to enrich. The earliest commentators understood this as referring to the vastness of the universe and Allah’s power to expand its provision and space. In modern times, the discovery of the expanding universe has given an additional layer of scientific resonance to this verse, and while the Quran is not a book of science, such alignments can strengthen the faith of those who already believe. The essential theological point is that the universe is not static; it is under continuous divine management. The One who built it and continues to expand it has no difficulty in bringing about the Day of Judgment and creating a new heavens and earth. The verse connects directly to the theme of resurrection. The deniers ask how dead bones can be brought back to life. Allah replies by pointing to the universe itself, which He built and continues to expand. The power required for that is infinitely greater than the power required to resurrect human bodies.

Therefore, resurrection is a small matter for the One who holds the cosmos in His grip.

=48=

From the vastness of the universe, the focus narrows down to the earth beneath our feet. Allah declares that He spread out the earth, making it a suitable dwelling place for living creatures.

The Arabic verb "farashna" conveys the image of preparing a bed or a carpet, something that is laid out for rest and comfort. The earth has been spread with a surface that is neither too hard nor too soft, with soils that can sustain vegetation, with plains and valleys that facilitate travel and habitation. It has been made a stable platform, not a constantly shaking or inhospitable terrain. The exclamation "How superbly did We smooth it out!" celebrates the perfection of this creative act.

The Arabic word "mahidun" refers to the One who prepares a smooth, even place, and the verse is praising Allah for the excellence of this preparation. The earth is not merely spread; it is spread with a smoothness and a suitability that testify to the careful wisdom of the Creator. The surface of the earth, with its precise combination of elements, its atmospheric protection, and its hydrological cycles, is a unique environment perfectly tailored for life.

This verse also carries a subtle echo of the theme of resurrection. A bed is prepared for someone to rest and then rise from. The earth is spread as a resting place for humanity in this life, but the time will come when humanity will rise from this bed for the judgment. The smoothness of the earth is also a sign of Allah’s mercy. He could have made the earth jagged, hostile, and uninhabitable. Instead, He smoothed it out so that life could flourish. This act of mercy is a proof that the same Creator who prepared this earthly home has also prepared the home of the Hereafter for those who believe, a home far more perfect than this one.

=49=

This verse presents a universal principle of creation that has profound implications for both science and spirituality. Allah states that He created pairs of all things.

The word "zawjayn" refers to two complementary counterparts, male and female, positive and negative, night and day, sweet and salty, heaven and earth. The creation in pairs extends beyond biological life to the fundamental structure of reality. In the plant world, many species have male and female parts. In the animal world, reproduction depends on pairs. In the physical world, there are pairs of forces, pairs of charges, and pairs of particles. Even abstract concepts exist in pairs: truth and falsehood, mercy and justice, hope and fear, this world and the Hereafter. The purpose of mentioning this pairing is stated clearly: "so perhaps you would be mindful." The observation that all things are created in pairs should lead the human mind to reflect, to remember, and to draw conclusions about the Creator. The most immediate conclusion is that the presence of pairs points to the Oneness of the One who created them. Pairing is a sign of dependence. Each member of a pair needs the other to complete its function. The male needs the female, the day needs the night, the positive needs the negative. This inherent dependence of all things upon their complements proves that nothing in creation is self-sufficient. Only the Creator is singular, independent, and without need. The reflection on pairs should also lead one to think of the ultimate pair: this life and the next. Just as night is followed by day and rest is followed by activity, this temporary worldly life will be followed by the eternal life of the Hereafter. The deniers who believe only in this life are ignoring the most obvious pattern woven into all of creation. The verse is an invitation to use reason and observation to arrive at the truth, moving from the visible pairs all around to the invisible realities of faith.

=50=

After laying out the signs in the universe and the lessons of history, the surah issues a direct, urgent command. The Prophet is told to proclaim to the people: "Flee to Allah!

" The Arabic word "firru" is the command to flee, to run with urgency and desperation, like a person escaping from a fire or a predator. This is not a calm, leisurely suggestion. It is a cry of alarm. The imagery is striking. In this world, people flee from danger. They flee from collapsing buildings, from rising floods, from attacking enemies. But here, the command is to flee to Allah, the only true source of safety. The danger from which one is fleeing is the consequence of one’s own sins and the punishment that awaits those who reject the truth. The only safe refuge is Allah’s mercy, forgiveness, and protection. This concept of fleeing to Allah is beautifully explained by the scholars. It means to abandon everything that displeases Him and to run with your heart and actions toward everything that pleases Him. It is a flight from the self, from the world, from the devil, and from sin, toward the vast expanse of divine mercy. The Prophet is then instructed to add that he is truly sent by Allah with a clear warning. The warning is clear, meaning there is no ambiguity, no hidden agenda, no obscure language. The people cannot claim they did not understand. The message has been delivered with absolute clarity: the resurrection is real, the judgment is certain, Paradise is for the righteous, and the Fire is for the deniers. The urgency of the command to flee is heightened by the fact that the warner is among them, speaking their language and living among them. To ignore such a clear warning delivered by such a trustworthy messenger is the height of foolishness.

=51=

The command to flee to Allah is immediately followed by the core condition of that flight: abandoning all false gods. Fleeing to Allah necessarily means fleeing from the worship of anything else. The verse explicitly forbids setting up another god alongside Allah.

This is the essence of tawhid, the absolute oneness of God in His lordship, His worship, and His names and attributes. The prohibition covers every form of shirk, whether it be the worship of idols, the deification of prophets or saints, the blind following of desires or ideologies, or the attachment of the heart to wealth, status, or power in a way that rivals the devotion due to Allah alone. The Quraysh were being told that their ancestral idols, Al-Lat, Al-Uzza, Manat, and the others, were false and powerless. But the verse also speaks to every human being. Anything that one loves, fears, or hopes in to the degree that should be reserved for Allah has been set up as another god. The repeated phrase "I am truly sent by Him with a clear warning to you" underscores the seriousness of the mission. The warning encompasses both the immediate consequences of shirk in this life, which are spiritual ruin and the loss of purpose, and the eternal consequences in the Hereafter, which are punishment and exclusion from Paradise.

The repetition also serves as a rhetorical emphasis. The Prophet is not speaking on his own behalf or from his own ideas. He is a messenger, delivering a warning from the Lord of the worlds. Those who reject the warning are not rejecting a human being; they are rejecting the One who sent him. This verse connects back to the earlier condemnation of the liars who are steeped in ignorance. The flight to Allah and the rejection of all false gods is the exact opposite of the heedlessness and confusion described earlier. It is the path of clarity, purpose, and salvation.

=52=

This verse provides a profound consolation to the Prophet and a diagnosis of the persistent pattern of denial throughout human history. The Quraysh were calling Muhammad a magician or a madman, but they were not original in their slander. Every messenger who came to the nations before them was met with the exact same accusation. The similarity is striking and is presented as evidence that the problem is not with the messengers or their messages, but with a stubborn, recurring pattern of human arrogance. When people do not want to accept the truth, they resort to labels that allow them to dismiss the messenger without engaging with the message. "Magician" implies that the person is a trickster, someone who deceives the eyes and manipulates the crowd. "Madman" implies that the person is irrational, someone whose words do not deserve serious consideration. These two labels are a convenient pair. If the miraculous signs are undeniable, call it magic. If the logic is unassailable, call it madness. The historical context is clear. Pharaoh called Moses a magician. The Quraysh oscillated between calling Muhammad a poet, a soothsayer, a magician, and a madman, as noted earlier in the surah. The accusation is not new, and the verse implies that the repetition of this pattern across centuries is itself a sign that the messengers are genuine. If all true prophets receive the same insults, then receiving those insults is a mark of prophethood, not a disproof of it. For the Prophet Muhammad, hearing this verse must have been a source of immense comfort. He was being told that he is walking the same path as every noble messenger before him, and the ridicule he faces is not a sign of failure but of authenticity.

The verse also warns the accusers. They are repeating the words of the destroyed nations. If they continue in the footsteps of those nations in their mockery, they should expect to end up in the same footsteps in their destruction.

=53=

This verse addresses a rhetorical question that highlights the strange uniformity of the deniers’ accusations. Did the later generations inherit this phrase, this cliché of "a magician or a madman," as a legacy passed down from one generation to the next?

The question suggests that it is as if the deniers throughout history had a shared script, a common manual of slander that they all consulted. The word "tawasaw" implies a mutual bequest, a tradition that is handed down. The verse is pointing out the absurdity of such a consistent yet baseless reaction. If the messengers were all false, why would their opponents across vastly different cultures, languages, and eras all independently land on the exact same accusations? The more logical explanation is that the consistent reaction is a symptom of the same spiritual disease: transgression.

The verse then provides the real answer. They have not simply inherited a phrase; they have inherited a nature. They are all a transgressing people, a qawmun taghun.

The word "tagha" refers to rebellion, exceeding all bounds, and flooding over limits. The root cause of their shared accusation is their shared transgression. A transgressing heart responds to truth with rejection and mockery, regardless of time or place. The uniformity of the reaction is not cultural; it is psychological and spiritual. The verse thus shifts the focus from the words spoken to the hearts that speak them. The deniers of Mecca are not merely repeating an inherited insult; they are manifesting the same transgressive nature that characterised the people of Noah, ‘Âd, Thamud, Lot, and Pharaoh. This analysis is both a warning and an explanation. It strips away the illusion that the Quraysh had any genuine intellectual objection to the message. Their rejection stems from a corrupt inner state, a state of transgression that they share with all the destroyed nations that came before them.

=54=

This verse delivers a directive that might seem surprising at first: the Prophet is told to turn away from the deniers. After all the warnings and the clear arguments, he is instructed to withdraw his attention from those who persist in their mockery and rejection. The reason given is that he will not be blamed for their continued disbelief. The mission of a messenger is to deliver the message with clarity, to warn, and to invite. Once that duty has been fulfilled, the accountability shifts entirely to the recipients. The Prophet’s heart was deeply burdened by the rejection of his people. He longed for their guidance, and their insults wounded him. This verse is a release and a comfort. It tells him that his responsibility has limits. He is not accountable for their choice to disbelieve. He has done his part, and now he can detach himself emotionally from their stubbornness. This turning away is not an abandonment of the duty to preach, as the next verse will clarify, but an abandonment of the emotional entanglement that drains the messenger and yields no result. It is a strategic and psychological withdrawal. The deniers thrive on attention and reaction. By turning away, the Prophet denies them the validation of his distress and preserves his own energy for those who are receptive.

The verse also carries a subtle warning to the deniers. When a prophet turns away from a people, it signals that the period of active invitation may be drawing to a close, and what follows could be the period of judgment. For the believers, this verse teaches a valuable lesson in prioritisation. Not every battle is worth fighting. Not every opponent deserves a response. There are times when the most effective action is to withdraw and leave the arrogant to the consequences of their own choices, while focusing one’s energy on those who genuinely seek the truth.

=55=

Immediately after the command to turn away from the obstinate deniers, the verse issues a balancing instruction: continue to remind. This clarifies that turning away was never meant to be a complete cessation of preaching. The distinction is between those who are hardened in mockery and those who have receptive hearts. The Prophet is to turn away from the mockers, but he is to persist in reminding those who can benefit.

The word "dhakkir" means to remind, to call to remembrance, to awaken what is already latent in the human soul. Every human being is born with a natural disposition toward the truth, the fitrah. Reminders activate that disposition, bringing it from dormancy to active consciousness.

The verse then gives the reason for persisting in the reminder: reminders certainly benefit the believers. The reminder serves multiple functions for the believer. It strengthens their faith when it wavers. It deepens their understanding of truths they already accept. It provides guidance for new situations they face. It pushes them toward higher levels of righteousness and consciousness of Allah. And it protects them from the heedlessness that the earlier verses described as the disease of the deniers. The believer is not a static entity. Faith fluctuates, and the human heart is constantly pulled in different directions by desires, distractions, and doubts. Regular reminders act as a shield and a nourishment.

The verse also teaches a principle of effective communication. The speaker must know the audience. There are those who will never listen, and persisting with them beyond a certain point becomes a waste of the precious resource of prophetic words. But there are others who are hungry for guidance, and for them the reminder is like rain on fertile soil. The Prophet is being instructed to redirect his effort from the barren ground to the receptive ground, and in doing so, to find encouragement in the fact that his words are not in vain. They are bringing benefit to the believers who are the true audience of the Quran.

=56=

This verse is one of the most concentrated statements of purpose in the entire Quran. It declares, in the direct words of Allah, the fundamental reason for the existence of jinn and humankind. They were not created for play, for amusement, for aimless wandering, or for mere survival and reproduction. They were created for a single, exalted purpose: to worship Allah.

The Arabic word "ya'budun" comes from the root of 'ibadah, which encompasses worship, servitude, devotion, and obedience. Worship in this comprehensive sense is not limited to rituals like prayer and fasting. It includes every act of the heart, tongue, and limbs that is pleasing to Allah and done with the intention of drawing near to Him. A merchant conducting business with honesty and seeking lawful provision is engaged in worship. A parent raising children with love and teaching them righteousness is engaged in worship. A person removing a harmful object from the road is engaged in worship. The entire scope of a human life can be transformed into a continuous act of worship when it is aligned with divine guidance and motivated by the desire to please Allah. This verse answers the deepest human questions: Why am I here? What is the meaning of life?

The answer is not found in wealth, power, pleasure, or fame. It is found in a conscious, loving, and willing submission to the Creator.

The verse also mentions jinn alongside humans, reminding us that we are not the only morally accountable creation. The jinn, like us, were created for the same purpose and will be judged on the same Day. The verse stands as a powerful rebuttal to the heedlessness described earlier. Those who are lost in conflicting views and steeped in ignorance are living in contradiction to the very purpose of their creation. They are like a tool that has disconnected itself from the function for which it was made. To live without worshipping Allah is to exist in a state of profound existential failure, no matter how successful one appears by worldly standards.

=57=

This verse completes the declaration of purpose by dispelling any misconception that Allah’s command to worship Him stems from any need on His part. He is Al-Ghani, the Absolutely Self-Sufficient. He does not seek provision from His creation, nor does He require them to feed Him.

The mention of provision and feeding covers all forms of sustenance and support. Unlike human masters who need the service of their servants, Allah needs nothing from anyone. If every single jinn and human being were to worship Him with the worship of the most pious among them, it would add nothing to His dominion. If they were all to disobey Him with the disobedience of the worst sinners, it would diminish nothing from His sovereignty. This absolute independence of the Creator is a fundamental aspect of Islamic theology. The purpose of creation, which is worship, is entirely for the benefit of the worshipper. Worship purifies the soul, gives meaning to life, provides moral direction, and leads to eternal bliss. Allah does not benefit from it. This understanding elevates the believer’s relationship with Allah. Worship is not a burdensome tax paid to a needy deity. It is a gift of mercy from a Lord who gains nothing but gives everything.

The verse also contains a subtle but profound argument. The false gods worshipped by the polytheists, whether idols, kings, or saints, need their worshippers to bring them food, to maintain their temples, to defend them, and to serve them. But the true God, Allah, needs none of that. He is the Provider who feeds and sustains all, while He Himself is neither fed nor sustained.

This contrast is a powerful proof of tawhid. The One who needs nothing is the only One worthy of worship. Those who need everything are not gods; they are fellow creatures, dependent on the same Provider. The verse thus both comforts the believer and refutes the polytheist.

=58=

Following the statement that Allah needs no provision from His creation, this verse declares the complementary truth: He is the Supreme Provider for all of creation. The Arabic word "Razzaq" is an intensive form, meaning the One who provides constantly, abundantly, and to all. Every morsel of food, every breath of air, every drop of water, and every moment of life is a provision from Him. While the previous verse negated any need on Allah’s part, this verse affirms His complete sufficiency and His role as the sole sustainer. The flow is logical and powerful. I do not need you to provide for Me, because I am the One who provides for you and for everything that exists.

The verse then adds two more attributes of majesty: "Lord of all Power" and "Ever Mighty." The first, "Dhul Quwwatil Mateen," describes Allah as the possessor of power that is firm, unshakeable, and enduring. His power does not fluctuate, weaken, or fade. It is absolute and constant.

The second, "Al-Mateen," the Ever Mighty, reinforces this idea of unyielding strength. Together, these attributes paint a picture of complete self-sufficiency, absolute authority, and irresistible power.

For the believer, this verse is a source of immense security. The One who commands me to worship Him is the same One who provides for me without any need of my provision, and who has the power to protect, sustain, and reward me. I can devote myself to His worship with full trust that my worldly needs are in the hands of the most capable Provider. For the denier, the verse is a reminder of their complete dependence. They strut on the earth with arrogance, yet every heartbeat and every breath is a provision they receive from the very God they deny. The verse serves as a conclusion to the argument about the purpose of creation. Worship is the right of the Provider upon the provided. To enjoy His provision and then refuse His worship is the deepest ingratitude and the most profound injustice.

=59=

The surah now returns to the direct warning to the deniers, having completed the majestic passage on the purpose of creation. The wrongdoers, those who persist in their shirk, their mockery, and their rejection of the clear warning, are given a definitive sentence. They will certainly have a share of the torment just like the share of their predecessors, the companions of the past who walked the same path of transgression.

The word "dhanub" translated as share can also mean a portion, a lot, or a destined punishment. The idea is that the cup of punishment is filled for each generation of wrongdoers, and the Meccan deniers will drink from the same cup that was served to the people of Noah, ‘Âd, Thamud, Lot, and Pharaoh.

The second part of the verse forbids the Prophet from being pressured by their mockery. They used to ask for the punishment to be hastened, saying "When is this promise, if you are truthful?" and "Bring us what you threaten us with." The verse instructs that they should not be allowed to rush the divine timetable. Allah’s justice operates on His own schedule, not on the demands of the insolent. The hastening they ask for is not a sign of courage but of foolhardiness. They think the delay disproves the threat, but the delay is a mercy that gives them time to repent. When they ask to hasten it, they are essentially asking to shorten the very mercy that is keeping them alive.

The verse also provides a practical strategy for the believer when dealing with mockers who demand immediate proof of divine punishment. The believer should not feel compelled to defend the exact timing of unseen events. The certainty of the event does not depend on its immediacy. The believer rests in the knowledge that the promise is true, and its timing is with the Most Wise.

=60=

The surah closes with a powerful declaration of woe upon the disbelievers as they confront the Day that they were repeatedly warned about. The word "wayl" is a strong expression of impending destruction, grief, and calamity. It is a threat and a lament combined, painting a picture of the utter ruin and despair that will envelop them when the reality they mocked becomes their inescapable present. The Day is described as "their Day" because it is the day appointed specifically for their reckoning, the day on which all their deeds will be presented, and the day on which they will taste the full consequences of their denial.

The phrase "which they are warned of" connects back to the entire surah. The winds, the clouds, the ships, the angels, the heavens in their marvellous design, the signs on earth and within themselves, the stories of the destroyed nations, the command to flee to Allah, and the declaration of the purpose of creation; all of these were warnings pointing to this Day. They had every opportunity to heed. They had a clear warner in their midst. They had the signs spread across the cosmos. And yet they remained heedless and mocking. On that Day, the warnings will cease, and the reality will begin. The "woe" is not a temporary discomfort. It is an eternal state of loss and regret. The surah thus ends exactly where it began, with the emphasis on the certainty of the judgment.

The first verses swore by the winds and the angels that the promise is true and the judgment will occur. The last verse pronounces the woe upon those who refused to believe that oath. The surah has come full circle.

For those who have read or listened with an open heart, the message is clear: flee to Allah, worship Him alone, and prepare for the Day that is as certain as your own speech. The surah leaves the reader with a sense of urgency, a renewed awareness of purpose, and a sober contemplation of the choice between the Gardens and the Fire.
