# Surah Ali 'Imran (The Family of 'Imran, 3) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 121–130

The last batch ended on a shield: if you are patient and mindful of God, their schemes will not harm you (3:120). These ten verses open the shield into a field. The field is Uḥud.

The surah has been arguing doctrine, covenant, community, and discernment. At verse 121 it turns, without ceremony, to a morning in the third year after the Hijrah: the Prophet ﷺ leaving his household at dawn to post the believers at their stations for battle. What follows is not a war chronicle. It is a moral x-ray of a community under fire — two groups about to lose heart (122), Badr recalled as a gratitude-lesson when they were few (123), the promise of angelic reinforcement conditional on patience and *taqwā* (124–125), the theology of help that comes only from God (126–127), the stripping of the Prophet himself of any private share in the decision of mercy or punishment (128–129), and then — abruptly to a modern ear, deliberately to the surah's — a command against consuming *ribā* doubled and redoubled (130).

Uḥud is the occasion the classical tafsir agrees on for this block. After Badr's shock, Quraysh came in force — the reports say about three thousand — toward Madinah. The Prophet ﷺ consulted; the younger men pressed to go out; he gave way to the majority against his own first preference to fight from the city. On the way, ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy withdrew with about three hundred. Two Anṣārī clans — Banū Salimah and Banū Ḥārithah — nearly lost heart and then held. The archers left their post against orders when the first success appeared; the cavalry came through the gap; the line broke; the Prophet ﷺ was wounded in the face and a tooth was broken; the cry went up that he had been killed; some fled, some stood. Quraysh did not take Madinah. The Muslims buried their dead and walked home carrying a lesson the rest of the surah will keep unpacking.

Five cruxes govern the reading. The first is the two groups of 122 and what "about to cower" means without slandering people God Himself reassured. The second is the angelic numbers at 124–125 and their relationship to Badr (8:9) and to the condition of *ṣabr* and *taqwā* that Uḥud did not fully meet. The third is 128 — *laysa laka min al-amri shay'* — revealed when the wounded Prophet asked how a people who bloodied their prophet could prosper, or when he invoked against named leaders: the decision of mercy or punishment is God's alone. The fourth is why *ribā* appears at 130 inside a battle-narrative. The fifth is the standing caution about reports: names, numbers, and tactical detail are sīrah-grade; the Qur'an's own claims are the floor.

The batch begins a long Uḥud movement that will run well past verse 130. These ten verses are the opening frame: dawn, wavering, memory of Badr, angels conditional, help from God alone, the Prophet's hands empty of the final say, and a community told that how it handles money is not separate from how it stands in a line of battle.

## Verse 121

**"˹Remember, O Prophet,˺ when you left your home in the early morning to position the believers in the battlefield. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing."**

In simple terms: remember, O Prophet, when you left your home in the early morning to set the believers at their posts for the fight. God hears everything and knows everything.

**The surah's camera opens on a dawn.** *Wa-idh ghadawta min ahlika* — when you went out in the morning from your family. The supplied half-bracketed "Remember, O Prophet" correctly marks *idh* as a call to recall and the addressee as the Prophet ﷺ. The verse is spoken *to* him, about a morning he lived, so that the community that reads it forever after can stand inside that morning's meaning.

**"To position the believers in the battlefield."** *Tubawwi'u al-mu'minīna maqāʿida li-l-qitāl.*

*Tubawwi'u* is to assign places, to settle people into positions. *Maqāʿid* are seats, stations, posts. The Prophet ﷺ is not only a man walking out to fight. He is arranging a line — posting archers, placing wings, giving the field a shape. The classical reports put the archers on the hill with a clear order not to leave their post whether the army seemed winning or losing. The verse itself does not narrate the later breach. It freezes the morning *before* the breach: the Prophet doing the work of command under God's hearing and knowledge.

**"And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing."** *Wa-Allāhu samīʿun ʿalīm.*

Two names close the verse, and they are not decoration. *Samīʿ* — He heard what was said that morning: the consultation the night before, the pressure of the young men, the Prophet's own preference, the orders given to the archers, the private fears in the ranks. *ʿAlīm* — He knew what had not yet happened: who would hold, who would leave the post, who would stand when the cry went up that the Prophet had been killed. The morning is already inside a knowledge that outruns the morning.

**Why begin here.** A community that has just been told it is the best raised for humankind (110), that has been given *laysū sawā'* (113), that has been warned about *biṭānah* (118), is now walked onto a field where all of that will be tested. Titles do not hold posts. *Taqwā* does — or fails to. Verse 121 is the door from ethics into ordeal.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** Most readers will never post archers on a hill. They will know the shape of a morning when a hard day has to be arranged: the conversation before the meeting, the roles assigned, the order given that will be tested when success appears early and discipline feels optional. *Samīʿun ʿalīm* over that morning means the arrangement is not only management. It is witnessed. The post you assign, and the post you abandon, are both heard.

**And the spiritual centre.** The Prophet leaves *ahlahu* — his family, his household. The personal cost of command is named in the first clause. Leadership in this verse is not abstraction. It is a man walking out of a home toward a line he must set, under a God who hears and knows.

## Verse 122

**"˹Remember˺ when two groups among you ˹believers˺ were about to cower, then Allah reassured them. So in Allah let the believers put their trust."**

In simple terms: remember when two groups among you were about to lose heart — then God became their ally and steadied them. So in God let the believers put their trust.

**The first crack in the line, named without cruelty.**

**"When two groups among you were about to cower."** *Idh hammat ṭā'ifatāni minkum an tafshalā.*

*Hammat* — they intended, they were on the point of, they nearly. *An tafshalā* — to lose heart, to fail in courage, to collapse inwardly. The verb is about what was *about to* happen, not what was completed. The supplied "cower" is strong and fair.

**Who were the two groups?** The tafsir and sīrah identify them, with broad agreement, as Banū Salimah (of the Khazraj) and Banū Ḥārithah (of the Aws) — two Anṣārī clans who, after Ibn Ubayy's withdrawal with three hundred, felt the numerical thinness and nearly turned back, then held. Bukhārī carries the identification. The verse's own mercy is that it does not name them in the text. God recorded the near-failure and the recovery without stamping the clans forever in the recited Book. That is a literary and moral choice worth noticing.

**"Then Allah reassured them."** More literally: *wa-Allāhu waliyyuhumā* — and God was their Protecting Ally. The supplied "reassured them" catches the effect; the Arabic names the relationship. God as *walī* of the two groups who nearly broke. Not after they became perfect. While they were on the edge. The same God who joins hearts at 3:103 steadies hearts at 3:122.

**"So in Allah let the believers put their trust."** *Wa-ʿalā Allāhi fa-l-yatawakkali al-mu'minūn.*

The *fa* makes the trust a conclusion drawn from the near-collapse and the divine alliance. Because God was their *walī* when they nearly failed, the believers' *tawakkul* belongs on Him. Trust is not the opposite of posting stations (121). Trust is what keeps a posted station from becoming a private panic.

**A pastoral caution.** This verse has sometimes been used to shame communities for fear. The text does the opposite of cheap shame. It records that fear rose in good people, that God was still their ally, and that the right response is trust in Him — not a performance of fearlessness. The Companions who nearly lost heart and then stayed are not the villains of Uḥud. The villains, insofar as the later verses will name causes, are disobedience to clear orders and the love of immediate spoils. Nuance here is fidelity.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** Every serious collective effort has a Banū Salimah moment: the meeting after the numbers look wrong, the night before the launch, the quiet calculation that leaving would be rational. Verse 122 does not say the calculation is never rational. It says that when God is *walī*, the near-departure can become a staying, and the staying is then remembered as His allyship, not as your heroism alone. *Tawakkul* is what you do with the morning after you almost left.

## Verse 123

**"Indeed, Allah made you victorious at Badr when you were ˹vastly˺ outnumbered. So be mindful of Allah, perhaps you will be grateful."**

In simple terms: God had already given you victory at Badr when you were few and weak. So be mindful of God, so that you might be grateful.

**Badr is brought into Uḥud as memory, not as nostalgia.**

**"Indeed, Allah made you victorious at Badr when you were vastly outnumbered."** *Wa-la-qad naṣarakumu Allāhu bi-Badrin wa-antum adhillah.*

*Adhillah* — weak, few, low in the world's measure. The supplied half-bracketed "vastly" before "outnumbered" is interpretive emphasis on that weakness. Badr was roughly three hundred against about a thousand. The victory was *naṣr* from God — the same root that has threaded the surah from the disciples' *anṣār* to the helpers the rejecters do not have.

**"So be mindful of Allah, perhaps you will be grateful."** *Fa-ttaqū Allāha laʿallakum tashkurūn.*

*Taqwā* again, now ordered as the route to *shukr*, gratitude. The logic is sharp. Badr was gift. Uḥud is test. The way to inhabit the gift without entitlement, and the test without despair, is *taqwā* that produces gratitude. Ingratitude after Badr is one of the moral climates in which Uḥud's mistakes become possible: the sense that victory is owed, that posts can be left early, that the end is already written in our favour.

**Why recall Badr here.** Because the community at Uḥud is not a community that has never seen help. It is a community that has seen help when it was weaker than it is now. The argument against panic is not "you are strong." It is "you were weaker, and He helped you." Memory of gift is supposed to fund present trust. When it funds present presumption instead, the field changes.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** Every community, and every person, has a Badr — a time when the numbers did not work and the outcome still came as gift. Verse 123 asks what that memory is doing now. If it is producing *taqwā* and *shukr*, it is doing its Qur'anic job. If it is producing the feeling that one is too favoured to need discipline, it has been corrupted into the opposite of its purpose.

**Badr as moral precedent, not tactical template.** The verse does not say "fight Uḥud the way you fought Badr." It says God helped you when you were *adhillah*, so have *taqwā* so that gratitude becomes possible. The difference matters. Uḥud was not lost because the Muslims failed to copy Badr's formation. Parts of the day went wrong because the moral posture of Badr — dependence, obedience, refusal of early spoils — was not held when the first success appeared. Memory without *taqwā* becomes nostalgia. Memory with *taqwā* becomes *shukr*.

**The half-bracket "vastly."** The Arabic is simply *wa-antum adhillah* — while you were weak / low / few. "Vastly outnumbered" is a fair dynamic rendering of the historical situation and of the word's force. It is still a choice: *adhillah* can also carry the social sense of being the low ones of the earth, not only a headcount. Both senses serve the verse. God helped the low. The low are told to become grateful through *taqwā*, not proud through memory.

**Connection backward and forward.** Backward to 3:13, where Badr was already a *āyah* for those with eyes. Forward to 3:165, where the community will ask "how did this happen to us?" and be answered that it was from themselves. Verse 123 plants the gratitude that 165 will test. A people who remember Badr as gift are less likely to treat Uḥud as betrayal. A people who remember Badr as entitlement are already half-prepared to leave a post.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** Every community has a "Badr" — a day when it was small and God carried it. The danger is not forgetting the day. The danger is using the day as a licence. *Fa-ttaqū Allāha laʿallakum tashkurūn* is the anti-licence. Be mindful, so that the old victory produces thanks rather than swagger. Gratitude is not a feeling only. It is the discipline that keeps the next morning's posts manned.


===VERSE-END===

## Verse 124

**"˹Remember, O Prophet,˺ when you said to the believers, “Is it not enough that your Lord will send down a reinforcement of three thousand angels for your aid?”"**

In simple terms: remember, O Prophet, when you said to the believers: is it not enough for you that your Lord will reinforce you with three thousand angels sent down?

**The Prophet's own voice enters the text**, reporting a reassurance he gave.

**"When you said to the believers."** The half-bracketed "O Prophet" again marks the addressee of the framing. What follows is his speech to the ranks.

**"Is it not enough that your Lord will send down a reinforcement of three thousand angels for your aid?"** *A-lan yakfiyakum an yumiddakum rabbukum bi-thalāthati ālāfin min al-malā'ikati munzalīn.*

*Yumiddakum* — reinforce you, extend supply to you. *Munzalīn* — sent down. Three thousand. The question-form *a-lan yakfiyakum* — is it not enough for you — is pastoral. It addresses a fear of numbers with a number from the unseen.

**Badr or Uḥud?** The tafsir tradition records a real discussion. One line connects the angelic promise to Badr, where 8:9 speaks of a thousand angels and the fighting of angels is strongly attested. Another line, reading the flow from 121, keeps 124–125 inside the Uḥud address. Ibn Kathīr notes both and observes that the two passages are close in meaning. A careful reading can say: the Prophet ﷺ used the Badr-logic of angelic help to steady Uḥud; the Qur'an records that steadying; and 125 will add the condition that unlocks a further number. Whether angels fought at Uḥud is a separate question 125's condition bears on.

**What the verse does not do.** It does not turn the battle into a spectator spectacle of angels. The next verses will insist that help is only from God and that the reinforcement was *bushrā* — good news — for the heart. Angels here are not a replacement for posting stations and holding them. They are God's way of telling a frightened line that the unseen is not empty.

**The number and the speaker.** Three thousand angels. The classical discussion links this to Badr's reinforcement (8:9 — a thousand, then the reports of three thousand and five thousand in the wider tradition). Some read 124–125 as primarily about Badr recalled inside the Uḥud discourse; others as a promise extended toward Uḥud under condition. Both readings have classical weight. What the text itself makes unavoidable is that the Prophet ﷺ speaks reinforcement into fearful hearts, and that the next verse will attach a condition the community must hear.

**"Will it not suffice you?"** *A-lan yakfiyakum.* The question is pastoral. It does not scold the desire for help. It offers help in a form that should be enough. Sufficiency is a theological category here: what God sends is enough for the need He is addressing. The need being addressed is the heart that looks at Quraysh's three thousand and feels the thinness after Ibn Ubayy's withdrawal.

**Angels "sent down."** *Munzalīn* — being sent down. The passive keeps the agency with God. The Prophet announces; God sends. This prepares 126's insistence that *naṣr* is only from God. Angels are not an independent force the community can requisition. They are a form of God's act.

**Why the verse matters before the condition.** If 125's *ṣabr* and *taqwā* arrived without 124's offer, the condition would feel like a locked gate. With 124 first, the gate is an open offer under terms. God is not reluctant to help. He is unwilling to let help become a substitute for the moral posture help is meant to sustain.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** The modern equivalent of wanting angels without condition is wanting outcomes without the inner work the outcome requires — wanting the job without the integrity, the reconciliation without the apology, the communal strength without the personal obedience. Verse 124 is generous. Verse 125 will be exacting. Read them together or misread both.


===VERSE-END===

## Verse 125

**"Most certainly, if you ˹believers˺ are firm and mindful ˹of Allah˺ and the enemy launches a sudden attack on you, Allah will reinforce you with five thousand angels designated ˹for battle˺."**

In simple terms: of course — if you remain firm and mindful of God, and the enemy comes at you in a sudden rush, your Lord will reinforce you with five thousand angels specially marked.

**The number rises, and a condition is attached.**

**"Most certainly, if you are firm and mindful of Allah."** *Balā in taṣbirū wa-tattaqū.*

*Balā* — yes, indeed, the particle of affirmation after a negative question. *In taṣbirū wa-tattaqū* — if you are patient/steadfast and if you have *taqwā*. The same pair that closed verse 120 as the shield against schemes now opens as the condition of angelic reinforcement in battle. The surah is ruthlessly consistent. Character is security. Character is also the gate of unseen help.

**"And the enemy launches a sudden attack on you."** *Wa-ya'tūkum min fawrihim hādhā.* From this rush of theirs, this immediate coming. The scenario is the enemy's rapid advance — exactly the kind of shock that breaks undisciplined lines.

**"Allah will reinforce you with five thousand angels designated for battle."** *Yumdidkum rabbukum bi-khamsati ālāfin min al-malā'ikati musawwimīn.*

Five thousand. *Musawwimīn* — marked, distinguished, designated, wearing marks. The tafsir discusses what the marking means (marked horses, distinctive signs); the text's force is that the help is specific, appointed, not vague.

**The condition and Uḥud's outcome.** Ibn Kathīr states the hard reading clearly: the promise was conditional on *ṣabr* and *taqwā*; at Uḥud the Muslims were not fully patient — they ran — and so did not receive the support of even one angel in the way Badr had. Other readers soften the absolute; the grammatical condition stands either way. What must not be done is to read 124–125 as a blank cheque that Uḥud cashed in full. The Qur'an is about to narrate failure. The condition explains how promise and failure can sit in one Book without contradiction: the promise had terms.

**Connection to 8:9.** At Badr the request for help and the answer of a thousand angels (then successive reinforcements in some reports) stand as the precedent the Prophet ﷺ is drawing on. The God of Badr is the God of Uḥud. The variable is not God. The variable is whether the line holds the terms.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** People love the five thousand. They skip the *in taṣbirū wa-tattaqū*. Verse 125 is a mirror for every spirituality that wants reinforcement without discipline, breakthrough without *taqwā*, the unseen without the post held on the hill. The angels are God's to send. The condition is yours to meet — or not.

**Five thousand and the mark.** *Musawwimīn* — marked, distinguished, signed. The classical pictures vary: wool on the horses' forelocks, white marks, known banners. What the word forces is recognisability. These are not anonymous forces. They are designated. The imagination is meant to see help as real, not as metaphor — and still, after 126, not as the source.

**The condition is the verse's centre of gravity.** *Ṣabr* and *taqwā* — the same pair that nullified *kayd* at 120 — are now the terms on which the larger reinforcement arrives. Ibn Kathīr and others read Uḥud against this condition with hard honesty: when the archers left the post and the line broke, the condition was not held, and the day did not become a second Badr of angelic fighting. Other voices nuance how far the unseen help still operated. The text's own clarity is the *if*. Promise without the *if* would make the later autopsy impossible. The *if* without the promise would make Badr's memory a taunt. Both are given.

**"This is good news only" waits one verse.** Already 125's offer is large. 126 will stop the large offer from becoming a superstition. Read them as a pair: generous condition, then theological relocation of *naṣr*.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** Conditions in divine promises are not tricks. They are the shape of the help matching the shape of the human being helped. A heart without *ṣabr* and *taqwā* cannot carry certain kinds of victory without being destroyed by them. The archers' early success without the condition became the door of the reverse. Verse 125 is not God being difficult. It is God refusing to fortify a posture that will not hold the fortification.


===VERSE-END===

## Verse 126

**"Allah ordained this ˹reinforcement˺ only as good news for you and reassurance for your hearts. And victory comes only from Allah—the Almighty, All-Wise—"**

In simple terms: God made this promise of reinforcement only as good news for you and to put your hearts at rest. Victory comes only from God — the Almighty, the All-Wise.

**The theology under the angels.**

**"Allah ordained this only as good news for you and reassurance for your hearts."** *Wa-mā jaʿalahu Allāhu illā bushrā lakum wa-li-taṭma'inna qulūbukum bihi.*

*Bushrā* — good news, glad tidings. *Li-taṭma'inna qulūbukum* — so that your hearts might be at peace, reassured. The angels are not the engine of victory in the way a pagan army might think of divine mascots. They are God's way of steadying hearts. The real engine is named next.

**"And victory comes only from Allah — the Almighty, All-Wise."** *Wa-mā al-naṣru illā min ʿindi Allāhi al-ʿazīzi al-ḥakīm.*

*Mā … illā* — the exclusivity formula. Help is from nowhere else. *Al-ʿAzīz* — the Almighty, who cannot be overpowered. *Al-Ḥakīm* — the All-Wise, who places help where wisdom places it, including withholding it when conditions are not met, including teaching through a wound. The two names together block two errors: the error that God cannot help, and the error that God must help on our schedule and terms.

**Why this verse matters after 124–125.** Without 126, the angel-verses could feed a magical mentality: collect the right number of unseen allies and win. With 126, the angels are demoted to *bushrā* and the heart's calm, and *naṣr* is relocated to God alone. Uḥud's coming pain will be readable only inside this theology. If help is only from God, then a day without the help we wanted is still a day inside His *ʿizzah* and *ḥikmah* — not a day outside His reach.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** The modern form of wanting angels without 126 is wanting outcomes without surrender: techniques, guarantees, spiritual formulas that make God a vending machine. Verse 126 breaks the machine. Good news, yes. Calm hearts, yes. Victory from Him alone — and He is Wise, including when the day does not look like Badr.

**Bushrā is not a small word.** In the Qur'an's lexicon, *bushrā* is what prophets bring, what the angels bring to those who said "our Lord is God" and then went straight, what arrives as the opposite of fear. To call the angelic reinforcement *bushrā* is to place it in the register of gospel, not in the register of weapon inventory. The hearts that need calm are the same hearts that nearly cowered at 122 and that must hold posts at 121. Calm is not optional soft feeling. It is combat equipment.

**"Reassurance for your hearts."** *Li-taṭma'inna qulūbukum bihi.* The *bihi* — by it / through it — points back to the making of the promise. God makes the promise so that hearts settle. Settled hearts fight differently, obey differently, recover differently. Uḥud's later panic when the cry went up that the Prophet had been killed is the negative image of this *iṭmiʾnān*: hearts that have lost their centre.

**The exclusivity formula again.** *Wa-mā al-naṣru illā min ʿindi Allāh.* The surah has been teaching *naṣr*-vocabulary since the disciples asked to be God's helpers and since those who reject were told they have no helpers. Now the lesson is turned inward: even your helpers, even your angels, do not relocate the source. The source is *ʿindi Allāh* — from God's presence, from His side.

**Al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm as the pair that survives defeat.** If the only divine name over battle were *al-ʿAzīz*, a loss could feel like God's absence. *Al-Ḥakīm* beside it means a loss can still be inside wisdom — teaching, sorting, exposing the love of spoils, preparing a community for longer fidelity. Uḥud without *al-Ḥakīm* is either meaningless pain or proof against God. Uḥud with both names is a severe mercy the rest of the surah will interpret.

**Connection to 3:13 and 3:165.** The sight of the two hosts at Badr was already an *āyah*. The question after Uḥud — "whence is this?" — will be answered partly by pointing the community at itself. Verse 126 is the hinge theology that makes both readings possible: help is from God, so Badr was God; help is from God under wisdom, so Uḥud is not God-abandoned.


===VERSE-END===

## Verse 127

**"to destroy a group of the disbelievers and humble the rest, causing them to withdraw in disappointment."**

In simple terms: so that He may cut off a portion of those who disbelieved, or restrain them so they go back frustrated, having failed in their aim.

**The purpose-clause of the help.**

**"To destroy a group of the disbelievers and humble the rest, causing them to withdraw in disappointment."** The Arabic is more spare: *li-yaqṭaʿa ṭarafan min alladhīna kafarū aw yakbitahum fa-yanqalibū khā'ibīn* — that He may cut off a flank / a portion of those who disbelieved, or suppress them, so they turn back disappointed.

*Ṭaraf* — an edge, a portion, a flank. Not necessarily the whole army destroyed. A cutting of a part. *Yakbitahum* — to humiliate, restrain, throw down. *Khā'ibīn* — empty-handed, having failed to get what they came for.

**Uḥud in this light.** Quraysh came to wipe out the shame of Badr and, if possible, Madinah's new power. They killed seventy Muslims. They wounded the Prophet ﷺ. They did not take the city. They did not destroy the community. They went home without the decisive end they wanted. In the verse's vocabulary, a portion was cut, and the rest withdrew *khā'ibīn* in the strategic sense that matters for the religion's survival — even though the day felt, and was, a wound. Holding the wound and the frustration of the enemy at once is part of reading Uḥud as the Qur'an reads it, not only as a defeat-table.

**Connection to 126.** Because *naṣr* is from God alone, the "cutting" and the "frustrating" are His acts, not the automatic product of numbers or angels. Badr was one shape of that act. Uḥud is another. The same *ʿAzīz*, the same *Ḥakīm*.

**Li- of purpose.** The verse continues the purpose of the help God alone gives. Help is not for Muslim swagger. It is *li-yaqṭaʿa* — that He may cut — and *aw yakbitahum* — or that He may throw them down / frustrate them. The goals are God's goals against disbelief's project, not the ego-goals of a victorious party.

**"A portion" not "all."** *Ṭarafan min alladhīna kafarū* — a flank, an edge, a portion of those who disbelieved. The Qur'an does not script total annihilation as the only acceptable outcome of *naṣr*. A cut that stops an existential threat to the community can be *naṣr*. A withdrawal in frustration can be *naṣr*. Readers who can only recognise victory as spectacle will misread both Badr and Uḥud — Badr as pure triumph without leftover enemies, Uḥud as pure loss without strategic frustration of Quraysh's aim.

**Khā'ibīn — the disappointed.** *Khā'ib* is the one who fails to obtain what he sought. Quraysh sought the crushing of the Madinan project and the erasing of Badr's shame in a final key. They killed, they wounded, they did not finish. The verse's word for their return is not "triumphant." It is *khā'ibīn*. Holding that word beside the Muslim dead is part of adult reading. Both are true. The Qur'an refuses to let either cancel the other.

**Why this lands after 126.** Because if *naṣr* is only from God, then the cutting and the disappointing are His metrics, not ours. We do not get to redefine *naṣr* as whatever would have made us feel best at sundown. We receive the day under *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm* and learn His metrics over time — which is what the long Uḥud commentary after verse 130 will force the community to do.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** Not every answered prayer looks like the answer you pictured. Sometimes the "cut" is partial. Sometimes the opponent goes home without having gotten the thing that would have ended you, and that is the help, even while you bandage your own face. Verse 127 trains the eye to recognise frustration of evil as a form of help, not only spectacular win as a form of help.


===VERSE-END===

## Verse 128

**"You ˹O Prophet˺ have no say in the matter. It is up to Allah to turn to them in mercy or punish them, for indeed they are wrongdoers."**

In simple terms: you have no share in the decision. It is for God whether He turns to them in mercy or punishes them. They are wrongdoers.

**One of the great stripping-verses of the Qur'an**, addressed to the Prophet ﷺ at the point of his own blood.

**"You, O Prophet, have no say in the matter."** *Laysa laka min al-amri shay'.*

Nothing of the *amr* — the matter, the decision, the command — is yours. The half-bracketed "O Prophet" is correct. This is not a general slogan first. It is a sentence spoken to the wounded Messenger about the people who wounded him.

**The reported occasion.** Multiple authentic routes converge. In Bukhārī and Muslim and Aḥmad: at Uḥud the Prophet ﷺ's face was cut, a tooth broken, blood on his face, and he said something like, "How can a people prosper who have bloodied their Prophet while he calls them to their Lord?" Or he invoked in prayer against specific Quraysh leaders. Then this verse was revealed. The decision — mercy or punishment — is not his to assign. It is God's.

The reports that he later prayed, "O God, forgive my people, for they do not know," sit in the same moral world even when attached to other occasions in the sīrah: the Prophet's default under injury is not a permanent curse. The verse does not scold him for pain. It relocates the final say.

**"It is up to Allah to turn to them in mercy or punish them."** *Aw yatūba ʿalayhim aw yuʿadhdhibahum.*

*Yatūba ʿalayhim* — turn to them in *tawbah*, grant them the gift of repentance and receive it. *Aw yuʿadhdhibahum* — or punish them. Both remain open. The people who bloodied the Prophet ﷺ are not, by that act alone, locked out of mercy by the Prophet's mouth. God may still turn to them. History would later show some of Uḥud's enemy leadership entering Islam. The verse left that door on God's hinge, not on a wounded man's understandable impulse.

**"For indeed they are wrongdoers."** *Fa-innahum ẓālimūn.* The openness of mercy does not rewrite the moral fact. They are *ẓālimūn* — people who put things out of place, who wronged. Punishment would be just. Mercy would be gift. Neither is the Prophet's to decree.

**What this verse does to prophethood's portrait.** The Messenger is the best of creation in the Muslim confession. He is not a partner in the final disposal of servants. *Laysa laka min al-amri shay'* is *tawḥīd* applied at the most sensitive moment — when the man most entitled to feel that the matter is his is told it is not. If he has no share in the *amr*, no one else does either.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** The transferable force is fierce and kind. Fierce: you do not get to script the end of your enemies' stories. Kind: you do not have to. The rage that wants a final curse, the grief that wants a final lock, the activism that wants to be the door of heaven and hell — verse 128 takes that burden off human shoulders, including prophetic ones, and leaves it with God. Your job under injury is the job the Prophet ﷺ kept: call, stand, bleed if you must, and leave the *amr* where it belongs.

**And the spiritual centre.** A religion that will strip its own Prophet of the final say in the moment of his wounding is a religion serious about God. This verse is among the reasons.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 129

**"To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. He forgives whoever He wills, and punishes whoever He wills. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful."**

In simple terms: to God alone belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. He forgives whom He wills and punishes whom He wills. And God is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**The cosmic ground under 128.**

**"To Allah alone belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth."** Ownership again — continuous with 3:109 and 3:83. Because He owns the servants, He decides their end. The Prophet ﷺ does not own them. The community does not own them. The enemy does not own themselves in the final sense.

**"He forgives whoever He wills, and punishes whoever He wills."** *Yaghfiru li-man yashā'u wa-yuʿadhdhibu man yashā'.*

The twin *yashā'* — whom He wills — is not arbitrariness. It is sovereignty. The will that forgives and the will that punishes are the will of the *ʿAzīz* and *Ḥakīm* already named, the will that left the door open at 3:89 and closed the ransom at 3:91. Human beings do not get a vote on the will. They get a revelation about how to live inside it.

**"And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful."** The verse does not end on punishment. It ends on *Ghafūr* and *Raḥīm*. After a verse that authorised punishment as a live option against wrongdoers, the names that close the sequence are forgiveness and mercy. That is the same pedagogy as 86–89: wall, then gate; severity, then the Names that outrun severity for anyone who turns.

**Connection forward to 130.** Forgiveness and mercy are not only about enemy combatants. They are the climate in which a community is about to be told to stop eating *ribā*. A community that wants God's mercy while consuming people through usury has not heard 129 as the ground of 130.

**Ownership as the ground of mercy and punishment.** The sequence is deliberate. First: everything in the heavens and the earth is His. Then: He forgives whom He wills and punishes whom He wills. Then: He is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. Sovereignty is not announced in order to make God a tyrant in the imagination. It is announced in order to take the final keys out of human hands — including prophetic hands, including activist hands, including the hands of the wounded who want the story ended in blood.

**Yaghfiru and yuʿadhdhibu as paired wills.** The verse does not say He forgives the good and punishes the bad as a mechanical slot machine. It says whom He wills. The rest of the Qur'an fills the content of that will: He has made Himself bound to mercy, He accepts *tawbah*, He does not wrong people by an atom's weight, He has forbidden oppression to Himself. "Whom He wills" in a vacuum would be terror. "Whom He wills" inside the whole Book is sovereignty that has already told you what it loves and what it hates.

**Why end on Ghafūr Raḥīm after authorising punishment.** Because 128 left both doors open — *tawbah* or *ʿadhāb* — and 129 must not let the punishment door become the only door the reader remembers. The Names that close the verse are the climate of the open door. A community that has been wronged, as Madinah had been wronged at Uḥud, needs to hear that God can punish *and* that His closing self-description here is forgiveness and mercy. Otherwise grief hardens into a theology of permanent curse.

**Connection to 3:31 and the following of the Prophet.** Love of God is tied to following the Messenger, and following the Messenger includes following him in not seizing the *amr* of people's final ends. Verse 129 is what you stand on when you refuse to play God over your enemies: they belong to the Owner of the heavens and the earth. So do you.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** The practical fruit is a strange freedom. You may name wrong — the verse itself calls them *ẓālimūn* in 128. You may seek justice by every lawful means. You may not close the book on a human soul as if you held the *mulk*. The parent writing off a child, the community writing off a sinner, the heart writing off an enemy nation — all of that impulse meets 129. Ownership is God's. Forgiveness is His to give. The Names He leaves you with, after blood on the Prophet's face, are *Ghafūr* and *Raḥīm*.


===VERSE-END===

## Verse 130

**"O believers! Do not consume interest, multiplying it many times over. And be mindful of Allah, so you may prosper."**

In simple terms: O you who believe — do not devour usury, multiplying it over and over. Be mindful of God, so that you may prosper.

**The turn that surprises only readers who think war and money are different moral worlds.**

**"O believers! Do not consume interest, multiplying it many times over."** *Lā ta'kulū al-ribā aḍʿāfan muḍāʿafah.*

*Lā ta'kulū* — do not eat, do not devour. The Qur'an's verb for *ribā* is the verb of consumption: something that eats the one who eats it. *Al-ribā* — the excess, usury, the increase taken without the risk and equity of trade that 2:275 distinguishes. *Aḍʿāfan muḍāʿafah* — doubled and redoubled, multiplied and compounded.

**Is only compound interest forbidden?** No. The classical consensus, reading this verse with 2:275–279, is that the "doubled and redoubled" describes the *form ribā took* in the jāhiliyyah and the *direction it always tends* — not a restriction that makes simple interest lawful. Al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī, and the fiqh tradition treat the phrase as emphasis and as a picture of how *ribā* works in practice: the debt that cannot be paid is deferred for an increase; the increase is added to the principal; the next year doubles again; the borrower is eaten. Even a "simple" *ribā* practice, reinvested, becomes *aḍʿāfan muḍāʿafah*. The full prohibition is in al-Baqarah. This verse names the practice at its most visibly predatory and orders *taqwā* as the way out.

**Why here, after Uḥud?** Several complementary answers appear in the tradition and in careful modern reading:

1. **Moral unity of the community under stress.** A community that is about to re-learn discipline on a battlefield must also re-learn discipline in the market. The same *taqwā* that holds a post holds a contract.
2. **War and debt.** Battles produce need. Need produces borrowing. Borrowing under jāhiliyyah rules produces *ribā* that doubles the wounded community's wounds. The verse cuts that path before the next verses continue the Uḥud autopsy.
3. **Badr's spoils and Uḥud's loss.** The economic psychology of victory and defeat — greed for spoils that contributed to leaving the archers' post — is not unrelated to the economic psychology of *ribā*. Both are ways of trying to secure increase without the full moral cost of trust in God.
4. **Sequence toward God and the Fire.** Verses 130–131 (131 falls in the next batch) will pair the ban on *ribā* with fear of the Fire prepared for the disbelievers — a stern adjacency that makes usury a matter of the end, not only of regulation.

**"And be mindful of Allah, so you may prosper."** *Wa-ttaqū Allāha laʿallakum tufliḥūn.*

*Taqwā* again — the batch's keyword with 122's trust, 123's gratitude, 125's condition, 120's shield. *Tufliḥūn* — that you may prosper, succeed, flourish. The same *falāḥ* that closed 3:104 on the community of mission. Here *falāḥ* is attached to leaving *ribā*. Real prosperity is not the doubled return. It is the success that *taqwā* makes possible before God.

**Connection to 2:275–279.** This verse does not replace al-Baqarah's longer treatment. It re-activates it inside Āl 'Imrān's war-and-community argument. The reader who wants the fiqh of *ribā* in full must go there. The reader who wants to know why a battle-sūrah cares about *ribā* is being answered here: because the God who posts you at dawn (121) and strips your Prophet of the final say (128) is the same God who forbids you to eat people through money (130).

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** *Ribā* in the full classical sense is a technical legal category; applying it to modern finance is a live scholarly field and not something a verse-commentary should settle in a paragraph. What the verse makes unavoidable for any honest reader is the moral shape: increase that multiplies itself on the back of distress, contracts that double when the debtor cannot pay, systems that call the eating of the weak "growth." A community coming out of Uḥud — or out of any collective wound — is told not to fund its recovery by becoming a predator in the market. *Taqwā* is the prosperity strategy. Doubling the debt of the desperate is not.

**And the spiritual centre of the ten verses.** Dawn. Near-collapse. Memory of Badr. Angels as good news under condition. Help from God alone. A flank cut, an enemy frustrated. A wounded Prophet told he owns none of the final decision. Ownership of the heavens and the earth; forgiveness and punishment as He wills; the Names *Ghafūr* and *Raḥīm*. And then: do not eat *ribā* doubled and redoubled — fear God, that you may flourish.

The field and the market are one moral world. Uḥud has begun to teach that. The next batches will keep teaching it in blood, burial, and the long argument with the community about why the day went as it did.

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

These ten verses open the Uḥud movement of Āl 'Imrān. They are frame, not full autopsy — the autopsy continues after 130 — but the frame already contains the theology the autopsy will need.

**121–123 set the morning and the memory.** The Prophet ﷺ leaves his household at dawn to post the believers; God is All-Hearing, All-Knowing (121). Two groups nearly lose heart; God is their *walī*; so let believers trust in Him (122). Badr is recalled: you were weak and He helped you; so have *taqwā*, that you may be grateful (123). Ordeal is entered through arrangement, near-failure without shame, and gratitude-memory rather than entitlement-memory.

**124–127 set the unseen and its terms.** The Prophet ﷺ reassures with three thousand angels (124). Yes — if you have *ṣabr* and *taqwā* and the enemy rushes you, five thousand marked angels (125). God made this only as good news and heart-calm; victory is from Him alone, the Almighty, the Wise (126) — to cut off a flank of the disbelievers or restrain them so they withdraw disappointed (127). Promise without condition would make Uḥud unintelligible. Condition without promise would make Badr unintelligible. The surah gives both, and relocates *naṣr* to God.

**128–129 strip the Prophet and name the Owner.** You have no share in the decision — whether He turns to them in mercy or punishes them; they are wrongdoers (128). To Him belong the heavens and the earth; He forgives whom He wills and punishes whom He wills; He is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful (129). Revealed in the atmosphere of the Prophet's wound and his question or invocation, these verses are *tawḥīd* at the point of maximum human entitlement to rage. Mercy remains possible for wrongdoers. The door is God's.

**130 joins the market to the field.** Do not devour *ribā* doubled and redoubled; have *taqwā*, that you may prosper. Not a restriction of the prohibition to compound rates — a picture of *ribā*'s real tendency, continuous with 2:275–279. Placed here because a wounded community must not fund its life by eating the distressed, and because the same *taqwā* that holds a post holds a contract.

**Threads carried and extended.**

- ***Ṣabr and taqwā*** — shield against *kayd* (120) become condition of angelic help (125) and the path to *falāḥ* without *ribā* (130).
- ***Tawakkul*** — explicit at 122 after God is named *walī* of the nearly-broken.
- ***Naṣr*** — from God alone (126), continuous with the surah's long *n-ṣ-r* thread; Badr as prior *naṣr* (123).
- ***Tawḥīd of the amr*** — 128 strips even the Prophet of the final disposal of servants; pairs with 3:79–80's refusal of human lordship and 3:109's return of all matters to God.
- **Mercy open after wrong** — 128–129 with 3:89; wrongdoers may still be turned to in *tawbah*.
- **Economy and ordeal** — 130 with 3:91–92 and 3:116–117: wealth's tense, ground, and predation are never far from the surah's moral centre.

**Where the supplied translation has chosen, it has been flagged.** Half-bracketed additions ("Remember, O Prophet," "believers," "vastly," "for battle," "reinforcement," "designated," "O Prophet" at 128, "alone") are interpretive aids, mostly exact. "Cower" for *tafshalā* is strong and fair. "Reassured them" for *wa-Allāhu waliyyuhumā* names the effect more than the *walī*-relationship — restored in commentary. "Multiply it many times over" for *aḍʿāfan muḍāʿafah* is accurate dynamic equivalence for doubled-and-redoubled. "Interest" for *ribā* is the standing modern rendering and carries the usual need for fiqh precision beyond the verse. None of these is distortion; each is a choice.

**The verse to leave with is 128**, read under 126 and toward 130. Help is from God alone. The final say about mercy and punishment is God's alone — even when the Prophet bleeds. And the community that wants to flourish after a wound does not double the debts of the desperate. Uḥud has started to speak. The next batch will keep listening.

**Other works**
- Sīrah and maghāzī — Uḥud's order of events as background, held as historical narrative under the Qur'an's authority rather than as a second scripture
- Works of fiqh — *ribā* definitions; the relationship of 3:130 to 2:275–279; consultation (*shūrā*) as the backdrop to going out
- Works of ʿaqīdah — *tawḥīd* of the *amr*; the Prophet's humanity and wound; hope of *tawbah* for wrongdoers

- Modern Muslim commentary — Maududi, Muftī Muḥammad Shafīʿ, Ibn ʿĀshūr — on Uḥud's moral architecture and the placement of *ribā*, used under source-priority discipline

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given, because this is a synthesized commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and inventing precise references would be worse than omitting them.

Five places in this batch required particular care.

**The first is the Uḥud frame itself.** Numbers (three thousand Quraysh, seven hundred Muslims after defection, seventy dead) and tactical detail are sīrah-grade and vary in reports. I have used them as widely attested background, not as Qur'anic claims. The text's own claims — dawn posting, two groups nearly failing, Badr's prior help, conditional reinforcement, help from God alone, the Prophet's empty hands in the *amr* — are the floor.

**The second is 3:122.** "About to cower" is near-failure, not completed desertion. God as *walī* of those groups blocks cheap shaming. Banū Salimah and Banū Ḥārithah are named in Bukhārī-linked identification, not in the verse.

**The third is 3:124–125 and the angels.** Connection to Badr (8:9) noted; condition of *ṣabr* and *taqwā* stressed; Ibn Kathīr's hard reading that Uḥud did not meet the condition and so did not receive Badr-like angelic fighting recorded as a major classical view without being forced as the only view.

**The fourth is 3:128.** Occasion of the wound and the Prophet's words/invocation is multiply attested. The verse strips him of the final say without stripping him of prophethood or of legitimate human pain. Mercy remains open for *ẓālimūn*; punishment remains just. Later entry into Islam of some enemy figures is noted as history that fits the open door, not as proof the verse "predicted" named individuals.

**The fifth is 3:130 and *ribā*.** "Doubled and redoubled" is descriptive and emphatic, not a loophole for simple interest — continuous with 2:275–279 and the classical consensus. Modern financial application is flagged as a live fiqh field beyond this verse's job. The placement after Uḥud is read as moral unity of field and market under *taqwā*.

**On the translation.** "Cower," "reassured," "vastly outnumbered," "designated," "multiplying it many times over," and "interest" for *ribā* are choices noted above. Half-bracketed voice-markers are helpful. The translation is not wrong; it has chosen, and the reader is entitled to know what was chosen.

Two boundaries observed throughout. No Companion who nearly lost heart has been treated as a villain; the text's own mercy toward them is normative. And no reading of 128 has been allowed to become a quiet reduction of the Prophet ﷺ — the verse exalts God by limiting even His beloved's share in the *amr*, which is the opposite of diminishing the beloved. The batch stops at the threshold of the longer Uḥud autopsy. Waiting for authorisation to continue.
