# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 161–170

This batch opens in severity and closes in the ordinary, and the seam between them is the argument of the surah. The curse is pronounced on those who die in rejection, with the condition stated precisely (161–162). Then the oneness of God is declared, in the sentence that is the religion's centre, followed immediately by mercy (163). Then seven signs in the created world are enumerated for those who reason (164). Then the measure of faith is given — not as correctness of belief but as intensity of love — and the rivals are named (165). Then the scene on the Day when leaders disown followers and followers wish to disown leaders back, and every connection is severed (166–167). Then the religion turns to the ordinary: eat what is lawful and good, and do not follow Satan step by step (168). Then his three commands, of which the third and last is speaking about God without knowledge (169). And finally the ancestral argument, stated in the objectors' own words and dismantled by a single question (170).

---

## Verse 161

**"Surely those who disbelieve and die as disbelievers are condemned by Allah, the angels, and all of humanity."**

In simple terms: those who reject and die while they are rejecters — upon them is the curse of Allah, and of the angels, and of all people together.

*Inna alladhina kafaru wa matu wa hum kuffarun* — "indeed those who disbelieved and died **while they were disbelievers**."

**And this clause is the whole weight of the verse, and it must not be read past.**

*Wa hum kuffar* is a circumstantial clause describing the state at the moment of death. The curse is not attached to having disbelieved at some point in a life. It is attached to dying in that state. A person who rejected and then believed is not in this verse. A person who rejected for forty years and turned at the end is not in this verse.

The definitive corroboration is 4:17–18: "The repentance accepted by Allah is only for those who do wrong in ignorance [or carelessness] and then repent soon [after]... But repentance is not [accepted] of those who [continue to] do evil deeds up until, when death comes to one of them, he says, 'Indeed, I have now repented' — **nor of those who die while they are disbelievers**."

So the verse is not a closing statement about people. It is a statement about a **state at a moment**, and the moment is death. Which means the correct pastoral reading of 161 is not "this is final for those people" but "the door stands open until that moment, and it is the only door that closes."

*Ula'ika 'alayhim la'natu Allahi wa al-mala'ikati wa al-nasi ajma'in* — "those — upon them is the curse of Allah, and the angels, and all people together."

*La'n* is from a root meaning to drive away and to distance. From Allah it is expulsion and distance from His mercy. And note the comprehensiveness: Allah, the angels, and *al-nas ajma'in* — all people, together. The parallel is 2:159, immediately preceding, where the concealers are cursed by Allah and by *al-la'inun*, all who curse.

The commentators located the cursing by "all people" on the Day of Resurrection, when the believers will curse the rejecters — a scene the Qur'an stages elsewhere, as at 40:47 and 14:21 and 33:67–68, where the weak and the arrogant argue within the Fire.

For practice, three things, and the first is a guardrail that has been maintained throughout this commentary and must be restated here.

**No individual is named by this verse.** We do not know anyone's final state. A person can be known to us as a rejecter for the whole of the life we observed, and we still do not know what happened in the last hour of it, or what was in the heart that we never saw. The verse describes a category whose membership is known to Allah. Our business is the conveying, and 2:119's rule stands: no one may declare a specific person an inhabitant of the Fire.

The second is the urgency, and it is real precisely *because* the door closes. The verse is not tender, and it is not meant to be. There is a version of religious speech that treats the Hereafter as a matter of tone and reassurance, and this verse is the antidote to it. Something is at stake, and the stake is stated.

And the third is the converse, which is where most readers actually live. If the curse attaches to the state at death, then the entire span before that is available. There is no point in a person's life at which return is closed to him — not after a long rejection, not after public rejection, not after persecuting believers, not after concealing what he knew. The verse names one closure and it is at death. Every other apparent closure is a feeling, not a ruling.

## Verse 162

**"They will be in Hell forever. Their punishment will not be lightened, nor will they be delayed ˹from it˺."**

In simple terms: abiding therein forever. The punishment will not be lightened for them, nor will they be given respite.

*Khalidina fiha* — "abiding therein eternally."

The crux is *khulud* — eternal abiding — and the disagreement about it must be stated honestly rather than suppressed.

**The position of Ahl al-Sunnah, held by consensus across the schools and by the overwhelming majority of classical scholarship, is that the punishment of those who die in rejection is eternal and without end**, and that the Qur'anic statements of *khulud* are to be taken in their plain sense. The supporting texts are numerous and consistent in meaning: 2:39, 3:88, 4:56, 4:168–169, 43:74 ("Indeed, the criminals will be in the punishment of Hell, abiding eternally").

A minority position exists holding that the Fire will eventually come to an end, or that the stay of its inhabitants is not eternal. It is attributed to some later writers, and reports to that effect are transmitted from a small number of early figures. **The scholarly assessment of those transmitted reports is that they are weak and not established**, and the classical literature — including the extended treatments of the question by Ibn Taymiyyah and Ibn al-Qayyim — examines them and does not accept the doctrine they are cited for. The point is worth knowing so that a reader who encounters the view is not unsettled by it, and worth bounding so that a marginal position resting on weak narration is not presented as an open question.

*La yukhaffafu 'anhumu al-'adhabu* — "the punishment will not be lightened for them." *Yukhaffaf* is form II of the root meaning lightness: it will not be made lighter, reduced, or eased.

*Wa la hum yunzarun* — "nor will they be given respite." *Yunzarun* is form IV of a root meaning to look and to wait: they will not be given time, or a postponement, or a reprieve.

The parallel is 3:88: "abiding eternally therein. The punishment will not be lightened for them, nor will they be reprieved." And 43:75: "They will not be granted any reduction, and they therein are in despair."

For practice, the essential thing in this verse is the distinction it does *not* state but which the reader will supply if it is not supplied for him.

**Eternity in the Fire is for those who die in rejection. It is not for believers who sinned.**

That distinction is what prevents both of the errors this verse otherwise produces in a reader. The first error is despair: a person who has faith and a great many sins reads "forever" and concludes that he is included. The second is presumption: a person who has faith and few sins reads the exclusion and concludes that he is safe.

The text that settles both is recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim: the Prophet ﷺ said that **whoever has in his heart an atom's weight of faith will come out of the Fire.** The tradition's position, built on the massively transmitted hadiths of intercession, is that believing sinners are punished according to their sins and then — by Allah's permission and for those with whom He is pleased, as established at 2:255 and 20:109 — brought out.

So the two negations in this verse — no lightening, no respite — apply to the state described in verse 161. They are not a description of what happens to a Muslim who failed.

And there is a second application in *la hum yunzarun*, the denial of respite. The word is about delay, and the denial of it means there is no interval in which the matter can be revisited. That is why the tradition treats the present life as the only place where the sentence can still be changed: not because the next life is unjust, but because the next life is the execution of what was settled here.

## Verse 163

**"Your God is ˹only˺ One God. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful."**

In simple terms: and your God is one God. There is no god except Him, the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful.

*Wa ilahukum ilahun wahidun* — "and your God is one God."

*Ilah* is from a root meaning to worship, and it denotes the one who is worshipped. Note the construction: *ilahukum* — your God — *ilahun* — is a God — *wahidun* — one. The subject and the predicate are the same word, so the oneness is stated in the strongest available form: the God you worship is, as to His identity, one.

*La ilaha illa huwa* — "there is no god except Him."

**And this sentence is the centre of the religion, and its grammar is itself the doctrine.**

*La* here is the absolute negative — the particle that negates the entire genus. Not "there is no god among these" but "there is no god, categorically, at all." *Ilaha* is indefinite and in the accusative, which is the construction for a total negation. *Illa huwa* — except Him.

The tradition states this as two movements and insists on both: **nafy** (negation) and **ithbat** (affirmation). And the ordering matters. The negation comes first and it is total.

That is the thing that makes this sentence different from "God is one." A person can say "there is one God" and still worship other things alongside Him — the pre-Islamic Arabs did exactly that, affirming Allah as creator while taking intercessors besides Him (10:18, 39:3, 29:61–63). But a person cannot say "there is no god except Him" and then fear, hope in, or obey anything else absolutely. The negation is what forecloses the exception, and the affirmation is what prevents the negation from becoming mere emptiness. Negation without affirmation is denial. Affirmation without negation permits association. The sentence requires both.

And there is a precision about *ilah* that the theologians made and that matters for how the sentence is understood. *La ilaha* does not mean "nothing is worshipped" — because plenty of things are worshipped. It means **nothing deserves worship** (*la ma'buda bi-haqqin*). The sentence is a claim about worth, not a claim about practice.

*Al-rahmanu al-rahim* — "the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful."

**And note what is attached to the declaration of oneness.** These are the two Names that open every surah of the Qur'an but one, and here they close the verse of *tawhid*.

That is deliberate, and the significance is large. The oneness of God is not announced as a bare claim to power. It is announced with mercy attached, immediately, in the same sentence. The God who is one is *al-Rahman al-Rahim* — and the tradition has always read the two Names as differing in scope: *al-Rahman* for the mercy that encompasses all creation in this life, and *al-Rahim* for the mercy particular to the believers, in this life and the next, with the difference in their grammatical form (*fa'lan* indicating fullness and transience of application, *fa'il* indicating the established attribute).

Compare the other great *tawhid* verses and you see the pairing shift with the argument: 20:8 pairs it with "to Him belong the most beautiful names"; 2:255 pairs it with *al-'Aliyy al-'Azim*; 112 pairs it with *al-Samad*. Here it is mercy — because this verse follows two of the severest in the surah.

For practice, two things.

The first is the test the sentence supplies, which is more searching than it looks. If *la ilaha illa huwa* is a claim about worth and not merely about existence, then the practical question is: what do you actually treat as deserving ultimate regard? The tradition's standard formulation is that worship is constituted by absolute love, absolute hope, absolute fear and absolute obedience — and that whoever gives any of those four to something other than God has given it the thing the sentence reserves. That is a harder test than "do I believe in one God," and it is the test the sentence is making.

And the second is the mercy. A person who has just read two of the most terrifying verses in the Qur'an is given, in the very next sentence, the declaration of oneness followed immediately by *al-Rahman al-Rahim*. The order of the surah is itself a piece of pastoral care. Severity is never left as the last word in this book, and here the correction comes within one verse.

## Verse 164

**"Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth; the alternation of the day and the night; the ships that sail the sea for the benefit of humanity; the rain sent down from Allah from the skies, reviving the earth after its death; the scattering of all kinds of creatures throughout; the shifting of the winds; and the clouds drifting between the heavens and the earth—˹in all of this˺ are surely signs for people of understanding."**

In simple terms: in the creation of the heavens and the earth, the alternation of night and day, the ships that course the sea with what benefits people, the water Allah sends down from the sky giving life to the earth after its death, the scattering of every creature across it, the turning of the winds, and the clouds held in service between the heaven and the earth — in all of that are signs for a people who reason.

This is one of the great sign-verses of the Qur'an, and it enumerates seven — though the commentators count them with some variation, grouping or separating the last two.

**1. *Khalq al-samawati wa al-ard*** — the creation of the heavens and the earth. The fact of it, the scale of it, the order in it.

**2. *Ikhtilaf al-layli wa al-nahar*** — the alternation of the night and the day. The word carries both "alternation" — their succeeding one another — and "difference" — their being unlike each other. Both readings are transmitted, and both are true: the succession that makes time habitable, and the variation in length that makes the seasons.

**3. *Al-fulk allati tajri fi al-bahr bima yanfa'u al-nas*** — the ships that course through the sea with what benefits people. Note that the sign is not the vessel alone. It is the vessel *carrying benefit*. The named thing is commerce, provision, connection between peoples. The Qur'an consistently presents the sea's utility as the sign rather than its mere existence (14:32, 16:14, 17:66, 45:12).

**4. *Ma anzala Allahu min al-sama'i min ma'in fa-ahya bihi al-arda ba'da mawtiha*** — the water sent down from the sky, by which the earth is given life after its death.

And this sign is doing double duty. The revival of dead land is the Qur'an's primary analogy for the resurrection, and the connection is made explicit elsewhere: 50:9–11 — "and We have given life thereby to a dead land. **Thus is the resurrection**." Also 7:57, 25:48–50, 30:24, 35:9. So the fourth sign is simultaneously a proof of power and an argument for the Hereafter, in a surah that opened by asserting the unseen (2:3) and arguing for resurrection (2:28, 2:56).

**5. *Wa baththa fiha min kulli dabbatin*** — and His scattering across it every kind of creature. *Baththa* is to spread and disperse; *dabbah* is any creature that moves. The sign is the sheer variety of living things distributed over a surface that, without the preceding sign, would be dead.

**6. *Wa tasrifi al-riyah*** — and the turning of the winds. *Tasrif* is directing and varying: now from this quarter, now that; now gentle, now driving; some bearing rain, some pollinating, some destroying. The comparison is 15:22, "And We have sent the fertilising winds and sent down water from the sky."

**7. *Wa al-sahabi al-musakhkhari bayna al-sama'i wa al-ard*** — and the clouds, made subservient, between the heaven and the earth. *Musakhkhar* is form II of a root meaning to subdue and press into service: the cloud is held in service. And the observable fact is the one the verse points at — water is heavy, and it hangs. It does not fall until it is commanded to, and it does not drift away. The suspension itself is the sign.

*La-ayatin li-qawmin ya'qilun* — "surely signs for a people who reason."

**And note how the verse ends: by naming the faculty required.** Not for those who look, or those who hear, but for those who *ya'qilun* — who bind things together, who reason. The signs are there for anyone; the *recognition* requires an act of the intellect.

The definitive parallel is 3:190–191: "Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of the night and the day are signs for those of understanding — those who remember Allah standing, sitting and on their sides, and reflect upon the creation of the heavens and the earth, [saying]: 'Our Lord, You did not create this aimlessly; exalted are You. Protect us from the punishment of the Fire.'"

And note where the reasoning ends in that verse: not in data, not in an argument, but in *dhikr* and in a supplication. Reflection on the creation terminates in worship. That is the shape the Qur'an gives to thinking about the natural world, and it is the reason the tradition has never separated contemplation of creation from consciousness of the Creator.

**On the question of modern science, a caution that must be stated rather than assumed.** Some modern writers read specific contemporary findings into this verse's seven signs. The rule that governs this commentary, and that governs the sound tradition, is that scientific findings may serve as *illustration* and never as *proof*, and that the verse's argument must not be made to depend on them. A proof that changes when the science changes was never a proof. The verse's seven signs were signs to a desert trader in the seventh century standing under the sky at night, and they are signs to a reader today with a telescope — which is precisely the point. They operate by observation and reasoning, which are available in every age, and nothing in the argument is hostage to a later discovery. Where correspondences with modern findings are genuine they deepen appreciation; they do not establish the claim, and inventing them would be a fabrication.

For practice, three things.

The first is the obligation to think. The verse does not merely permit reflection on the natural world; it commends it and attaches it to the faculty of reason. The tradition's immense scientific and observational output is downstream of verses like this one, and a religious life that never looks at the sky has neglected something this verse names.

The second is the content of the fourth sign, taken personally. The earth dies and is revived — visibly, annually, everywhere. The Qur'an presents that as the standing, observable refutation of the claim that the dead are not raised. A person who has watched a dry season break has seen the argument.

And the third is the closing. *Li-qawmin ya'qilun.* The verse is addressed to the reasoning faculty, which means the person who reads it and feels nothing is being told that the deficiency is in the exercise of a faculty he has. The remedy is not more information. It is to actually do the thing the last word names.

## Verse 165

**"Still there are some who take others as Allah’s equal—they love them as they should love Allah—but the ˹true˺ believers love Allah even more. If only the wrongdoers could see the ˹horrible˺ punishment ˹awaiting them˺, they would certainly realize that all power belongs to Allah and that Allah is indeed severe in punishment."**

In simple terms: and among people are those who take rivals besides Allah, loving them as Allah should be loved — while those who believe are stronger in love for Allah. And if only those who wronged could see, when they see the punishment, that power belongs entirely to Allah, and that Allah is severe in punishment.

*Wa mina al-nasi man yattakhidhu min duni Allahi andadan* — "and among the people are those who take, besides Allah, rivals."

***Andad*** is the plural of *nidd*: an equal, a peer, a counterpart. And note the word's earlier appearance in this surah, at 2:22: "So do not make rivals [*andadan*] to Allah while you know." The surah opened its argument with the prohibition and returns to it here, at the point where it is explaining what the rivals are.

*Min duni Allahi* — besides Allah, in place of Allah. And *wa min al-nas man* — "and among the people is he who" — is indefinite: a category, not all people.

*Yuhibbunahum ka-hubbi Allahi* — "loving them as [they should] love Allah."

The grammarians discussed whether this means "they love them as they love Allah" or "they love them with the love due to Allah." Both converge on the same thing, which is the heart of the verse:

**The problem is not that they love things. It is that they love them with the love that belongs to God.**

That precision matters enormously and prevents a serious misreading. Islam does not require the extinction of love for created things. Love of spouse, children, parents, home, place and work is natural, and much of it is commanded and rewarded. What the verse prohibits is giving a created thing the place of the Creator — the absolute love, the absolute fear, the absolute hope, the absolute obedience that the previous verse reserved with *la ilaha illa huwa*.

*Wa alladhina amanu ashaddu hubban lillahi* — "and those who believe are **stronger** in love for Allah."

***Ashaddu hubban*** — stronger in love. And note what the verse does *not* say. It does not say the believers love only Allah. It says their love for Allah is **stronger** — more intense, more firmly rooted — than the love others give their rivals, and than any other love they themselves have.

So the love of created things is not abolished. It is outweighed and ordered. And the measure of faith given in this verse is not the correctness of a person's beliefs, or the completeness of his practice, but **the intensity of his love**.

That is one of the most striking statements in the Qur'an, and it is the foundation of the entire tradition of divine love (*mahabbah*) in Islam.

The test of the claim is 3:31: "Say: if you love Allah, then follow me; Allah will love you." Love is verified by following, not by feeling.

And the test of the *strength* is 9:24: "Say: if your fathers, your sons, your brothers, your wives, your relatives, the wealth you have acquired, the commerce you fear will decline, and the dwellings you are pleased with are more beloved to you than Allah and His Messenger and striving in His cause, then wait until Allah executes His command."

*Wa law yara alladhina zalamu idh yarawna al-'adhaba* — "and if only those who wronged could see, when they see the punishment..."

Note the structure. *Law yara* is a wish in the present — if only they could see **now** — and *idh yarawna* is the future: **when** they see the punishment. So the verse is: if only they could see now what they will see then. And the apodosis — the consequence of the "if only" — is elided. The grammarians noted the elision and its effect: the consequence is too great to be stated, so the sentence breaks off.

For practice, three things.

The first is the *andad* question, which is the one to sit with. A *nidd* is anything given the place that belongs to God. In the seventh century those were stones and intercessor-deities. For most readers now they are apt to be other things — a career treated as absolute, a person whose disapproval is unbearable, a political identity, an image of oneself, a standard of living, a fear of the future. The test is the one the previous verse supplies: what do you treat as deserving ultimate regard? The verse's diagnosis is not idolatry in the museum sense; it is misplaced ultimacy.

The second is *ashaddu hubban* as the measure, and it is both consoling and demanding. Consoling, because a person whose practice is weak is not thereby told his faith is nothing — the measure is love, and love can be strong in a person whose works are few. Demanding, because a person whose practice is strong is not thereby told he has arrived — the measure is love, and love can be weak in a person whose works are many.

And the third is the structure of the final clause. "If only they could see now what they will see then." Nearly every failure in religious life has this shape: a person acts as he does because the consequence is future and therefore faint, and the pleasure is present and therefore vivid. The verse's entire remedy is to make the future present — which is what *dhikr*, and reflection on the Hereafter, and the company of those who remember, are for.

## Verse 166

**"˹Consider the Day˺ when those who misled others will disown their followers—when they face the torment—and the bonds that united them will be cut off."**

In simple terms: when those who were followed disown those who followed, and they see the punishment, and the connections are severed with them.

*Idh tabarra'a alladhina ittubi'u min alladhina ittaba'u* — "when those who were followed disown those who followed."

*Tabarra'a* is form V of a root meaning to be quit of and free from: to disown, to declare oneself clear of, to dissociate. *Alladhina ittubi'u* is the passive — those who were followed: the leaders, the authorities, the exemplars, the ones whose word settled things. *Min alladhina ittaba'u* — from those who followed them.

The scene is the Day, and the thing being dissolved is the relationship that was the source of the leaders' power in this life. The followed disown the followers.

The Qur'an stages this exchange several times, and the fullest version is 14:21: "And they will come out before Allah all together, and the weak will say to those who were arrogant, 'Indeed, we were your followers, so can you avail us anything against the punishment of Allah?' They will say, 'If Allah had guided us, we would have guided you. It is all the same for us whether we show intolerance or are patient: there is for us no place of escape.'"

Also 40:47, where the exchange takes place inside the Fire and the arrogant say: "Indeed, all of us are in it." And 33:67–68, where the followers say: "Our Lord, indeed we obeyed our masters and our dignitaries, and they led us astray from the [right] way. Our Lord, give them double the punishment and curse them with a great curse." And 37:27–33, where the followers say to the leaders "you used to come at us from the right" — that is, you presented what we were doing to us as right — and the leaders answer "rather, you were not believers, and we had over you no authority."

*Wa ra'aw al-'adhaba* — "and they see the punishment."

*Wa taqatta'at bihimu al-asbab* — "and the connections are severed with them."

***Al-asbab*** is the plural of *sabab*: a cause, a means, a connection, and — in its oldest sense — a rope. The commentators gave three readings, and they converge:

**(a) The relationships** — kinship, alliance, loyalty, love, party. Everything that bound people together in this life.
**(b) The means** — the things relied upon: wealth, status, power, the leaders themselves.
**(c) The ropes** — literally, what a person hangs onto or climbs by.

All three are transmitted, and the verse's point holds on any of them: **everything a person hung onto is cut.**

For practice, two things.

The first is the warning to followers, and it is one of the most practical in the Qur'an. The verse does not say the leaders were weak. It says they will disown you. Which means: **do not attach yourself to any person or movement in a way that would require them to save you.** No leader, teacher, party, movement, institution or community can carry you there. The attachment is not forbidden — following is natural and instruction is necessary — but an attachment that transfers your standing to someone else's account is an attachment to something that will be dissolved.

The practical test is small and concrete: **do you know why you follow, or only whom?** A person who can state the warrant for what he holds is holding it himself. A person who holds it because So-and-so holds it is holding a *sabab*, and the verse says what happens to *asbab*.

And the second is the warning to leaders, which the verse also carries. The one who is followed bears a burden for having been followed, and the disowning is described as *his* act — he is the one who severs it, and he cannot carry what he took on. That is why the tradition is so severe about speaking without knowledge and so careful about the one who gives a ruling or sets a direction. The followers in 33:68 ask that their leaders be given double.

## Verse 167

**"The ˹misled˺ followers will cry, “If only we could have a second chance, we would disown them as they disowned us.” And so Allah will make them remorseful of their misdeeds. And they will never ˹be able to˺ leave the Fire."**

In simple terms: and those who followed will say: if only we had another turn, we would disown them as they have disowned us. Thus Allah shows them their deeds as regrets upon them. And they will not be leaving the Fire.

*Wa qala alladhina ittaba'u law anna lana karratan fa-natabarra'a minhum kama tabarra'u minna* — "and those who followed will say: if only we had a return, then we would disown them as they have disowned us."

*Karratan* is a turn, a second go, another round — from the root meaning to return and to repeat.

**And note the symmetry, and note what is missing from it.** The previous verse had the leaders disowning the followers. This verse has the followers wishing to disown the leaders. The relationship is undone in both directions.

But look at what they wish for. Their first desire, on being disowned, is not "if only we had believed" and not "if only we had followed the messenger." It is: **if only we could disown them back.**

That is one of the most devastating psychological observations in the Qur'an. The enmity is stronger than the regret. Even there, even seeing the punishment, what they want is not God — it is retaliation against the people who led them there. The relationship that corrupted them is still the thing they are oriented toward.

*Kadhalika yurihimu Allahu a'malahum hasaratin 'alayhim* — "thus Allah shows them their deeds as regrets upon them."

***Hasarat*** is the plural of *hasrah*: a gnawing, bitter grief — from a root meaning to be exhausted, to be stripped, to be unable. And the crux is the construction: **their deeds become their regret.**

The punishment is not only something inflicted from outside. It is the showing of a life as it actually was. The very acts they performed, seen at last without the justifications that made them bearable, are themselves the torment.

The parallels state this plainly. 3:30: "On the Day every soul will find what it has done of good present before it, and what it has done of evil, it will wish that between itself and that [evil] was a great distance." And 99:6–8: "That Day, the people will depart separated to be shown their deeds. So whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it."

*Wa ma hum bi-kharijina mina al-nari* — "and they will not be coming out of the Fire." The same statement as verse 162, and the same guardrail applies: the eternity is for those described in verse 161, and the hadith of the atom's weight of faith governs the case of the believing sinner.

For practice, two things.

The first is the *hasarat*, and the way the verse makes the punishment interior. Most people imagine judgment as a verdict read out. This verse describes it as a showing: you are shown what you did, and the seeing is the pain. That has an implication for this life, which is that the seeing is partly available now. A person who can look honestly at his own deeds — without the story he tells about them — is doing in advance what this verse says will be done then. The difference is that now the seeing can be followed by repair, and then it cannot. That is what the tradition means by *muhasabah*, self-examination: doing the seeing on this side of the line.

And the second is the symmetry, applied. The follower's wish is not for God but against the leader. Which means the corruption of the relationship persists past the point where it could have done him any good. A person who is attached to someone wrongly, or opposed to someone obsessively, should note that the attachment or the opposition does not automatically dissolve at death — the verse shows it surviving into the Fire. The time to dissolve it is now, and dissolving it is a form of freedom rather than a loss.

## Verse 168

**"O humanity! Eat from what is lawful and good on the earth and do not follow Satan’s footsteps. He is truly your sworn enemy."**

In simple terms: O mankind, eat from what is in the earth, lawful and good, and do not follow the footsteps of Satan. Indeed he is to you a clear enemy.

*Ya ayyuha al-nasu kulu mimma fi al-ardi halalan tayyiban* — "O mankind, eat from what is in the earth, lawful and good."

Note at once that the address is *ya ayyuha al-nas* — **O mankind**, not "O you who believe." The permission to eat what is lawful and good is universal, and it is stated before any restriction is mentioned.

***Halalan tayyiban*** — and the pairing is the crux, because these are two different kinds of word.

*Halal* is a legal category: what is permitted, what has been released from prohibition. *Tayyib* is a quality: good, pure, wholesome, pleasant.

The two are not identical, and the verse requires both. Something can be legally permitted and not wholesome. And the fact that the Qur'an pairs them means the religion's concern in what a person consumes is not exhausted by the legal ruling.

The juristic principle this verse supports is that the default in created things is permissibility, and that prohibition comes only by specific text. The parallels are consistent: 5:88 — "And eat of what Allah has provided for you [which is] lawful and good"; 16:114 — "So eat of what Allah has provided for you [which is] lawful and good, and be grateful for the favour of Allah, if it is [indeed] Him that you worship"; 7:32 — "Say: Who has forbidden the adornment of Allah which He has produced for His servants and the good things of provision?"

And note the verb: **eat**. Food is the most ordinary, repeated, bodily thing a human being does. The religion legislates at that level. There is a version of piety that treats the body and its needs as irrelevant to the religious life, and this verse is written at the opposite end of the scale: the very first instruction after two verses describing the Fire is about what to have for dinner.

*Wa la tattabi'u khutuwati al-shaytani* — "and do not follow the footsteps of Satan."

***Khutuwat*** is the plural of *khutwah*: a single step, a pace.

**And this is one of the most psychologically precise images in the Qur'an.** Satan is not described as arriving or as seizing. He is described as walking, and the prohibition is against following his *steps*.

The commentators drew out what the image implies, and it is the thing to take from this verse: the movement is incremental. No one is asked for the whole thing at once. The path is walked one step at a time, and at no single step does the walker feel that he has departed — because each step is only slightly further than the last. The prohibition is therefore aimed at the beginning of the path rather than the end of it, which is why the tradition is so insistent on the small concessions: the glance, the conversation, the small dishonesty, the minor compromise. None of them is the destination. All of them are steps.

The phrase recurs with the same meaning. 24:21: "O you who have believed, do not follow the footsteps of Satan. And whoever follows the footsteps of Satan — indeed, he enjoins immorality and wrongdoing." And 2:208, in this same surah: "O you who have believed, enter into peace completely, and do not follow the footsteps of Satan. Indeed, he is to you a clear enemy" — the identical closing.

*Innahu lakum 'aduwwun mubin* — "indeed he is to you a clear enemy."

*Mubin* is form IV of the root meaning to be clear: manifest, evident, unmistakable. The point of the adjective is that there is nothing to work out. The enmity is not ambiguous, not a matter of interpretation, and not negotiable.

The definitive cross-reference is 35:6: "Indeed, Satan is an enemy to you; so take him as an enemy. He only invites his party to be among the companions of the Blaze." And 36:60: "Did I not enjoin upon you, O children of Adam, that you not worship Satan — [for] indeed, he is to you a clear enemy?"

**Two theological notes that belong here and that prevent two common errors.**

First, Satan is of the jinn, not a fallen angel. 18:50: "he was of the jinn and defied the command of his Lord." Also 7:12 and 38:76. The distinction is not cosmetic — it bears on the question of how a creature with a command and a choice disobeyed.

Second, **his power over a person is invitation only.** 14:22: "And Satan will say when the matter has been concluded: Indeed, Allah had promised you the promise of truth. And I promised you, but I betrayed you. **But I had no authority over you except that I invited you, and you responded to me. So do not blame me; but blame yourselves.**" And 15:42: "Indeed, My servants — you have no authority over them, except those who follow you of the deviators."

For practice, three things.

The first is *halalan tayyiban*, and the application is broader than food. The pairing of a legal category with a quality is a model for how religious judgment works: it is not enough that something is permitted. The question of whether it is good, wholesome, and becoming is a separate question, and the religion asks it. A person who only ever asks "is this allowed?" is operating with half the verse.

The second is the *khutuwat*, and the practical rule it gives: **watch the steps, not the destination.** Most people resolve not to arrive somewhere and then walk there gradually. The verse's discipline is to notice the small increments, which is why the tradition's counsel is always about the beginning of a path rather than its end — the first glance, the first small dishonesty, the first missed prayer treated as nothing.

And the third is the 14:22 guardrail, which is one of the most important in the religion. Satan's authority is invitation. He cannot compel. Which means the blame that the followers in 33:68 tried to place on their leaders — and that every person tries to place somewhere — does not transfer. "I was led to it" is a description of how temptation works and not a defence of the choice. That is severe, and it is also the statement of a person's actual dignity: he is the one who responded.

## Verse 169

**"He only incites you to commit evil and indecency, and to claim against Allah what you do not know."**

In simple terms: he only commands you to evil and indecency, and to say about Allah what you do not know.

*Innama ya'murukum bi-al-su'i wa al-fahsha'i* — "he only commands you to evil and to indecency."

*Al-su'* is that which is bad and harmful. *Al-fahsha'* is from a root meaning excess and ugliness, and in the Qur'an it most often denotes sexual immorality — at 17:32, 12:24, 24:19 — while carrying a broader sense of whatever is grossly indecent. The commentators variously distinguished the two as lesser and greater, or as what harms others and what is shameful in itself, or as wrongdoing generally and sexual immorality specifically. All three readings are transmitted.

*Wa an taqulu 'ala Allahi ma la ta'lamun* — "and that you say about Allah what you do not know."

**And this third item is the verse's centre of gravity, and it is one of the most consequential clauses in the Qur'an.**

Note the structure. Satan is described as commanding three things: evil, indecency, and — **speaking about God without knowledge.**

The third is not obviously a sin in the way the first two are, and it is placed last, as the climax. And its inclusion is the thing that most needs saying, because it is the one readers skip.

**Speaking about God without knowledge is listed alongside evil and sexual immorality as one of Satan's three commands.**

The definitive corroboration is 7:33: "Say: My Lord has only forbidden immoralities — what is apparent of them and what is concealed — and sin, and oppression without right, and that you associate with Allah that for which He has not sent down authority, **and that you say about Allah that which you do not know**." There too it is the last and climactic item.

And 7:28 combines this verse's elements directly: "And when they commit an immorality, they say, 'We found our fathers doing it, and Allah has ordered us to do it.' Say: Indeed, Allah does not order immorality. **Do you say about Allah that which you do not know?**" — which is the bridge from this verse to the next.

And 17:36 states the general principle: "And do not pursue that of which you have no knowledge. Indeed, the hearing, the sight and the heart — about all those [one] will be questioned."

For practice, this verse is a warning and it lands hardest on precisely the people most confident it is not about them.

The person who declares something lawful or unlawful without warrant. The person who says "Allah wants this" about a matter on which the texts are silent or on which the scholars differ. The person who attributes a motive to God — that this illness is a punishment for that sin, that this blessing is an approval of that life. The person who speaks about the unseen, about the Day, about who is saved and who is not, about the state of the dead.

And three specifically contemporary forms, because they are the common ones: the preacher who says "God promises you X if you do Y" where no text promises it; the writer who states a ruling as though the disagreement did not exist; and the ordinary person who says, in argument, "God will judge them for that" — about someone, for something, with no warrant.

The discipline the verse calls for is small and unglamorous and it would change most religious conversation: **when speaking about God, know what your warrant is, and where you have none, say "I do not know."** The tradition treats *"I do not know"* as half of knowledge, and the reported statements of the early scholars declining to answer — "I do not know" being the answer of Malik and others to questions they had no text for — are the lived form of this verse.

And note the pairing with 168. The religion governs the body — what you eat — and the tongue — what you say about God. And the verse lists the tongue's sin alongside the body's worst ones rather than beneath them.

## Verse 170

**"When it is said to them, “Follow what Allah has revealed,” they reply, “No! We ˹only˺ follow what we found our forefathers practicing.” ˹Would they still do so,˺ even if their forefathers had ˹absolutely˺ no understanding or guidance?"**

In simple terms: and when it is said to them, "follow what Allah has sent down," they say: rather, we follow what we found our fathers upon — even if their fathers understood nothing and were not guided?

*Wa idha qila lahumu ittabi'u ma anzala Allahu qalu bal nattabi'u ma alfayna 'alayhi aba'ana* — "and when it is said to them: follow what Allah has sent down, they say: rather, we follow what we found our fathers upon."

*Bal* is a flat rejection, not a qualification. *Alfayna* is "we found" — form IV of a root meaning to find and to come upon. So: what we came upon our fathers doing.

This is the ancestral argument, and it is the argument the Qur'an reports more often than almost any other, always in the same words and always answered the same way:

5:104: "And when it is said to them, 'Come to what Allah has revealed and to the Messenger,' they say, 'Sufficient for us is that upon which we found our fathers.' **Even though their fathers knew nothing, nor were they guided?**"

31:21: "And when it is said to them, 'Follow what Allah has revealed,' they say, 'Rather, we will follow that upon which we found our fathers.' **Even if Satan was inviting them to the punishment of the Blaze?**" — which adds the detail that the inherited path may be Satan's path.

43:22–24: "Rather, they say, 'Indeed, we found our fathers upon a religion, and we are in their footsteps following'... [Each warner] said, **'Even if I brought you better guidance than that upon which you found your fathers?'**"

*Awa law kana aba'uhum la ya'qiluna shay'an wa la yahtaduna* — "even if their fathers understood nothing and were not guided?"

*Awa law* — "even if?" — is the interrogative of remoteness: the question pushes the principle to its limit to see whether it survives there.

And the double negation is total: *la ya'qiluna shay'an* — they understand **nothing** at all — and *la yahtaduna* — and they were not guided.

**And note the thread with verse 164.** There the signs were for *qawmin ya'qilun* — a people who reason. Here the fathers are *la ya'qiluna shay'an* — they reason not at all. The surah established the faculty seven verses ago and now names its complete absence as the disqualification of the ancestors being followed. Ancestry without reason is not a transmission of guidance; it is a transmission of nothing.

**And the logic of the refutation is worth laying out, because it is not what most people think it is.**

The Qur'an does not answer by saying "your fathers were bad people." It does not impugn them at all. It answers by showing that the *principle* is self-defeating:

If your criterion is that your fathers did it, then your criterion holds regardless of whether your fathers possessed knowledge or guidance. So: would you follow them if they understood nothing? The implied answer is yes — you would, because ancestry is your stated criterion. And the fact that you would is the proof that ancestry is not actually operating as a criterion of truth for you. It is operating as a preference, wearing the clothing of a principle.

That is a logical dismantling, not an insult, and it is why the argument can be applied in any setting.

For practice, the necessary care here is that this verse must not be read as a condemnation of tradition as such — because that reading would destroy the religion's own transmission.

**Recall 2:132:** Abraham and Jacob bequeathed the religion to their sons, and the tradition has always held that the religion reaches us by transmission — through teachers, through chains, through communities, through fathers and mothers. Transmission is not the enemy. It is the mechanism.

What this verse condemns is **transmission operating as its own warrant.** The distinction is this: the transmitted thing is to be followed *because it can be traced to revelation*, not because it is inherited. And when the inherited thing is shown to lack that warrant, the inheritance does not preserve it.

That distinction resolves the apparent tension between 132 and 170 cleanly. 132: fathers transmit the religion, deliberately, by bequest. 170: the fact that fathers practised something is not evidence that it is from God. Both are true, and the religion needs both.

And the application inside the Muslim community is direct and uncomfortable. Every group in this community inherits things from its "fathers" — its teachers, its school, its movement, its culture, its party. Most of what any Muslim believes and practises he received rather than derived, and that is normal and unavoidable. The verse's question is whether the inheritance is being held *because it is inherited* or because it can be traced.

The practical test is one question, and it is the verse's own: **would I still hold this if I learned my people had no warrant for it?** A person who can say yes to that — who has checked, at least sometimes, and would let go — is holding the thing by its warrant. A person who cannot is holding it by inheritance, and 170 is addressed to him.

And note the closing, which is a mercy rather than a threat. The verse ends with a question, not a sentence. It leaves the objector room to answer.

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

Two halves, and the seam is argumentative rather than arbitrary.

**The severity and the oneness (161–167).** The curse is pronounced, with the condition stated precisely — not on having rejected but on dying in that state, which is the only closure the Qur'an recognises (161). Eternal abiding, with no lightening and no respite, and with the distinction drawn that this is the state of the rejecter and not of the believing sinner (162). Then, in the next sentence, the oneness of God — negated absolutely, affirmed singly, and closed with mercy (163). Then seven signs in the created world, enumerated for those who reason, with the argument for resurrection folded into the fourth of them (164). Then the measure of faith given as the intensity of love rather than the correctness of belief, and the rivals named as whatever is given the place that belongs to God (165). Then the Day on which the followed disown the followers, every connection is severed (166), and the followers' first wish is not for God but against their leaders — while their own deeds are shown to them as the substance of their regret (167).

**The ordinary and the inherited (168–170).** Then the register drops to the level of daily life: eat what is lawful and good, and do not follow the steps of Satan, who is a clear enemy whose only authority is invitation (168). Then his three commands — evil, indecency, and, climactically, saying about God what you do not know (169). And then the ancestral argument, in the objectors' own words, dismantled by a single question that exposes the principle as a preference (170).

Three threads run through the batch. First, **the condition is the final state, not a past state** — the curse attaches to dying, and every moment before that is open (161), and the eternity is bounded by that same condition (162, 167). Second, **reasoning is commanded and its absence disqualifies** — the signs are for those who reason (164), the ancestors are disqualified by not reasoning (170), and speaking about God without knowledge is named among Satan's three commands (169). And third, **transmission is the mechanism but never the warrant** — the religion is bequeathed (132) and the bequest does not make the inherited thing true (170), just as what is permitted is not thereby good (168).

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given. This is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and supplying precise references from memory would mean inventing them. Where the commentators and jurists differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved: the counting of the signs in verse 164; the distinction between *su'* and *fahsha'* in verse 169; the three readings of *asbab* in verse 166; and the grammatical readings of *ka-hubbi Allahi* in verse 165.

Transmitted reports have been flagged as reports throughout, and the grading of the reports behind the minority position on the eternity of the Fire has been stated as weak and not established, with the majority position identified as the consensus of Ahl al-Sunnah rather than merely as one view among several.

Five boundaries have been observed. On verses 161–162: the condition has been stated as the state at death rather than any prior state, the eternity has been explicitly bounded to those who die in rejection so that a believing sinner is not swept into it, the atom's-weight-of-faith hadith has been given as the governing text, and the standing rule has been restated that no individual is declared an inhabitant of the Fire. On verse 164: modern scientific findings have been permitted as illustration only and forbidden as proof, with the reason given — that a proof which changes when the science changes was never a proof. On verse 165: the precision has been made that the prohibition is against loving creatures *in God's place* and not against loving them, so the verse is not read as demanding lovelessness. On verse 168: Satan's authority has been limited to invitation, per 14:22, so that the verse cannot be used to transfer blame for a choice. And on verse 170: the condemnation of the ancestral argument has been explicitly bounded so that it does not condemn transmission as such, with 2:132 cited to show that the surah itself establishes the religion as a bequest — the distinction drawn is between inheritance as mechanism and inheritance as warrant.

The question of the eternity of the punishment is the one place in this batch where a live doctrinal disagreement has had to be reported rather than resolved, and it has been reported with the relative weight of the positions stated: the eternal position as the consensus of Ahl al-Sunnah, and the contrary position as a minority view resting on narration assessed as weak.
