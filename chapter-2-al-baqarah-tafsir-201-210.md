# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 201–210

This batch closes the pilgrimage passage and then turns to the profile of a kind of person. The corrected prayer is given, in contrast to the prayer of the verse before — asking for good in this world **and** the next, and for protection from the Fire (201) — with the promise of a share earned and a reckoning that is swift (202). Then the appointed days of the pilgrimage, with blame removed explicitly from both the one who leaves early and the one who stays (203). Then the portrait: the man whose speech impresses, who calls God to witness his sincerity, and who is in fact the fiercest of opponents (204); who works corruption and destroys what sustains life (205); and whose defining mark is that when told to fear God, pride carries him off (206). Then the contrast — the one who sells himself for God's pleasure (207). Then the command to enter wholly rather than selectively, with the prohibition of Satan's footsteps repeated in the identical words used at the start of the surah (208); the word for the believer who slips, and the remedy offered (209); and finally the question that ends the passage: what are they waiting for, when waiting for the definitive sign would end everything (210)?

---

## Verse 201

**"Yet there are others who say, “Our Lord! Grant us the good of this world and the Hereafter, and protect us from the torment of the Fire.”"**

In simple terms: and among them are those who say: our Lord, give us good in this world and good in the Hereafter, and protect us from the punishment of the Fire.

*Wa minhum man yaqulu rabbana atina fi al-dunya hasanatan wa fi al-akhirati hasanatan wa qina 'adhaba al-nar* — "and among them is he who says: our Lord, give us in this world a good, and in the Hereafter a good, and shield us from the punishment of the Fire."

**This verse is the second half of a pair, and it only makes sense read with 2:200.** There, the prayer was *rabbana atina fi al-dunya* — "our Lord, give us in this world" — with nothing after it, and the verdict was "no share in the Hereafter." Here the same address is used, and the content is completed.

And note what is **not** being corrected. The fault in 200 was not asking for this world. It was asking for this world **only**. The religion does not ask a person to stop wanting things in this life; the verse itself asks for them. *Hasanatan* in this world is a legitimate request, made in the words of the Qur'an.

***Hasanatan*** — "a good" — from the root of *husn*, beauty and goodness. The commentators were asked what the *hasanah* of each life is, and their answers vary because the word is deliberately wide. For this world they named: a righteous spouse, lawful and sufficient provision, beneficial knowledge, worship and obedience, health, and contentment with what is given. For the Hereafter they named: Paradise, forgiveness, and — in the reading of several — the highest good of all, which is the beholding of God.

The indefiniteness is the point. The verse does not specify which good, which leaves the supplicant free to name what he actually needs, and it protects him from prescribing to God what form the good should take.

*Wa qina 'adhaba al-nar* — "and shield us from the punishment of the Fire."

**And note the root.** *Qina* is the imperative of *waqa*, to shield and to guard — **the same root as *taqwa***. So the prayer asks, in a single word, for the very thing that *taqwa* is: to be placed behind a shield. The verse's closing petition and the surah's governing theme are the same word.

**The Sunnah on this verse is emphatic, and it is the single most useful thing here.**

Recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Anas ibn Malik: **the supplication the Prophet ﷺ made most often was: "Our Lord, grant us good in this world and good in the Hereafter, and protect us from the punishment of the Fire."**

And recorded by Muslim, also from Anas: the Prophet ﷺ went to visit a sick man who had wasted away until he was like a fledgling, and he said: "Were you supplicating for something, or asking Him for it?" The man said: "I used to say: O Allah, whatever You are going to punish me with in the Hereafter, bring it forward for me in this world." And the Prophet ﷺ said: **"Glory be to God! You cannot bear it — or you are not able to. Why did you not say: 'Our Lord, grant us good in this world and good in the Hereafter, and protect us from the punishment of the Fire'?"**

For practice, three things.

The first is to use the words themselves. There is a supplication that the Prophet ﷺ made more than any other, and it is this one, and the tradition has preserved both its wording and its frequency. A person who does not know what to ask for can ask for this and has asked for everything: provision and spouse and sufficiency and knowledge in this life, Paradise and forgiveness and the sight of God in the next, and to be shielded from the Fire. It is short enough to memorise in a minute and wide enough to cover a life.

The second is the sick man's error, which is more common than it sounds. He did not ask for something bad — he asked for his purification to be completed quickly, which sounds pious. But he prescribed the *timing and the mechanism* to God, and he asked for punishment to be brought forward. The Prophet ﷺ's correction was not "do not seek purity" but "do not dictate how." A person who prays "take everything from me so I can be purified" is in this man's position, and the tradition's answer is to ask for good and for shielding instead, and to leave the method to God.

And the third is the structure of the request: this world, the next, and protection. The three cover every legitimate want. A person whose prayer life has drifted into asking only for the first has the diagnosis of 2:200 and the remedy of 2:201, in adjacent verses.

## Verse 202

**"It is they who will receive a ˹heavenly˺ reward for the good they have done. Surely Allah is swift in reckoning."**

In simple terms: those have a share from what they earned, and Allah is swift in account.

*Ula'ika lahum nasibun mimma kasabu* — "those — for them is a share from what they earned."

*Nasib* is an allotted portion, a share. And note the preposition: *mimma kasabu* — **from what they earned**. Not from their lineage, not from their community's standing, not from a claim.

That is the same principle the surah has stated twice already in identical words: 2:134 and 2:141, *laha ma kasabat wa lakum ma kasabtum* — "for it is what it earned, and for you is what you earned." The thread is unbroken. Every reward in this surah is attached to earning, and none to belonging.

*Wa Allahu sari'u al-hisab* — "and Allah is swift in reckoning."

***Sari' al-hisab*** — and the crux is how a reckoning of every human being who ever lived can be called swift.

The commentators gave two answers. The first is about **knowledge**: Allah does not need to count, or to recollect, or to weigh sequentially. His knowledge is immediate and complete, so the account of all creatures is not a process but an act. The second is about **manner**: the reckoning itself takes no measurable duration in the way a human audit would, though the Day has its own stations and its own length.

The parallels are consistent: 14:51 — "That Allah may recompense every soul for what it earned. Indeed, Allah is swift in account." And 40:17 — "This Day every soul will be recompensed for what it earned. No wrong [will be done] this Day. Indeed, Allah is swift in account."

**And here is the thing the tradition has always stressed alongside the swiftness, because without it the phrase produces nothing but terror.**

There are **two** reckonings spoken of in the sources, and the distinction is the whole pastoral weight of this verse.

Recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from 'A'ishah: the Prophet ﷺ said, **"There is no one who is reckoned except that he is punished."** 'A'ishah said: "But does Allah not say, 'He will be reckoned with an easy reckoning' (84:8)?" And he said: **"That is only the presentation [of the deeds]. But whoever is scrutinised in the reckoning will be ruined."**

So there is the *'ard* — the presentation, in which a person is shown his deeds and acknowledges them, and which is what 84:8 means by the easy reckoning — and there is the *munaqashah* — the scrutiny, in which every item is examined against him, and from which no one emerges unscathed. Every human being will have the first. The believer prays to be spared the second.

And the hadith that shows what the presentation is like, recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Ibn 'Umar, is one of the most moving in the whole corpus: the Prophet ﷺ said that **Allah will bring the believer close and screen him, and will say: "Do you recognise this sin? Do you recognise that sin?" and he will say, "Yes, my Lord" — until, when he thinks he is ruined, Allah says: "I screened it for you in the world, and I forgive it for you today."** And he is given his book of good deeds.

**So: swift in reckoning, and the reckoning of the believer is a screening and a forgiving.** The two belong together and neither should be read without the other.

For practice, three things.

The first is *mimma kasabu*, which closes the door on every inherited claim. The share is from what a person earned. That is the surah's answer to every version of "but I belong to the right people," and it has now been stated four times in these verses.

The second is the two reckonings, and it is the thing that should govern how a person thinks about the Day. Most believers carry an undifferentiated dread of judgment, as though it were one event with one outcome. The tradition distinguishes the showing, which everyone has and which for the believer ends in forgiveness, from the scrutiny, which is what to seek refuge from. The prayer to make is the one the tradition makes: *Allahumma hasibni hisaban yasiran* — O Allah, reckon me an easy reckoning.

And the third is the private conference hadith, which is worth holding in the memory. The believer is brought close — not paraded — and is screened, and is made to acknowledge, and is then told that what he was shielded from in this life is forgiven in that one. The implication for this life is direct: a person who screened his own sins rather than publicising them, and who screened the faults of others rather than exposing them, is dealing with a Lord who does the same for him.

## Verse 203

**"And remember Allah during ˹these˺ appointed days. Whoever departs swiftly on the second day is not sinful, neither are those who stay behind ˹till the third—seeking additional reward˺, so long as they are mindful ˹of their Lord˺. And be mindful of Allah, and know that to Him you will ˹all˺ be gathered."**

In simple terms: and remember Allah during counted days. Whoever hastens [away] in two days — no sin upon him; and whoever stays behind — no sin upon him, for the one who is mindful. And be mindful of Allah, and know that to Him you will be gathered.

*Wa-dhkuru Allaha fi ayyamin ma'dudatin* — "and remember Allah during counted days."

***Ayyamin ma'dudatin*** — "counted days" — and this is the third occurrence of the phrase in the surah, and the three are worth setting side by side. At 2:80 it was the People of the Book's false claim that the Fire would touch them only for "counted days." At 2:184 it was the fast — a real limitation, described in consoling language. Here it is the appointed days at Mina, the days of *tashriq* — the eleventh, twelfth and thirteenth of Dhu al-Hijjah.

The pattern in the surah is consistent: **the religion's obligations are repeatedly described in the language of fewness and limit.** Whatever the religion asks, it asks for a number, and the number is small.

The days named are those of Mina, and the remembrance commanded in them is the *tashriq* remembrance — the *takbir* that follows the prayers and accompanies the stoning. The Sunnah describes the character of those days. A narration recorded by Muslim states: **"The days of Tashriq are days of eating, drinking, and remembrance of Allah."** And a narration recorded by Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi, al-Nasa'i and Ibn Majah names the day of 'Arafah, the day of sacrifice and the days of Tashriq as the festival of the people of Islam, and as days of eating and drinking.

*Fa-man ta'ajjala fi yawmayni fa-la ithma 'alayhi wa man ta'akhkhara fa-la ithma 'alayhi* — "so whoever hastens in two days, no sin upon him; and whoever stays behind, no sin upon him."

**And note the structure, which is the most useful thing in the verse.** The declaration *la ithma 'alayhi* — "no sin upon him" — is made **twice**, once for each option. The one who leaves on the second day is declared blameless. The one who stays for the third is declared blameless. Neither is deficient.

That is a template worth noticing, because it is the opposite of how religious communities normally handle a choice. The ordinary pattern is that one option is safe and the other is suspect — the stricter one is praised and the lighter one is quietly stigmatised, or vice versa. The verse refuses both stigmas in the same sentence.

The jurists differed on which of the two is **preferable** — some holding that staying for the third is superior because it is more worship, others that departing is preferable — while agreeing that both are lawful and neither carries sin. That disagreement is about virtue, not about permission.

*Li-mani ittaqa* — "for the one who is mindful."

And note the qualifier. The absence of sin attaches to *the one who feared God*. The commentators read this as a real condition rather than a description: the concession is clean for the person who had been mindful in his pilgrimage and did not use the licence as an opening for negligence. The relief is genuine, and it is not a cover.

*Wa-ttaqu Allaha wa-'lamu annakum ilayhi tuhsharun* — "and be mindful of Allah, and know that to Him you will be gathered."

*Tuhsharun* is from the root of *hashr*, the gathering.

**And here is the closing observation, which is the reason this verse ends the pilgrimage passage so well.** The pilgrimage is the closest thing in this life to the Gathering: an enormous crowd, stripped of its usual distinctions, dressed identically, standing on open ground, answering a single call. A person who has performed the Hajj has already rehearsed the thing the verse closes with. And the verse says so — it moves from the days at Mina to the day on which everyone will be gathered, because the one is the image of the other.

For practice, three things.

The first is the doubled acquittal, and its application is far wider than Mina. Wherever there is a genuine lawful choice — in a ruling, a school, a level of observance, a manner of worship — the correct posture is to declare both parties blameless. Most religious friction between Muslims is not about orthodoxy; it is about one group treating another group's lawful choice as a deficiency. This verse acquits both, explicitly, in one sentence.

The second is the *ma'dudat* pattern. When something is asked of you, the surah's habit is to tell you how little it is. That is worth imitating in how the religion is handed to other people: state the obligation, and state its limit, in the same breath.

And the third is the Gathering. The pilgrim standing at Mina is standing in a rehearsal. Anyone who has been should be able to feel what the verse is pointing at: the absence of rank, the sameness of the dress, the crush of the crowd, and the single direction everyone is facing. That is the image the Qur'an uses for the Day, and the Hajj is the one place a person can practise it before it arrives.

## Verse 204

**"There are some ˹hypocrites˺ who impress you with their views regarding worldly affairs and openly call upon Allah to witness what is in their hearts, yet they are your worst adversaries."**

In simple terms: and among the people is he whose speech impresses you in the life of this world, and he calls Allah to witness what is in his heart, while he is the fiercest of opponents.

*Wa mina al-nasi man yu'jibuka qawluhu fi al-hayati al-dunya* — "and among the people is he whose speech impresses you in the life of this world."

*Yu'jibuka* is form IV of the root of wonder and admiration: it pleases you, it impresses you, it astonishes you.

**And note what impresses: *qawluhu* — his speech.** Not his character, not his conduct, not his record. His words. This is a person who is persuasive, fluent, and convincing when he talks — and note the scope: *fi al-hayati al-dunya*, in the matters of this life. He is impressive about the things of this world.

**Which makes this the Qur'an's warning about eloquence as a credential, and it is badly needed.** The person described is the one who speaks well. And the verse's first move is to tell the listener that the impression made by speech is not evidence of anything.

The transmitted reports variously connect this verse to a specific individual of the time known for fair speech and hidden hostility; other commentators read it as a type rather than a named person. Both readings are transmitted, and the second is the more useful, because types recur.

*Wa yushhidu Allaha 'ala ma fi qalbihi* — "and he calls Allah to witness what is in his heart."

Form IV of the root of *shahada*: he makes Allah a witness, he calls upon Allah to testify to what is in his heart.

So the profile continues: not only is he persuasive, but he invokes God as a guarantor of his own sincerity. He swears to what is hidden in him.

**And there is an observation here that the commentators drew and that is worth stating carefully.** The person who must call God to witness his own sincerity is a person whose sincerity is not otherwise evident. Genuine sincerity shows; it does not need to be sworn to. This is not a prohibition of oaths — oaths have their place — but it is the identification of a pattern: fluency plus self-certification. When the two appear together, the verse says, be careful.

*Wa huwa aladdu al-khisam* — "while he is the fiercest of opponents."

***Aladdu al-khisam*** — from a root meaning vehement and unyielding; *aladd* is the elative, and *al-khisam* is the plural of *khasim*, an adversary. So: the most fiercely hostile of adversaries, the most relentless in dispute. Some commentators took *khisam* as the verbal noun, giving "most fierce in disputation." Both readings are transmitted.

**And the hadith on this phrase is one of the most severe in the religion.** Recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from 'A'ishah, the Prophet ﷺ said: **"The most hateful of men to Allah is the fierce disputer [*al-addu al-khisam*]."**

For practice, three things.

The first is the warning about eloquence, and it is the verse's most urgent contemporary application. The community is repeatedly taken in by the fluent speaker, the confident writer, the persuasive presence — and the verse says that the impression produced by speech is not a credential. The test the tradition supplies is not "does he speak well" but "does he act accordingly," and the next verse supplies the answer in this case: he does not.

The second is the self-certification pattern. A person who constantly vouches for his own motives, who prefaces everything with "God knows my heart," who swears to his own sincerity, is describing himself in this verse's terms. It is worth asking, of oneself before anyone else, whether one's sincerity needs to be announced or whether it is simply visible.

And the third is *aladdu al-khisam* as a self-test, and it is uncomfortable. The phrase describes a person who is **both** maximally skilled in argument **and** maximally hostile in using it. Many people are one or the other. The verse names the combination, and the hadith names it as the most hateful to God. The practical question is narrow: when I am in a dispute, am I trying to establish what is true, or to win? And would those around me describe me as relentless?

## Verse 205

**"And when they leave ˹you˺, they strive throughout the land to spread mischief in it and destroy crops and cattle. Allah does not like mischief."**

In simple terms: and when he turns away, he strives in the land to spread corruption in it and to destroy the crops and the livestock. And Allah does not love corruption.

*Wa idha tawalla sa'a fi al-ardi li-yufsida fiha* — "and when he turns away, he strives in the land to spread corruption in it."

***TAWALLA*** — and this is a genuine crux with two transmitted readings. Form V of the root of *wilayah*: it can mean **to turn away and depart**, and it can mean **to be given authority, to take charge**.

On the first reading — which is the one the majority held — the sense is: when he leaves your company, having impressed you, he goes out and works corruption. The eloquence was for the audience; the conduct is what happens out of sight. On the second reading, the sense is darker still: when he is in a position of authority, he uses it to spread corruption.

**And the two are not mutually exclusive, and together they complete the portrait of 204.** In public: impressive speech and sworn sincerity. Out of sight — or in power — corruption.

*Sa'a* is from the root of striving and going about purposefully. Note that the same word names the ritual walking between Safa and Marwa at 2:158 — *al-sa'y*. The sacred *sa'y* of the pilgrimage and the corrupt *sa'y* of this man are the same verb: strenuous movement toward an end. The difference is entirely in what is being striven for.

*Li-yufsida fiha* — to spread *fasad* in it. *Fasad* is from a root meaning to be spoiled, ruined, unsound — the opposite of *salah*, soundness and righteousness. Corruption is not merely wrongdoing; it is the spoiling of what was sound.

*Wa yuhlika al-hartha wa al-nasl* — "and destroys the crops and the livestock."

***Al-harth*** is the tilled land and what grows on it — from the root of ploughing and cultivation. ***Al-nasl*** is offspring and progeny — from a root meaning to beget.

And the crux is which *nasl* is meant. The majority of the commentators read it as **livestock** — the animals, alongside the crops, being what sustains a community's life. Others read it as progeny in the wider sense, including human offspring, so that the destruction named covers both the food supply and the people. Both readings are transmitted, and the translation before us takes the first.

**And note what the two nouns together cover: the means of sustenance and the bearers of life.** The corruptor is identified by what he destroys. He does not merely sin; he ruins what feeds people and what continues them.

*Wa Allahu la yuhibbu al-fasada* — "and Allah does not love corruption."

**And this clause carries a theological point that must be stated carefully, because it is where many readers come undone.**

If Allah wills everything that occurs, how can He not love corruption?

The position of Ahl al-Sunnah is that **will and love are not the same thing when predicated of Allah**, and the tradition distinguishes two kinds of will. There is the **creative will** (*iradah kawniyyah*), by which everything that exists does exist — including corruption — because nothing occurs outside His decree. And there is the **legislative will** (*iradah shar'iyyah*), which is His command, His pleasure and His love. Corruption occurs by the first and is contrary to the second.

So corruption is not outside His decree, and it is not loved by Him, and neither statement negates the other. The same distinction is at work at 2:190 — "Allah does not love the transgressors" — and at 2:276.

The definitive parallel is 28:77: "And do good as Allah has done good to you, **and do not seek corruption in the land. Indeed, Allah does not like the corrupters**." And 7:56: "And do not cause corruption on the earth after its reformation."

For practice, three things.

The first is the gap between public and private, which is what the two readings of *tawalla* expose together. The test of a person is what he does when the audience is gone, and — the harder test — what he does when he has power. The verse names both.

The second is the definition of corruption as the spoiling of sustenance. *Harth* and *nasl* are what feed and continue a community. Which makes this a verse with real reach into areas the classical commentators could not have enumerated: the poisoning of land and water, the manipulation of food supply, the destruction of livelihoods, the economic arrangements that ruin a people's ability to sustain itself. The corruptor in this verse is identified by his effect on what keeps people alive.

And the third is the will-and-love distinction, which is not a piece of technicalia but a practical release. A person who sees corruption flourishing, apparently unchecked, is being shown that the existence of a thing and the approval of a thing are not the same — and that the One who does not love it has not lost control of it either.

## Verse 206

**"When it is said to them, “Fear Allah,” pride carries them off to sin. Hell will be their proper place. What an evil place to rest!"**

In simple terms: and when it is said to him, "Fear Allah," pride seizes him in sin. So Hell is enough for him — and wretched is the resting place.

*Wa idha qila lahu ittaqi Allaha akhadhat-hu al-'izzatu bi-al-ithmi* — "and when it is said to him: fear Allah, pride takes hold of him in sin."

***AL-'IZZAH*** — from the root of might, power, honour and rarity. It is the word for strength and glory, and in this context for the pride that refuses to yield. The commentators glossed it variously as arrogance, as self-importance, and as *hamiyyah* — the partisan zeal, the tribal or personal honour that will not accept correction.

*Bi-al-ithmi* — "in sin," "with sin," "unto sin." The commentators differed on the preposition: that pride seizes him **and carries him into** sin, or that it seizes him **because of** the sin he is already in, or that he takes pride **in** the sin. The readings converge on the same thing: the pride and the sin are bound together, and the correction does not separate them but tightens them.

**And here is the verse's centre, and it is one of the most practically useful diagnostics in the whole Qur'an.**

The trigger is the plainest possible reminder: someone says *ittaqi Allah* — be mindful of God. Not an insult, not an accusation, not a challenge. The simplest and most ordinary piece of religious advice there is.

**And the response is the diagnosis.** The person described does not consider it, or accept it, or reject it quietly. He is seized. Pride takes hold of him and carries him further into the sin he was being warned away from.

Which means: **the measure of a person is not whether he sins, but what happens when he is told to fear God.** Everyone sins. The question the verse puts is what occurs at the moment of correction — whether the reminder produces a turn or a reaction. And the reaction described is disproportionate to the reminder, which is itself the evidence: a person who is well does not respond to ordinary advice with a seizure of pride.

*Fa-hasbuhu jahannamu* — "so Hell is sufficient for him." *Hasb* is sufficiency, enough, what one is content with. The phrasing is economical and severe: he wanted greatness, and what is enough for him is Hell.

*Wa la-bi'sa al-mihad* — "and wretched is the resting place."

*Mihad* is from the root of spreading and preparing — a bed, a cradle, a place of rest.

**And note the irony the closing completes.** He was seized by *'izzah*, by the demand for honour and greatness. And his *mihad* — his prepared resting place, the bed made ready for him — is Hell. The thing he reached for and the thing prepared for him are exact opposites, and the verse sets them within four words of each other.

For practice, two things, and the first is the whole value of the verse.

**How do you respond when someone says "fear Allah"?**

This is worth sitting with honestly, because most people can name their pattern immediately. Some go cold. Some go quiet and hold it. Some become angry at the person who said it. Some produce a list of the speaker's faults. Some agree loudly and change nothing. And some — the ones the verse is describing — are seized and go further into the thing they were warned about.

The verse is not asking a person never to feel the sting. Correction stings; that is normal. What it identifies is when the sting becomes a propulsion away from God rather than toward Him. And the practical discipline follows directly: **receive the reminder from anyone, in any form, and separate the message from the messenger.** The verse's trigger is being *told* — and the tradition is emphatic that Allah may send the reminder through a person you do not like, or through a child, or through an enemy.

And the second is the *mihad*, held against the *'izzah*. A person who is pursuing honour — in a community, a career, a family, a field — should ask what is being prepared. The verse's answer is that the two can diverge completely, and that what is being made ready is the opposite of what was being reached for. That is not an argument against ambition; it is a warning about the kind of honour that cannot bear to be corrected.

## Verse 207

**"And there are those who would dedicate their lives to Allah’s pleasure. And Allah is Ever Gracious to ˹His˺ servants."**

In simple terms: and among the people is he who sells himself, seeking the pleasure of Allah. And Allah is Kind to [His] servants.

*Wa mina al-nasi man yashri nafsahu ibtigha'a mardati Allahi* — "and among the people is he who sells himself, seeking the good pleasure of Allah."

***YASHRI NAFSHU*** — "he sells his self" — and this is the verse's centre and its beauty.

**And note that this completes the surah's commerce imagery, which has been running since its opening.** At 2:16 the hypocrites "purchased misguidance with guidance" — a trade that brought no profit. At 2:86 they "bought the life of this world with the Hereafter." At 2:102 they sold their souls for magic, and the verse said: "Miserable indeed was the price for which they sold their souls." At 2:175 the concealers "purchased misguidance with guidance and punishment with forgiveness."

**And here, at 2:207, the trade is finally made the right way round: a man sells himself, and what he receives is the pleasure of God.**

That is the difference. The others sold something of infinite worth and received something worthless. This one sells the self — which is exactly what a person has to give — and the purchaser is God, and the price is His pleasure.

*Ibtigha'a mardati Allahi* — "seeking the pleasure of Allah." *Ibtigha'* implies seeking earnestly, going after. *Mardah* is from the root of being pleased and well-satisfied. So: he sells himself in eager pursuit of God's being pleased with him.

**And the transmitted reports give the verse a face.** They connect it to **Suhayb ibn Sinan al-Rumi**, who, when he emigrated from Makkah to Madinah, was pursued and blocked by the Quraysh; and he said to them that if he gave them his wealth they would let him go, and they agreed, and he handed over what he had and left with nothing. When he arrived, those who met him said his trade had profited — and the reports state that this verse came concerning him. Other commentators read the verse as general, describing anyone who gives himself for God's pleasure. Both readings are held, and the general reading does not exclude the particular one.

*Wa Allahu ra'ufun bi-al-'ibad* — "and Allah is Kind to [His] servants."

*Ra'uf* is from the root of pity and tenderness: the Kind, the Gentle. The same word closed 2:143 — *inna Allaha bi-al-nasi la-ra'ufun rahim*.

And note that it says *bi-al-'ibad* — to **the servants**, generally, without restriction to the believers. So the verse names a transaction and then describes the party on the other side of it: the One who buys is Kind to those who serve Him.

For practice, three things.

The first is the commerce thread, read as a whole. The surah has given five instances of buying and selling, four bad and one good, and the difference between them is not the intensity of the transaction but its direction. Everyone is trading. Everyone is spending a life. The only question is what is being received for it, and 2:207 states the one transaction in which the price is worth more than the thing sold.

The second is the meaning of "selling oneself," which is more concrete than it sounds. A person who has sold something no longer owns it and does not consult himself about how it is used. So the image is not "working for God" — a person can work for God while retaining ownership of himself, his time, his money and his plans. It is: the self is no longer his to allocate. What he does with his body, his hours, his wealth and his life is decided by the purchaser.

And the third is Suhayb's enactment, which is why the reports matter. He gave actual wealth, in a specific moment, to be able to reach God and His messenger. The verse is not asking for a feeling; it is describing what a person does when the transaction is real, and the reports show a man doing it, and the Prophet ﷺ's circle describing it as a profitable trade. Most people will not be asked for everything. Everyone is asked for something, and the question is whether the handing over is real or nominal.

## Verse 208

**"O believers! Enter into Islam wholeheartedly and do not follow Satan’s footsteps. Surely he is your sworn enemy."**

In simple terms: O you who believe, enter into *al-silm* entirely, and do not follow the footsteps of Satan. Indeed, he is to you a clear enemy.

*Ya ayyuha alladhina amanu udkhulu fi al-silmi kaffatan* — "O you who believe, enter into *al-silm* entirely."

***AL-SILM*** — and there are two transmitted readings, which are closer to each other than they first appear. The majority take it as **Islam**, the religion: enter into Islam. Others take it as **peace**, reconciliation and safety: enter into peace.

And the root is the same in both: *s-l-m*, which yields *salam* (peace), *islam* (submission) and *salama* (safety). The two readings are therefore two faces of one idea — **Islam is the peace that comes from surrender.** To enter it is to enter the safety that follows from handing oneself over.

***KAFFATAN*** — and again two transmitted readings. The majority: **entirely, wholly** — take all of it. Others: **all together** — enter collectively, all of you.

**And on the majority reading, this is the definitive verse against selective religion, and it has a direct parallel inside this surah.** At 2:85 the People of the Book were asked: "Do you believe in some of the Scripture and reject the rest?" — and the answer given there was that the reward for that is disgrace in this life and the harshest punishment in the next. Here the same principle is stated positively, to the believers: **enter wholly.**

The two verses belong together, and the application to the Muslim is uncomfortable and exact. The selective religion the surah condemns is not a Jewish or Christian failing; it is the universal human one of taking the parts of the revelation that suit and setting aside the parts that cost. The verse addresses people who have already believed and tells them that entry is not complete.

*Wa la tattabi'u khutuwati al-shaytani* — "and do not follow the footsteps of Satan."

**And note that this is the second occurrence of the identical prohibition in the surah.** At 2:168: "O mankind... and do not follow the footsteps of Satan. Indeed, he is to you a clear enemy." Here, to the believers, in the same words, with the same closing. It occurs a third time at 24:21.

The *khutuwat* analysis given at 2:168 should be read with this verse rather than repeated: the image is of **steps**, and therefore of gradualism. Satan is not described as seizing but as walking, and the prohibition is against following the increments — the small concession that does not feel like a departure because each step is only slightly further than the last.

**And the repetition itself is the point.** The prohibition was given to mankind at 2:168 and is repeated to the believers at 2:208. If the believers did not need it, it would not be repeated. The surah is telling the community that conversion does not retire the hazard.

*Innahu lakum 'aduwwun mubin* — "indeed, he is to you a clear enemy." Identical to the close of 2:168. *Mubin* — manifest, evident, unmistakable. There is nothing to work out and nothing to negotiate.

For practice, three things.

The first is *kaffatan*, and it supplies a question that most people avoid: **which parts of the religion have I declined to enter?** Not which parts do I fail at — everyone fails — but which have I decided are not really binding, or not for me, or not for this time of life. The verse addresses believers and commands entry, which means it assumes the entry can be partial and is charging the reader to complete it.

The second is the repetition of the *khutuwat*. The fact that the identical warning is given twice in one surah, once to humanity and once to the believers, is the surah's own comment on how persistent the hazard is. A person who has been practising for decades is the addressee of this verse, not its exception.

And the third is the pairing of the two commands. Enter wholly, and do not follow the steps. The two are one movement: the completeness of the entry is what leaves no foothold, and the steps are what produce the incompleteness. A partial entry is not a neutral state — it is the space the steps are taken in.

## Verse 209

**"If you falter after receiving the clear proofs, then know that Allah is indeed Almighty, All-Wise."**

In simple terms: but if you slip after the clear proofs have come to you, then know that Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.

*Fa-in zalaltum min ba'di ma ja'atkumu al-bayyinatu* — "so if you slip after the clear proofs have come to you."

***ZALALTUM*** — and the word choice is the first thing to notice. From a root meaning to lose one's footing, to slip, to err. **It is not the word for disbelief, and it is not the word for rebellion.** It is the word for a slip.

The commentators differed on its force here — some taking it as apostasy, others as falling into sin and error — and the majority took the lesser sense: slipping, erring, losing footing. The gentleness matters. The verse is addressed to people who have received the proofs and then lost their footing, and it names the failure with a word that does not foreclose the recovery.

*Min ba'di ma ja'atkumu al-bayyinatu* — "after the clear proofs have come to you."

*Bayyinat* is the clear evidences — the word that has run through the surah at 2:87, 2:92, 2:99, 2:159 and 2:185.

**And the aggravating circumstance is the same one the surah has been naming all along.** 2:22: "do not make rivals to Allah while you know." 2:42: "do not conceal the truth while you know." 2:145 and 2:120: "after the knowledge that has come to you." 2:159: "after We made it clear." 2:188: "knowing that it is a sin." The pattern is consistent: **knowledge is what makes an act what it is.** Slipping is not excused by ignorance when the proofs have arrived.

*Fa-'lamu anna Allaha 'azizun hakim* — "then know that Allah is Almighty, All-Wise."

**And the crux is why these two Names, at this moment.**

*'Aziz* — the Mighty, the Invincible, the One who is not overcome. *Hakim* — the All-Wise, the One whose decree is sound.

The commentators read the pairing as both a warning and a reorientation. As a warning: you cannot escape Him, and slipping does not put you out of His reach. As a reorientation: the One you have slipped away from is not weakened by it, and His response is wise rather than arbitrary.

**And note what the verse does not say.** It does not say "then you are lost," or "then there is no hope," or even "then repent" — though repentance is of course the required response and is established everywhere else. It says: **know Allah.**

That is the remedy offered to the person who has slipped, and it is a profoundly merciful thing to put at this point. The first move after a fall is not self-punishment, and not despair, and not a flurry of compensating activity. It is right knowledge of who you have to do with.

The supporting texts are consistent: 39:53 — "Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of Allah. Indeed, Allah forgives all sins." And 3:135 — "And those who, when they commit an immorality or wrong themselves, remember Allah and seek forgiveness for their sins — and who can forgive sins except Allah? — and do not persist in what they have done while they know."

For practice, three things.

The first is the word *zalaltum*. A person who has fallen should notice that the Qur'an names his condition with a word for slipping, and should therefore not treat the slip as a landing. The difference between a slip and a fall is whether one gets up, and the verse's vocabulary leaves the getting up entirely available.

The second is the aggravation of the *bayyinat*, and it cuts against a very common self-defence. "I did not realise," "I did not know it was that serious," "I had not been told" — these are real excuses for a person who genuinely did not know, and they are not available to a person who did. The surah has now named the knowledge condition five or six times. A person who has received the proof and slipped after it is in this verse, and the honest response is not to reach for an excuse but to take the verse's remedy.

And the third is that remedy: **know Allah.** After a fall, the temptation is either to minimise it or to be swallowed by it. The verse prescribes neither. It prescribes right knowledge of God — that He is Mighty, so the slip did not remove you from His reach; and Wise, so what follows is measured rather than arbitrary. Which is to say: the way back after a slip begins with theology, not with feeling.

## Verse 210

**"Are they waiting for Allah ˹Himself˺ to come to them in the shade of clouds, along with the angels? ˹If He did˺, then the matter would be settled ˹at once˺. And to Allah ˹all˺ matters will be returned ˹for judgment˺."**

In simple terms: do they wait for anything except that Allah should come to them in shades of cloud, and the angels — whereupon the matter would be settled? And to Allah all matters are returned.

*Hal yanzuruna* — "do they wait?" A rhetorical question of rebuke, of the kind the surah has used before. The demand for a decisive sign has been the refrain of the rejecters throughout — at 2:118, "If only Allah would speak to us or a sign would come to us."

*Illa an ya'tiyahumu Allahu fi zulalin mina al-ghamami wa al-mala'ikatu* — "except that Allah should come to them in shades of cloud, and the angels."

**And here is the crux, and it is one of the most important creedal points in the surah.**

The verse speaks of a **coming** (*ityan*) of Allah. And the tradition has held two readings of this, both of which must be stated rather than one being presented as the other's refutation.

**The position of the Salaf** — and the position held by the early generations and by the Athari school — is to **affirm the coming as a real attribute of Allah, as it is stated, without asking how** (*bi-la kayf*), while denying resemblance to creation and denying that it involves movement, displacement or containment. He comes as befits Him, and the *how* is not enquired into.

**The position of many later scholars** — including most of the Ash'ari and Maturidi tradition — is to read the coming **figuratively**, as the coming of His command, or His judgement, or His signs, or the manifestation of His power, on the grounds that movement and change of place are impossible for God.

Both readings are held within the tradition, and both are held alongside the same rule: **neither likening Him to creation (*tashbih*) nor denying what He has affirmed of Himself (*ta'til*).** This is the same framework I have applied at every attribute in this surah — to *istawa* at 2:29, to the "Face" at 2:115, to "with-ness" at 2:153, and to nearness at 2:186 — and it is applied here for the same reason.

And note the detail that tells against a crude literalism in either direction: the verse says *fi zulalin min al-ghamam* — **in shades of cloud.** Clouds are created things and cannot contain God. So whatever the coming is, it is not a coming inside a vehicle.

The parallel texts are 6:158 — "Do they await except that the angels should come to them, or that your Lord should come, or that some of the signs of your Lord should come?" — and 89:22: "And your Lord comes, and the angels, rank upon rank." Against these, 16:33 speaks of "the **command** of your Lord" coming, which is the textual support the figurative reading rests on.

*Wa qudiya al-amru* — "and the matter would be settled."

**And this is the verse's deepest point, and it is a mercy rather than a threat.**

If that came, the matter would be finished — which means the time of testing would be over, and repentance would no longer be possible.

6:158 states exactly this: "The Day that some of the signs of your Lord will come, no soul will benefit from its faith as long as it had not believed before, or had earned through its faith some good."

**Which means the delay is the mercy.** The rejecter demands the definitive sign. The verse answers: the definitive sign is what would end your opportunity. Its absence *is* the space in which you can still turn. Every day in which the sign has not come is a day in which the door is still open.

*Wa ila Allahi turja'u al-umur* — "and to Allah all matters are returned." From the root of *ruju'*, returning — the same return the surah named at 2:28, 2:156 and 2:203.

For practice, three things.

The first is the creedal point, handled as the tradition handles it. A reader who affirms the coming without asking how, and a reader who reads it as the coming of the command, are both within the tradition, and neither should be presented to the other as having abandoned the text. What is not available to either is to treat God as though He were a body that moves between locations.

The second is the mercy of the delay, and it is the verse's great gift. A person who says "I would believe if I saw a sign" should be told what the Qur'an says about that: the sign he is asking for is the one that would close his account. The fact that it has not come is not evidence against God. It is the condition under which faith is still possible and still counts. Which means the demand for the sign is self-defeating in a way the demander rarely notices — he is asking for the thing that would make his asking pointless.

And the third is the application to the believer's own waiting. Most people live waiting for some future circumstance — a clarity, an event, a change — before they will begin in earnest. The verse's question is a general one: **what are you waiting for?** The only thing that would settle the matter definitively is the thing that ends the time for acting. Everything short of that is delay, and the delay is where the work is.

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

Two halves, and the join is at 206–207.

**The prayer, the days, and the reckoning (201–203).** The corrected prayer is given beside the failed one: good in this world **and** the next, and shielding from the Fire (201) — the supplication the Prophet ﷺ made most often. Then the promise: a share from what was earned, and a reckoning that is swift — with the tradition's essential distinction between the presentation, which every believer passes through to forgiveness, and the scrutiny, which no one survives (202). Then the appointed days at Mina, with blame removed explicitly from **both** the one who leaves early and the one who stays, conditioned on mindfulness, and closed with the Gathering that the pilgrimage rehearses (203).

**Two kinds of person (204–210).** The hypocrite: speech that impresses, God invoked as witness to a heart that is not sound, and underneath it the fiercest hostility (204); corruption that spoils what sustains and continues life (205); and the definitive mark — that when told to fear God, pride carries him off (206). Then the contrast, in a single verse: the one who **sells himself** seeking God's pleasure, completing the surah's commerce imagery and reversing the bad trades of 2:16, 86, 102 and 175 (207). Then the command to enter wholly rather than selectively, with the prohibition of Satan's footsteps repeated in the identical words used at 2:168 — and repeated *to the believers*, which is itself the warning (208). Then the word for slipping, and the remedy, which is knowledge of God rather than despair (209). And finally the question that closes: what are you waiting for, when the only thing that would settle the matter is the thing that would end the time for turning (210)?

Three threads hold the batch. First, **the commerce of the self** — four bad trades given earlier in the surah, and the one good one given here. Second, **eloquence, correction and completeness** — the impressive speaker (204), the refusal of correction (206), the partial entry (208), and the slip after clarity (209) are four versions of the same failure: the outward and the inward diverging. And third, **the delay is mercy** — the reckoning is swift, but the definitive sign has not come, and its absence is the space in which turning is still possible.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given. This is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and supplying precise references from memory would mean inventing them. Where the commentators and jurists differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved: the two readings of *tawalla* in verse 205; whether *al-nasl* means livestock or progeny; the readings of *al-silm* and of *kaffatan* in verse 208; the force of *zalaltum* in verse 209; and which of the two departures at Mina is preferable.

Transmitted reports have been flagged as reports throughout: the connection of verse 207 to Suhayb ibn Sinan al-Rumi and his emigration, with the note that other commentators read the verse as general. The grading of the narration on the days of Tashriq has been noted as transmitted rather than asserted.

Four boundaries have been observed. On verse 202: the two reckonings have been distinguished, because a verse about swift account read without the hadith of 'A'ishah produces undifferentiated terror rather than the mixture of awe and hope the tradition intends. On verse 205: the distinction between the creative and legislative will has been stated plainly, since without it "Allah does not love corruption" appears to contradict His decree, and readers break on exactly this point. On verse 206: the severity of the portrait has been kept alongside the observation that the trigger is the plainest possible reminder — so that the verse functions as a self-test rather than only as a description of others. And on verse 210: both readings of the coming have been given, consistent with the treatment of every other attribute in this surah, with neither presented as the other's refutation and with the guardrail restated against likening God to creation on either side.

The most important practical point in the batch is in verse 210 and has been stated as such: the definitive sign that the rejecter demands is the one that would end his opportunity, so the delay he complains of is the mercy that keeps the door open.
