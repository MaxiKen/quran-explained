# Surah Ali 'Imran (The Family of 'Imran, 3) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 111–120

The last batch ended on a title and a condition. Verse 110 named this community the best raised *for* humankind — on the terms of enjoining right, forbidding wrong, and believing in God — and closed with the surah's standing precision: some of the People of the Book are believers, most are *fāsiqūn*.

These ten verses work out what that diagnosis means in practice. First a reassurance and a limit: they will not harm you except annoyance; in battle they flee and find no help (111). Then a hard historical-moral sentence: disgrace wherever they are found, except under a rope from God or a rope from people; wrath and wretchedness for rejecting the signs and killing the prophets unjustly (112). Then — immediately, deliberately — the great balancing unit the surah has been earning the right to say: **they are not all alike**. Among the People of the Book is an upright community that recites God's revelations through the night in prostration (113), believes, enjoins, forbids, races to good, and ranks among the righteous (114); no good they do is denied, and God knows the mindful (115).

Then the economics of false security: wealth and children will not avail the disbelievers against God; they are residents of the Fire (116); what they spend is like a harvest struck by a freezing wind — God did not wrong them; they wronged themselves (117). Then the inward warning that pairs with 100 and 118's technical word: do not take *biṭānah* — intimates, the inner lining — from outside your own, from people who will not miss a chance to harm you (118). You love them and they do not love you; you believe in the whole Book; they say "we believe" to your face and bite their fingertips in rage alone (119). Good grieves them, evil delights them — but if you are patient and God-mindful, their schemes will not harm you; God knows what they do (120).

Four cruxes govern the reading. The first is 112's two ropes — *ḥabl min Allāh* and *ḥabl min al-nās* — covenant-protection, not a cancellation of the moral charge. The second is 113–115: who is the upright community — those of the People of the Book who entered Islam, or a wider upright remnant — and how the unit must be held against both erasure and flattery. The third is *biṭānah* at 118: intimate confidants, not ordinary neighbourliness, justice, or kindness. The fourth is the psychology of 119–120 — love unanswered, performed belief, rage in private, envy of good — and *ṣabr* plus *taqwā* as the shield.

The standing caution is now the surah's own thesis statement: **laysū sawā'** — they are not all alike. Everything in this batch that is severe is about a party and an act. Everything in this batch that is praise is about a party and an act. Inherited guilt remains outside the Qur'an's vocabulary.

## Verse 111

**"They can never inflict harm on you, except a little annoyance. But if they meet you in battle, they will flee and they will have no helpers."**

In simple terms: they cannot really harm you — only annoy you a little. If they fight you they will turn their backs and run, and after that they will have no one to help them.

**A reassurance placed directly after the "best community" title.** Verse 110 named the mission. Verse 111 names a limit on the opposition. The community that enjoins and forbids is not being sent into a panic. It is being told the scale of the threat.

**"They can never inflict harm on you, except a little annoyance."** *Lan yaḍurrūkum illā adhan.*

*Lan* — strong future negation of real damage. *Adhan* is hurt of a lighter kind: insult, irritation, verbal wound, the grit of hostility that does not amount to decisive harm. The distinction is deliberate. The Qur'an does not say the believers will feel nothing. It says what they will feel does not rise to the level of *ḍarar* that undoes them. Annoyance is real. Ruin is not in the enemy's gift.

**"But if they meet you in battle, they will flee."** *Wa-in yuqātilūkum yuwallūkumu al-adbār.*

*Yuwallū al-adbār* — they will turn their backs to you. The image is flight, not stalemate. In the Madinan field this matched observed patterns in several encounters; the verse states it as a general rule under the conditions of the community that holds its rope. It is not a mechanical prediction that every enemy line always breaks. It is a divine assessment of a particular opposition's spine when met with a community that believes and stands.

**"And they will have no helpers."** *Thumma lā yunṣarūn.*

The root *n-ṣ-r* returns once more. Disciples as *anṣār* (3:52). No *nāṣir* for the rejecters (3:56). The covenant demanding *la-tanṣurunnahu* (3:81). No helpers for those who die on disbelief (3:91). Now: after flight, no help. The one who will not hold God's rope does not find a substitute rope that holds in battle.

**How this verse sits after 100 and 110.** Verse 100 warned the believers not to obey a reversing faction. Verse 110 gave them a title conditional on mission. Verse 111 removes a fear that would make both impossible: the fear that the opposing party is stronger than it is. A community that overestimates its enemies will obey them (100). A community that underestimates the cost of mission will drop the conditions of 110. Verse 111 calibrates the fear.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** Most readers are not mustering for a Madinan battle. The transferable force is the calibration. Hostility that is mostly noise — mockery, social exclusion, professional friction — is *adhan*. It should be named honestly and not inflated into catastrophe. Inflating annoyance into existential defeat is how a community talks itself into the obedience verse 100 forbade. The verse does not ask for naivety about real danger. It asks for proportion.

**And the spiritual centre.** "No helpers" is not only about them. It is about where help actually comes from. The community that holds fast to God (101) and to the rope (103) already has the help that matters. The opposition's missing helpers are the negative image of that fact.

**The grammar of reassurance.** *Lan yaḍurrūkum* is not a promise that no believer will ever be wounded. The Prophet ﷺ and the Companions were wounded, and Uḥud is about to narrate a wound at community scale. The verse's force, in its place after 110 and before the Uḥud block, is about the *opposing party's capacity to decide the believers' fate*. They cannot. They can annoy. They can meet you and flee. They cannot author the end of the story. That distinction matters pastorally: it keeps fear from becoming theology.

## Verse 112

**"They will be stricken with disgrace wherever they go, unless they are protected by a covenant with Allah or a treaty with the people. They have invited the displeasure of Allah and have been branded with misery for rejecting Allah’s revelations and murdering ˹His˺ prophets unjustly. This is ˹a fair reward˺ for their disobedience and violations."**

In simple terms: disgrace is stamped on them wherever they are found, unless they have a covenant-protection from God or a treaty-protection from people. They drew God's anger on themselves and misery was stamped on them, because they rejected God's revelations and killed the prophets without right. That was because they disobeyed and went beyond the limits.

**One of the sternest verses in the surah about a historical community under judgment**, and one that must be read with every guardrail the surah itself has taught.

**"They will be stricken with disgrace wherever they go."** *Ḍuribat ʿalayhimu al-dhillatu ayna mā thuqifū.*

*Ḍuribat* — struck, stamped, imposed. *Al-dhillah* — lowliness, ignominy, the opposite of the honour that holding the Book should have meant. *Ayna mā thuqifū* — wherever they are found. The language is the language of a condition that follows a people under a particular moral description, not the language of a private insult.

**"Unless they are protected by a covenant with Allah or a treaty with the people."** *Illā bi-ḥablin min Allāhi wa-ḥablin min al-nās.*

This exception is load-bearing, and it is often skipped by readers who want only the severity.

*Ḥabl* — rope — the same word as 3:103's rope of God. Here it is the rope of **protection**: a binding covenant. *Ḥabl min Allāh* is read classically as the covenant-protection that runs through God's own bond — in historical practice, the protected status that Islamic law would later systematise for the People of the Book under Muslim rule (*dhimmah*), and more broadly any divine covenantal cover. *Ḥabl min al-nās* is a rope from people — treaties, alliances, the protection of a polity or tribe that takes them under its bond.

The exception means the verse is **not** a licence for unlimited humiliation. Where a rope exists — God's or people's — the stamp of *dhillah* as social exposure is lifted by protection. Madinan and later Islamic practice of treaties with Jewish and Christian communities sits inside this exception, not against it. A reading of 112 that erases the two ropes has left the verse.

**"They have invited the displeasure of Allah and have been branded with misery."** *Wa-bā'ū bi-ghaḍabin min Allāhi wa-ḍuribat ʿalayhimu al-maskanah.*

*Bā'ū* — they returned with, they incurred, they went home carrying. Wrath is not a mood God happened to be in. It is what their acts drew. *Al-maskanah* — wretchedness, misery, the stamp of neediness and abasement. The pairing *dhillah* / *maskanah* appears also at 2:61 in the long address to the Children of Israel; the surah is in deliberate continuity with al-Baqarah's diagnosis.

**"For rejecting Allah's revelations and murdering His prophets unjustly."** *Dhālika bi-annahum kānū yakfurūna bi-āyāti Allāhi wa-yaqtulūna al-anbiyā'a bi-ghayri ḥaqq.*

Two crimes, both already named in the Qur'an's address to earlier communities (2:61; 2:87; 2:91; 3:21; 3:181–183). Rejection of the signs. Killing of the prophets without right. The phrase *bi-ghayri ḥaqq* — without right — is not a suggestion that some prophet-killing might have been rightful. It is the Qur'an's way of underlining the injustice: there was no legal or moral ground. The prophets' blood was shed against every claim of right.

**"This is a fair reward for their disobedience and violations."** The supplied half-bracketed "a fair reward" and "His" before "prophets" are interpretive. The Arabic closes with *dhālika bi-mā ʿaṣaw wa-kānū yaʿtadūn* — that is because they disobeyed and used to transgress. Disobedience and *iʿtidā'*, going past the limit. The cause is conduct, repeatedly.

**Who is "they"?** In the flow after 110–111, the primary referent is the defiant majority among the People of the Book as the surah has been diagnosing them — continuous with 2:61's address to the Children of Israel and with this surah's own prosecutions of a *party*. The verse is not a racial curse on every Jew or Christian in every age. It is a moral-historical sentence about a pattern of rejection and violence against revelation, with an explicit treaty-exception still in force. The next three verses will make the refusal of flattening unmistakable.

**A necessary modern caution.** This verse has been torn out of the surah and used to underwrite contempt, conspiracy thinking, and abuse. That use is a betrayal of the text. The same surah is about to say *laysū sawā'* and to praise an upright community among the People of the Book. The same Qur'an permits marriage and food relations with the People of the Book (5:5), commands justice even toward hostility (5:8; 60:8), and recognises a protected status the two ropes already imply. A reader who can quote 112 and cannot quote 113 has not read the surah. A politics that wants 112 without the ropes and without 113–115 is not tafsir. It is a weapon.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** The transferable structure, stripped of the Madinan particularity, is still severe and still useful: communities that kill their truth-tellers and cover their signs inherit a kind of disgrace that treaties can shelter but not erase as a moral fact. The modern forms of killing prophets are rarely literal. They are the destruction of the person who tells the truth inside a tradition — the whistleblower, the honest scholar, the friend who refuses the lie. Verse 112's logic says that a community which does that should not expect the honour of the Book it still carries. The honour and the conduct have been divorced.

**And the spiritual centre of the exception.** Even under wrath, God leaves ropes. Protection remains possible. That is not sentimentality. It is the same God who left the door open at 3:89. Severity in this Book is almost never sealed shut in history. The ropes are the proof.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 113

**"Yet they are not all alike: there are some among the People of the Book who are upright, who recite Allah’s revelations throughout the night, prostrating ˹in prayer˺."**

In simple terms: but they are not all the same. Among the People of the Book is an upright community that recites God's revelations through the hours of the night, prostrating in prayer.

**The hinge on which the surah's entire treatment of the People of the Book turns.** After the severity of 112 — and after 110's "most are rebellious" — the text refuses the flattening that both hostility and flattery want. *Laysū sawā'* — they are not equal, they are not all alike.

**"Yet they are not all alike."** Four words in English; two in Arabic. The whole ethics of reading this surah is in them. Every *ṭā'ifah*, every *farīq*, every *minhum* we have tracked since the early batches was preparing for this sentence. The Qur'an will prosecute a party without prosecuting a people. Verse 113 is the thesis made explicit.

**"There are some among the People of the Book who are upright."** *Min ahli al-kitābi ummatun qā'imah.*

*Ummatun qā'imah* — a community standing, upright, steadfast. *Qā'imah* is the active participle of standing: a community that stands for the right, that remains on its feet in obedience. The supplied translation's "upright" is exact enough; "standing" is more literal and carries both moral and postural force — especially next to the night-prostration that follows.

**"Who recite Allah's revelations throughout the night, prostrating in prayer."** *Yatlūna āyāti Allāhi ānā'a al-layli wa-hum yasjudūn.*

Night recitation. Prostration. This is not a vague moral compliment. It is a description of a worshipping community — people of the Book whose nights are marked by God's āyāt and by *sujūd*. The image deliberately matches the best of what this ummah also aspires to. The surah is not praising a theoretical type. It is praising a recognisable piety.

**Who is this upright community?** Two classical readings, both early:

**First:** those among the People of the Book who embraced Islam — ʿAbdullāh ibn Salām, Thaʿlabah ibn Saʿyah, Usayd ibn Saʿyah, Asad ibn ʿUbayd, and others named in the reports from Ibn ʿAbbās and Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq. On this reading, *laysū sawā'* distinguishes the People of the Book who entered the faith from those who opposed it, and "People of the Book" is retained as their former title to mark the possibility open to others. Ibn Kathīr develops this strongly.

**Second:** a wider remnant of uprightness among the People of the Book — those who held sincerely to what they had of truth, recited, prostrated, and stood for right — whether or not every individual in the description had yet formally entered the final community. The verse's present-tense worship and the parallel at 3:199 (*wa-inna min ahli al-kitābi la-man yu'minu bi-Llāh…*) keep a door of recognition open.

The responsible reading holds both without forcing a false choice. The strongest early occasion-reading points to those who accepted the Prophet ﷺ. The grammar and the surah's wider habit of praising faith and works wherever they are found (2:62; 3:199; 5:69) refuse to make the praise a closed historical footnote. What cannot be done is to use 113 to cancel 112, or 112 to cancel 113. The surah said both, in this order, on purpose.

**Connection back to 3:75 and 3:110.** Verse 75 opened by praising the trustworthy among the People of the Book before condemning the cheats. Verse 110 ended with *minhumu al-mu'minūn*. Verse 113 is the fullest expansion of that instinct. The surah has never needed a monolithic enemy. It has needed a truthful map.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** *Laysū sawā'* is a discipline before it is a doctrine. It is the refusal to let a headline, a wound, or a political season turn a whole community into a single moral object. The believer who has been harmed by someone from a group, and who then holds the whole group under 112 without remainder, has lost this verse. The believer who, out of embarrassment or fashion, cannot admit the real patterns 112 names has also lost this verse. The upright night-reciters are the surah's way of keeping both eyes open.

**And the spiritual centre.** The praise is not vague tolerance. It is specific: revelation, night, prostration. The Qur'an recognises its own family of acts wherever they appear. A piety that recites God through the night and puts the forehead on the ground is not a stranger to this Book, even when its passport still says otherwise. Verse 113 is confident enough to say so.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 114

**"They believe in Allah and the Last Day, encourage good and forbid evil, and race with one another in doing good. They are ˹truly˺ among the righteous."**

In simple terms: they believe in God and the Last Day, they enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong, and they race each other in doing good. Those are the ones who are truly among the righteous.

**The upright community of 113 is now given its full moral profile.** Five marks, and they are not accidental. They are almost a mirror of what 3:104 and 3:110 required of this ummah.

**"They believe in Allah and the Last Day."** *Yu'minūna bi-Llāhi wa-al-yawmi al-ākhir.* Faith in God and in accountability. The two beliefs that make the rest of a moral life weight-bearing. Without the Last Day, enjoining and forbidding become taste. With it, they become trust.

**"Encourage good and forbid evil."** *Wa-ya'murūna bi-al-maʿrūfi wa-yanhawna ʿan al-munkar.* The same pairing that defined the mission of 104 and the best-community conditions of 110. The surah is deliberately using one vocabulary for the best of this ummah and the best among the People of the Book. The rope is recognisable by the work it produces.

**"And race with one another in doing good."** *Wa-yusāriʿūna fī al-khayrāt.* Not merely doing good. Racing toward it. *Musāraʿah* is competing in haste — the opposite of the delay, the committee, the "someone else will." The upright community is marked by speed toward *khayr*.

**"They are truly among the righteous."** *Wa-ulā'ika min al-ṣāliḥīn.* The supplied half-bracketed "truly" marks the identifying force of *ulā'ika*. *Al-ṣāliḥīn* — the righteous, the ones whose inner and outer have been put right. Membership in that class is by the marks just listed, not by census.

**The mirror of 110 is the point.** Best community: enjoin, forbid, believe (110). Upright community among the People of the Book: believe, enjoin, forbid, race to good (114). The surah is not jealous of the overlap. It is teaching that the moral structure of salvation-facing life is one structure. Where it appears, it is named. Where it is absent, the absence is named. That is honesty, not diplomacy.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** "Race to good" is a diagnostic. In a family, a mosque, a team: who moves first toward the need, and who moves first toward the argument? Verse 114's upright community is defined by the first habit. A community that has perfected the language of 112 and lost the speed of 114 has inverted the surah.

**Why the Last Day is named with God.** Faith in God alone can, in some mouths, become a private metaphysics. Faith in God *and the Last Day* makes metaphysics moral. The upright community is not only theistic. It is accountable. That is why it can enjoin and forbid without the enjoining collapsing into taste or power. The same pairing structures 2:62 and 3:199. The Qur'an trusts a monotheism that remembers the morning of judgment.

**The shared vocabulary as a theological claim.** When the surah uses *amr bi-l-maʿrūf* and *nahy ʿan al-munkar* of this upright remnant and of the best community alike, it is saying that the moral law is not a tribal brand. It is recognised where it is lived. A reader who needs the good of "our side" to be a different species of good from the good of "their upright ones" has not accepted what 114 is doing.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 115

**"They will never be denied the reward for any good they have done. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of those mindful ˹of Him˺."**

In simple terms: any good they do will never have its reward denied them. And God knows full well those who are mindful of Him.

**The guarantee that closes the balancing unit.**

**"They will never be denied the reward for any good they have done."** *Wa-mā yafʿalū min khayrin fa-lan yukfarūhu.*

*Lan yukfarūhu* — it will not be covered, denied, made to disappear. The root *k-f-r* here is the sense of covering over a debt of gratitude — the opposite of recognising a good. God does not do that. Every *khayr* done by this upright community is seen and kept. The verse is continuous with the whole Qur'anic insistence that no atom's weight of good is lost (99:7–8; 18:30).

There is a reading-variant discussion in the tradition between second-person and third-person forms ("you will not be denied" / "they will not be denied"); both are transmitted and both preach. The supplied translation takes the third-person sense that matches the flow about the upright community.

**"And Allah has perfect knowledge of those mindful of Him."** *Wa-Allāhu ʿalīmun bi-al-muttaqīn.*

The supplied half-bracketed "perfect" and "of Him" are interpretive aids around *ʿalīmun bi-al-muttaqīn* — Knowing of the God-mindful. The good is not only counted. The people who live under *taqwā* are known. The night-reciters of 113, the racers of 114, are not anonymous to God. *ʿAlīm* here is comfort: nothing of their *taqwā* falls into a gap.

**How 113–115 must be held with 3:85 and 2:62.** Readers sometimes feel a system-pressure: if 3:85 says a way other than *islām* will not be accepted, how can 113–115 praise an upright community among the People of the Book? The classical answers are not all identical, but the honest options include: (1) the upright community is those who entered Islam (Ibn ʿAbbās's strong line); (2) the praise of works and *taqwā* is real and God loses no good, while final acceptance of a *dīn* remains the subject of 3:85; (3) 2:62 / 5:69 and 3:113–115 describe sincere faith and works in terms those verses set, and must be held alongside the finality and covenant claims of this surah without erasing either side. What cannot be done is to quote only 3:85 as a blunt instrument against 113–115, or only 113–115 as a solvent of 3:85. This tafsir has already refused that false choice at verse 85; it refuses it again here.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** "No good denied" is a sentence for anyone who has done right in a place that did not record it — the hidden charity, the unthanked patience, the night prayer no one saw. Verse 115 says the ledger that matters does not run on human acknowledgement. And it says this, specifically, about people the surah's other verses have been willing to prosecute. Even there, God does not steal wages.

**The name *al-muttaqīn* at the close.** The unit began with night recitation and prostration (113), moved through faith and moral mission (114), and ends on God's knowledge of the God-mindful. *Taqwā* is the thread. The upright are not being praised for identity. They are being praised for a stance God knows from the inside. That is continuous with 3:102's *ḥaqqa tuqātihi* and with 3:120's coming shield of *ṣabr* and *taqwā*. The surah keeps returning to the same centre of gravity.

**Holding the unit as a whole.** 113 without 114 could be aestheticised into "they pray nicely." 114 without 115 could be read as a job description without a wage. 115 without 113–114 could become a vague optimism that God pays everyone for everything. Together: a described community, a described ethics, a described guarantee. That is how the surah balances 112 without neutralising it.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 116

**"Indeed, neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever."**

In simple terms: the wealth and the children of those who disbelieve will not help them at all against God. They are the ones who will be the people of the Fire, remaining there forever.

**After the upright are guaranteed their good, the disbelievers are stripped of their false securities.**

**"Neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah."** *Inna alladhīna kafarū lan tughniya ʿanhum amwāluhum wa-lā awlāduhum min Allāhi shay'an.*

Wealth and children — the two treasures Āl 'Imrān's world, and every world, treats as final insurance. The verse says they do not avail *min Allāh* — against God, from God's side, as a defence before Him. Continuous with 3:10 and 3:91: the earth's fill of gold does not ransom; here even the ordinary treasures of a successful life do not.

**"It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever."** *Wa-ulā'ika aṣḥābu al-nāri hum fīhā khālidūn.*

Residents — *aṣḥāb* — companions of the Fire, people whose place it is. *Khālidūn* again. The subject is those who disbelieve as a settled state, continuous with 91's "die as disbelievers." The living who turn remain under 89.

**Bring it into the present.** The modern form of this false security is not only a vault and a private school. It is the belief that a life stacked with provision and offspring is already a verdict in one's favour. Verse 116 unstacks the verdict. Provision is real. Children are a blessing. Neither is a shield against God. The upright poor person and the disbelieving rich person do not enter the end with the same currency.

**Connection forward and back.** Back: 3:10 already said the wealth and children of those who disbelieve will not avail them against God, and that they are fuel for the Fire. Verse 116 restates the principle after the balancing unit, so that no one mistakes 113–115's generosity for a general amnesty. Forward: 117 will explain what happens to the *spending* of such a life — the harvest and the wind. 116 strips the stockpile; 117 strips the outlay. Together they close the economics of a life built without the foundation the upright community of 113–115 has.

**"Residents of the Fire"** — *aṣḥāb al-nār* — is identifying language. It names a settled belonging. The caution already stated at 91 still holds: the subject is the one who disbelieves as state and trajectory, not the living person still able to take the door of 89. Precision about states is part of not turning the Qur'an into a stamp for every face we dislike.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 117

**"The good they do in this worldly life is like the harvest of an evil people struck by a bitter wind, destroying it ˹completely˺. Allah never wronged them, but they wronged themselves."**

In simple terms: what they spend in this life is like a field of harvest belonging to people who wronged themselves, then a freezing wind struck it and destroyed it. God did not wrong them; they wronged themselves.

**A parable for spending that has no foundation.**

**"The good they do in this worldly life"** — more literally, what they spend: *mā yunfiqūna fī hādhihi al-ḥayāti al-dunyā.* The supplied translation's "the good they do" catches the intended public meaning of their outlays — charity, patronage, the visible generosity of people without faith's foundation. The Arabic's centre is *infaq*, spending.

**"Like the harvest of an evil people struck by a bitter wind, destroying it completely."** *Kamathali rīḥin fīhā ṣirrun aṣābat ḥartha qawmin ẓalamū anfusahum fa-ahlakathu.*

A wind in which there is *ṣirr* — intense cold, a freezing blast. It strikes the tilth of a people who wronged themselves and destroys it. The image is agricultural and total: a year's work, gone in a night of cold. That is what spending without the right foundation is, in the Hereafter's accounting. Not that kindness in the world has no worldly effect. That as *harvest toward God*, it does not survive the wind.

The parable is close kin to 2:264–266's images of spending for show and of a garden struck by fire or whirlwind. The Qur'an teaches economics with weather.

**"Allah never wronged them, but they wronged themselves."** *Wa-mā ẓalamahumu Allāhu wa-lākin anfusahum yaẓlimūn.*

The closing clause is the theology under the parable. The wind is not injustice from God. The wrong was already in the selves who built on what cannot stand. Continuous with 3:108's "God desires no injustice for the worlds." The Fire and the failed harvest are not God stealing wages. They are wages paid in the currency the spenders chose.

**Connection to 3:91–92.** Gold that cannot ransom after death on disbelief (91). Spending from what one loves as the road to *birr* (92). Verse 117 is the middle term: spending that looks like *birr* from outside, without the foundation, freezes and dies. The difference was never the amount. It was the ground.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** Much modern philanthropy is wind-vulnerable in exactly this sense: branding, tax position, reputation, the photo. Verse 117 does not say stop spending. It says build the field on something the cold cannot kill — faith, *taqwā*, the face of God as the addressee of the gift. Abū Ṭalḥah's garden at 3:92 stands; this harvest falls. Same act of spending. Different ground.

**"They wronged themselves"** as the last word of the economics block. The surah will not let the failed harvest become a story about a cruel season. The wind is real; the wrong was prior. That is the same structure as 3:86's withholding of guidance from the *ẓālimīn* and 3:108's refusal of *ẓulm* toward the worlds. God does not author the injustice of a foundationless life. The builders do. Repentance, when it comes, is among other things the rebuilding of the foundation before the next season's wind.

**Why the parable is agricultural.** A harvest is time, labour, hope, and exposure. It is the opposite of a quick transaction. The Qur'an chooses that image so the reader feels the waste — not a coin lost, but a year lost. Foundationless moral effort is not nothing in history; it can feed people, fund hospitals, shift policies. The verse's claim is eschatological and foundational: as *what reaches God as accepted harvest*, it does not survive the cold. Holding both the worldly good and the eschatological verdict without collapsing one into the other is the adult reading.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 118

**"O believers! Do not associate closely with others who would not miss a chance to harm you. Their only desire is to see you suffer. Their prejudice has become evident from what they say—and what their hearts hide is far worse. We have made Our revelations clear to you, if only you understood."**

In simple terms: O you who believe — do not take as close intimates people outside your own who will not miss any chance to harm you. They love what distresses you. Hatred has already shown itself from their mouths, and what their hearts hide is greater. We have made the revelations clear to you, if you will understand.

**The technical word is *biṭānah*, and getting it right is the difference between wisdom and xenophobia.**

**"Do not associate closely with others…"** The Arabic is *lā tattakhidhū biṭānatan min dūnikum* — do not take a *biṭānah* from other than yourselves.

*Biṭānah* is the inner lining of a garment — the layer against the body. By metaphor it means the innermost circle: confidants, private advisors, the people who hear what the public does not hear and who have access to the community's soft tissue. Al-Qurṭubī and others stress this: not "friends" in the general civic sense, not neighbours, not trading partners, not people one is kind to. The *inner lining*.

*Min dūnikum* — from other than yourselves, from outside your own. In the Madinan context the "own" is the believing community; the outsiders in view are those whose posture toward that community is hostile — hypocrites, and some among the People of the Book who were working against the new polity, as the *asbāb* material from Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid describes: believers taking hypocrites and certain Jewish allies as intimates through old kinship, neighbourhood, and treaty ties, at risk of being turned.

**"Who would not miss a chance to harm you."** *Lā ya'lūnakum khabālan.* They spare you no *khabāl* — corruption, ruin, the unravelling of your affairs. The verse is not describing a neutral outsider. It is describing a type that is actively glad of the community's damage.

**"Their only desire is to see you suffer."** *Waddū mā ʿanittum.* They love what distresses you. Envy as structure, not as moment.

**"Their prejudice has become evident from what they say — and what their hearts hide is far worse."** *Qad badati al-baghḍā'u min afwāhihim wa-mā tukhfī ṣudūruhum akbar.*

Hatred has already appeared from their mouths. The tongue has slipped enough for a careful person to notice. And the breast hides more than the mouth has shown. The verse trains a kind of literacy: read the mouth, and then assume the heart's store is larger, not smaller.

**"We have made Our revelations clear to you, if only you understood."** *Qad bayyannā lakumu al-āyāti in kuntum taʿqilūn.* The condition is *ʿaql* — understanding, the use of the mind. The signs are clear. The failure, if it comes, will not be for lack of revelation. It will be for lack of thinking.

**What this verse is not.** It is not a ban on kindness to non-Muslims (60:8). It is not a ban on justice toward them (5:8). It is not a ban on marriage and food relations the Qur'an itself permits (5:5). It is not a ban on ordinary friendship, neighbourliness, workplace cooperation, or civic life. It is a ban on placing people who wish the community's ruin in the *inner lining* — the advisory and secret-bearing circle. A politics that turns 118 into general enmity has left the word *biṭānah* behind. A naivety that places the community's secrets in hostile hands has left the verse unread.

**Reported occasion.** Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid: revealed about believers who had taken hypocrites and some Jewish men as intimates through kinship and old alliance. The warning is against that intimacy as a vector of harm and possible reversion. Hold as illuminating occasion.

**Hadith colour.** Abū Saʿīd's report in Bukhārī and Nasā'ī — that God has not sent a prophet or appointed a caliph except with two *biṭānahs*, one commanding good and one evil — underlines how seriously the tradition takes the inner circle. The quality of the lining shapes the garment.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** Every life has a *biṭānah* whether it uses the word or not: the three people who hear the unguarded plan, the group chat where the real decisions happen, the advisor whose voice outweighs the room. Verse 118 asks who is in that lining, and what they want for you. In a workplace, it is the mentor who only mentors you toward their advantage. In a family, the relative who collects confidences to use them. In a community, the charming outsider who wants the membership list and the fracture points. The verse does not say befriend no one. It says: the lining is not a job for someone who loves your distress.

**And the spiritual centre.** "What their breasts conceal is greater" is a sentence about other people, and then, if the reader is honest, a sentence about the self. Every breast conceals more than its mouth. The believer who takes this only as a tool for reading enemies, and never as a call to clean his own concealed envy, has taken half the medicine.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 119

**"Here you are! You love them but they do not love you, and you believe in all Scriptures. When they meet you they say, “We believe.” But when alone, they bite their fingertips in rage. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “˹May you˺ die of your rage!” Surely Allah knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart."**

In simple terms: here you are — you love them, but they do not love you; you believe in the whole Book. When they meet you they say "we believe," but when they are alone they bite their fingertips in rage against you. Say: die in your rage. God knows fully what is hidden in the breasts.

**The psychology of unanswered love and performed belief.**

**"Here you are! You love them but they do not love you."** *Hā'antum ulā'i tuḥibbūnahum wa-lā yuḥibbūnakum.*

Asymmetry. The believers' default, shaped by the whole Book they accept, includes a reach of love — kinship-love, neighbour-love, the wish for the other's guidance — that is not returned. The verse does not scold the believers for loving. It names the fact that the love is one-way, so that the one-way-ness stops being a surprise and starts being data.

**"And you believe in all Scriptures."** *Wa-tu'minūna bi-al-kitābi kullihi.*

You believe in the Book in its entirety — the Qur'an and, in the surah's theology, the truth of the earlier revelations as originally given. They, in the pattern the surah has described, believe selectively: their Book when it suits, not the confirming messenger, not the whole. The asymmetry of love sits on an asymmetry of faith. You have made room for their revelation; they have not made room for yours.

**"When they meet you they say, 'We believe.' But when alone, they bite their fingertips in rage."** *Wa-idhā laqūkum qālū āmannā wa-idhā khalaw ʿaḍḍū ʿalaykumu al-anāmila min al-ghayẓ.*

Two theatres. Public: the confession of belief. Private: the fingertips between the teeth — an Arabic image of helpless rage, the body biting itself because it cannot bite you. Hypocrisy here is not only false doctrine. It is false affect: warmth in the doorway, fury in the corridor.

**"Say, 'May you die of your rage!'"** *Qul mūtū bi-ghayẓikum.*

The supplied half-bracketed "O Prophet" and "May you" mark voice and force. The command is to the Prophet ﷺ to say this — a revelatory rebuke, not a believer's everyday curse to be freely deployed. The point is that the rage will not achieve its object; it will consume its holder. God knows what the breasts hide (*inn Allāha ʿalīmun bi-dhāti al-ṣudūr* — the supplied "hidden in the heart" is exact in force).

**Connection to 3:118 and 3:100.** The *biṭānah* warning (118) explained why the inner circle cannot be recruited from this type. Verse 100 warned against obeying a reversing faction. Verse 119 shows the type from the inside: love not returned, belief performed, rage stored. The three verses are one education in discernment.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** Unanswered love is one of the hardest discipleships. The verse does not say stop being a person who loves. It says stop being a person who confuses love with access. You may wish someone's good and still refuse them the lining of your life. And the two-theatre pattern — kind in public, corrosive in private — is not only a Madinan hypocrite's pattern. It is a workplace pattern, a family pattern, a social-media pattern. Fingertips still find their way between teeth. The cure the next verse will name is not counter-rage. It is *ṣabr* and *taqwā*.

**The body language of rage.** Biting the fingertips is not a casual idiom. It is the body turning on itself when it cannot reach its object — a picture of hatred that has nowhere useful to go and so spends itself on the hater. The verse lets the believer see that private theatre so the public "we believe" stops being confusing. Confusion is often the point of the performance. Clarity is the believer's defence.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 120

**"When you ˹believers˺ are touched with good, they grieve; but when you are afflicted with evil, they rejoice. ˹Yet,˺ if you are patient and mindful ˹of Allah˺, their schemes will not harm you in the least. Surely Allah is Fully Aware of what they do."**

In simple terms: if good comes to you they are grieved; if evil touches you they rejoice. But if you endure patiently and are mindful of God, their scheming will not harm you at all. God is fully aware of what they do.

**The last movement of the batch, and the practical shield.**

**"When you are touched with good, they grieve; but when you are afflicted with evil, they rejoice."** *In tamsaskum ḥasanatun tasu'hum wa-in tuṣibkum sayyi'atun yafraḥū bihā.*

Envy in two directions. Your *ḥasanah* — good, success, relief, blessing — pains them. Your *sayyi'ah* — setback, wound, loss — delights them. The relationship is not competitive in a healthy sense. It is zero-sum in their chest: your up is their down, your down is their up. That is the emotional structure the believers are being asked to see clearly so they stop expecting ordinary goodwill from it.

**"Yet if you are patient and mindful of Allah, their schemes will not harm you in the least."** *Wa-in taṣbirū wa-tattaqū lā yaḍurrukum kayduhum shay'an.*

Two conditions; one promise.

*Ṣabr* — patience, steadfastness, the refusal to be swung by their joy or their grief into panic or revenge.

*Taqwā* — the mindfulness of 3:102, the guardedness before God that keeps the response clean.

Under those two, *kayduhum* — their plot, their scheme — does not harm you *shay'an*, not in anything. Continuous with 111's *lan yaḍurrūkum illā adhan*: the harm they can do is limited, and under *ṣabr* and *taqwā* even the scheme fails.

This is one of the Qur'an's most practical security doctrines. The defence against a certain kind of enemy is not first a counter-plot. It is character. Patience and *taqwā* are not soft options. They are the conditions under which *kayd* loses its purchase.

**"Surely Allah is Fully Aware of what they do."** *Inna Allāha bi-mā yaʿmalūna muḥīṭ.*

*Muḥīṭ* — encompassing, surrounding, aware in a way that leaves nothing outside the circle. Their schemes are inside God's awareness before they are inside your patience. The believer's *ṣabr* is not blind. It is held under a larger encompassment.

**How the batch ends where the next battle-narrative can begin.** Verses 121 and following will move into Uḥud and the architecture of a military defeat and its moral causes. Verse 120 is the right door into that material: patience and *taqwā* as the conditions under which plots — and, soon, the battlefield's own tests — do not have the last word.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** You will know this emotional structure if you have ever watched someone flinch at your good news and brighten at your bad. The verse names it so you can stop writing alternative explanations that protect you from the truth. And then it tells you what to do: not a campaign of counter-schadenfreude, not a performance of equal coldness, but *ṣabr* and *taqwā*. Under those, the scheme does not get the harm it wanted. Sometimes the scheme's only remaining power is the power you give it by becoming like it. Verse 120 refuses that gift.

**And the spiritual centre of the ten verses.** From the limited harm of 111, through the severity and the ropes of 112, through the great *laysū sawā'* of 113–115, through the false securities of 116–117, through the *biṭānah* and the unanswered love and the private rage of 118–119, the batch ends on two words that have been the surah's medicine all along: patience, and mindfulness of God. The best community of 110, the joined hearts of 103, the open door of 89 — none of them survive long without these two. With them, even *kayd* is smaller than it looks.

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

These ten verses are the surah doing two things at once, on purpose: telling the truth about a hostile pattern, and refusing to turn that pattern into a total claim about a people.

**111–112 name limit and judgment.** Harm is capped at annoyance; in battle they flee and find no help (111). Disgrace is stamped where they are found, **except** under a rope from God or a rope from people; wrath and wretchedness for rejecting signs and killing prophets without right; cause named as disobedience and transgression (112). The two ropes are not a footnote. They are the verse's own guardrail against unlimited humiliation, and they underwrite treaty and protection in historical practice.

**113–115 are the thesis the whole surah has been building toward.** *Laysū sawā'* — they are not all alike. An upright community among the People of the Book recites God's āyāt through the night in prostration (113); believes, enjoins, forbids, races to good, ranks among the righteous (114); no good they do is denied; God knows the mindful (115). Whether read primarily as those who entered Islam (Ibn ʿAbbās) or as a wider upright remnant also held by the grammar and by 3:199, the unit forbids both erasure and flattery. Severity without 113 is a weapon. Praise without 112 is a sentiment. The surah gives both.

**116–117 strip false securities.** Wealth and children do not avail against God; residents of the Fire forever (116). Spending without foundation is a harvest hit by a freezing wind; God did not wrong them — they wronged themselves (117). Continuous with 3:91–92: gold's tense and ground decide whether it saves or freezes.

**118–120 train discernment and name the shield.** Do not take *biṭānah* — the inner lining — from those who love your ruin; hatred shows in the mouth and hides more in the breast (118). You love them; they do not love you; you believe in the whole Book; they perform belief in public and bite their fingertips in private rage (119). Your good grieves them; your evil delights them — but if you have *ṣabr* and *taqwā*, their *kayd* does not harm you; God encompasses what they do (120).

**Threads carried and extended.**

- ***Laysū sawā' / party-precision*** — the explicit climax of *ṭā'ifah*, *farīq*, *minhum* tracked since early in the surah; continuous with 3:75 and 3:110.
- ***Ḥabl*** — rope of God for holding together (3:103) and ropes of protection from God and people (3:112); same word, two offices.
- ***N-ṣ-r*** — no helpers after flight (3:111), continuous with 3:52, 56, 81, 91.
- ***Amr / nahy / belief*** — the upright community's marks (114) mirror the best community's conditions (110) and the mission of 104.
- ***Biṭānah*** — inner lining, not general friendship; pairs with the obedience-warning of 100.
- ***Ṣabr and taqwā*** — the shield of 120; *taqwā* continuous with 102's *ḥaqqa tuqātihi*.
- **Wealth's ground** — 116–117 with 91–92: ransom fails, loved spending saves, foundationless spending freezes.

**Where the supplied translation has chosen, it has been flagged.** Half-bracketed additions ("His" prophets, "a fair reward," "in prayer," "truly," "perfect," "completely," "O Prophet," "May you," "hidden," "believers," "Yet," "of Allah") are interpretive aids. "Annoyance" for *adhan* is exact. "Covenant" / "treaty" for the two *ḥabl*s at 112 is responsible dynamic equivalence for protection-ropes. "Associate closely" / the sense of intimate association for *biṭānah* is warmer modern English for a technical metaphor — the commentary restores "inner lining." "Prejudice" for *baghḍā'* is slightly soft; the Arabic is hatred, malice. "Mindful" for *taqwā*/ *muttaqīn* remains the translation's standing warm choice. None of these is distortion; each is a choice.

**The verses to leave with are 113 and 120, held together.** They are not all alike — so never flatten a people. And if you are patient and God-mindful, the scheme of those who are not upright will not get what it wants from you. Precision about others; character in the self. That is the batch's double discipline.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given, because this is a synthesized commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and inventing precise references would be worse than omitting them.

Five places in this batch required particular care.

**The first is 3:112.** Severity about disgrace, wrath, and the killing of prophets is real and has Qur'anic precedent (2:61 and parallels). I have stated it firmly, kept the two ropes of protection visible as the verse's own exception, insisted that the cause is conduct (*ʿaṣaw wa-kānū yaʿtadūn*), and refused every use of the verse as a racial or unlimited licence. The next three verses are part of the reading of this one.

**The second is 3:113–115.** I have set out the strong Ibn ʿAbbās line (those who entered Islam) and the wider reading that the grammar and 3:199 also allow, held them without a false forced choice, and again refused to let 3:85 and 113–115 cancel each other. *Laysū sawā'* is the surah's own non-negotiable.

**The third is 3:117's parable.** Foundationless spending freezes; this does not make worldly kindness worthless as worldly kindness, and it does not cancel 3:92's spending from what one loves on the right ground. Ground decides.

**The fourth is 3:118, *biṭānah*.** Inner lining, confidants, advisors — not general friendship, not justice, not 60:8 kindness. The verse is wisdom about trust under hostility. Inflating it into xenophobia, or deflating it into nothing, are both misreadings.

**The fifth is 3:119's "die of your rage."** Addressed as a command to the Prophet ﷺ to speak a revelatory rebuke, not as a free-floating curse for everyday sectarian use. The psychology (unanswered love, performed belief, private rage) is the lasting teaching.

**On the translation.** "Covenant" and "treaty" at 112 responsibly render protection-ropes. "Associate closely" gestures at *biṭānah* but needs the lining-metaphor restored in commentary — done. "Prejudice" for *baghḍā'* is soft for hatred. "Bright" is not in this batch; "mindful" continues as the standing choice for *taqwā*. Half-bracketed aids generally help. The translation is not wrong; it has chosen, and the reader is entitled to know what was chosen.

Two boundaries observed throughout. No living community has been placed under 112's stamp as a racial verdict; the verse tracks conduct and leaves ropes. And *laysū sawā'* has been allowed to do its full work — the upright night-reciters are as much the surah's voice as the prosecutions that precede them. Precision about parties; refusal of inherited guilt; *ṣabr* and *taqwā* as the shield. That is where the batch leaves the reader, at the threshold of the Uḥud narrative.
