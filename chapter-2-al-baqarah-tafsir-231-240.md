# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 231–240

This batch finishes the passage on divorce and then does something unexpected with it.

The first two verses close the last two loopholes in the law of divorce: the husband who takes his wife back in order to harm her (231), and the family that blocks her from going back to a man she wants (232). Then the passage moves to what happens to the children and the money (233, 236, 237), to the widow (234, 240), and to the strange interim period in which a woman is waiting and a man is thinking about her (235).

And then, in the middle of all of it, **verse 238 says: guard the prayers.**

That interruption is not an accident and it is not an editorial slip. The commentators have always remarked on it, and it is the most pastorally intelligent thing in the batch: the surah will not let a person lose his prayer while he is losing his marriage. The prayer is placed at the exact point where a human being is most likely to abandon it.

One further thing to watch across these ten verses. The phrase *bi-al-ma'rūf* — "according to what is recognised as good" — appears in **every single one** of the legislative verses here: 231, 232, 233, 234, 236 and 240. Six times in ten verses. The Qur'an is not giving a schedule of amounts. It is giving a principle and leaving the quantum to circumstances, to means, and to what a community recognises as decent. That is a jurisprudential decision of the first importance, and it is almost invisible if you are reading for numbers.

## Verse 231

**"When you divorce women and they have ˹almost˺ reached the end of their waiting period, either retain them honourably or let them go honourably. But do not retain them ˹only˺ to harm them ˹or˺ to take advantage ˹of them˺. Whoever does that surely wrongs his own soul. Do not take Allah’s revelations lightly. Remember Allah’s favours upon you as well as the Book and wisdom He has sent down for your guidance. Be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things."**

In simple terms: and when you divorce women and they have reached their term, then retain them according to what is recognised as good, or release them according to what is recognised as good — and do not retain them harmfully, in order to transgress. Whoever does that has wronged his own soul. And do not take the signs of God in jest. And remember God's favour upon you, and what He has sent down to you of the Book and the Wisdom, admonishing you with it. And fear God, and know that God is Knowing of everything.

*Wa idhā ṭallaqtumu al-nisā'a fa-balaghna ajalahunna fa-amsikūhunna bi-ma'rūfin aw sarriḥūhunna bi-ma'rūf, wa lā tumsikūhunna ḍirāran li-ta'tadū, wa man yaf'al dhālika fa-qad ẓalama nafsah, wa lā tattakhidhū āyāti Allāhi huzuwan, wa-dhkurū ni'mata Allāhi 'alaykum wa mā anzala 'alaykum mina al-kitābi wa al-ḥikmati ya'iẓukum bih, wa-ittaqū Allāha wa-'lamū anna Allāha bi-kulli shay'in 'alīm.*

**This verse repeats the two-door structure of 2:229 and then closes a door that 2:229 had left open.**

2:229 said: *either retain according to what is good, or release with excellence.* Two options. But a person determined to cause harm can walk through the first door while doing the opposite of what it is for — he can "retain" while making the retention intolerable, in order to force her to buy her way out, or to punish her, or to stop her marrying anyone else.

**So 2:231 names that as a third, forbidden option:** *wa lā tumsikūhunna **ḍirāran li-ta'tadū*** — "and do not retain them **harmfully, in order to transgress**."

Two words doing precise work. ***Ḍirāran*** — harm, from *ḍarar*. And ***li-ta'tadū*** — "in order to transgress": the *li* of purpose. The verse is not describing an unfortunate side-effect. It is naming an **intention**. The harm is the point.

**Which means the ruling attaches to the motive, not only to the outcome.** A man who keeps his wife during her waiting period because he wants the marriage to work has done what 2:228 permitted. A man who keeps her in order to make her life unliveable has done what this verse forbids — and the outward act is identical. Only the intention distinguishes them, which is why the verse closes with *God is Knowing of everything*.

*Wa man yaf'al dhālika fa-qad **ẓalama nafsah*** — "and whoever does that has **wronged his own soul**."

**Note carefully who the verse names as the victim: himself.** Not "he has wronged her" — though of course he has. The Qur'an here says that the man who uses the law as a weapon has wronged **his own soul**.

That is not a softening. It is the same logic the surah used at 2:204–205 for the man whose speech impresses and who then works corruption in the earth: *the corruption is registered against him.* And it is the Qur'an's consistent way of speaking about sin — wrongdoing damages the wrongdoer first, and the harm he does to others is real but is not the whole accounting. **A person destroying someone else's life is also destroying his own, and the verse says so out loud so that he will stop.**

*Wa lā tattakhidhū **āyāti Allāhi huzuwan*** — "and **do not take the signs of God in jest**."

**This is one of the most-quoted and most-needed clauses in the entire surah, and its placement is what gives it its force.**

It comes immediately after the prohibition on harmful retention — which means its primary meaning in context is precise and devastating: **using a mechanism the law gave you for one purpose in order to achieve the opposite purpose is taking God's signs as a joke.** The law gave you the right of return so that you could repair your marriage. If you use it to make your wife's life unendurable, you are not "using your rights." You are laughing at the text.

And that definition reaches far beyond divorce. **Every legal form used to do what the law forbids in substance is this verse's subject.** The contract that is a loan with the word "sale" written on it. The "gift" that is a bribe. The technical compliance that is defiance wearing a lawyer's collar. The verse names the category, and it does not require the person to think of himself as mocking anything — only that he be using God's words against God's intent.

**The Sunnah supplies the concrete case.** Reported by Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī and Ibn Mājah from Abū Hurayrah, and graded *ḥasan* by al-Tirmidhī: **"There are three matters in which earnestness is earnest and jest is jest: marriage, divorce, and taking back [a wife]."** A person who says "I divorce you" and then says "I was only joking" does not get to decide afterwards what he was doing. The words were not his to define.

*Wa-dhkurū **ni'mata Allāhi 'alaykum** wa mā anzala 'alaykum mina **al-kitābi wa al-ḥikmati** ya'iẓukum bih* — "and remember **God's favour upon you**, and what He has sent down to you of **the Book and the Wisdom**, admonishing you with it."

**Two things here, and both matter.**

**The first is the reminder of favour.** Before the prohibition, before the warning: remember what you have been given. This is the same rhetorical move the surah made to the Children of Israel — *remember My favour which I bestowed upon you* (2:47, 2:122) — and it works the same way. **Gratitude is being used as the motive for obedience.** A person who is actually counting what he has been given does not treat the Giver's words as a toy.

**The second is the phrase *al-kitāb wa al-ḥikmah* — the Book and the Wisdom.** This exact pair has already appeared twice in this surah: in Ibrāhīm's prayer, that God would raise a messenger "who recites Your signs to them, **teaches them the Book and the Wisdom**, and purifies them" (2:129), and in God's own description of what He sent: "as We have sent among you a messenger from yourselves... **teaching you the Book and the Wisdom**" (2:151).

**On the standard reading of the tradition, "the Wisdom" (*al-ḥikmah*) here is the Sunnah.** Which means this verse belongs to the surah's running argument that the Prophet's teaching is revelation alongside the recited text — the same argument that makes the hadith of 'Ā'ishah, or the hadith of the wife of Thābit ibn Qays, or the hadith of the wife of Rifā'ah, authoritative rather than merely interesting. **A person who takes the Book and sets the Wisdom aside has taken half of what God says He sent down.**

*Wa-ittaqū Allāha wa-'lamū anna Allāha **bi-kulli shay'in 'alīm*** — "and fear God, and know that God is **Knowing of everything**."

*Bi-kulli shay'in* — of **everything**. The most comprehensive form of the attribute, placed at the end of a verse about a motive. **He knows the intention you have not admitted to yourself yet.** That is the verse's last word on the man who is harming his wife while telling himself he is exercising a right.

**For practice.** This verse is the answer to a situation counsellors meet constantly: the husband who pronounces a revocable divorce, takes her back in the last days of the waiting period, and repeats it — sometimes for years, keeping her permanently one step from being dismissed, never secure, never free. The verse names it (*ḍirāran*), names the intent (*li-ta'tadū*), and names who is actually being destroyed (*nafsah*). **And it names what it is to do this with God's words: taking them in jest.** There are few verses in the Qur'an that speak more directly to a widespread abuse, and none that speaks more plainly.

## Verse 232

**"When you have divorced women and they have reached the end of their waiting period, do not prevent them from re-marrying their ex-husbands if they come to an honourable agreement. This is enjoined on whoever has faith in Allah and the Last Day. This is purer and more dignifying for you. Allah knows and you do not know."**

In simple terms: and when you divorce women and they have reached their term, do not hinder them from marrying their husbands, when they agree among themselves according to what is recognised as good. That is what is admonished with it: whoever among you believes in God and the Last Day. That is purer for you and more purifying. And God knows, and you do not know.

*Wa idhā ṭallaqtumu al-nisā'a fa-balaghna ajalahunna fa-lā ta'ḍulūhunna an yankiḥna azwājahunna idhā tarāḍaw baynahum bi-al-ma'rūf, dhālika yū'aẓu bihi man kāna minkum yu'minu bi-Allāhi wa al-yawmi al-ākhir, dhālikum azkā lakum wa aṭhar, wa Allāhu ya'lamu wa antum lā ta'lamūn.*

**The transmitted occasion is in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī*, and it is the perfect case study.**

Ma'qil ibn Yasār had given his sister in marriage to a Muslim man during the lifetime of the Messenger of God ﷺ. She stayed with him for a while, and he divorced her — once — and did not take her back until her waiting period had elapsed. Then the two of them wanted each other again, and he came forward with the other suitors to ask for her hand.

Ma'qil said to him: **"O you who have no sense! I honoured you and married her to you, and you divorced her. By God, she will never return to you."**

And he was a man who had sworn to it.

Then this verse was revealed: *do not prevent them from marrying their husbands.* And Ma'qil said: **"I hear and obey, O Messenger of God"** — and in the fuller narrations, recorded by Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī, Ibn Jarīr, Ibn Abī Ḥātim, al-Bayhaqī and al-Ḥākim, with al-Tirmidhī grading it authentic, he married her to the man and **expiated his oath.**

Some narrations name the sister as Jamīlah and the husband as 'Abdullāh ibn 'Āṣim. A parallel report, transmitted by al-Ṭabarī, tells the same story about Jābir ibn 'Abdillāh and his cousin.

**Now notice how much of this batch is present in that one story.**

**The first connection is to 2:224 and 2:225.** Ma'qil had **sworn an oath** that she would never go back. When the verse came, he broke the oath and expiated it — which is exactly the ruling of 2:224 ("do not make God a hindrance, through your oaths, to doing good and setting things right between people") and exactly the hadith ("whoever swears an oath and then sees something better than it, let him do what is better and expiate his oath"). **The passage legislated the principle in 224–225 and then, seven verses later, gave the worked example.** The oath was sworn in a good cause — defending his sister's dignity — and it still had to be broken, because a better thing had become visible.

**The second connection is to 2:229.** In the narration, the husband was not a bad man. One version says plainly: *the man was not bad, and the woman wanted to go back to him.* **Nobody was at fault.** The marriage had failed in the way marriages sometimes fail, both parties wanted to try again, and one man's wounded pride was the only obstacle. And God sent a verse about it.

**And the third is the phrase the narration puts in the Prophet's mouth about why.** In the transmitted wording: **"God knew his need for his wife and her need for her husband"** — and He revealed the verse.

**Sit with that.** The revelation is attributed to *their need*. Not to a legal defect, not to a technical error, not to anyone's rights having been violated. **God saw two people who needed each other and one person standing in the way, and He sent a verse.** That is the theology of this passage in a sentence, and it is the opposite of the way this verse is usually deployed — as a dry permission. It is permission, yes, but it is permission issued because of need.

**Now the wording.**

*Fa-lā **ta'ḍulūhunna*** — "do not **hinder** them."

*'Aḍl* is to obstruct, to hold back, to squeeze. It is the same root used at 4:19 ("do not hold them back in order to take part of what you gave them") and 24:33 (do not compel your slave-women to prostitution). **In every instance the word describes the strong obstructing the one who cannot get past them.**

**And note whom it addresses.** The verb is plural, but the object is *hunna* — "them" — and the people being addressed are the ones with the power to prevent. **This is a verse to the guardian, the father, the brother, the family.** As al-Ṭabarī and the commentators note, the prohibition is aimed at the relatives against whose wishes a woman generally cannot act.

Which is why the verse says *an yankiḥna **azwājahunna*** — "from marrying **their husbands**." Not "from marrying," and not "from taking other husbands." As Ṭabāṭabā'ī observes, if the verse meant any husband it would have said "from marrying" or "from taking husbands." **It says *their* husbands — the ones who divorced them.** This is a verse specifically about remarriage to a former husband, and the obstacle it removes is the family's.

*Idhā **tarāḍaw baynahum bi-al-ma'rūf*** — "when **they agree among themselves**, according to what is recognised as good."

**The condition is the couple's mutual agreement — not the guardian's approval of the match.** *Tarāḍaw* is a reciprocal form: each consents to the other. And *bi-al-ma'rūf* again: the agreement must itself be decent — a real marriage, not a sham, not a coerced arrangement, not one contracted for a hidden purpose.

*Dhālika **yū'aẓu bihi** man kāna minkum **yu'minu bi-Allāhi wa al-yawmi al-ākhir*** — "that is what is **admonished** with it: whoever among you believes in God and **the Last Day**."

**Note what the appeal rests on.** Not on the law, and not on the woman's rights — though both are real. It rests on **faith in God and the Last Day.**

**And that is a sharp piece of psychology.** *'Aḍl* — obstruction of this kind — is driven by pride and resentment. Ma'qil was not acting from principle; he was acting from "I honoured you and you did this to me." **Nothing outweighs a wounded ego except the Hereafter.** A man who does not believe he will stand before God has no reason to give up the satisfaction of saying no. The verse therefore addresses the only motive strong enough to move him.

*Dhālikum **azkā lakum wa aṭhar*** — "that is **purer** for you and **more purifying**."

Two purity words in a row: *azkā*, from *zakā* — to grow, to be pure, to flourish; and *aṭhar*, from *ṭuhūr* — purity. **And notice who is purified: *lakum*, "for you" — the guardian.** The verse is telling Ma'qil that his resentment is the impurity, and that letting go is what cleanses him. **Obstruction was making him dirty, not making her safe.**

*Wa Allāhu ya'lamu wa antum lā ta'lamūn* — "and God knows, and you do not know."

The identical closing as 2:216 — the verse about fighting, where the same asymmetry was the ground of obedience. Here it is gentler and more intimate: **He knows what they need. You do not.** Which is exactly what the narration says God knew.

**The contemporary application is immediate and extremely common.** The family that will not allow a daughter to return to the man she divorced twice and still loves. The brothers who treat the divorce as an insult to the family honour rather than as an event in their sister's life. The guardians who convert a woman's consent into their own. **2:232 is addressed to them by name, and its ruling is that their veto is not theirs to exercise once the couple agree honourably.**

And there is a second, quieter application for the woman in that position: **the verse exists.** Her case is in the Qur'an. A Companion of the Prophet ﷺ stood where her family is standing, and God sent a verse down about it, and the Companion said "I hear and obey."

## Verse 233

**"˹Divorced˺ mothers will breastfeed their offspring for two whole years, for those who wish to complete the nursing ˹of their child˺. The child’s father will provide reasonable maintenance and clothing for the mother ˹during that period˺. No one will be charged with more than they can bear. No mother or father should be made to suffer for their child. The ˹father’s˺ heirs are under the same obligation. But if both sides decide—after mutual consultation and consent—to wean a child, then there is no blame on them. And if you decide to have your children nursed by a wet-nurse, it is permissible as long as you pay fairly. Be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah is All-Seeing of what you do."**

In simple terms: and mothers shall breastfeed their children two complete years, for whoever wishes to complete the nursing. And upon the one to whom the child is born is their provision and their clothing, according to what is recognised as good. No soul is charged except to its capacity. No mother shall be harmed through her child, and no father through his child. And upon the heir is the like of that. Then if the two desire weaning, by mutual consent between them and consultation, there is no blame upon them. And if you wish to seek nursing for your children, there is no blame upon you, when you hand over what you have given according to what is recognised as good. And fear God, and know that God is Seeing of what you do.

*Wa al-wālidātu yurḍi'na awlādahunna ḥawlayni kāmilayni li-man arāda an yutimma al-raḍā'ah, wa 'alā al-mawlūdi lahu rizquhunna wa kiswatuhunna bi-al-ma'rūf, lā tukallafu nafsun illā wus'ahā, lā tuḍārra wālidatun bi-waladihā wa lā mawlūdun lahu bi-waladih, wa 'alā al-wārithi mithlu dhālika, fa-in arādā fiṣālan 'an tarāḍin minhumā wa tashāwurin fa-lā junāḥa 'alayhimā, wa in aradtum an tastarḍi'ū awlādakum fa-lā junāḥa 'alaykum idhā sallamtum mā ātaytum bi-al-ma'rūf, wa-ittaqū Allāha wa-'lamū anna Allāha bi-mā ta'malūna baṣīr.*

*Wa al-wālidātu yurḍi'na awlādahunna **ḥawlayni kāmilayni*** — "and mothers shall breastfeed their children **two complete years**."

**The word *kāmilayn* — "complete" — is doing deliberate work.** The commentators note that in pre-Islamic Arabian usage, saying someone suckled a child "two years" could mean a year and part of another, since a fraction was counted as a whole. The Qur'an adds *complete* to close that: **two full years, not two years by the loose reckoning.**

**And the two-year figure is cross-referenced twice elsewhere in the Qur'an, which produces one of the most striking instances of the text explaining itself.**

At 31:14: "and his weaning is in **two years**."

At 46:15: "and his carrying and his weaning is **thirty months**."

**Thirty months minus twenty-four months leaves six.** And the classical jurists derived from exactly this pair a **minimum gestational period of six months**, which they then used to decide cases of legitimacy — a child born six months or more after a marriage is attributed to it; less, and it is not. That derivation is standard in the books of *fiqh* and it is a genuinely remarkable piece of juristic arithmetic worked from two verses in two different surahs.

**It should be reported as what it is: a juristic inference from the texts, not a biological claim made by the texts.** Modern obstetrics does not recognise six months as a viable gestational period in the ordinary sense. The classical ruling was a legal construction for establishing lineage, and it served the law's purpose of protecting the child's attribution. **A reader should be able to see the derivation, admire it, and know what kind of thing it is.**

*Li-man arāda an yutimma al-raḍā'ah* — "for whoever wishes to **complete** the nursing."

**Note the conditional: for whoever wishes.** The two years are the period of the *complete* course, not a mandatory minimum. A couple who wean earlier by mutual agreement incur no blame — the verse goes on to say so explicitly. So the two years state the full measure, and the permission to do less is built in.

*Wa 'alā **al-mawlūdi lahu** rizquhunna wa kiswatuhunna bi-al-ma'rūf* — "and upon **the one to whom the child is born** is their provision and their clothing, according to what is recognised as good."

**This phrase is worth stopping on, because the Arabic does not say "the father."**

It says *al-mawlūdu lahu* — literally, "**the one to whom** the child is born." A periphrasis where "the father" (*al-ab*, *al-wālid*) was available. The commentators note the construction and its effect: **the sentence is built around the child.** The child is born *to* someone, and that someone carries the cost.

**And note what is owed: *rizquhunna wa kiswatuhunna* — provision (food) and clothing, for the nursing mother.** Not a wage for the nursing, but her maintenance. The father supports the mother while she is breastfeeding his child. The commentators discuss the relationship between this and 65:6 ("and if they breastfeed for you, give them their payment"), and the majority reconcile them: **2:233 is maintenance during the marriage; 65:6 is a wage after an irrevocable divorce.** The schools differ on whether a divorced nursing mother is owed maintenance, a wage, or maintenance-and-a-wage, and on whether she may demand a wage at all. That disagreement is real and should be consulted rather than flattened.

*Lā tukallafu nafsun illā wus'ahā* — "**no soul is charged except to its capacity**."

**This is one of the great principles of the law, and it appears here, of all places, in a verse about child support.**

Its presence means the father's obligation is **measured by his means, not by a fixed tariff.** A poor man owes what a poor man can bear. That is a mercy to him — and, read the other way, it is also a warning to the wealthy one, for whom the same sentence sets a higher figure.

**And it is worth noting the bookend:** the identical clause appears at **2:286**, the closing verse of the surah: "God does not charge a soul except to its capacity." The surah states the principle in the middle of its practical legislation and then closes with it as the governing mercy of the whole law. When we reach 2:286, this verse will be waiting for it.

*Lā **tuḍārra** wālidatun bi-waladihā wa lā mawlūdun lahu bi-waladih* — "**no mother shall be harmed through her child, and no father through his child**."

**The word is *ḍirār* — the same root as 2:231's "do not retain them harmfully."** The surah has just forbidden a husband from harming his wife through the mechanism of the law; now it forbids either parent from harming the other **through the child**.

**And this is, in practice, the most important clause in the verse.** The prohibition is symmetrical — *no mother... and no father* — and it names the specific instrument: the child.

What does it look like? The mother who refuses the father access, or threatens to, in order to win a financial point. The father who withholds maintenance, or threatens to take the child, in order to force her back. The parent who uses the child as a messenger, a spy, a bargaining chip, or a weapon of absence. **Every one of those is named in this clause, and it is named as *ḍirār* — the same category the surah has been legislating against since 2:231.**

*Wa 'alā **al-wārithi** mithlu dhālika* — "and upon the **heir** is the like of that."

The obligation does not evaporate with the father's death. The commentators differ over whose heir is meant — the child's heir (so that if the child has wealth and no father, the cost falls on the child's estate and its heir) or the father's heir. The majority take it as the child's. The practical point is not in dispute: **the child's support has a payer, and if the first payer dies the obligation passes rather than lapsing.**

*Fa-in arādā **fiṣālan 'an tarāḍin minhumā wa tashāwurin*** — "then if the two desire **weaning, by mutual consent between them and consultation**."

**Two conditions, and the second is the interesting one.** Not merely *tarāḍin* — mutual consent — but ***tashāwurin***: consultation, deliberation, talking it through together.

**Consent is not enough; they have to discuss it.** Either one can agree sullenly, or under pressure, or without having thought about it. The verse requires the actual conversation. And this is one of the earliest appearances in the Qur'an of the consultation principle applied to a decision inside a household — the same root that will govern the community's affairs at 42:38 ("and whose affair is [determined by] consultation among themselves"). **The principle that governs the polity begins in the nursery.**

*Wa in aradtum an **tastarḍi'ū** awlādakum* — "and if you wish to **seek nursing for** your children" (i.e., to engage a wet-nurse).

*Fa-lā junāḥa 'alaykum **idhā sallamtum mā ātaytum bi-al-ma'rūf*** — "there is no blame upon you **when you hand over what you have given**, according to what is recognised as good."

**Note the verb: *sallamtum* — you hand over.** Not "promise," not "agree," not "arrange." The payment must actually be **delivered**. And that is a protection for a woman in a peculiarly vulnerable position — a hired nurse, dependent, inside someone else's house, with no leverage. The permission to hire her is conditioned on her actually being paid.

**That clause should be read by every employer of domestic labour.** The condition attached to using a woman's labour is that she receives what was agreed, actually and in hand.

*Wa-ittaqū Allāha wa-'lamū anna Allāha bi-mā ta'malūna **baṣīr*** — "and fear God, and know that God is **Seeing** of what you do."

*Baṣīr* — the Seeing. And it is the right closing for a verse about what happens **inside a house**, between two people, over a child, where no court and no neighbour will ever have evidence. **The visibility that matters is God's.**

**One modern note, offered as corroboration rather than proof.** Public-health bodies across the world recommend exclusive breastfeeding for about six months and continued breastfeeding alongside other food up to two years or beyond. That is an established orientation in the field rather than a single finding, and it is worth mentioning only in this limited way: **the Qur'an's stated full course and the current recommendation are not in conflict.** The ruling does not rest on that agreement, and would not be weakened if the recommendation changed.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 234

**"As for those of you who die and leave widows behind, let them observe a waiting period of four months and ten days. When they have reached the end of this period, then you are not accountable for what they decide for themselves in a reasonable manner. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do."**

In simple terms: and those of you who die and leave wives behind — they wait by themselves for four months and ten days. Then when they have reached their term, there is no blame upon you concerning what they do with themselves, according to what is recognised as good. And God is Aware of what you do.

*Wa alladhīna yutawaffawna minkum wa yadharūna azwājan yatarabbaṣna bi-anfusihinna arba'ata ashhurin wa 'ashran, fa-idhā balaghna ajalahunna fa-lā junāḥa 'alaykum fīmā fa'alna fī anfusihinna bi-al-ma'rūf, wa Allāhu bi-mā ta'malūna khabīr.*

**The first thing to notice is the change of measure.** The divorced woman waits **three *qurū'*** — a variable, biological interval that is itself disputed (2:228). The widow waits a **fixed calendar period: four months and ten days.**

**Why the difference? The commentators' answer is structural.** In the case of divorce there is a husband who may take her back, so the period is tied to her cycle — the thing that establishes whether she is carrying *his* child and whether the marriage can be resumed. In the case of death there is no one to take her back. The only purpose left is to establish whether there is a pregnancy. **So the term is set as a definite, countable interval: four months and ten days, knowable to the day, with no need for anyone to examine her cycles.**

The ten days are generally explained as the margin added to the four months to ensure that a possible pregnancy has become manifest. Whatever the precise wisdom, the effect is a **fixed, public, verifiable term** — no dispute about it, no calculation, no dependency on anyone's word.

*Yatarabbaṣna **bi-anfusihinna*** — "they wait, holding themselves." The same reflexive construction as 2:228. **She is the subject of her own waiting.** She is not described as being kept.

*Fa-idhā balaghna ajalahunna fa-lā junāḥa 'alaykum fīmā fa'alna **fī anfusihinna bi-al-ma'rūf*** — "then when they have reached their term, there is no blame upon you concerning what they do **with themselves**, according to what is recognised as good."

**Note the address and the object.** The "you" here is the community and the family; the object is *what she does with herself*. The verse is telling the people around her: **when the term ends, she decides, and you have no complaint.** This is the same protection as 2:232, stated even more broadly — not just "do not block her from this man" but "do not hold her to account for how she conducts her own life after the term."

*Bi-al-ma'rūf* — the sixth appearance of the phrase in this batch, and again it is the only limit set: what she does must be in the recognised good way. That is the whole of the restriction.

*Wa Allāhu bi-mā ta'malūna khabīr* — "and God is **Aware** of what you do." *Khabīr* — the One who knows the inner reality of things, from *khubr*, intimate knowledge. Fits a verse in which the outer compliance is easy and the inner interference is what needs watching.

**The Sunnah on the widow's waiting period is emphatic and is in the two *Ṣaḥīḥ*s.** Recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Zaynab bint Abī Salamah and others: the Prophet ﷺ said, **"It is not lawful for a woman who believes in God and the Last Day to mourn for a dead person for more than three days, except for her husband — four months and ten days."**

And there is a narration that belongs to this verse and to 2:240 together, and it is the hadith of **Furay'ah bint Mālik ibn Sinān**, the sister of Abū Sa'īd al-Khudrī. Her husband went out after some of his slaves who had run away, and when he reached a place called al-Qadūm they killed him. She came to the Messenger of God ﷺ and asked whether she could return to her own family among Banū Khudrah, since her husband had left her no residence he owned and no maintenance. **He told her no** — and then, when she had left and was in her room, he called her back and said: **"Stay in your home until the term reaches its end."** And she said: so I observed my waiting period of four months and ten days in my husband's house. And 'Uthmān ibn 'Affān, during his caliphate, sent for her and asked her about it, and she told him, **and he judged the same way.**

This narration is recorded by Mālik in the *Muwaṭṭa'*, and by Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī, al-Nasā'ī and Ibn Mājah, and it is the proof the jurists use for the widow's obligation to remain in the marital home during her waiting period.

**Note what the narration shows about the Prophet's manner.** He first told her she could go — on the basis of what she had said, that there was no residence and no maintenance. Then he called her back. **He changed his answer after thinking about it.** That is a detail the tradition preserved without embarrassment, and it is worth noting: he was not delivering opinions he had not considered, and when he saw more in it he said so before she had gone anywhere.

**A note on the juristic difference.** The Schools agree on the four months and ten days as the widow's waiting period, and agree on the exception: **if she is pregnant, her term ends when she gives birth** (65:4 — "and those who are pregnant, their term is until they give birth"). They differ on the details of the mourning conduct, and on whether the obligation to remain in the house during the term is a right of the estate's or a duty upon her.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 235

**"There is no blame on you for subtly showing interest in ˹divorced or widowed˺ women or for hiding ˹the intention˺ in your hearts. Allah knows that you are considering them ˹for marriage˺. But do not make a secret commitment with them—you can only show interest in them appropriately. Do not commit to the bond of marriage until the waiting period expires. Know that Allah is aware of what is in your hearts, so beware of Him. And know that Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Forbearing."**

In simple terms: and there is no blame upon you in what you hint of a proposal to women, or what you conceal within yourselves. God knows that you will be mentioning them. But do not make a secret appointment with them, except that you say a recognised word. And do not resolve upon the knot of marriage until the prescribed term reaches its end. And know that God knows what is in your souls, so beware of Him — and know that God is Forgiving, Forbearing.

*Wa lā junāḥa 'alaykum fīmā 'arraḍtum bihi min khiṭbati al-nisā'i aw aknantum fī anfusikum, 'alima Allāhu annakum satadhkurūnahunna, wa lākin lā tuwā'idūhunna sirran illā an taqūlū qawlan ma'rūfan, wa lā ta'zimū 'uqdata al-nikāḥi ḥattā yablugha al-kitābu ajalah, wa-'lamū anna Allāha ya'lamu mā fī anfusikum fa-iḥdharūh, wa-'lamū anna Allāha ghafūrun ḥalīm.*

**This verse legislates the interim — the period in which a woman is not available and a man is already thinking about her. And it is remarkably knowing about what that is like.**

*Wa lā junāḥa 'alaykum fīmā **'arraḍtum** bihi min khiṭbati al-nisā'i* — "there is no blame upon you in what you **hint** of a proposal to women."

*'Arraḍtum* is from *ta'rīḍ* — indirect speech, allusion, saying something whose surface meaning is innocuous and whose intended meaning is clear to the hearer. Its opposite is *taṣrīḥ*, explicit statement. **The verse permits the first and forbids the second** — for now.

The commentators give examples of the permitted allusion: a man says to the widow in her waiting period, "I am interested in marrying," or "I hope God provides you with a good husband," or he praises her to her family without addressing her. Deniable, decent, and not a contract.

*Aw **aknantum fī anfusikum*** — "or what you **conceal within yourselves**."

**Note that this too is declared blameless.** Not merely the hint that escapes, but the intention deliberately held inside. The verse says: the thought is not a sin.

*'Alima Allāhu annakum **satadhkurūnahunna*** — "**God knows that you will be mentioning them**."

**That clause is a small masterpiece of realism and mercy.** The verse does not pretend the thought is absent, does not command anyone to stop thinking about her, and does not treat the interest as shameful. It says: **God knows you are going to think about her.** Of course you are. And then it regulates what you do about it.

This is the Qur'an's characteristic method, and it has appeared throughout this batch: it legislates for people as they are. It does not require a man to stop noticing a woman, or a woman to stop wanting her former husband, or anyone to pretend to an interiority they do not have. **It takes the given fact and builds a fence around it.**

*Wa lākin lā **tuwā'idūhunna sirran*** — "but do not make a **secret appointment** with them."

The meaning of *sirr* here is **disputed**, and the readings are worth having:

**The first and dominant reading** — the one the majority follow — is a **secret commitment of marriage**: a clandestine contract or promise made before the waiting period has ended, which would have the effect of an engagement without the form of one and would bind her while she is still formally bound.

**The second reading**, reported from Ibn 'Abbās and others, is that it means **secret sexual relations** — fornication carried on under cover of an understanding.

**A third** reads it as a secret agreement not to marry anyone else.

The majority position is the first, and the supplied translation follows it. **But the second reading explains why the verse's vocabulary is so loaded** — *sirr*, *aknantum fī anfusikum*, *mā fī anfusikum* — the whole verse is about the traffic between what is hidden and what is done.

*Illā an taqūlū **qawlan ma'rūfan*** — "except that you say a **recognised word**."

The exception: the decent, allusive remark. Not a promise, not a contract, not a secret — a word that a community would recognise as proper.

*Wa lā ta'zimū **'uqdata al-nikāḥi*** — "and do not resolve upon the **knot of marriage**."

**The Arabic for marriage here is a *knot*.** *'Uqdah* — a tying, from the same root as *'aqd*, contract. Marriage in this verse is not an abstraction; it is a thing tied. And the verb is *ta'zimū* — do not **resolve**, do not tie it firmly. The two verbs of the passage run in sequence: **you may hint; you may not resolve.**

*Ḥattā yablugha **al-kitābu** ajalah* — "until the **prescribed term** reaches its end."

**A note on the Arabic:** *al-kitāb* here does not mean the scripture. It is used in the sense of an **ordained term** — the decreed period. The commentators note this usage, and it appears elsewhere in the Qur'an. The appointed writing has to run out.

*Wa-'lamū anna Allāha ya'lamu mā fī anfusikum **fa-iḥdharūh*** — "and know that God knows what is in your souls, **so beware of Him**."

*Wa-'lamū anna Allāha **ghafūrun ḥalīm*** — "and know that God is **Forgiving, Forbearing**."

**And this is the sequence I most want a reader to hold.** *He knows what is in your souls — so beware of Him. And He is Forgiving, Forbearing.*

Threat and pardon, in one breath, in that order. **The warning is real, and then the reassurance is given in the same sentence.** This is the same pairing as 2:225, and it is the same pastoral instinct: a verse that has just told you that your hidden thoughts are fully known does not leave you there. It closes with *Ḥalīm* — the One who does not seize.

**For anyone who has felt guilty for finding themselves thinking about someone newly widowed or divorced, this verse is the answer.** The thought is acknowledged, permitted, and not held against you. What is regulated is the act. He knows — and He is Forbearing.

**The contemporary forms are obvious enough.** The secret engagement contracted during the waiting period. The "we're just talking" arrangement that everyone knows is an agreement. The families who settle everything before the term is out and treat the waiting period as a formality. The verse says: you may speak; you may not bind. **Speak, and wait.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 236

**"There is no blame if you divorce women before the marriage is consummated or the dowry is settled. But give them a ˹suitable˺ compensation—the rich according to his means and the poor according to his. A reasonable compensation is an obligation on the good-doers."**

In simple terms: there is no blame upon you if you divorce women while you have not touched them nor specified for them an obligation. But provide for them — upon the wealthy according to his measure, and upon the straitened according to his measure — a provision according to what is recognised as good: an obligation upon the doers of good.

*Lā junāḥa 'alaykum in ṭallaqtumu al-nisā'a mā lam tamassūhunna aw tafriḍū lahunna farīḍatan, wa matti'ūhunna 'alā al-mūsi'i qadaruhu wa 'alā al-muqtiri qadaruhu, matā'an bi-al-ma'rūfi ḥaqqan 'alā al-muḥsinīn.*

**The ruling is short and its reasoning is worth spelling out, because the modern reader often misses why a verse like this is needed at all.**

*Mā lam tamassūhunna aw tafriḍū lahunna farīḍatan* — "while you have not **touched** them nor **specified** for them an obligation."

Two conditions: no consummation, and no bridal gift fixed. On the face of it, nothing has happened and nothing is owed. **And that is exactly the conclusion the verse forecloses.**

*Wa **matti'ūhunna*** — "but **provide for them**."

*Mattū* / *tamti'* — to give a *mut'ah*, a provision, a parting gift. The verb is imperative. **Something is owed, and the verse says so before anyone can argue otherwise.**

**Why? Because something *has* happened.** The woman was proposed to, contracted, and released. She was, in the community's eyes, a bride. She returns to the marriage market in a changed position — no longer untouched in the social sense, having been spoken for and then not. **The verse does not let a man walk away with nothing on the ground that "nothing happened."**

That is a serious piece of social legislation in one clause, and it is aimed at a specific and very old pattern: the man who contracts, delays, and discards, having incurred nothing.

*'Alā **al-mūsi'i qadaruhu** wa 'alā **al-muqtiri qadaruhu*** — "upon the **wealthy according to his measure**, and upon the **straitened according to his measure**."

**This is *lā tukallafu nafsun illā wus'ahā* (2:233) applied precisely.** The gift is scaled to the man's means, not to a tariff.

And note what the scaling protects in **both** directions. It protects the poor man from being required to give what he cannot — *'alā al-muqtiri qadaruhu*, upon him is *his* measure. And it protects the woman from the wealthy man giving a token — *'alā al-mūsi'i qadaruhu*, upon him is *his* measure, which is more. **The scale is the same; the amount is not.**

*Matā'an bi-al-ma'rūfi **ḥaqqan 'alā al-muḥsinīn*** — "a provision according to what is recognised as good: **an obligation upon the doers of good**."

**Note the noun: *al-muḥsinīn* — the doers of *iḥsān*, excellence.** Not "upon the believers," not "upon the Muslims." *Upon the doers of good.*

**And that phrasing generated a genuine juristic disagreement about whether the *mut'ah* here is obligatory or recommended.** Some held that because it is stated as due "upon the doers of good," it is an ethical obligation of the excellent rather than an enforceable legal duty; on this reading it is strongly recommended (*mandūb / mustaḥabb*). Others held it is legally obligatory for the unconsummated divorce with no specified gift. **The disagreement should be reported as a disagreement.** And it is worth noting that the next-but-one verse in the surah, 2:241, will state a provision for divorced women as *ḥaqqan 'alā al-muttaqīn* — "an obligation upon the **God-fearing**" — a different noun, and the two are read together.

**What is not disputed is the direction of travel.** The verse closes the escape route that says "nothing was agreed, so nothing is owed." **Something is owed.**

**The contemporary shape of this.** The engagement broken off a week before the wedding, with the families counting deposits and the woman left with the social cost and no compensation. The contract signed and never acted on. The verse says: **give her something, scaled to your means, and let it be a decent amount.** Not because the law can always enforce it, but because this is what the doers of good do.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 237

**"And if you divorce them before consummating the marriage but after deciding on a dowry, pay half of the dowry, unless the wife graciously waives it or the husband graciously pays in full. Graciousness is closer to righteousness. And do not forget kindness among yourselves. Surely Allah is All-Seeing of what you do."**

In simple terms: and if you divorce them before you have touched them, and you have already specified for them an obligation, then [give] half of what you specified — unless they forgo it, or the one in whose hand is the knot of marriage forgoes. And that you forgo is nearer to *taqwā*. And do not forget grace between you. Indeed, God is Seeing of what you do.

*Wa in ṭallaqtumūhunna min qabli an tamassūhunna wa qad faraḍtum lahunna farīḍatan fa-niṣfu mā faraḍtum, illā an ya'fūna aw ya'fuwa alladhī bi-yadihi 'uqdatu al-nikāḥ, wa an ta'fū aqrabu li-al-taqwā, wa lā tansaw al-faḍla baynakum, inna Allāha bi-mā ta'malūna baṣīr.*

**The arithmetic first: if the gift was specified, half is owed on an unconsummated divorce. Then the verse does something more interesting than arithmetic.**

*Illā an **ya'fūna*** — "unless **they** [the women] **forgo** it."

***'Afw*** — the same root as 2:219's *al-'afw*, "the surplus." Here it means waiving a right one has, generously. The woman may say: keep it.

*Aw ya'fuwa alladhī bi-yadihi **'uqdatu al-nikāḥ*** — "or the one in whose hand is the **knot of marriage** forgoes."

**Who is that?** This is a genuine disagreement and it is worth stating.

**The majority reading**, reported from 'Alī and Ibn 'Abbās in one narration and followed by the majority of the jurists and by al-Ṭabarī, is that it is **the husband** — the one who holds the bond and can either release it or pay it in full. On this reading the husband may waive his half-exemption and pay the full amount. The supplied translation follows this reading.

**The second reading**, reported from some early authorities, is that it is **the guardian** (*walī*), on the ground that he is the one who ties the knot for the woman.

The dominant position is the husband, and the practical effect is that either party may move the settlement upward: **she may give up her half, or he may give up his half-exemption.**

*Wa an ta'fū **aqrabu li-al-taqwā*** — "and that you forgo is **nearer to *taqwā***."

**This phrasing is precise and it deserves attention.** The verse does not say "forgoing *is* *taqwā*." It says it is ***nearer to*** *taqwā* — *aqrabu*.

**Which means two things at once.** It praises the waiver — it is the closer path, the better course. And it refuses to condemn the person who takes his or her strict entitlement. **The half is lawful. Insisting on it is not sin. But the waiver is nearer to the mark.**

That is a sophisticated moral distinction, and it is the sort of thing that gets flattened when people read scripture looking for either permissions or prohibitions. The verse is doing neither. **It is ranking.** The same structure appeared at 2:184, where fasting was better for you *if you knew* — good, and something better.

**And note: the verse ends the arithmetic and then says there is something past the arithmetic.**

*Wa lā tansaw **al-faḍla** baynakum* — "and do not forget **grace** between you."

***Faḍl*** — bounty, graciousness, the thing given beyond what is owed. **Not justice. Grace.**

**This is, I think, the most beautiful sentence in the batch, and it is placed at the end of a verse about how to divide a settlement when a marriage has collapsed before it began.** Having settled exactly who owes what to whom — half, or the waiver, or the full amount — the verse says: **and now do not forget to do each other favours.**

Do not let the settlement be the end of the relationship. Do not reduce the whole thing to a balance sheet. Send the thing that is not owed. Ask after her family. Speak well of her. Return the book she lent you with something else. **Grace, not justice, is the last word the verse leaves you with** — and the last word between two people who did not manage to be married to each other.

*Inna Allāha bi-mā ta'malūna **baṣīr*** — "indeed, God is **Seeing** of what you do."

The same closing as 2:233. **He sees the grace too.** Which is the point: the favours that are not owed, and that no court could enforce, are the ones this verse is asking for — and the only witness to them is the One who sees.

**The contemporary application.** The broken engagement with a large mahr or an expensive ring. The argument about who keeps what. The two families who have stopped speaking. The verse says: settle what is owed — half of what was agreed — and then **do not forget grace between you.** And it names the grace as the thing closest to *taqwā*, which means the way you conduct the end of this is a religious matter, not merely a social one.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 238

**"Observe the ˹five obligatory˺ prayers—especially the middle prayer—and stand in true devotion to Allah."**

In simple terms: guard the prayers, and the middle prayer — and stand before God, devoutly obedient.

*Ḥāfiẓū 'alā al-ṣalawāti wa al-ṣalāti al-wusṭā, wa qūmū lillāhi qānitīn.*

**This is the shortest verse in the batch and one of the most consequential in the surah. And its placement is the first thing to address.**

**Verses 231 through 237 are all about divorce. Verse 238 says: guard the prayers. Verses 239 continues the prayer of fear. Verse 240 returns to widows.**

The interruption is deliberate, and the commentators have always remarked on it. Several readings, and they are complementary rather than competing:

**The first is pastoral.** Divorce is the crisis in which a person most reliably loses his religion. The prayer is the first thing to go when everything else is coming apart — and the surah places the reminder at exactly the point of collapse. **It will not let you lose your *ṣalāh* while you are losing your marriage.**

**The second is structural.** The whole passage from 2:221 has been about the most absorbing human concern there is — whom you marry, what happens in the marriage, how it ends. A person can live inside that concern for years and forget everything else. **The verse breaks in like an alarm.**

**The third is theological.** The prayer is the thing that holds when nothing else does. In a passage about bonds being dissolved, the surah points at the one bond that is not being dissolved.

**Now the verb.**

***Ḥāfiẓū*** — "**guard**." Not *aqīmū*, "establish," which is the verb used at 2:43 and 2:110. *Muḥāfaẓah* is to watch over something that is under threat, to keep it from being lost. **The verb choice itself tells you the prayer is at risk.** You do not have to guard something that is safe.

*Wa al-ṣalāti **al-wusṭā*** — "and the **middle** prayer."

**And now the famous question: which prayer is it? The disagreement is real, it is old, and it is worth laying out honestly.**

**The majority position is that it is *'Aṣr*.** The evidence is the hadith of the Battle of the Trench, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from 'Alī ibn Abī Ṭālib: on the day of al-Aḥzāb, with the confederates besieging Madinah, the Messenger of God ﷺ said: **"They have diverted us from the middle prayer until the sun set. May God fill their graves and their houses with fire"** — and in the wording of Muslim: **"the middle prayer, that is, the 'Aṣr prayer"** — and he then prayed it between Maghrib and 'Ishā'.

The same is reported from Ibn Mas'ūd and al-Barā' ibn 'Āzib in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*. And Samurah ibn Jundub reported that the Prophet ﷺ said: **"The middle prayer is the 'Aṣr prayer"** — recorded by Aḥmad and al-Tirmidhī, who graded it *ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ*.

Al-Tirmidhī and al-Baghawī state that this is the position of the majority of the Companions and those after them. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr attributes it to the majority of the scholars of *athar*. Al-Dumyāṭī lists it as the view of 'Umar, 'Alī, Ibn Mas'ūd, Abū Ayyūb, 'Abdullāh ibn 'Amr, Samurah ibn Jundub, Abū Hurayrah, Abū Sa'īd, Ḥafṣah, Umm Ḥabībah, Umm Salamah, Ibn 'Abbās and 'Ā'ishah. Among the later authorities: al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, Ibrāhīm al-Nakha'ī, Qatādah, al-Ḍaḥḥāk, al-Kalbī, Muqātil, Abū Ḥanīfah, Aḥmad, Abū Dāwūd and Ibn Mundhir.

**But there are other positions, sincerely held and reported from major figures:**
- **Ẓuhr** — reported from Zayd ibn Thābit, Usāmah ibn Zayd, Abū Sa'īd al-Khudrī and 'Ā'ishah.
- **Subḥ (Fajr)** — reported from 'Alī and Ibn 'Abbās in the *Muwaṭṭa'*, and this was Mālik's own preference: "Of all I have heard on the matter, I prefer what 'Alī and Ibn 'Abbās said."
- **Maghrib**, **'Ishā'**, **Jumu'ah**, the **'Īd** prayer, and even the view that it is one of the five unidentified.

**The disagreement should be reported as a disagreement.** What can be said without controversy: the weight of the transmitted evidence points to 'Aṣr, the majority of the tradition has held 'Aṣr, and — this is the practical upshot — **the disagreement is never a reason to neglect any of them.** A person who is unsure which is "the middle prayer" has one safe course: guard all five. That is what the verse commands in its first clause anyway.

**A note that must be given because it is a well-known objection.** There is a narration in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* from al-Barā' ibn 'Āzib to the effect that the verse was at one time recited with "and the 'Aṣr prayer" in place of "and the middle prayer." **The established position of the tradition is that this was an explanatory gloss — a reciter clarifying the meaning while reciting — and not part of the revealed 'Uthmānic text.** The same phenomenon is recorded elsewhere in the hadith literature, where a Companion's explanatory words were transmitted alongside the verse by a narrator and later mistaken for the text. It does not indicate a change in the Qur'an, and the reading is not part of any canonical *qirā'ah*. The point should be stated plainly because the narration is used, routinely and dishonestly, to argue otherwise.

**Two further hadith that belong to this verse.** In *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī*, from Ibn 'Umar: **"Whoever misses the 'Aṣr prayer, it is as though his family and his property had been taken from him."** That is among the severest warnings attached to a single prayer anywhere in the hadith literature. And in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*: **"That is the prayer of the hypocrite: he sits watching the sun until it is between the horns of the Devil, then he rises and pecks four [units], remembering God in them only a little."**

*Wa qūmū lillāhi **qānitīn*** — "and stand before God, **devoutly obedient**."

**The meaning of *qunūt* is itself disputed, and the dispute is instructive.**

The word covers **obedience, humble submission, silence, and supplication** — and the commentators take it variously. The reading that the Sunnah supports most directly is **silence and stillness**: recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Zayd ibn Arqam, who said: "We used to speak during the prayer — a man would speak to his brother at his side about his need — until the verse 'and stand before God devoutly obedient' was revealed, and **we were commanded to be silent and forbidden to speak.**"

**So the verse's second half legislates the interior of the prayer.** The first half guards the *times*; the second half guards the *manner*. And note the pairing with 2:239's closing — *as He taught you* — which says the manner is not yours to invent.

**For practice: the two clauses are the whole diagnosis of a bad prayer.** Missing it (*ḥāfiẓū*) and being absent in it (*qānitīn*). Most people have one problem or the other. The verse treats both.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 239

**"If you are in danger, pray on foot or while riding. But when you are safe, ˹take time to˺ remember Allah for teaching you what you did not know."**

In simple terms: and if you fear, then [pray] on foot or mounted. Then when you are secure, remember God as He taught you what you did not know.

*Fa-in khiftum fa-rijālan aw rukbānā, fa-idhā amintum fa-dhkurū Allāha kamā 'allamakum mā lam takūnū ta'lamūn.*

*Fa-in khiftum **fa-rijālan aw rukbānā*** — "and if you fear, then **on foot or mounted**."

*Rijālan* is the plural of *rājil*, one going on foot. *Rukbānā* is a plural of *rākib*, mounted — on a horse, a camel, and by extension any conveyance.

**The ruling is the concession of *ṣalāt al-khawf*, the prayer of fear.** In a state of genuine danger, the prayer is performed walking or riding, **in whatever direction the person or the mount is facing** — the orientation to the *qiblah* is waived, the stillness is waived, and the form is reduced to what can be done. The bowing and prostration become gestures where even those cannot be performed fully.

**And the logic of the concession is worth stating: the prayer is not suspended.** It is not "pray later when things are calm." **It is performed, in the condition available.** The obligation survives the emergency; only its form bends. That is the opposite of the conclusion most people reach on a hard day.

**A juristic note that should be reported as a difference.** The schools differ on the detailed forms of *ṣalāt al-khawf* — how it is conducted in congregation, whether the ranks split and alternate, and how many forms are transmitted. And there is a genuine disagreement about **whether the concession remains after the Prophet's time**: the majority hold that it remains as long as the condition of fear exists, since the verse is unqualified; a minority held it was tied to his era. The majority position is the one acted upon.

*Fa-idhā **amintum*** — "then when you are **secure**."

The contrast is exact: *khiftum* (you fear) / *amintum* (you are secure). **The concession lasts exactly as long as the condition, and not a moment longer.** That is the rule for every dispensation in the law: it is bounded by its cause.

*Fa-dhkurū Allāha **kamā 'allamakum mā lam takūnū ta'lamūn*** — "then remember God **as He taught you what you did not know**."

**Three things are packed into this clause, and all three matter.**

**The first: *dhikr* is named as the thing to return to, not the prayer-form.** When security returns, what is asked for is remembrance — the inward reality of which the prayer is the outward shape. The concession relaxed the shape; it never relaxed the remembrance.

**The second: *kamā 'allamakum* — "**as** He taught you."** The adverb governs the *manner*. Remember God **in the manner He taught you**, not in a manner of your own devising. This is the principle of *tawqīf* — worship is defined by what has been taught, not by what seems fitting — the same principle I noted at 2:198 (*kamā hadākum*) and at 2:151 (*He teaches you what you did not know*). **It is stated here precisely at the point where the form has been most relaxed**, which is when a person is most tempted to improvise: you may bow with your eyes, but you may not invent a prayer.

**And the third is the reminder inside the reminder: *mā lam takūnū ta'lamūn* — "what you did not know."** Everything you know about how to approach God, you were taught. **That is a check on the person who would modify the worship, and it is also a comfort in the emergency** — the God you are turning to in the frightening moment is the God who taught you when you knew nothing.

**The contemporary applications are numerous and the verse is meant for all of them, not only for battle.** The person who cannot stand. The surgery, the hospital bed, the flight, the shift, the journey, the house in which it is not safe to be seen praying. The mother with a newborn. The soldier. The refugee on the move. In every one of these the ruling is the same: **pray in the condition available; do not wait for the condition to improve; and when it improves, return to the form you were taught.**

And the verse's closing is a gentle word to anyone who has had to pray badly for a season: *He taught you what you did not know.* The one who taught you once is not confused by the version you managed to offer.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 240

**"Those of you who die leaving widows should bequeath for them a year’s maintenance without forcing them out. But if they choose to leave, you are not accountable for what they reasonably decide for themselves. And Allah is Almighty, All-Wise."**

In simple terms: and those of you who die and leave wives behind — a bequest for their wives: provision for a year without turning them out. Then if they leave, there is no blame upon you concerning what they do with themselves, according to what is recognised as good. And God is Exalted in Might, Wise.

*Wa alladhīna yutawaffawna minkum wa yadharūna azwājan waṣiyyatan li-azwājihim matā'an ilā al-ḥawli ghayra ikhrāj, fa-in kharajna fa-lā junāḥa 'alaykum fī mā fa'alna fī anfusihinna min ma'rūf, wa Allāhu 'azīzun ḥakīm.*

**This verse is the classic case study in the discipline of *naskh* — abrogation — and it is worth handling carefully, because it is also the verse that best answers the most common objection people raise about abrogation.**

**The ruling in the text is clear: a widow is to be provided for, out of her husband's estate, for a full year, and she is not to be turned out of the house during that time.** If she chooses to leave of her own accord, no blame attaches to anyone.

**And the overwhelming majority of the commentators and jurists hold that this ruling was abrogated — by two different verses, for two different parts of it.**

**The waiting period of a year** was abrogated by 2:234's **four months and ten days**. This is reported from Mujāhid, al-Ḥasan, 'Ikrimah, Qatādah, al-Ḍaḥḥāk, al-Rabī' and Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān, and al-Bukhārī records it from Mujāhid. Ibn 'Abbās, in the narration transmitted through 'Alī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah, said: "When a man died and left a widow, she used to remain in his house for a year for her waiting period, receiving her provision throughout. Then God revealed the verse of four months and ten days."

**The maintenance and residence** were abrogated by the **verses of inheritance (4:12)**, which give the widow a fixed share — a quarter if there is no child, an eighth if there is — so that she no longer depends on a bequest. This is reported from Ibn 'Abbās in the narration recorded by Ibn Abī Ḥātim, and from 'Aṭā'.

**'Aṭā' quoted Ibn 'Abbās** to the effect that the verse abrogated the requirement that she spend her waiting period with her husband's family, so that she now spends it wherever she wishes. **And al-Shāfi'ī's position — the main dissent worth knowing — is that the right to *residence* during the waiting period remains**, even though the maintenance does not.

**And there is a narration that supplies the counter-evidence on residence:** the hadith of Furay'ah bint Mālik, discussed at 2:234, in which the Prophet ﷺ told her **"Stay in your home until the term reaches its end"** — recorded by Mālik, Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī, al-Nasā'ī and Ibn Mājah, and followed by 'Uthmān's own judgement. So the question of the widow's residence during the 'iddah is genuinely disputed, with the hadith on one side and the abrogation reading on the other.

**Now the objection, and it is a good one, and it was raised by a Companion to a Caliph.**

**Al-Bukhārī records that Ibn al-Zubayr said to 'Uthmān ibn 'Affān:** this verse — *those of you who die and leave wives behind* — has been abrogated by the other verse (2:234). **So why do you write it in the Qur'an?** (Meaning: why is it still in the Book, and still recited, if its ruling has been lifted?)

And 'Uthmān replied: **"O my nephew, I will not shift anything of it from its place."**

**That exchange is one of the most important things in the whole science of *'ulūm al-Qur'ān*, and it answers the objection completely.**

**A verse whose ruling has been abrogated remains in the Qur'an, remains recited, and remains the word of God — and it remains there by the deliberate decision of the Caliph who supervised the copying of the codex, when a Companion of the Prophet put the question to him directly.** The reason is that the ordering of the Qur'an is by revelation, not by chronology or by continuing legal effect. 2:240 stands where God's command placed it. **Its ruling was lifted; its recitation was not. And the verse is still recited in every prayer and in every Ramadan, fourteen centuries later, by people most of whom do not know that its ruling was superseded.**

**Which raises the question people actually ask: what is the use of an abrogated verse?**

Several answers, and all of them are real.

**The first: it tells you what the law was,** and therefore how the law developed — which is essential for anyone doing *fiqh* rather than reading a rulebook.

**The second: it shows the mercy of the transition.** A year's maintenance and residence, followed by a fixed inherited share. The widow's protection was not withdrawn; it was **converted from a bequest that depended on the estate's goodwill into a fixed share that could not be willed away.** Reading the two verses together shows the direction of travel, and the direction is toward more secure provision, not less.

**The third, and the deepest: it is still recited, and recitation is worship.** The Muslim who recites 2:240 in his night prayer is rewarded for the recitation whether or not he knows the jurisprudence. The Qur'an is not only a law code; it is a *dhikr*.

*Wa Allāhu **'azīzun ḥakīm*** — "and God is **Exalted in Might, Wise**."

The same closing as 2:228 and 2:220, and it carries the same weight. *'Azīz* — nothing compels Him, so the abrogation is not a revision forced by circumstance. *Ḥakīm* — **Wise**, which is precisely the attribute that makes abrogation intelligible: the One who legislates across time knows what suits each stage of it, and changing a ruling is not changing His mind.

**A final practical note.** The schools are agreed that a bequest to an heir is not enforceable without the consent of the other heirs — the Prophet ﷺ said, in the narration recorded by Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī and Ibn Mājah, **"No bequest to an heir"** — and this is one more indication that 2:240's bequest belongs to an earlier stage of the legislation, before the inheritance shares were fixed. The widow's share is now fixed by 4:12, and it does not depend on anyone's will.

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis: Verses 231–240

**The arc.** The batch closes the last two loopholes in the law of divorce — the husband who takes her back to harm her (231) and the family that blocks her return (232) — then legislates what happens to the children and the money (233, 236, 237), to the widow (234, 240), and to the interval in which she is waiting and he is thinking (235). And then, in the middle of it, **the prayer (238), and the prayer under threat (239).**

**The governing theme is *ḍirār* — harm.** 2:231 forbids retaining a wife "harmfully, in order to transgress." 2:233 forbids either parent being "harmed through their child." The same root, the same prohibition, six verses apart. **The whole passage is a campaign against using the law's own mechanisms as instruments of injury** — the right of return used as a weapon, the child used as leverage. And in both places the verse names the *intention*: *li-ta'tadū*, "in order to transgress." What is forbidden is not the unfortunate outcome. It is the aim.

**The second governing feature is *bi-al-ma'rūf*, which appears in every legislative verse of the batch** — 231, 232, 233, 234, 236, 240. Six times. **The Qur'an is not giving a tariff.** It gives a principle and leaves the quantum to means, to circumstance, and to what a community recognises as decent. That is why the tradition developed the role of *'urf* (custom) in *fiqh*: the text itself refers the measure to it. A reader looking for numbers in this passage will not find them, and that absence is the ruling.

**Three things in this batch are answered by the Sunnah in ways that change the reading.**

The first: **2:232's ruling is a story.** Ma'qil ibn Yasār swore his sister would never go back to the man she wanted, and when the verse came he broke the oath and expiated it — which is 2:224 enacted, seven verses after it was legislated. And the narration's own explanation of why the verse came is: **"God knew his need for his wife and her need for her husband."**

The second: **2:238's "middle prayer" is identified by a battle.** The Trench hadith tells us both which prayer it is and why it is the one most needing to be guarded — because it is the one that actually got lost.

The third: **2:240 is the abrogation case study, and the objection to it was raised and answered in the first generation.** Ibn al-Zubayr asked 'Uthmān why an abrogated verse was still in the Book, and 'Uthmān said: *I will not shift anything of it from its place.* **A verse whose ruling has been superseded is still the word of God, still recited, still rewarded — and it remains in place by the decision of the Caliph who was asked the question directly.**

**And the placement of 238–239 deserves to be named as the batch's most pastorally intelligent feature.** The surah has spent fourteen verses on the most absorbing human crisis there is. It stops, without transition, and says: guard the prayers. Then: and if you cannot do them properly, do them walking, do them riding, do them however you can — and when the danger passes, remember God as He taught you. **It is a verse written for a person whose life is falling apart and whose religion is the first casualty.**

**Where the supplied translation resolves an ambiguity, it has been marked.** "The one in whose hand is the knot of marriage" (237) is rendered as the husband, which is the majority reading but not the only one, and the alternative has been given. *Al-kitāb* at 235 is an ordained term, not the scripture. And the identification of the middle prayer as *'Aṣr*, which the supplied translation does not make, has been supplied from the hadith with the full range of disagreement attached.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given. This is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and supplying precise references from memory would mean inventing them. Where the commentators and jurists differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved: which prayer is the middle prayer, with the full range of transmitted views; the meaning of *sirr* in verse 235; who holds "the knot of marriage" in verse 237; whether the *mut'ah* of verse 236 is obligatory or recommended; whether the nursing mother is owed maintenance, a wage, or both; whether the widow's residence during the waiting period survives the abrogation of verse 240; and whether *ṣalāt al-khawf* remains after the Prophet's time.

Transmitted reports have been flagged as reports throughout, including the naming of Ma'qil ibn Yasār's sister and her husband, and the parallel report concerning Jābir ibn 'Abdillāh. One narration has been addressed directly because it is used in bad faith: the report of al-Barā' ibn 'Āzib concerning an explanatory gloss recited with 2:238, which is not part of the revealed text and not part of any canonical reading.

Three boundaries have been observed. On verse 233: the classical derivation of a six-month minimum gestation from 46:15 and 31:14 has been reported as a juristic inference and not as a biological claim, with the difference from modern obstetrics acknowledged. On verse 238: the disagreement about the middle prayer has been given in full, and the practical conclusion drawn from it is that the safe course is to guard all five — not that the question is settled by assertion. And on verse 240: the abrogation has been reported as the majority position with the reasoning and the transmitted reports behind it, al-Shāfi'ī's dissent on residence has been noted, and 'Uthmān's answer to Ibn al-Zubayr has been given as the definitive response to the objection that an abrogated verse should not be in the Book.

The most important practical sentence in the batch is in verse 237, and it is three words: **do not forget grace.** The verse settles the arithmetic exactly — half of what was agreed, or the waiver — and then says that the arithmetic is not the end. *Wa lā tansaw al-faḍla baynakum.* Between two people who could not manage to be married to each other, the last thing the Qur'an asks for is the thing that was never owed.
