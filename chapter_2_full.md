## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:1

> Alif-Lãm-Mĩm.

**Commentary:**

Sūrah al-Baqarah, the longest chapter of the Qur'an and the first of the Madinan revelations in the present order of the *muṣḥaf*, opens not with a sentence but with three isolated letters: *Alif-Lām-Mīm*. These are among the *ḥurūf muqaṭṭaʿāt* — the “disconnected letters” — with which twenty-nine sūrahs of the Qur'an begin. They are not words. They do not conjugate. They do not take prefixes or suffixes. They stand, as the reciters have always recited them, as independent phonemes, each given its full measure: *alif* prolonged, *lām* prolonged, *mīm* prolonged, so that the opening of the Cow is itself a recitation of the alphabet of revelation. The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that this manner of counting is itself an act of worship. In a hadith reported by al-Tirmidhī, he said, “Whoever recites a letter from the Book of Allah will have a good deed, and the good deed is multiplied tenfold. I do not say that *Alif-Lām-Mīm* is a letter, but *alif* is a letter, *lām* is a letter, and *mīm* is a letter.” The first verse of al-Baqarah is therefore, in the Prophet’s (ﷺ) own arithmetic of reward, three letters and thirty good deeds at the threshold of the longest sūrah.

The Arabic of these signs is the Arabic of the alphabet itself. *Alif*, *lām*, and *mīm* are three of the twenty-eight letters from which every word of the Qur'an, and every word of human Arabic, is composed. The mufassirūn have long noted that the *ḥurūf muqaṭṭaʿāt* are drawn from this same closed set, and that the sūrahs which open with them almost invariably proceed, in the next breath, to speak of the Book, the revelation, or the Qur'an. Al-Baqarah is the pattern: *Alif-Lām-Mīm* — then, immediately, “This is the Book.” Sūrah Āl ʿImrān (3:1–3), Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt (29:1–2), Sūrah al-Rūm (30:1–2), Sūrah Luqmān (31:1–2), and Sūrah al-Sajdah (32:1–2) repeat the same three letters and the same movement toward the Book. Other openings vary the letters — *Alif-Lām-Rā* at Yūnus (10:1), *Alif-Lām-Mīm-Ṣād* at al-Aʿrāf (7:1), *Yā-Sīn* (36:1), *Qāf* (50:1), *Nūn* (68:1) — but the grammar of the gesture is constant: the Book announces itself by displaying the raw material of which it is made. Al-Rāzī and a number of later exegetes understood this as a challenge encoded in the very first sound of the sūrah. These are your letters, O Arabs, the letters of your poetry and your oratory; from them this Book is composed; produce the like of it if you are able. The challenge will be made explicit at 2:23; it is already sounded, wordlessly, at 2:1.

What the letters *mean* is a question the scholars have treated with a humility that is itself part of the verse’s teaching. A large and early current — associated with Abū Bakr, ʿUmar, ʿUthmān, ʿAlī, and Ibn Masʿūd (may Allah be pleased with them), and adopted as the cautious position of al-Ṭabarī — holds that the *ḥurūf muqaṭṭaʿāt* are among the *mutashābih*, the unclear, whose interpretation is known to Allah (الله) alone. “None knows its interpretation except Allah” (3:7). On this view the servant’s first lesson in the longest sūrah is not a doctrine but a posture: there are realities in the Speech of Allah (الله) that the creature does not decode, and faith includes the willingness to stand before a door that does not open. Ibn Kathīr reports this as the position of the majority of the early generations. Al-Qurṭubī, who catalogues more than twenty opinions, records among them the view that the letters are names of the sūrahs; that they are names of Allah (الله) Himself, or fragments of His names; that they are oaths by which Allah (الله) swears; that *alif* stands for Allah, *lām* for Jibrīl, and *mīm* for Muḥammad (ﷺ); and that they are a means of arresting the attention of a people who were accustomed to talk through recitation. None of these is demonstrably false; none is demonstrably the intended sense. The diversity itself is a sign. The Qur'an begins al-Baqarah by placing the believer in front of a meaning he cannot exhaust, so that the Book that follows is received as gift rather than as a puzzle the reader has already solved.

Ibn ʿAbbās, in reports preserved by al-Ṭabarī, is associated with more than one of these readings, including the parsing of the letters as an oath and as a compressed divine name. The later Ḥanbalī tradition, including Ibn Taymiyyah, tended to return to the first position: we recite what was recited, we believe that it is from Allah (الله), and we do not speak of Allah (الله) without knowledge. Ibn al-Qayyim observed that the wisdom of such openings includes the breaking of pride. A people who prided themselves on the mastery of language are met, at the gate of the Book, with language that is not even language yet — letters awaiting a Word. The grammatical schools of Baṣrah and Kūfah debated whether the letters are indeclinable nouns, whether they occupy the place of a subject, whether a suppressed verb (“recite”) stands behind them. The debates are honorable; they do not close the verse. The reciter who says *Alif-Lām-Mīm* in tarāwīḥ, in the recitation of a child learning *qirāʾah*, in the slow reading of a sick believer, is doing what the Companions did: giving each letter its due and leaving its secret with its Speaker.

Theologically the verse is a first lesson in the difference between the Creator’s knowledge and the creature’s. The Qur'an will fill hundreds of verses with law, narrative, promise, and threat; it will define *taqwā*, *īmān*, *kufr*, and *nifāq* with a precision that has occupied jurists and theologians for fourteen centuries. It chooses, before all of that, to begin with what is not explained. This is not obscurantism. It is *tawḥīd* applied to knowledge. “And you have not been given of knowledge except a little” (17:85). The servant who cannot interpret *Alif-Lām-Mīm* is the same servant who cannot interpret the Decree, the essence of the Divine, the unfolding of the unseen. The letters train him, at the outset, not to demand that every āyah yield itself to his method. In an age that treats mystery as a defect and unexplained remainder as a failure of the text, this opening is a mercy. It tells the modern reader, trained to search and to swipe until every question has a snippet-answer, that the Book of Allah (الله) is not a database. It is Speech. Speech may begin with a pause, a letter, a silence that is still revelation.

The practical fruit of the verse is therefore reverence in recitation and patience in study. The believer does not skip the disconnected letters as if they were decorative. He recites them as the Prophet (ﷺ) recited them, with their *madd*, their separation, their weight. He does not invent a private code for them, nor does he scorn those who have offered interpretations in the long conversation of the *ummah*. He holds, with the early majority, that the safest knowledge is to say “Allah knows best,” and he holds, with the challenge-reading, that these letters are also a sign of the Qur'an’s inimitability, soon to be argued in this same sūrah. Between those two holdings the heart is kept both humble and confident: humble because it does not know, confident because the Book that follows these letters is the Book in which “there is no doubt.” The letters are the veil; the next verse is the unveiling. Al-Baqarah teaches, from its first sound, that the path into the Book is not the path of the master who has already understood, but the path of the servant who begins by admitting that some of the Speech of his Lord is a secret kept for the Lord Himself.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:2

> This is the Book! There is no doubt about it—a guide for those mindful ˹of Allah˺,

**Commentary:**

The disconnected letters give way at once to a declaration: *dhālika al-kitābu lā rayba fīhi hudan lil-muttaqīn*, “This is the Book! There is no doubt about it — a guide for those mindful of Allah.” The Arabic does not in fact begin with “this” (*hādhā*), the near demonstrative, but with *dhālika*, “that,” the far demonstrative. The grammarians and the mufassirūn have lingered over the choice. *Dhālika* is used of what is exalted, remote in rank, lifted above the speaker’s hand. Ibn ʿAbbās and the early authorities explained that the Book is pointed to as something high and noble, as one points to a thing whose worth is not in its nearness but in its station. Al-Zamakhsharī and those who followed his linguistic method added that *dhālika* may also look back to the promise already known: the Book that had been awaited, the Scripture whose coming the People of the Book had been told to expect, the revelation of which the Fātiḥah had just asked to be made a guide. “That” Book — the one you have heard of, the one whose letters you have just recited — is this. The far demonstrative is therefore not distance of absence but distance of majesty. The servant is not handed a familiar object; he is shown a Book whose rank is above him, and then invited to walk by its light.

*Al-kitāb*, “the Book,” is definite. The definite article here is for *ʿahd*, for a known and identified reality, and also for *kamāl*, for completeness. It is not “a book” among books. It is the Book, the Scripture that gathers what the previous scriptures gathered and more, the writing that is recited and the writing that is preserved. The root *k-t-b* denotes gathering, inscribing, joining letters into a binding. The Qur'an calls itself *kitāb* to insist that it is not scattered oratory but a composed, preserved, written speech, even when it is heard. “Indeed, it is We who sent down the Reminder, and indeed, We will be its Guardian” (15:9). The same sūrah will later say, “The month of Ramaḍān is that in which the Qur'an was revealed as a guide for humanity with clear proofs of guidance and the standard” (2:185), so that this verse’s claim is echoed and expanded: a guide, with proofs, a criterion. Sūrah al-Sajdah opens with the same letters and the same insistence: “The revelation of the Book, about which there is no doubt, is from the Lord of the worlds” (32:2). Sūrah Fuṣṣilat adds the other side of *lā rayba*: “Falsehood cannot approach it from before it or from behind it” (41:42). The Book is protected in its origin and in its transmission; doubt is not an honest remaining question but a refusal of what has already been made clear.

The word *rayb* is more than *shakk*, ordinary uncertainty. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī distinguished them: *shakk* is a hesitation between two possibilities; *rayb* is a disturbance, an inner agitation, a suspicion that unsettles the soul. *Lā rayba fīhi* therefore means not only that the Book is free of factual error, but that it contains nothing that should trouble a sound heart with the restlessness of mistrust. Al-Ṭabarī reports that the phrase may be read as a statement of fact — there is no doubt in it — and as a prohibition of the heart — let there be no doubt in it. Ibn Kathīr combines both: the Book is objectively certain, and the servant is commanded to receive it as such. The *mufassirūn* also discussed the pause. Some recited with a stop after *lā rayba*, “no doubt,” then *fīhi hudan*, “in it is guidance”; others joined *lā rayba fīhi* as a single clause, “no doubt about it,” then took *hudan* as a second predicate of *al-kitāb*. Both readings are attested and both are true: the Book is beyond suspicion, and guidance is in it.

*Hudan*, “a guide” or “guidance,” is indefinite. Indefiniteness here is for *taʿẓīm*, for greatness: it is guidance of a kind that cannot be fully specified, guidance vast enough to cover creed and law and healing of the chest. The Fātiḥah taught the servant to ask, “Guide us along the Straight Path”; this verse answers that the path has a map, and the map is this Book. Yet the guidance is not equally taken by every reader. It is *lil-muttaqīn*, “for those who are mindful,” those who have *taqwā*. The root is *w-q-y*, to protect, to place a barrier between oneself and harm. *Taqwā* is the act of shielding the soul from the displeasure of Allah (الله) by obeying Him and avoiding what He has forbidden. The Prophet (ﷺ) pointed to his chest three times and said, “Taqwā is here” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim), teaching that it is first a state of the heart, not a costume of the limbs. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb asked Ubayy ibn Kaʿb what *taqwā* was, and Ubayy asked him whether he had walked a path of thorns; ʿUmar said yes, and Ubayy said, “You gathered yourself and were careful; that is *taqwā*.” The Book is a guide, but it guides those who have already begun to care where they step.

This restriction is not an exclusion of mercy; it is a description of receptivity. Rain falls on rock and on soil; only the soil yields. “Say, ‘It is, for those who believe, a guidance and a healing. But as for those who do not believe, there is deafness in their ears, and it is upon them blindness’” (41:44). The same speech that saves one heart hardens another, not because the speech changed, but because the vessel did. Al-Qurṭubī notes that *taqwā* here includes the first movement of caution that makes a person willing to listen, so that the verse does not describe only the perfected saint but anyone who approaches the Book with the fear of being wrong before his Lord. Ibn Taymiyyah wrote that guidance has degrees, and that the *muttaqūn* are those whom Allah (الله) increases in guidance because they used what they already had: “As for those who are guided, He increases them in guidance and grants them their righteousness” (47:17). The Book meets the servant at the station of his piety and then raises him.

For the present age the verse is a judgment on two opposite errors. One error is the culture of permanent doubt, which treats suspicion as sophistication and certainty as naiveté. The Qur'an does not forbid questions; it forbids *rayb*, the restless refusal to trust what has been made clear. A generation that can fact-check a headline in seconds and still believe it has no access to truth is sick with *rayb*, not with intelligence. The other error is to treat the Book as a relic, admired, quoted, and left unused. The verse does not say “a monument for those who revere it”; it says “a guide for those who are mindful.” Guidance is for walking. The believer who recites this verse is being told that the longest sūrah of the Qur'an — with its laws of fasting, debt, marriage, fighting, and charity — is not an archive. It is a road. Those who fear Allah (الله) will find that road lit; those who do not will find in the same pages only argument and dust. The Book is one. The difference is *taqwā*.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:3

> who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and donate from what We have provided for them,

**Commentary:**

The *muttaqūn* of the previous verse are now described, and the first mark named is not a visible deed but an invisible conviction: *alladhīna yuʾminūna bil-ghayb*, “who believe in the unseen.” The verb *yuʾminūna* is from *īmān*, itself from the root *ʾ-m-n*, which yields *amn* (safety) and *amānah* (trust). To believe is to grant security to a report, to entrust oneself to it, to become safe in it. *Īmān* is therefore not a thin opinion. It is a confidence that settles in the heart, is confessed by the tongue, and is confirmed by the limbs, as the classical definition of the majority of the *salaf* has it. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “Faith has seventy-odd branches, the highest of which is the saying ‘There is no god but Allah,’ and the lowest of which is removing harm from the road, and modesty is a branch of faith” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The *muttaqūn* are those in whom this tree has taken root, and its first soil is the *ghayb*.

*Al-ghayb* is that which is hidden from the senses and from unaided reason. The root *gh-y-b* means to be absent, to be beyond presence. Al-Rāghib explained that *ghayb* is whatever is absent from the range of perception, whether it is absent in principle — such as the essence of Allah (الله), the angels, the jinn, Paradise, the Fire, the Decree — or absent in circumstance, such as what is happening now in a house one is not in. In this verse the intended sense, as Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Masʿūd, and the mass of the mufassirūn held, is the former: belief in what Allah (الله) and His Messenger have reported of the realities that the eye has not seen. Al-Ṭabarī records the interpretation “they believe in what is hidden from them of the Garden, the Fire, the resurrection, the Bridge, and the Balance,” and also “they believe in Allah, His angels, His books, His messengers, and the Last Day.” The two lists are one list. The famous hadith of Jibrīl, recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, makes the same inventory the definition of *īmān*: “to believe in Allah, His angels, His books, His messengers, the Last Day, and to believe in the Decree, its good and its evil.” This verse, at the opening of al-Baqarah, is that definition in a single phrase. Before the Qur'an teaches a single ruling of fasting or debt, it teaches that the righteous are people of the unseen.

The order is itself instruction. Belief in the unseen is named before prayer and before spending because the unseen is the root of both. A man will not stand in the dark before a Lord he does not believe is there, and he will not give away wealth he can see for a reward he cannot see unless the unseen is more real to him than the seen. “They give the food, despite their love for it, to the needy, the orphan, and the captive, saying, ‘We feed you only for the Face of Allah; we wish no reward from you nor thanks’” (76:8–9). The Face of Allah (الله) is unseen; the food is seen; they prefer the unseen. This is why the Prophet (ﷺ) said, “The excellence of the believer’s faith is that he knows Allah is with him wherever he is,” and why he praised those who would believe without having seen him: “Blessed are those who have believed in me without seeing me” (reported by Aḥmad and others). The Companions believed in a Messenger they could see and in a Lord they could not; later generations believe in both as unseen, and their *īmān* is, in that respect, the very *īmān* this verse names.

Then come the two great deeds that prove the unseen has reached the limbs: *wa yuqīmūna al-ṣalāh*, “and they establish prayer,” and *wa mimmā razaqnāhum yunfiqūn*, “and from what We have provided them they spend.” *Iqāmat al-ṣalāh* is not merely “they pray.” The verb *aqāma* means to set up, to make stand, to keep from falling. Establishing prayer is to perform it with its conditions, pillars, times, stillness, and presence of heart, not to drop it as a hurried gesture. The Qur'an never praises those who “do” prayer with a thin verb; it praises those who *establish* it. “Successful are the believers, who are humble in their prayers” (23:1–2). The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “The first of the servant’s deeds to be judged on the Day of Resurrection will be the prayer; if it is sound, the rest of his deeds will be sound, and if it is corrupt, the rest of his deeds will be corrupt” (reported by al-Ṭabarānī and others with supporting paths). He also said, “The covenant between us and them is the prayer; whoever leaves it has disbelieved” (al-Tirmidhī). Prayer is thus the daily proof that the unseen God is being served in the seen world. It is the pillar, as the hadith says: “The head of the matter is Islam, its pillar is prayer, and its peak is *jihād*” (al-Tirmidhī).

Spending, *infāq*, is from *n-f-q*, to make a thing pass out, to expend. The verse does not say “they spend their wealth”; it says “from what We have provided them.” The pronoun of the Provider is placed before the spending so that the giver never forgets he is a passer-on, not an owner in the absolute sense. *Razaqnāhum* — “We provided them” — includes wealth, and also knowledge, strength, time, and status; the *muttaqūn* spend from each as the need of the moment requires. The preposition *min* (from) indicates a portion, not the whole, which the jurists have taken as a basis for the fact that charity does not require self-destruction. “They ask you what they should spend. Say, ‘What is beyond your needs’” (2:219). Yet the praise here is not of the minimum. It is of a character that has been trained by the unseen to let go of the seen. Zakāt is the obligatory core of this spending; *ṣadaqah* is its supererogatory sky. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “Charity does not decrease wealth” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim), and “The upper hand is better than the lower hand” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī). Ibn al-Qayyim noted that prayer is the servant’s right-of-Allah in his body, and spending is the servant’s right-of-Allah in his property, and that the Fātiḥah’s “You alone we worship and You alone we ask for help” is enacted in these two: worship in the prayer, help sought and then shared in the spending.

The contemporary force of the verse is sharp. A culture of materialism treats the unseen as the unreal, prayer as private therapy, and wealth as an identity. This verse rebuilds the human being in the opposite order: first the unseen, then the body in worship, then the property in circulation. The believer who recites it is being measured. Does he believe in what he cannot photograph? Does his prayer stand, or does it collapse into a leftover minute? Does he spend as a trustee, or clutch as a claimant? Al-Baqarah will legislate all three at length — creed, *ṣalāh*, *infāq* — but it introduces them here as the portrait of the people for whom the Book is a guide. The guidance of 2:2 is not an abstraction. It looks like this: a heart at rest in the *ghayb*, a body that establishes the prayer, and a hand that opens because the Provider is remembered in the gift.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:4

> and who believe in what has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ and what was revealed before you, and have sure faith in the Hereafter.

**Commentary:**

The portrait of the *muttaqūn* continues, and the second *īmān* is now specified as to its object: *wa alladhīna yuʾminūna bimā unzila ilayka wa mā unzila min qablika*, “and who believe in what has been revealed to you and what was revealed before you,” then *wa bil-ākhirati hum yūqinūn*, “and in the Hereafter they have sure faith.” The verse is joined to the previous by *wa* (and), and the mufassirūn have asked whether this describes the same group with further qualities or introduces a second group. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr report both views. One reading, associated with Ibn ʿAbbās and others, is that verses 2:3–4 together describe the believers among this *ummah*: they believe in the unseen, pray, spend, believe in the Qur'an and in the previous scriptures, and are certain of the Hereafter. Another reading, also early, is that 2:3 describes the believers of Arabia, while 2:4 describes the sincere among the People of the Book who accepted the Prophet (ﷺ). Both readings can stand. The qualities are one religion. Whether the person came from idolatry or from a previous scripture, the *muttaqī* of this opening is the one who receives all of revelation and is certain of what is after death.

The two verbs of sending down are worth the grammarian’s pause. *Unzila ilayka*, “what was sent down to you,” uses the form of *inzāl*, which can denote sending down as a whole or as an accomplished fact, and the address is to the Prophet (ﷺ). *Mā unzila min qablika*, “what was sent down before you,” uses the same root *n-z-l*, to descend, to come from above. Revelation is always a descent. It does not rise from the earth as philosophy does; it comes down from the Lord of the worlds. The Qur'an elsewhere distinguishes *nazzala* (the intensive, for the Qur'an sent down in stages) from *anzala* (for the Torah and the Gospel sent down as wholes): “He has revealed to you the Book in truth, confirming what came before it, and He revealed the Torah and the Gospel” (3:3). Here both are gathered under *unzila* so that the believer’s duty is stated without academic distinction: whatever Allah (الله) has sent down, you believe in it. This is the universality of Islamic faith. The Muslim does not begin history at the seventh century. He believes in what was given to Nūḥ, Ibrāhīm, Mūsā, ʿĪsā, and all the prophets (peace be upon them), as this sūrah will later make the believer say: “We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the descendants, and what was given to Moses and Jesus and the prophets from their Lord; we make no distinction between any of them” (2:136). To reject a previous prophet is to reject the One who sent him. To accept the Qur'an while scorning the original Torah or Gospel as if Allah (الله) had never spoken before is also a thinning of this verse.

Yet belief in the previous scriptures, as the mufassirūn are careful to say, is belief in them as they were revealed, not as they may have been altered. “They distort words from their places” (4:46), and “a party of them used to hear the word of Allah then distort it after they had understood it, knowingly” (2:75). The believer honors Mūsā (peace be upon him) and the Book given to him; he does not submit to every later interpolation as if it were still that Book. The criterion is the Qur'an itself, “confirming what is with them” (2:41) and guarding over it. Ibn Kathīr notes that this verse was a proof against the Jews of Madinah who claimed to believe in what was sent to them while refusing what was sent to Muḥammad (ﷺ), and against any Muslim who would later treat the Qur'an as sufficient while holding a secret contempt for the prophetic chain that preceded it. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “I am the nearest of the people to Jesus son of Mary in this world and the next; there is no prophet between him and me” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī), and he said, “Do not believe the People of the Book and do not deny them, but say: we believe in Allah and what was revealed to us and what was revealed to you” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī). The middle path of this hadith is the middle path of the verse: neither gullibility toward corrupted text nor arrogance toward revealed origin.

Then the verse rises from the past of revelation to the future of return: *wa bil-ākhirati hum yūqinūn*. *Al-ākhirah* is the Last, the abode after this one, from *ʾ-kh-r*, that which comes after. *Yūqinūn* is not the same as *yuʾminūn*. *Īmān* is belief; *yaqīn* is certainty that has settled, the removal of doubt as a possibility of the heart. Al-Rāghib described *yaqīn* as knowledge that has become stable. The Qur'an uses it of those whose conviction has passed beyond argument: “And in the Hereafter they are certain.” The fronting of *bil-ākhirati* before the verb is *taqdīm* for concern and exclusivity of emphasis: as for the Hereafter, it is they who are certain of it. Many claim to believe they will die; few live as those who are sure. The *muttaqūn* are of the few. “Those who are certain that they will meet their Lord and that to Him they will return” (2:46). The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “The clever one is he who takes account of himself and works for what is after death, and the helpless one is he who follows his desires and merely hopes in Allah” (al-Tirmidhī). *Yaqīn* of the *ākhirah* is what makes the spending of the previous verse rational and the prayer of the previous verse urgent.

Al-Qurṭubī and others noted that the Hereafter is singled out because it was, and remains, the doctrine most denied. The pagans of Makkah granted Allah (الله) as Creator yet denied resurrection: “Does he promise you that when you have died and become dust and bones you will be brought forth?” (23:35). The materialist of this age grants a universe and denies a reckoning. The verse makes sure faith in the Last Day a condition of the piety for which the Book is a guide. Without the *ākhirah*, *taqwā* has no court, prayer has no witness beyond habit, and spending has no return. With it, every hour is an investment. Ibn al-Qayyim wrote that certainty of the Hereafter is a light in the heart that makes the world look like a crossing, not a home. The modern believer, surrounded by a culture that treats death as an embarrassment and the grave as the end, recites this verse as a recovery of sanity. He believes in the Book in his hands, in the books that came before it, and in the Day when both the reader and the rejecter will stand. That threefold faith — this revelation, the revelations before, the Life after — is the spine of the people whom verse 5 will call successful.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:5

> It is they who are ˹truly˺ guided by their Lord, and it is they who will be successful.

**Commentary:**

After the qualities comes the verdict: *ulāʾika ʿalā hudan min rabbihim wa ulāʾika humu al-mufliḥūn*, “It is they who are truly guided by their Lord, and it is they who will be successful.” The demonstrative *ulāʾika*, “those,” is the far demonstrative used of persons, and the rhetoricians of Arabic have said that pointing to the *muttaqūn* with a far pointer is for *tafkhīm*, for honor and elevation. They have been described in 2:2–4; now they are set at a distance of dignity, as one points to people whose rank is high. The sentence is repeated: *ulāʾika* … *wa ulāʾika*. The repetition is not filler. The first clause awards them guidance; the second awards them success. Al-Zamakhsharī noted that repeating the demonstrative makes each prize independent, so that guidance is not a mere means swallowed into success, nor success a vague word detached from guidance. They are upon guidance, and they are the successful. Two judgments, two honors, one people.

The preposition in *ʿalā hudan* is as important as the noun. They are not merely “in” guidance, as one is in a room one might leave; they are *upon* it, as one is upon a mount or upon a raised path. Ibn ʿAbbās and the early mufassirūn pictured guidance as a steed they ride, or as high ground they occupy. Al-Rāghib observed that *ʿalā* gives the sense of establishment and mastery: the guided are borne by the guidance, not burdened by it. *Hudan* is again indefinite, as in 2:2, for greatness and for a guidance whose kinds are many: guidance of knowledge, of action, of steadfastness, of being led to Paradise. And it is *min rabbihim*, “from their Lord.” The source is named so that no one ascribes the light to his own cleverness. The *Rabb* of the Fātiḥah, the Nurturer and Sustainer, is the one who placed them on this path. “Whoever Allah guides, he is the rightly guided; and whoever He leaves astray, you will never find for him a protecting guide” (18:17). The verse is therefore both praise of the servant and confession of the Lord’s gift. Ibn Taymiyyah repeatedly returned to this balance: the servant acts, and the Lord creates the act; the servant is praised for *taqwā*, and the *taqwā* is from Allah (الله).

*Al-mufliḥūn* is from *falāḥ*, a word whose root *f-l-ḥ* in the language of the Arabs means to split the earth, to till, to succeed in a way that yields harvest. The farmer is *fallāḥ* because he cuts the soil and waits for what comes. *Falāḥ* is therefore success that is not a windfall but a yield — the outcome of seed, patience, and rain. In the Qur'an's moral lexicon it is the comprehensive success of this world and the next, the attainment of what is sought and the salvation from what is feared. “Successful indeed are the believers” (23:1). “He has succeeded who purifies it, and he has failed who buries it” (91:9–10). “Remember Allah much so that you may be successful” (8:45). The definite article and the pronoun *hum* together produce *ḥaṣr*, restriction: *they* are the successful, they and not others. Al-Qurṭubī and the specialists of rhetoric have said that *ulāʾika humu al-mufliḥūn* closes the door on rival claimants. Rank, wealth, lineage, and the applause of the age are not *falāḥ*. *Falāḥ* belongs to those whose description has just been given: people of *taqwā*, of the unseen, of established prayer, of spending, of all revelation, of certainty in the Last Day.

The Prophet (ﷺ) used to recite in his *qunūt* and in his teaching words that unpack this success. He said, “O Allah, I ask You for guidance, piety, chastity, and self-sufficiency” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim), joining *hudā* and *tuqā* as this opening joins them. He said, “The world is a prison for the believer and a paradise for the disbeliever” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim), so that the *mufliḥ* may not be confused with the comfortable. He drew a line in the dust and said, “This is the path of Allah,” then lines to its sides, and recited, “This is My straight path, so follow it” (6:153), teaching that the guidance *upon* which the successful ride is a single road, not a marketplace of private truths. Ibn al-Qayyim wrote that *falāḥ* has an outer face — Paradise, deliverance from the Fire, the pleasure of Allah (الله) — and an inner face already tasted in this life: the sweetness of faith, the coolness of prayer, the rest of reliance. The verse promises both. “Whoever does good, whether male or female, while being a believer, We will surely cause him to live a good life, and We will surely give them their reward according to the best of what they used to do” (16:97).

The placement of this verse is the hinge of the sūrah’s opening. Verses 1–5 are the first panel: the Book, the pious, their qualities, their reward. Verses 6–7 will open the second panel: those who persist in disbelief. Verses 8–20 will open the third: the hypocrites. The Qur'an teaches by contrast. Before the darkness is described, the light is named, so that the reader knows what is being lost when it is lost. Al-Ṭabarī remarks that Allah (الله) began His Book by describing those who have *falāḥ* so that the servant would desire their station and then be warned against its opposites. The structure is pastoral before it is polemical.

For the reader today, “success” is a word that has been captured by markets and metrics. It means audience, income, visibility, the optimization of the self. This verse confiscates the word and returns it to its Owner. The successful are not those who have been guided by their brand but those who are upon guidance from their Lord. A person may fail every worldly examination and be of the *mufliḥūn*; a person may collect every credential of the age and be of those whom the next verses declare sealed. The believer who recites 2:5 in the prayer, having just recited the Fātiḥah’s plea for the Straight Path, is being told that the path has passengers already, that they are known by the marks of 2:3–4, and that their end is not a slogan but *falāḥ* — a harvest that death does not interrupt. To want that harvest is to want their qualities. To claim their success without their qualities is the first movement of the hypocrisy the sūrah is about to unmask.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:6

> As for those who persist in disbelief, it is the same whether you warn them or not—they will never believe.

**Commentary:**

The sūrah turns, and the turn is sharp: *inna alladhīna kafarū sawāʾun ʿalayhim a-andhartahum am lam tundhirhum lā yuʾminūn*, “As for those who persist in disbelief, it is the same whether you warn them or not — they will never believe.” After five verses of light, the Book names those for whom the light is not a guide. The particle *inna* is for confirmation, as if to still a question already forming in the Prophet’s (ﷺ) heart: why do some hear and not accept? The answer is given before the stories of Israel, before the laws, before the parables of the hypocrites. There is a kind of *kufr* that has become a settled state, and for those who have reached it the warning and the silence are equal.

*Kafarū* is from *kufr*, whose root *k-f-r* means to cover, to conceal. The farmer is called *kāfir* in old Arabic because he covers the seed with soil; the night is *kāfir* because it covers the world. In the religious lexicon the *kāfir* is the one who covers the truth after it has appeared, who buries the blessing of revelation under denial. Al-Rāghib distinguished kinds of *kufr*: denial of the Creator, denial of a prophet, denial of a revealed law, and the *kufr* of ingratitude for blessing. Here the verb is in the past, *kafarū*, “they have disbelieved,” which the mufassirūn have read as indicating a *kufr* already established, not a passing doubt. Ibn ʿAbbās, in reports cited by al-Ṭabarī, related that this verse was sent down concerning leaders of Quraysh whose enmity had hardened — men such as Abū Jahl and his circle — of whom it had been known that they would not believe. Other reports extend it to all whose *kufr* has been sealed by Allah’s (الله) knowledge and justice. Ibn Kathīr prefers the general sense without denying the particular occasion: the wording is general, the cause may be particular, and the ruling follows the wording.

The equality, *sawāʾun ʿalayhim*, “it is the same upon them,” is a hard mercy to the Messenger (ﷺ). *Indhār*, warning, is his office. “O you wrapped in the cloak, arise and warn” (74:1–2). “You are only a warner, and for every people is a guide” (13:7). Yet warning is a means, not a compulsion of hearts. When the means has been delivered and the heart has refused with persistence, the warner is told that the outcome is no longer in his hand. The same sentence appears at the opening of Sūrah Yā-Sīn: “It is the same to them whether you warn them or do not warn them; they will not believe” (36:10). The repetition across the Qur'an is a consolation and a theology. Consolation, because the Prophet (ﷺ) is spared the despair of thinking that a more eloquent warning would have saved those who have chosen deafness. Theology, because guidance is Allah’s (الله) to give. “Indeed, you do not guide whom you like, but Allah guides whom He wills” (28:56). The verse does not forbid warning. The Prophet (ﷺ) continued to warn until his last breath. It tells him that some soil will not take the seed, and that this is not his failure.

*A-andhartahum am lam tundhirhum* uses the hamzah of equalization. The two sides — you warned them, you did not warn them — are placed on a scale that does not tilt. *Lā yuʾminūn*, “they will not believe,” is a future of refusal, not a denial of Allah’s (الله) power to guide whom He wills. The mufassirūn are careful here, because the verse has been abused by those who would strip the servant of responsibility and by those who would strip the Lord of decree. Ahl al-Sunnah hold both ends. The servant chooses *kufr* and persists in it; Allah (الله) knows, decrees, and, as the next verse will say, seals. The sealing is not an injustice done to a willing heart; it is a judgment upon a heart that has already willed its closing. “I will turn away from My signs those who are arrogant upon the earth without right” (7:146). “We will turn their hearts and their eyes just as they refused to believe in it the first time, and We will leave them in their transgression wandering blindly” (6:110). The “never” of this translation is the “will not” of a state that has become nature. It is not the closing of the door of repentance upon anyone who still turns. The Qur'an elsewhere says, “Say, ‘O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of Allah; indeed Allah forgives all sins’” (39:53). The people of 2:6 are not those who have sinned; they are those who have made disbelief their settled identity.

Al-Qurṭubī discusses whether the verse includes the People of the Book who knew the Prophet’s (ﷺ) description and still denied him, and whether it includes every obstinate rejecter until the Hour. The safer reading is the general one: whoever’s disbelief has reached the point of obstinate covering, the warning’s effect on him is null. The Prophet (ﷺ) was told of his uncle Abū Ṭālib, whom he longed to save, that “you do not guide whom you love.” He was told, when he grieved over his people, “Perhaps you will destroy yourself with grief that they do not believe” (26:3). This verse belongs to that pastoral of grief. It does not tell the caller to stop calling. It tells him to stop dying of the refusal.

In the present world the verse speaks to every teacher, parent, and preacher who has mistaken outcome for office. The office is *indhār* and *tabshīr*, warning and good news. The outcome is Allah’s (الله). A culture of metrics — conversions counted, engagement measured, hearts treated as funnels — is rebuked by *sawāʾun ʿalayhim*. Some will not believe, and their unbelief does not falsify the Book, does not diminish the Messenger (ﷺ), and does not authorize despair over those who have not yet hardened. The caller does what 74:2 commanded; he leaves what 28:56 reserved. Between those two verses he remains a servant, not a savior. The next verse will explain the inner mechanism of this refusal — the seal upon the heart — so that the reader understands that the equality of warning and silence is not because the warning is weak, but because a door inside the human being has been shut, and the One who shuts it is the Lord who had long left it open.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:7

> Allah has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and their sight is covered. They will suffer a tremendous punishment.

**Commentary:**

The refusal of the previous verse is now given its inner anatomy: *khatama Allāhu ʿalā qulūbihim wa ʿalā samʿihim wa ʿalā abṣārihim ghishāwah wa lahum ʿadhābun ʿaẓīm*, “Allah has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and their sight is covered. They will suffer a tremendous punishment.” Three gates of guidance are named — the heart, the hearing, the sight — and all three are closed. The human being receives truth through what he hears and what he sees, and he processes it in the *qalb*, the heart that in Qur'anic language is the seat of understanding, not merely of emotion. “They have hearts with which they do not understand, and they have eyes with which they do not see, and they have ears with which they do not hear” (7:179). When those instruments are sealed or covered, warning becomes, as 2:6 said, the same as silence.

*Khatama* is from *kh-t-m*, to seal, to stamp, to close a vessel with a mark that both shuts it and authenticates the shutting. A sealed letter is not read; a sealed vessel is not poured. Al-Rāghib wrote that *khatm* is a covering placed on a thing so that nothing enters it. Ibn ʿAbbās explained that Allah (الله) sealed their hearts so that no faith would enter them. Al-Ṭabarī reports the image used by some of the *salaf*: the heart is like a hand clenched, or like a vessel turned upside down, so that the reminder does not settle in it. The Qur'an uses sister-words for the same disaster: *ṭabʿ* (imprint, 4:155), *ran* (rust, 83:14), *qaswah* (hardness, 2:74), *ghulf* (wrappers, 2:88). Each image adds a shade. Seal emphasizes closure; rust emphasizes the slow stain of sin; hardness emphasizes the loss of feeling; wrappers emphasize a covering they themselves claimed. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “When the believer commits a sin, a black speck appears on his heart. If he repents, desists, and asks forgiveness, his heart is polished. If he increases, it increases, until it is the *rān* that Allah mentioned: ‘No! Rather, rust has covered their hearts because of what they used to earn’” (al-Tirmidhī). The seal of 2:7 is not the first speck. It is the end of a road of specks.

The verse attributes the seal to Allah (الله): *khatama Allāhu*. This is the point around which theologians have circled for centuries, and the Ahl al-Sunnah position is the middle one. The Qadariyyah, who denied the Decree, disliked that Allah (الله) should be said to seal, as if that made Him the author of *kufr*. The Jabriyyah, who denied human agency, took the seal as proof that the servant is a puppet. The Qur'an refuses both. It ascribes the seal to Allah (الله) because nothing occurs except by His creating, and it ascribes the cause to the servants because they earned it. “Allah has set a seal upon their hearts because of their disbelief” (4:155). “We will turn their hearts and their eyes just as they refused to believe in it the first time” (6:110). “I will turn away from My signs those who are arrogant upon the earth without right” (7:146). Ibn Taymiyyah wrote that punishment in kind is the Lord’s justice: they closed their hearts, so their hearts were closed; they refused to hear, so hearing was sealed; they turned away their eyes, so a covering was set upon them. The covering, *ghishāwah*, is a noun in the nominative, a new clause: upon their eyes is a veil. Some reciters and grammarians attached the seal to hearts and hearing only, and made the covering a separate judgment upon sight. The meaning in either parsing is that every aperture of guidance has been blocked.

*Samʿ* is singular, *abṣār* is plural, *qulūb* is plural. The mufassirūn have tasted this. Hearing is one, because the truth that is heard is one; hearts and sights are many, because the inner responses and the outward glances are diverse. Whether or not that subtlety is intended, the grouping is the grouping of 16:78: “Allah brought you out from the wombs of your mothers not knowing anything, and He made for you hearing and sight and hearts, so that you might be grateful.” The gifts that were given for gratitude have been turned into instruments of refusal, and so they are withdrawn in their useful sense. The disbeliever still hears sounds and sees shapes; he does not hear the reminder as reminder or see the sign as sign. “Deaf, dumb, and blind — so they will not return” (2:18). The later parable of the rainstorm will return to this sensory failure. Here it is stated as doctrine.

The ending is *wa lahum ʿadhābun ʿaẓīm*, “and for them is a tremendous punishment.” *ʿAdhāb* is from a root that includes sweetness in other forms, as if punishment were the bitter opposite of a pleasure they chose. *ʿAẓīm* is great in rank and in weight, not merely large. The punishment is unnamed here so that its greatness will not be reduced to a single image. It includes the Fire, and it includes the punishment of the sealed life itself: to walk the earth with a heart that cannot open, an ear that cannot receive, an eye that cannot recognize. Ibn al-Qayyim counted this worldly constriction among the punishments that precede the grave. The Prophet (ﷺ) used to seek refuge from “knowledge that does not benefit, a heart that does not humble itself, a soul that is not satisfied, and a supplication that is not heard” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). A sealed heart is the first of those disasters.

For the believer the verse is a warning before it is a description of someone else. Seals are earned. The hadith of the black speck makes every sin a candidate for rust. A person who mocks the reminder, who scrolls past every āyah, who treats the prayer as optional and the unseen as folklore, is practicing the motions of 2:6–7. The sūrah will soon describe hypocrites whose disease is increased; the mechanism is akin. And for the caller, the verse completes the consolation of 2:6. You did not fail because your warning was poorly designed. Some hearts have been sealed by their Lord after they sealed themselves. Continue to warn the unsealed. Pray for the turning of hearts, for “the hearts of the children of Ādam are between the two fingers of the Most Merciful; He turns them as He wills” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). Recite, as the Prophet (ﷺ) recited, “O Turner of the hearts, make my heart firm upon Your religion.” The tremendous punishment is for those whose gates are shut. The tremendous mercy is that, until they are shut, every speck can still be polished.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:8

> And there are some who say, “We believe in Allah and the Last Day,” yet they are not ˹true˺ believers.

**Commentary:**

A third company now enters the sūrah, and it is the company that will occupy the longest of the three opening portraits: *wa mina al-nāsi man yaqūlu āmannā billāhi wa bil-yawmi al-ākhiri wa mā hum bi-muʾminīn*, “And there are some who say, ‘We believe in Allah and the Last Day,’ yet they are not true believers.” The believers were described in four verses; the obstinate disbelievers in two; the hypocrites will be described, with their parables, through verse 20. The proportion is itself a teaching. Open *kufr* is visible and can be met. *Nifāq* is speech that copies faith and a heart that has not joined the tongue, and it is more dangerous to a community than the enemy outside the wall. Madinah, unlike Makkah, had this third type in numbers: men who entered Islam with their mouths because power had shifted, and who kept their inner allegiance to themselves, to their clan, or to the enemies of the Prophet (ﷺ). The most notorious of them was ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy ibn Salūl, whose hostility would surface in the campaigns and whose type is drawn here without his name, so that the description would outlive the man.

The verse begins *wa mina al-nās*, “and among the people.” *Min* is partitive: a portion of mankind, not a mythological species. Hypocrisy is a human possibility, not a monster from outside the species. *Man yaqūlu*, “who say,” places the entire identity of this group in *qawl*, in speech. Their religion is a sentence. They say *āmannā billāhi wa bil-yawmi al-ākhiri*, “we have believed in Allah and in the Last Day,” which is precisely the creed that 2:3–4 had made the mark of the *muttaqūn*. They have learned the right words. They have not learned the right state. The Qur'an will later quote them in Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn: “When the hypocrites come to you they say, ‘We bear witness that you are the Messenger of Allah.’ And Allah knows that you are His Messenger, and Allah bears witness that the hypocrites are liars” (63:1). The lying is not about the Prophet’s (ﷺ) messengerhood, which is true; the lying is about their witness. They say what is true as if they believed it, and they do not.

*Wa mā hum bi-muʾminīn* is a stronger denial than *laysū muʾminīn*. The extra *bāʾ* in *bi-muʾminīn* is the *bāʾ* of emphasis in a negative nominal sentence: they are not, in any real sense, believers. The tense of *āmannā* was past, a claimed completed act; the denial is a present identity. Al-Ṭabarī explains that Allah (الله) denied them the name of *īmān* because *īmān* in the language of the Qur'an is not the movement of the tongue alone. The *salaf* defined it as speech, belief, and deed. The Murjiʾah, who later tried to make faith only assent of the heart or only speech, are already answered by this verse: here is speech without faith. Ibn Kathīr notes that they specified Allah and the Last Day because that was the distinctive claim of the new religion over against the pagans, and because the Jews among whom some hypocrites sat also claimed those two. They picked the articles that would pass in the mosque. They did not mention angels, books, and messengers in this slogan, though 2:4 had required the books; their creed is a selection designed for display.

The Prophet (ﷺ) gave the community a diagnostic that has been memorized ever since. “The signs of the hypocrite are three: when he speaks he lies, when he promises he breaks it, and when he is entrusted he betrays” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). In another wording: “and when he disputes he transgresses.” And in the fourfold version: “Whoever has four traits is a pure hypocrite, and whoever has one of them has a trait of hypocrisy until he leaves it: when he is entrusted he betrays, when he speaks he lies, when he pledges he betrays, and when he disputes he transgresses” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī). The scholars distinguished *nifāq iʿtiqādī*, the hypocrisy of creed that takes a person out of Islam — the hypocrisy of this verse — from *nifāq ʿamalī*, the hypocrisy of deeds that is a grave sin but does not by itself constitute apostasy. The three signs may appear in a believer as wounds; in the people of 2:8 they are the whole body. Ibn Taymiyyah wrote that the hypocrites of Madinah were of the first kind, and that every age has both kinds, and that the safety of the servant is to fear the second so that he does not slide into the first.

Theologically the verse draws a line between *islām* of the tongue and *īmān* of the heart. The Qur'an draws it again at 49:14: “The Bedouins say, ‘We have believed.’ Say, ‘You have not believed, but say, “We have submitted,” for faith has not yet entered your hearts.’” Not every incomplete faith is hypocrisy; some is a beginning. Hypocrisy is the claim of completion while the heart is elsewhere. Al-Qurṭubī remarks that Allah (الله) exposed them here, at the gate of the Book, so that the *ummah* would not be naive. A community that cannot imagine a speaker of the *shahādah* as anything but a saint is a community already half-disarmed. The verse does not authorize inquisition of hearts; the Prophet (ﷺ) refused to kill hypocrites lest people say he kills his companions. It does authorize discernment of patterns, protection of the community, and a private fear directed at one’s own tongue.

In modern life the verse is almost too easy to apply and therefore must be applied first at home. Social religion — the profile that lists faith, the greeting that uses pious phrases, the public alignment with the language of the believers — can be *yaqūlu āmannā* without *īmān*. A person may speak of Allah (الله) and the Last Day because those words confer belonging, moral prestige, or safety, while the unseen of 2:3 has no claim on his spending, his contracts, or his private screen. The sūrah will spend the next twelve verses stripping this speech. The reader who is not a hypocrite in creed may still need the stripping. “O you who believe, why do you say what you do not do? Great is hatred in the sight of Allah that you say what you do not do” (61:2–3). The people of 2:8 say they believe in the Last Day. The test of that sentence is not its grammar. It is whether the Last Day has permission to rearrange a life.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:9

> They seek to deceive Allah and the believers, yet they only deceive themselves, but they fail to perceive it.

**Commentary:**

The speech of the previous verse is now named as a strategy: *yukhādiʿūna Allāha wa alladhīna āmanū wa mā yakhdaʿūna illā anfusahum wa mā yashʿurūn*, “They seek to deceive Allah and the believers, yet they only deceive themselves, but they fail to perceive it.” *Khudāʿ* is deception, the showing of a face that is not the face, the concealing of a purpose under a purpose. The form *yukhādiʿūn* is the third-form *mufāʿalah*, which can denote attempted mutual deception or the taking of a matter as a contest. They treat faith as a game of appearances against Allah (الله) and against those who have truly believed. The audacity of the first object — Allah (الله) — is the shock of the verse. Can a creature deceive his Creator? “He knows the secret and what is even more hidden” (20:7). “We created man and We know what his soul whispers to him, and We are closer to him than his jugular vein” (50:16). Deceiving Allah (الله) is impossible as an outcome. It is possible as an intention, and the intention itself is a second *kufr* laid upon the first.

The mufassirūn have explained the phrase in more than one way, and the ways complete each other. Ibn ʿAbbās and others said they deceive Allah (الله) only in their own estimation; they imagine that the show of Islam will benefit them with Him as it benefits them with people. Al-Ṭabarī prefers that “deceiving Allah” means deceiving the religion of Allah and the friends of Allah, a known idiom in which the name of the Lord is used of what is His. A third reading, which Ibn Kathīr mentions, is that they deal with Allah (الله) as a deceiver deals, and so they are described by their attempt, not by their success. All three readings leave the second object intact: they also seek to deceive *alladhīna āmanū*, the believers — to sit in their ranks, to marry among them, to share their spoils and their protection, to learn their secrets, and to harm them when the moment serves. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ returns to the same verb: “Indeed, the hypocrites seek to deceive Allah, but it is He who deceives them” (4:142). The tables are turned in that verse as they are turned here. The plot is a self-plot.

*Wa mā yakhdaʿūna illā anfusahum*: they deceive none but their own souls. The restriction *illā* is the restriction of reality against appearance. In appearance they have fooled a neighborhood; in reality they have fooled the only party who can be harmed by this fooling, which is themselves. The harm is delayed and therefore invisible to them, which is why the verse ends *wa mā yashʿurūn*, “and they do not perceive.” *Shuʿūr* is perception, the sensing of a thing as it is. The root *sh-ʿ-r* is also the root of *shiʿr* (poetry) and *shaʿr* (hair), things that lie on the surface; here the verb means they do not even have the surface-awareness of what they are doing to themselves. Al-Rāghib noted that *shiʿr* as knowledge is a fine sensing; its negation is a numbness. The hypocrite is anesthetized. He feels the social profit of his sentence “we believe” and does not feel the spiritual loss. “They deceive themselves” means, among other things, that they miss the Hereafter for a passport in this world, that they miss the purification of the heart for the safety of the tongue, and that on the Day when “the hypocrite men and hypocrite women will say to those who believed, ‘Wait for us that we may borrow from your light’” (57:13), there will be no light to borrow. The deception comes due.

The Prophet (ﷺ) lived with this company in Madinah and was forbidden to treat them as a simple enemy. He knew many of them by name; Ḥudhayfah ibn al-Yamān was entrusted with the list. He did not expose them publicly in ordinary circumstances, because the legal order of Islam is built on the outward, and because a community that begins to unmask hearts will tear itself apart. Yet he warned of their inner trade. “There will come a people who recite the Qur'an, but it will not go beyond their throats” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī), and “the most I fear for you is the hypocrite with a learned tongue” (reported by Aḥmad). Learned tongue, sealed heart: the combination is 2:8–9. Ibn al-Qayyim wrote that self-deception is the last prison, because the prisoner who knows he is a prisoner may want a key, while the prisoner who thinks the cell is a palace will fight the one who brings the key. *Wa mā yashʿurūn* is that palace.

The verse also teaches a law of moral action that the Qur'an repeats under many images: plots against Allah (الله) do not stay abroad; they come home. “The evil plot does not encompass except its own people” (35:43). “They plot and Allah plots, and Allah is the best of plotters” (8:30). The hypocrite thinks he has found a third way between faith and disbelief, a covered path that collects the benefits of both. The verse says there is no third way. There is faith, and there is a deception that is only self-deception. In contemporary life the applications are many and should be used with the same fear the Companions had, not as a stone thrown at others only. There is the deception of religious performance for career, for marriage market, for online reputation. There is the deception of using the community’s trust — its money, its secrets, its pulpits — while mocking it in private. There is the quieter deception of telling oneself “I believe” as a lullaby, so that no change of conduct is required. All of these are *yukhādiʿūn*. All of them bounce. The believer who recites the verse is invited to a brutal simplicity: stop managing the gaze of people, including the imagined gaze of a God you have reduced to an audience, and deal with Allah (الله) as the One who cannot be managed. “He knows the treachery of the eyes and what the chests conceal” (40:19). That knowledge is either terror or relief. For the hypocrite it is terror he does not yet feel. For the sincere it is the end of having to keep a double set of books.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:10

> There is sickness in their hearts, and Allah ˹only˺ lets their sickness increase. They will suffer a painful punishment for their lies.

**Commentary:**

The inner condition that 2:8–9 approached from speech and strategy is now named as medicine names a disease: *fī qulūbihim maraḍun fa-zādahumu Allāhu maraḍan wa lahum ʿadhābun alīmun bimā kānū yakdhibūn*, “There is sickness in their hearts, and Allah lets their sickness increase. They will suffer a painful punishment for their lies.” *Maraḍ* is illness, a departure of the body or the soul from its balance. Used of the heart, it is not a metaphor the Arabs found strange. They spoke of a sick heart as they spoke of a sick eye: a faculty that no longer performs its function. Al-Rāghib divided the heart’s illness into two families: the illness of doubt and the illness of desire. The hypocrite’s heart has both. Doubt, because he is not settled on the truth he recites; desire, because he wants the goods of faith without its costs. Ibn ʿAbbās interpreted the *maraḍ* here as *nifāq*, hypocrisy itself. Others said doubt, others envy of the Prophet (ﷺ), others love of dispute. Al-Ṭabarī gathers them under the one heading of a heart that is not sound. The sound heart, *al-qalb al-salīm*, is what Ibrāhīm (peace be upon him) hoped to meet his Lord with (26:89). The sick heart is its opposite.

The increase is the most fearful clause: *fa-zādahumu Allāhu maraḍan*. The *fāʾ* is of consequence. Because there was disease, Allah (الله) increased the disease. This is the same justice that sealed the hearts in 2:7, applied now to a heart that still pretends to be open. The Qur'an states the law repeatedly. “As for those in whose hearts is disease, it has increased them in filth upon their filth” (9:125). “We increase in guidance those who are guided” (19:76). Revelation is not neutral in its effect. The same sūrah, the same recitation, the same battle, increases one man in certainty and another in sickness, according to what he brings to it. Ibn Kathīr explains that every time a verse was sent down, or the Prophet (ﷺ) was given victory, the believers increased in faith and the hypocrites increased in rage, doubt, and concealment. “Whenever a sūrah is sent down, there are among them those who say, ‘Which of you has this increased in faith?’ As for those who believe, it has increased them in faith and they rejoice. But as for those in whose hearts is disease, it has increased them in filth upon their filth, and they die while they are disbelievers” (9:124–125). Al-Baqarah 2:10 is the seed of that later diagnosis.

The ascription of the increase to Allah (الله) raises the same theological question as the seal, and the same answer serves. He increases as Creator of all events and as Just Judge who punishes a chosen illness by letting it run. The servant is not a victim of a random virus. He lied, as the end of the verse will say; he preferred the double life; he met the reminder with irritation rather than with repentance; and the illness grew as fire grows when it is fed. Ibn Taymiyyah described faith and hypocrisy as two plants in the chest, each growing by what it is given. Truth, humility, and obedience water faith. Lies, pride, and secret contradiction water hypocrisy. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “Temptations are presented to the hearts like a reed mat, stick by stick. Any heart that absorbs them is marked with a black spot, and any heart that rejects them is marked with a white spot, until the hearts become two: a white heart like a smooth stone, which will not be harmed by any trial so long as the heavens and earth remain, and a black heart, dust-colored, overturned, that knows no good and rejects no evil except what it has absorbed of its desire” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The black heart is 2:10 in motion.

The punishment is *ʿadhābun alīm*, a painful punishment, paired with the *ʿadhābun ʿaẓīm* of 2:7. The obstinate disbeliever is promised a punishment that is tremendous; the hypocrite is promised a punishment that is painful. Both will meet the Fire, and the Qur'an elsewhere says that the hypocrites will be in its lowest depth: “Indeed, the hypocrites will be in the lowest depth of the Fire, and you will not find for them a helper” (4:145). Pain is emphasized here because hypocrisy is a crime of nerve and concealment; it is fitting that what was hidden is repaid with a torment that cannot be hidden. The cause is stated with *bāʾ* of price: *bimā kānū yakdhibūn*, “because of what they used to lie.” *Kānū* plus the imperfect gives habit. They were not people who slipped into a lie; they were people whose way was lying. The lie of 2:8 — “we believe” — is the mother-lie. From it come the broken promises, the betrayed trusts, the false oaths of 63:1–2. The Prophet (ﷺ) placed lying at the head of the signs of *nifāq* because the double life cannot be maintained without it. Truth would collapse the performance in a week.

Al-Qurṭubī and the jurists drew from this verse a warning against taking sins of the tongue lightly. A lie is not a puff of air. It is food for a disease. In an age whose public life runs on managed impression — branding, curation, “narrative” — the verse reads like a medical chart. The heart that must always be other than it seems will not remain at the same degree of sickness. It will be increased. The treatment is also implied by the cause. If lying feeds the disease, truthfulness is a medicine. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “Truthfulness leads to righteousness, and righteousness leads to Paradise. A man keeps telling the truth until he is recorded with Allah as a truthful one. Lying leads to wickedness, and wickedness leads to the Fire. A man keeps lying until he is recorded with Allah as a liar” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The two paths are the two increases of 2:10 and 9:124. The believer who recites this verse should not first hunt for hypocrites in the congregation. He should ask whether there is a *maraḍ* in his own chest — a doubt he will not study, a desire he will not restrain, a public piety he will not match in private — and he should treat it before the increase is decreed. Hearts are still, until they are not, between the two fingers of the Most Merciful.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:11

> When they are told, “Do not spread corruption in the land,” they reply, “We are only peace-makers!”

**Commentary:**

The hypocrites’ inner disease now shows itself in public language, and the language is a reversal of names: *wa idhā qīla lahum lā tufsidū fī al-arḍi qālū innamā naḥnu muṣliḥūn*, “When they are told, ‘Do not spread corruption in the land,’ they reply, ‘We are only peace-makers!’” The verse is built on a dialogue. Someone says to them — the “someone” may be the Prophet (ﷺ), or the believers, or the voice of revelation itself — *lā tufsidū fī al-arḍ*, do not make *fasād* in the earth. *Fasād* is corruption, spoiling, the unmaking of what was sound. The root *f-s-d* is the opposite of *ṣ-l-ḥ*, which is soundness, right order, peace, and reform. The entire ethical vocabulary of this verse is that pair. They are told not to spoil the land; they answer that they are the *muṣliḥūn*, the ones who put things right. The tragedy is not only that they spoil. It is that they have renamed the spoiling.

What was their *fasād* in Madinah? The mufassirūn, following Ibn ʿAbbās and the early authorities as recorded by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, describe a cluster of acts: secret alliance with the Jews and the pagans against the Prophet (ﷺ); spreading rumors that weakened resolve before battle; discouraging people from spending and from fighting; sowing suspicion between the Muhājirūn and the Anṣār; and living as a fifth column while claiming the privileges of Islam. ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy withdrawing with his men on the day of Uḥud is the later, visible fruit of this earlier root. *Fasād fī al-arḍ* in the Qur'an is wider than street crime. It includes *shirk*, which unmakes the right of Allah (الله) in His own earth; injustice, which unmakes the rights of servants; and the breaking of the bonds that Allah (الله) commanded to be joined, as 2:27 will soon say. “Do not spread corruption in the land after it has been set right” (7:56). The earth was set right by *tawḥīd* and law; *fasād* is the attempt to return it to disorder while remaining inside the city of the Muslims.

Their reply uses *innamā*, the particle of restriction: “we are *only* reformers.” *Innamā* cuts away every other description. They do not say “we also reform” or “we intend good.” They claim that reform is their exclusive function, that what looks like corruption to the naive is in fact expert repair. This is the most modern sentence in the opening of al-Baqarah. Every age has a party that calls its disruption *iṣlāḥ* and its opponents backward. The hypocrites of Madinah presented their dealings with the enemies of the Prophet (ﷺ) as diplomacy, their discouragement of fighting as peacemaking, their religious lukewarmness as sophistication. Al-Qurṭubī notes that the corruptor, when the corruption has become his habit and his interest, must call it by a good name, because the soul is ashamed to say “I am spoiling the earth.” Ibn al-Qayyim wrote at length, in his works on the diseases of the heart, that the dressing of vice in the clothing of virtue is itself a stage of *nifāq*. The liar calls himself tactful; the coward calls himself prudent; the traitor calls himself a bridge-builder. “Do not say, regarding the falsehood that your tongues describe, ‘This is lawful and this is unlawful,’ to invent lies against Allah” (16:116). The renaming is part of the crime.

The verb *qīla*, “it is said,” is passive. The speaker is not specified, which widens the verse. Anyone who commands this group to stop corrupting is answered with the same slogan. The passivity also slightly humiliates the objectors: their names are not recorded, but their sentence is. The Qur'an often preserves the slogans of its opponents with a precision that later history can still hear. “We are only peacemakers” could be printed on a banner. The next verse will tear the banner. Here the sūrah wants the reader to sit with the claim, to recognize it in his own century, and to notice that the claim is made *when they are told* — that is, after a command has already reached them. They are not uninformed. They are contradicted, and they contradict back. This is worse than the ignorance of 2:13’s “fools,” which they will project onto others. This is the refusal of a known prohibition.

The Prophet (ﷺ) described the believer’s relation to the earth in the opposite terms. “The Muslim is the one from whose tongue and hand the Muslims are safe” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). “There is no *adḥā* (harm) and no *ḍirār* (reciprocal harm)” (Ibn Mājah, Mālik). He said, “Whoever introduces into this affair of ours what is not from it, it is rejected” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim), which is a legal wall against the *fasād* that presents itself as improvement of the religion. Reform in Islam is return to what was made sound, not the invention of a new soundness. *Iṣlāḥ* is the work of the prophets: “I only want to reform as much as I am able” (11:88), said Shuʿayb (peace be upon him). The hypocrite borrows the prophets’ word and empties it.

For the present day the verse is a test of slogans. Nations, parties, and religious movements all claim to be *muṣliḥūn*. The Qur'an does not accept the claim as evidence. It looks at the land: are bonds being joined or cut? Is the name of Allah (الله) being established or being used as cover? Are the weak protected or consumed? “When he turns away, he strives throughout the land to spread corruption therein and destroy crops and cattle, and Allah does not like corruption” (2:205) — a later verse of this same sūrah that reads like a commentary on this earlier one. The believer is commanded both to avoid *fasād* and to refuse the narcotic of self-description. He does not answer a prohibition by announcing his good intentions. He looks at his hands. If they are spoiling, he stops, even if the age applauds the spoiling as peace. The next verse will say that they do not even perceive their own corruption. The first defense against that numbness is to let the command *lā tufsidū* remain a command, not a prompt for a better press release.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:12

> Indeed, it is they who are the corruptors, but they fail to perceive it.

**Commentary:**

The slogan of the previous verse is answered with a judgment that leaves no remainder: *alā innahum humu al-mufsidūn wa lākin lā yashʿurūn*, “Indeed, it is they who are the corruptors, but they fail to perceive it.” The verse is short, and its shortness is a slap. *Alā* is a particle of alerting, used to wake a listener who may have been half-persuaded by the hypocrites’ claim of *iṣlāḥ*. Ibn ʿAbbās and the rhetoricians treated *alā* as a tap on the shoulder: know this; do not be deceived. Then *innahum humu al-mufsidūn* piles emphasis upon emphasis. *Inna* confirms; the pronoun *hum* repeats the subject before the predicate; the definite *al-mufsidūn* makes corruption their identity, not an accident of their week. In Arabic rhetoric this is *ḥaṣr*, restriction: they are the corruptors, they and not the people they accuse, they and not the Prophet (ﷺ) whose city they quietly undermine. The ones who said “we are only peacemakers” are, in the speech of Allah (الله), the very opposite of what they said.

The pairing of 2:11 and 2:12 is a small school of how revelation treats propaganda. First it quotes the claim in the claimants’ own words, so that no one can say the Qur'an caricatured them. Then it inverts the claim with a definiteness the claim did not have. They said *innamā naḥnu muṣliḥūn*, a restriction of self-praise. Allah (الله) says *innahum humu al-mufsidūn*, a restriction of verdict. Human beings may debate intentions; the Lord of the servants names fruits. “You will know them by their fruits” is a gospel sentence; the Qur'anic equivalent is this pattern of quotation and exposure, which will recur throughout al-Baqarah and in Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn. Al-Ṭabarī comments that Allah (الله) declared them corruptors because they worked to extinguish Islam, to betray the Messenger (ﷺ), and to return the land to *shirk* and alliance with his enemies, all of which is the definition of *fasād fī al-arḍ*. Ibn Kathīr adds that their *fasād* included the private mockery of 2:14 and the trading of guidance in 2:16. Corruption is not only blood and fire. It is the slow poisoning of a community’s trust.

Then the refrain that already closed 2:9: *wa lākin lā yashʿurūn*, “but they do not perceive.” In 2:9 they did not perceive that they were deceiving only themselves. Here they do not perceive that they are the corruptors. The numbness has a new object. Self-deception has progressed from missing the rebound of the plot to missing the moral quality of the plot. They are not even in the place where a person says, “I know I am doing wrong and I will continue.” They have exited the category of guilty knowledge into the category of renamed wrong. Al-Rāghib’s note on *shuʿūr* as a fine sensing is again apt: the instrument that should taste *fasād* as bitter has been coated. Ibn al-Qayyim described this as a punishment in this world that precedes the Fire — the death of moral taste. A heart that cannot feel its own corruption is already in a kind of grave. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “Piety is what settles in the heart and what you would hate for people to discover, and sin is what agitates the soul and what you dislike that people should know” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, in the hadith of al-Nawwās ibn Samʿān). The hypocrite of 2:12 has lost that agitation. Nothing stirs. He can spoil a city and sleep.

The scholars of the heart, among them al-Ghazālī in his discussions of self-delusion and Ibn Taymiyyah in his treatments of hypocrisy, have said that the last veil is the veil of good names. When *fasād* is still called *fasād*, repentance is one step: stop. When *fasād* is called *iṣlāḥ*, repentance is two steps: rename, then stop. The second step is the harder, because it costs a person his story about himself. Communities can live for decades inside that second veil. They can build institutions whose stated purpose is reform and whose actual harvest is the breaking of what Allah (الله) ordered to be joined. The verse is therefore not only about a handful of men in the seventh century. It is about the possibility that a group’s entire moral vocabulary has been inverted, and that the inversion is invisible from inside. “And We will turn their hearts and their eyes just as they refused to believe in it the first time, and We will leave them in their transgression, wandering blindly” (6:110). Blind wandering is *lā yashʿurūn* made into a landscape.

The morphology of *al-mufsidūn* should not be missed. It is the active participle of *afsada*, the fourth form of *f-s-d*, which means not merely to be corrupt but to *make* corruption, to spread it, to cause sound things to fail. The *muṣliḥ* of their claim is the active participle of *aṣlaḥa*, to make sound. The two participles are professional titles. Allah (الله) awards them the title they refused and strips the title they stole. Later in this sūrah the same root will describe those who “violate Allah’s covenant after it has been affirmed, break whatever ties Allah has ordered to be maintained, and spread corruption in the land. It is they who are the true losers” (2:27), and still later the man who, when he turns away, “strives throughout the land to spread mischief in it and destroy crops and cattle. Allah does not like mischief” (2:205). Qārūn will be told, “Do not seek corruption in the land; Allah does not like the corruptors” (28:77). 2:12 is the first naming; the rest of the Book will fill in the job description. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “There is to be no harm and no reciprocating harm” (Ibn Mājah, Mālik), which is the legal wall against *ifsād*, and he said that faith and *fasād* cannot comfortably share a heart: the servant whose neighbor is not safe from his harm has not believed with a complete faith (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, in meaning).

There is a pastoral caution. The verse is a divine verdict upon a known type, not a license for every disputant to brand his opponent a corruptor. The Prophet (ﷺ) did not go through the streets of Madinah calling men *mufsidūn* by name on the basis of this āyah. He knew, he warned, he withheld spoils when necessary, he accepted outward Islam, and he left the secrets to Allah (الله). The believer who recites 2:12 should fear two things at once: being among those who spoil while claiming repair, and becoming a person who sees *fasād* in everyone but himself. The first fear is about numbness; the second is about a different disease, the disease of *ʿujb*, self-admiration, which also cannot perceive. Between them is the path of the *muttaqūn* of 2:2: they take the command *lā tufsidū* as addressed to themselves, they do not advertise themselves as *muṣliḥūn*, and they ask Allah (الله) to show them the truth as truth and grant them to follow it, and falsehood as falsehood and grant them to avoid it — the supplication the Prophet (ﷺ) used to make. Perception, *shuʿūr*, is itself a gift. The people of this verse were denied it. The reader is still in time to ask for it.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:13

> And when they are told, “Believe as others believe,” they reply, “Will we believe as the fools believe?” Indeed, it is they who are fools, but they do not know.

**Commentary:**

A second dialogue, parallel in structure to 2:11–12, now exposes the hypocrites’ contempt for the community of faith: *wa idhā qīla lahum āminū kamā āmana al-nāsu qālū a-nuʾminu kamā āmana al-sufahāʾu alā innahum humu al-sufahāʾu wa lākin lā yaʿlamūn*, “And when they are told, ‘Believe as others believe,’ they reply, ‘Will we believe as the fools believe?’ Indeed, it is they who are fools, but they do not know.” The command is simple: *āminū kamā āmana al-nās*, believe as the people have believed. *Al-nās* here, as Ibn ʿAbbās, Abū al-ʿĀliyah, and the majority of the mufassirūn explained, are the Companions of the Prophet (ﷺ) — the Muhājirūn and the Anṣār, those whose faith 2:3–5 described. “The people” are not an anonymous crowd. They are the known believers whose *īmān* has already been accepted by Allah (الله) as the pattern. To believe as they believe is to believe in the unseen, to establish prayer, to spend, to accept this revelation and the revelations before it, and to be certain of the Hereafter. It is not a call to imitation of custom. It is a call to join the caravan that is already on the road.

The hypocrites answer with a rhetorical question that is an insult: *a-nuʾminu kamā āmana al-sufahāʾ*. *Safah* is folly, lightness of intellect and of forbearance, the opposite of *ḥilm* and of sound judgment. The *safīh* in Arabic is the one whose reason does not weigh, whose soul is flighty, who spends without measure — which is why the jurists use *safah* of the spendthrift who must be restricted. To call the believers *sufahāʾ* was, in Madinah, to call men who had left homes and wealth, who stood in prayer at dawn, who gave preference to the emigrants over themselves, empty-headed. The insult is class-shaped as well as intellectual. Some of the hypocrites were chiefs of the Aws and Khazraj; some of the strongest believers were former slaves, the poor of the Suffah, the unadorned. Ibn Ubayy looked at that assembly and saw folly. The Qur'an looks at Ibn Ubayy and sees the same word, returned.

The return is exact, as in 2:12. *Alā innahum humu al-sufahāʾ*: indeed, it is they who are the fools. Restriction, emphasis, identity. Folly in the Qur'anic sense is not low education. It is the misweighing of what is heavy and what is light. “Who would turn away from the religion of Abraham except one who has made a fool of himself?” (2:130). The greatest *safah* is to sell the next world for this one, to mock the people of certainty, to prefer the alliance of enemies to the alliance of Allah (الله). Al-Ṭabarī writes that they were the fools because they preferred blindness to guidance. Ibn Kathīr notes that their mockery of the believers as simpletons is itself the proof of their simplicity: they thought the losing bargain was the winning one. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “The intelligent one is he who takes his soul to account and works for what is after death, and the incapable one is he who lets his soul follow its desire and merely hopes in Allah” (al-Tirmidhī). Intelligence in this hadith is eschatological. By that measure the poorly dressed Companion who prays at night is wiser than the chieftain who cannot see past his clan.

The closing of 2:13 is not *lā yashʿurūn* but *lā yaʿlamūn*, “they do not know.” The previous two diagnoses denied them perception, the sensing of a state. This one denies them knowledge. *ʿIlm* is the grasp of a thing as it is. They do not know they are fools; they do not know what faith is; they do not know who “the people” are. Al-Rāzī and others observed the variation: corruption they might have felt if their senses were alive; folly they would have known if their intellects had been used. Both instruments have failed. The Qur'an sometimes pairs the two failures: “They have hearts with which they do not understand” (7:179). Here the pairing is spread across verses. Ibn al-Qayyim remarked that when Allah (الله) denies a people *shuʿūr*, He is speaking of their numbness to their state, and when He denies them *ʿilm*, He is speaking of their emptiness of the very science they claim. The hypocrites claimed to be the people of understanding, the ones too shrewd to believe like the naive. The verse says they do not know.

There is a further cruelty, and a further mercy, in *kamā āmana al-nās*. The standard of faith is public and embodied. It is not a private gnosis the elite possess. “The people” believed — artisans, shepherds, women of Madinah, youths, the old. The religion is not an esoteric club. The hypocrite’s question, “Shall we believe as the fools believe?,” is the perennial temptation of the sophisticated: to need a faith that does not look like the faith of ordinary believers, a faith more complex, more ironic, more compatible with the salon. Every age has a class that cannot bear to be seen praying as the baker prays. The verse calls that embarrassment *safah*. The Prophet (ﷺ) ate as a servant eats, sat as a servant sits, and said, “I sit as a slave sits and I eat as a slave eats, for I am a slave” (reported in the collections of *zuhd*). To believe as “the people” believe is to accept that the highest intelligence is submission.

Today the insult “fool” is delivered in new dialects: unscientific, uncritical, unsophisticated, on the wrong side of history. Believers are told they would not pray, would not fast, would not guard the unseen, if they were truly educated. The verse does not forbid education. It forbids the equation of faith with folly and of cynicism with intellect. The people of 2:5 are upon guidance from their Lord; the people of 2:13 call that guidance foolishness and are named by what they named others. The reader is invited to choose his company. If he is ashamed of the believers, he has already begun to speak the question of this verse. If he can be told “believe as the people believed” and find in that sentence not humiliation but a home, he has understood what the hypocrites did not know.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:14

> When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.” But when alone with their evil associates they say, “We are definitely with you; we were only mocking.”

**Commentary:**

The double life is now shown in two rooms: *wa idhā laqū alladhīna āmanū qālū āmannā wa idhā khalaw ilā shayāṭīnihim qālū innā maʿakum innamā naḥnu mustahziʾūn*, “When they meet the believers they say, ‘We believe.’ But when alone with their evil associates they say, ‘We are definitely with you; we were only mocking.’” The verse is a piece of theater with two scenes and two scripts. Scene one is the meeting, *laqū*, a face-to-face encounter with those who have believed. The line is two words: *āmannā*, we have believed. Scene two is *khalaw*, they are alone, emptied of witnesses, withdrawn to *shayāṭīnihim*, their satans. The line is longer, because loyalty to the dark requires more reassurance: *innā maʿakum*, we are with you, certainly; *innamā naḥnu mustahziʾūn*, we were only mocking. The extra words are the extra cost of a double allegiance. Truth can be short. A cover story needs clauses.

*Shayāṭīnihim*, “their satans,” is one of the verse’s most discussed words. Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, Qatādah, and the mass of the mufassirūn, as al-Ṭabarī records, said these were the hypocrites’ chiefs and their allies among the Jews who opposed the Prophet (ﷺ) — human satans. The Qur'an calls human tempters by this name: “satanic humans and jinn” (6:112). A second reading includes the jinn-satans who whisper to them. The possessive *their* satans is terrible in either reading. These are not satans in the abstract. They are *theirs*: chosen company, the people they relax with when the believers have gone. A man’s *khalwah*, his privacy, is the true census of his allies. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “A man is upon the religion of his intimate friend, so let one of you look at whom he befriends” (Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī). He also said, “The example of a good companion and a bad companion is that of a seller of musk and a bellows-blower of the smith. The musk-seller will either give you some, or you will buy from him, or you will smell a good smell. The bellows-blower will either burn your clothes or you will smell a bad smell” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The hypocrites have chosen the bellows, and they return to it with a confession: we were only mocking.

*Istihzāʾ* is mockery, the making of a thing into *huzuʾ*, a joke. They do not tell their satans, “we are afraid” or “we are waiting.” They tell them that the entire performance of faith was a jest. Prayer, greeting, sitting in the circle of the Prophet (ﷺ) — a joke. This is a degree below ordinary hypocrisy of fear. It is hypocrisy of scorn. Sūrah al-Tawbah will quote the same mouth: “If you ask them, they will say, ‘We were only conversing and playing.’ Say, ‘Was it Allah and His verses and His Messenger you were mocking?’” (9:65). Mockery of the sacred is, in that passage, treated as *kufr* even when the mocker says he was playing. The people of 2:14 have made play their inner theology. Ibn Kathīr notes that this verse was a humiliation for them in this world: Allah (الله) disclosed the private meeting. They thought the second room was sealed. Revelation opened it. The believer in Madinah who heard this āyah understood that no *khalwah* is a *khalwah* from Allah (الله). “Do they not know that Allah knows what they conceal and what they reveal?” (2:77).

The grammar of *innā maʿakum* is loyalty in its strongest form. *Inna* plus the pronoun plus *maʿa* (with) is the language of alliance, the same preposition used of Allah (الله) being with the patient. They give to their satans the with-ness they refuse to the Messenger (ﷺ). Then *innamā* restricts their behavior among the believers to mockery alone, as if to say: do not be anxious, our chiefs; nothing of that performance was real. Al-Qurṭubī observes the psychological need here. The hypocrite must constantly re-earn the trust of the party he actually belongs to, because that party has seen him smiling with the other side. Treason requires twice the protestations of ordinary friendship. The believers, by contrast, are given only two words. The hypocrite spends his cheap coin on the people of truth and his expensive coin on the people of falsehood.

Theologically the verse completes the unmasking of *nifāq* as a social system, not only a private disease. There are meetings; there are associates; there is a script for each audience. The community is warned that a mouth which says *āmannā* is not self-authenticating. The warning is not a call to strip every Muslim in public. The Prophet (ﷺ) still prayed over the hypocrites until 9:84 forbade him in a specific case; he still accepted their outward claims. The warning is for discernment, and it is for self-audit. Which meeting relaxes me? Which company gets my *innā maʿakum*? In the age of private chats, encrypted groups, and public piety, 2:14 reads as if it were sent down for screens. A person may post *āmannā* and then, in the group that does not include the believers, say “we were only mocking.” The *khalwah* has multiplied. It has not become hidden from the One who said, “Not a secret conversation of three but He is the fourth” (58:7).

The believer’s opposite conduct is one speech in both rooms. ʿUmar (may Allah be pleased with him) said he wished to know which of two men he was, and the pious have always feared a difference between their *ʿalāniyah* (public) and their *sirr* (secret). The Prophet (ﷺ) taught the supplication, “O Allah, make my inner better than my outer, and make my outer good.” The people of this verse reversed that prayer. Their outer said faith; their inner, and their second outer with their satans, said mockery. The next verse will tell them that mockery is not a weapon they own. It will be turned back upon them by the One they thought was the object of the joke.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:15

> Allah will throw their mockery back at them, leaving them to continue wandering blindly in their defiance.

**Commentary:**

The mockery of the private room is taken out of the mockers’ hands: *Allāhu yastahziʾu bihim wa yamudduhum fī ṭughyānihim yaʿmahūn*, “Allah will throw their mockery back at them, leaving them to continue wandering blindly in their defiance.” The same root they used of themselves, *h-z-ʾ*, is now used of Allah (الله). The verb is in the present, *yastahziʾu*, a continuing act, not a single slap. The mufassirūn have been careful, as they always are when a divine act is named by a word that in creatures is a fault. Mockery as belittling the sacred is a sin. Mockery as Allah’s (الله) act is not a fault; it is *jazaʾ min jins al-ʿamal*, a repayment in the kind of the deed, a turning of the plot back on the plotter. Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ṭabarī, and Ibn Kathīr explain it as Allah (الله) making them a mockery, punishing them for their mockery, opening upon them a door of worldly illusion — safety, spoils, marriage among the Muslims — and then exposing them and punishing them, so that the joke is finally on the jester. Al-Qurṭubī lists the opinions and settles on the principle that Allah (الله) is named by the form of their act without being named by its ugliness, as He is called the Plotter against plotters (8:30) and the Deceiver of those who sought to deceive Him (4:142). The Names of repayment are not the Names of essence such as *al-Raḥmān*; they are the Lord’s justice speaking the sinner’s language.

The content of this divine “mockery” is partly in this world and fully in the next. In this world it includes the fact that the hypocrites are allowed to remain among the believers, collecting the benefits of a faith they despise, until the appointed disgrace. It includes the disclosure of their secrets in these very verses, so that the community hears the second script. It includes, on the Day of Resurrection, the scene already cited: “On the Day when the hypocrite men and hypocrite women will say to those who believed, ‘Wait for us that we may borrow from your light.’ It will be said, ‘Go back behind you and seek light.’ Then a wall will be placed between them with a door, its interior containing mercy, and its exterior facing toward it, punishment” (57:13). They mocked the believers’ light as folly; they will beg for a spark of it and be told to go back. That is *istihzāʾ* as eschatological irony. The Prophet (ﷺ) described other repayments in kind: “Allah is not hidden from, and whoever tries to deceive Allah, Allah deceives him” — the sense of 4:142 — and the hadith qudsī, “I am as My servant thinks of Me” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim), which, for the mocker, is not a comfort.

The second clause describes the state in which they are left: *wa yamudduhum fī ṭughyānihim yaʿmahūn*. *Yamudduhum* is from *m-d-d*, to extend, to stretch, to supply with more of the same. Allah (الله) extends them in their *ṭughyān*. *Ṭughyān* is excess, overflow, the going beyond bounds; the flood is *ṭughyān al-māʾ*, and the tyrant is *ṭāghūt* from the same overflowing. Their rebellion is not cut short; it is lengthened. This is *istidrāj*, the gradual leading on of the sinner by means of continued blessing and continued opportunity to sin, until he is taken. “We will lead them on gradually from where they do not know” (7:182). “Do they think that what We extend to them of wealth and children is because We hasten for them good things? Rather, they do not perceive” (23:55–56). The extension looks like success. It is a rope. Ibn Taymiyyah wrote that among the most terrible of punishments is to be helped to the sin one loves. The hypocrites love their double game; they are given more of it.

*Yaʿmahūn* is from *ʿamah*, blindness of the heart, a wandering that cannot find the road. It is not *ʿamā*, the blindness of the eye, though they are related. *ʿAmah* is to stray in confusion, to grope. “And We left them in their transgression, wandering blindly” (6:110). The picture is of people walking in a land without landmarks, still convinced they are the ones who see. The mockery, the extension, and the wandering are one mercy withheld. Guidance would have been a shortening of the *ṭughyān*, a pulling of the rope, a setting of landmarks. Instead they are left to the freedom they demanded. Al-Rāghib noted that *ʿamah* is used of one who cannot see the argument though it stands in front of him. The believers of 2:5 are *ʿalā hudan*, upon guidance, as on high ground. The hypocrites of 2:15 are in *ṭughyān*, as in a floodplain, wandering.

The Prophet (ﷺ) used to seek refuge from the state this verse describes. He taught, “O Allah, I seek refuge in You from knowledge that does not benefit, from a heart that does not humble itself, from a soul that is not satisfied, and from a supplication that is not heard” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). A heart in *ʿamah* is a heart that cannot humble itself. He also taught the constant prayer for the turning of hearts, because the line between being extended in good and being extended in *ṭughyān* is not always visible to the one being extended. Worldly ease is ambiguous. For the grateful it is *istidrāj* toward thanks; for the mocking it is *istidrāj* toward the flood. The believer reads 2:15 and does not smugly apply it only to a class in Madinah. He asks: is my continued health, my continued audience, my continued ability to speak about religion while my private room laughs, an extension of *ṭughyān*? If the question can still hurt, there is still *shuʿūr*. If it cannot, the wandering may already have begun.

The verse is also a comfort to the mocked. The Companions heard themselves called fools in 2:13 and heard their faith called a joke in 2:14. 2:15 tells them that the joke has a Lord, and He has picked it up. No mockery of Allah (الله), His verses, or His Messenger (ﷺ) remains in the mocker’s mouth. It is transferred to the divine court, where it is repaid without injustice and without haste. “Those who mock will be repaid for what they used to do” is the sense of many verses; this one makes the repayment share the mocker’s own verb. The people of faith may leave the insult where it was thrown. The One who “throws their mockery back at them” has not dropped it.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:16

> They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance. But this trade is profitless, and they are not ˹rightly˺ guided.

**Commentary:**

The portrait of the hypocrites, before it turns to parable, is sealed with a commercial image: *ulāʾika alladhīna ishtarawū al-ḍalālata bil-hudā fa-mā rabiḥat tijāratuhum wa mā kānū muhtadīn*, “They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance. But this trade is profitless, and they are not rightly guided.” *Ulāʾika* again, the far demonstrative of 2:5, now pointed at the opposite company. There it honored the successful; here it distances the bankrupt. The verb *ishtarawū* is from *sh-r-y*, which in Arabic can mean to buy or to sell, the two sides of a trade. They purchased *ḍalālah*, misguidance, and the price they paid was *al-hudā*, the guidance. The *bāʾ* in *bil-hudā* is the *bāʾ* of the price. Guidance was in their hand — by birth in a city that received the Prophet (ﷺ), by hearing the Qur'an, by sitting in the mosque, by the fitrah itself — and they handed it over for error. Ibn ʿAbbās said they chose disbelief in place of faith. Al-Ṭabarī explains that every hypocrite and disbeliever who turns from what he knows of the truth is this trader. The image will recur in the sūrah: “These are the ones who have purchased the life of this world at the price of the Hereafter” (2:86). “Miserable is the price for which they sold themselves” (2:90). The Qur'an is not ashamed of marketplace language. The Lord of the worlds speaks to a commercial people in the terms they think they have mastered, and He shows them a deal they have miscalculated.

*Al-ḍalālah* is from *ḍ-l-l*, to stray, to be lost, to disappear from the road. It is the third of the Fātiḥah’s three paths: the path of the blessed, the path of those under anger, the path of those who went astray. Here it is not a wandering that happened to them; it is merchandise they selected. *Al-hudā* is the same guidance upon which the people of 2:5 stand. The trade is therefore a precise inversion of the opening of the sūrah. The Book is “a guide for the mindful”; these have sold the guide. Ibn Kathīr notes that they had guidance available as the believers had it — the same recitation, the same Messenger (ﷺ) — and that this makes the bargain worse than the bargain of one who never saw the road. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “All of my *ummah* will enter Paradise except those who refuse.” They said, “Who would refuse?” He said, “Whoever obeys me enters Paradise, and whoever disobeys me has refused” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī). Refusal is a purchase of *ḍalālah* with the coin of *hudā*.

Then the audit: *fa-mā rabiḥat tijāratuhum*, so their trade has not profited. *Ribḥ* is profit, the surplus over capital. *Tijārah* is trade, a going back and forth of goods. The feminine verb agrees with *tijārah*: the business itself did not profit. In the language of the merchants of Makkah and Madinah this is the sentence no caravan wanted to hear. Capital gone, surplus none, the journey a loss. Al-Zamakhsharī, who loved this kind of usage, noted that the Qur'an ascribes the failure to the trade rather than only to the traders, as if even their commerce, were it a person, would admit bankruptcy. *Wa mā kānū muhtadīn*, and they were not guided ones. The second clause is not a repetition of the first. A man might lose money and still find the road home. These have lost the profit and the road. Ibn al-Qayyim wrote that every sin is a bad purchase, and that the hypocrite’s purchase is the worst because he spends the most valuable currency — guidance, the very thing asked for in every rakʿah of the Fātiḥah — on the least valuable good, a misguidance that does not even deliver the worldly safety it advertised. They wanted to keep both sides of the deal: the believers’ protection and the satans’ approval. They lost both. On the Day of Judgment they will have neither light (57:13) nor a pleasing station with those they tried to please.

The Prophet (ﷺ) used the same commercial metaphor in the other direction, for the winning trade. “Allah has purchased from the believers their lives and their properties in exchange for Paradise” (9:111). He said, “There is a trade that Allah loves,” and he mentioned the sincere fighter and the honest merchant in various reports, and he said, “The honest, trustworthy merchant is with the prophets, the truthful, and the martyrs” (al-Tirmidhī). Trade is not condemned. This trade is condemned. The difference is the item and the price. To sell the self to Allah (الله) is *falāḥ*; to sell guidance for error is the bankruptcy of 2:16. Al-Qurṭubī draws a legal and spiritual caution: a person may think he is clever in mixing, hedging, keeping a foot in each camp, and the verse names that cleverness a loss. The mixed portfolio is the one that fails.

For the modern reader the marketplace of the verse has only grown. Guidance is still on offer — the Book of 2:2, the prayer of 2:3, the certainty of 2:4 — and misguidance has more stalls than Madinah ever saw: ideologies that buy the heart in exchange for belonging, entertainments that buy the night in exchange for the sweetness of *qiyām*, reputations that buy the tongue in exchange for truth. The question is not whether one is in a market. The question is what is being sold. A believer can recite this verse against the hypocrites of a former city and still be, in a quieter way, trading: a little of the prayer for a little more of the world, a little of the unseen for a little more of being taken seriously by those who think the unseen is folly. The verse’s last words, *wa mā kānū muhtadīn*, are the opposite of 2:5’s *ʿalā hudan min rabbihim*. Two companies, two trades, two results. The parables that follow — the fire kindled and then extinguished, the rainstorm of thunder and lightning — will make the loss visible to the imagination. Here it is visible to the accountant. The books do not lie. The trade did not profit. The traders are not guided. The only remaining move, for anyone who still hears the verse as addressed also to himself, is to reverse the purchase while the market is open: to give back the *ḍalālah* and to take back, by repentance, the *hudā* that was never truly anyone’s to sell, because “guidance is Allah’s guidance” (2:120).

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:17

> Their example is that of someone who kindles a fire, but when it lights up all around them, Allah takes away their light, leaving them in complete darkness—unable to see.

**Commentary:**

The sūrah now leaves plain description for *mathal*, parable, because some truths enter the mind more deeply when they are seen than when they are only defined: *mathaluhum ka-mathali alladhī istawqada nāran fa-lammā aḍāʾat mā ḥawlahu dhahaba Allāhu bi-nūrihim wa tarakahum fī ẓulumātin lā yubṣirūn*, “Their example is that of someone who kindles a fire, but when it lights up all around them, Allah takes away their light, leaving them in complete darkness — unable to see.” *Mathal* is from *m-th-l*, to resemble, to set one thing beside another so that the unknown is read through the known. The Qur'an uses parables not as decoration but as a method of teaching, and it will soon defend that method: “Allah does not shy away from using the parable of a mosquito or what is even smaller” (2:26). This first parable of the Book is about light that was real and then was taken.

*Istawqada* is the tenth form of *w-q-d*, to kindle, to seek to light a fire. The hypocrite, or the one who entered Islam with a weak, mixed intention, kindles something: he says the *shahādah*, he sits in the light of the Prophet (ﷺ), he enjoys the legal and social illumination of the new community — safety of blood, marriage, inheritance, the warmth of belonging. Ibn ʿAbbās, as reported by al-Ṭabarī, said they believed at first and then disbelieved, so the fire is the light of faith that briefly shone. Mujāhid and others said the fire is the light of the Muslims among whom they sit, a borrowed brightness. Both readings are alive in the wording. *Fa-lammā aḍāʾat mā ḥawlahu*, when it had lit what was around him: the illumination is of the surroundings, *ḥawlahu*, not necessarily of the kindler’s inner chest. Light is on the walls. The room can be seen. For a moment the hypocrite walks as others walk.

Then the reversal: *dhahaba Allāhu bi-nūrihim*, Allah took away their light. The verb is *dhahaba bi-*, to go away with a thing, to remove it utterly. He does not say *adh-haba nūrahum* only; He says He went with their light, as if the light were escorted out. And it is *nūrihim*, their light, plural, after the singular kindler — a shift the rhetoricians love. The one who lit is a type; those who lose the light are a group. It is *nūr*, light, that is taken, not *nār*, fire. Ibn al-Qayyim, in his discussion of this parable in *Iʿlām al-muwaqqiʿīn* and in the *Madārij*, notes the precision: the fire’s heat and smoke, the harm of the flame, remain in meaning as punishment, while its useful brightness is removed. They keep the scorch and lose the sight. Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī both pause on this. The hypocrite remains in the heat of legal obligation, of exposure, of the coming Fire, and is denied the light of guidance that makes obligation a joy.

*Wa tarakahum fī ẓulumātin lā yubṣirūn*: He left them in darknesses, unable to see. *Ẓulumāt* is plural. The Qur'an almost always pluralizes darkness and singularizes light, because light is one in its source — “Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth” (24:35) — while darknesses are many: the darkness of *kufr*, of *nifāq*, of ignorance, of the grave, of the Fire. “He brings them out of darknesses into light” (2:257). Here the bringing-out is reversed. They are left, *tarakahum*, which is the leaving of abandonment, not the leaving of a gift. *Lā yubṣirūn* returns to the covering of the eyes in 2:7. The parable makes the covering visible: a man standing where a fire just was, the after-image fading, the night returning thicker than before. Anyone who has seen a campfire die in the desert knows that the darkness after light is worse than the darkness before it, because the eyes had opened and now cannot.

The mufassirūn connected this parable to the hypocrites of Madinah in a concrete way. They saw, by the Prophet’s (ﷺ) coming, a light of worldly benefit and a rumor of the unseen. They stepped into the circle. Then Allah (الله) withdrew from their hearts the light of *taṣdīq*, so that they remained in the circle’s heat — its wars, its laws, its recitations — without its illumination. On the Day of Judgment the same image is raised to the sky: the hypocrites will have a little light, then it will be struck out, and they will cry to the believers, “Wait for us, that we may borrow from your light” (57:13). 2:17 is the earthly rehearsal of 57:13. Ibn Kathīr cites this correspondence. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “The parable of the one who remembers his Lord and the one who does not is like the living and the dead” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī), and he said that faith wears out in the soul as a garment wears out, so ask Allah to renew faith in your hearts (reported by al-Ḥākim). Light that is not renewed is light that can be taken.

For a reader now the parable is about every borrowed brightness. There is the light of growing up in a Muslim home, which lights the surroundings — names, festivals, a moral vocabulary — without necessarily lighting the heart. There is the light of a conversion that was social, or emotional, or intellectual only, a fire kindled for warmth against a cold world, which Allah (الله) may leave as heat without *nūr* if it is not followed by the *īmān* of 2:3–4. There is the light of knowledge: a person kindles the fire of study, the room fills with concepts, and then the light is taken because the knowledge was for display, and he is left able to quote and unable to see. “The example of those who were entrusted with the Torah and then did not uphold it is like the donkey who carries volumes” (62:5). Donkeys feel the weight and not the meaning; the people of 2:17 stand in the smoke and not the sight.

The mercy inside the severity is that the verse is told to people who can still hear it. The abandoned of the parable are a warning, not yet, for every reciter, a finished autobiography. “Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth” (24:35): the source has not gone out. The servant who finds himself in a room where the fire of habit is still warm and the light of presence has dimmed is being told what is happening, so that he may ask, before the leaving is complete, for the light to be returned. The Prophet’s (ﷺ) daily supplication included, “O Allah, place light in my heart, light in my sight, light in my hearing… and make me light” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). That prayer is the opposite of this parable. 2:17 describes the taking. The prayer asks for the giving. Between them the believer lives, kindling, fearing, asking that what has lit the room around him will not be walked away with in the night.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:18

> They are ˹wilfully˺ deaf, dumb, and blind, so they will never return ˹to the Right Path˺.

**Commentary:**

The parable’s last frame is a medical sentence: *ṣummun bukmun ʿumyun fa-hum lā yarjiʿūn*, “They are wilfully deaf, dumb, and blind, so they will never return to the Right Path.” There is no verb of becoming. They are named as the conditions themselves — deafnesses, dumbnesses, blindnesses — three predicates in the nominative, thrown down like labels on a body. *Ṣumm* is the plural of *aṣamm*, deaf; *bukm* of *abkam*, mute; *ʿumy* of *aʿmā*, blind. The roots are physical. The Qur'an applies them to people whose ears, tongues, and eyes still work. “Have they not travelled through the land so that they have hearts with which to understand and ears with which to hear? For indeed, it is not the eyes that are blinded, but blinded are the hearts that are in the chests” (22:46). The wilfulness supplied by the translation is the wilfulness of 2:7 and 2:10: they closed the gates, so the gates are closed. Ibn ʿAbbās said they are deaf to the truth, mute from it, blind to it. Al-Ṭabarī: they do not hear it with an ear of acceptance, they do not speak it with a tongue of sincerity, they do not see it with an eye of insight. The senses that remain are for the world. The senses that were meant for revelation have been retired.

The order of the three is the order of reception, transmission, and perception. Hearing is first because this religion is heard: “So hear! And they will hear” is the rhythm of warning. The Prophet (ﷺ) was commanded, “Say, ‘I only warn you by revelation’” (21:45). To be deaf here is to have the *samʿ* of 2:7 sealed. Muteness is second because the tongue is the believer’s witness: he says *āmannā* and means it, he commands good, he forbids evil, he recites. The hypocrite’s tongue works in two rooms, as 2:14 showed, which is a kind of muteness toward the truth: he cannot speak it when it would cost him. Blindness is third because, after the fire of 2:17 has gone out, there is nothing to see. The three together are a complete unmaking of the human being as a receiver of guidance. “The worst of beasts with Allah are the deaf and dumb who do not reason” (8:22). “Do you think that most of them hear or reason? They are only like cattle; rather, they are further astray” (25:44). Cattle at least follow a shepherd. These do not return.

*Fa-hum lā yarjiʿūn*: so they do not return. *Rujūʿ* is going back, the return to a place one has left. What did they leave? The mufassirūn say: they do not return to guidance, to Islam as a reality, to the light that was around them, to Allah (الله). The Fātiḥah asked to be guided and not to be among the astray; this verse describes people who will not make that return journey. The negation is of their will and of their state, not of Allah’s (الله) power to bring back whom He wills. The door of repentance is open until the death-rattle, as the hadith says: “Allah accepts the repentance of the servant so long as he has not gurgling in the throat” (al-Tirmidhī). But the people described here are those who have made of deafness, muteness, and blindness a home. They do not *want* the path, so they do not return to it. Ibn Kathīr links *lā yarjiʿūn* to the fire: they do not return to the light that was taken. A man who cannot see cannot find the road back to a camp that has gone dark.

The verse is almost identical in cadence to 2:171, later in this sūrah, where the disbelievers who do not hear the call are “deaf, dumb, and blind, so they do not understand.” The difference is *lā yarjiʿūn* versus *lā yaʿqilūn*: the hypocrites of the fire-parable do not return; the disbelievers of the shepherd-parable do not reason. Return is the right verb for those who had kindled a fire and then lost it. They had a station to go back to. The open disbeliever of 2:6 never entered. This is why the hypocrites’ case is, in some texts, heavier: “The hypocrites will be in the lowest depth of the Fire” (4:145). They knew the way home and did not take it. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “The thing I fear most for you is the minor *shirk*.” They said, “What is the minor *shirk*?” He said, “Showing off” (Aḥmad). Showing off is a cousin of 2:14’s two rooms. He also said, “Whoever has a mustard seed of pride in his heart will not enter Paradise” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). Pride stops the return; it cannot bear to go back as a needy hearer, a speaker of truth, a seer who admits he had been blind.

Al-Qurṭubī and the jurists of the heart treat this verse as a warning against the death of the faculties by disuse. Hearing that is never used for the Qur'an becomes *ṣamm*. A tongue that is never used for truth, *dhikr*, and repair of relationships becomes *bukm* toward what matters. Eyes that are always on what is forbidden or always on distraction become *ʿumy* toward signs. “We will show them Our signs in the horizons and in themselves until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth” (41:53). Signs require a remaining sense. The people of 2:18 have no remaining sense for that showing. In a time when the ears are full of noise, the tongues are full of content, and the eyes are full of screens, the verse does not say that technology is *kufr*. It says that faculties can be so occupied that they are, for the purposes of return, as good as gone. The believer who is alarmed by that sentence still has hearing enough to be alarmed. The prescription is the opposite exercise: listen to the Book as the *muttaqūn* listen, speak as the truthful speak, look at the heavens and the earth as 2:164 will command. Return, while *rujūʿ* is still a verb that can take you as its subject. The next parable will show another weather — not a fire that dies, but a storm that never stops — for those who will not come back.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:19

> Or ˹those caught in˺ a rainstorm from the sky with darkness, thunder, and lightning. They press their fingers into their ears at the sound of every thunder-clap for fear of death. And Allah encompasses the disbelievers ˹by His might˺.

**Commentary:**

A second parable is offered with *aw*, “or,” not to cancel the fire but to add a weather: *aw ka-ṣayyibin mina al-samāʾi fīhi ẓulumātun wa raʿdun wa barqun yajʿalūna aṣābiʿahum fī ādhānihim mina al-ṣawāʿiqi ḥadhara al-mawti wa Allāhu muḥīṭun bil-kāfirīn*, “Or those caught in a rainstorm from the sky with darkness, thunder, and lightning. They press their fingers into their ears at the sound of every thunder-clap for fear of death. And Allah encompasses the disbelievers by His might.” *Ṣayyib* is a heavy rain, a downpour, from *ṣ-w-b*, to fall, to hit the mark, to pour. Rain in the Qur'an is often mercy: “And We sent down from the sky blessed water” (50:9). Here the same sky-water arrives as a storm in which the mercy is mixed with terror, because the people under it are not the people of 2:5. Ibn ʿAbbās and the early mufassirūn, as gathered by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, read the rain as the Qur'an itself — life for the soil of hearts — and the darknesses, thunder, and lightning as what the Qur'an contains of warning, threat, and the difficult commands that frighten the hypocrite. Another reading, not contradictory, is that the storm is the turmoil of the new religion in Madinah: its battles, its revelations that expose, its tests. The hypocrite lives under a sky that is always about to speak.

Three things are *in* the downpour: *ẓulumāt*, darknesses; *raʿd*, thunder; *barq*, lightning. Darknesses again in the plural, as in 2:17. Thunder is the sound of warning, the verses of threat, the news of the Day of Judgment. Lightning is the flash of clarity — a proof, a victory, a verse of promise — that briefly lights the ground. The hypocrite’s response is not to walk and not to look up. It is to put his fingers in his ears. *Yajʿalūna aṣābiʿahum fī ādhānihim*: they place their fingers in their ears. The Qur'an says *aṣābiʿ*, fingers, not fingertips, a well-known hyperbole of Arabic: they jam the whole finger as if they could cork the ear. *Mina al-ṣawāʿiq*, from the thunderbolts, the crashing strikes. *Ḥadhara al-mawt*, as a caution against death, in fear of death. Nūḥ’s (peace be upon him) people put their fingers in their ears in another way: “I called them, and they put their fingers in their ears, covered themselves with their garments, persisted, and were arrogant with great arrogance” (71:7). The gesture is the same across prophetic history: I will not hear, because hearing might kill the life I have chosen. The hypocrite fears that a verse of warning, if fully admitted, would be the death of his double life. So he corks the ear. He does not fear the real death. He fears the death of his arrangement.

Al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī pause on the psychology. The thunderbolt, *ṣāʿiqah*, is in reality more likely to kill whether the ear is open or not; fingers are not armor against the sky. The gesture is therefore irrational, which is the point. Panic does not calculate. It performs control where there is none. The hypocrite cannot stop revelation from arriving — the rain is *mina al-samāʾ*, from the sky, from above his jurisdiction — so he manages the only organ he thinks he owns. 2:7 had already said that the hearing is sealed; this verse shows the servant cooperating with the seal, stuffing the ear with his own hand. Ibn al-Qayyim wrote that people treat the Qur'an's threats as they treat thunder: a noise to be waited out. They do not treat them as they treat a fire in the house. The difference is *yaqīn* of the Hereafter, which 2:4 named as a mark of the *muttaqūn*. Without that certainty, thunder is weather. With it, thunder is a sermon.

The closing sentence lifts the camera from the crouching figures to the horizon: *wa Allāhu muḥīṭun bil-kāfirīn*. Allah (الله) encompasses the disbelievers. *Iḥāṭah* is to surround, to enclose, to have a thing within one’s knowledge and power so that nothing of it escapes. “And Allah encompasses them from behind” (85:20). “Indeed, He is encompassing of all things” (41:54). Fingers in ears do not create an outside. There is no outside. The storm is inside His *mulk*, and the men under the storm are inside it, and their fear of death is inside it, and the death they fear is His to give. The name *al-kāfirīn* at the end of a parable about hypocrites is a theological identification: this *nifāq*, at its root, is *kufr* that has not taken the courage to be open. Encompassment is the answer to their two rooms. They thought the private room was unencompassed. They thought the ear, once blocked, was unencompassed. The verse says the disbelievers are inside a circle whose circumference is the power of Allah (الله).

The Prophet (ﷺ) taught a different response to storm. When the sky growled, his face changed, and he walked in and out, and ʿĀʾishah (may Allah be pleased with her) asked him, and he said he feared as Ibrāhīm had feared, “This is a mercy from my Lord; I feared that it might be a punishment” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, in meaning). He did not put his fingers in his ears. He stood in the rain of ʿĀʾishah’s report at times, and let it soak him, and said it was newly come from its Lord. The believer hears thunder as a reminder, not as a thing to be cancelled. He seeks refuge, he does not seek deafness. “The thunder glorifies His praise, and the angels in awe of Him, and He sends the thunderbolts and strikes with them whom He wills” (13:13). That verse is the cosmology behind this parable. Thunder is *tasbīḥ* before it is threat. The hypocrite misses the *tasbīḥ* because he has decided that the only meaning of a loud sky is his possible death, and his possible death is the one thing he will not face.

In the present tense the rainstorm is every season in which the Book becomes loud: a death in the family, a public crisis, a verse that lands too close, a law of Allah (الله) that threatens a habit. The hypocrite’s technology of survival is volume control. Mute the reminder, curate the feed, leave the gathering when the sermon turns to the grave. The encompassing does not shrink. “Wherever you are, death will overtake you, even if you are in fortified towers” (4:78). The next verse will complete the weather: they walk in the flash and freeze in the dark, and if Allah (الله) willed He would take the hearing and the sight they are trying to manage with their fingers. 2:19 is the panic. 2:20 is the power under which the panic is small.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:20

> It is as if the lightning were about to snatch away their sight. Whenever lightning strikes, they walk in its light, but when darkness covers them, they stand still. Had Allah willed, He could have taken away their hearing and sight. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything.

**Commentary:**

The storm is slowed so that the hypocrite’s gait can be watched: *yakādu al-barqu yakhṭafu abṣārahum kullamā aḍāʾa lahum mashaw fīhi wa idhā aẓlama ʿalayhim qāmū wa law shāʾa Allāhu la-dhahaba bi-samʿihim wa abṣārihim inna Allāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr*, “It is as if the lightning were about to snatch away their sight. Whenever lightning strikes, they walk in its light, but when darkness covers them, they stand still. Had Allah willed, He could have taken away their hearing and sight. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything.” *Yakādu*, “it almost,” is the verb of near-occurrence. The lightning nearly snatches, *yakhṭafu*, their sights. *Khaṭf* is a sudden seizing, the snatch of a bird of prey, the grab that does not ask permission. Light, which in 2:17 was a mercy that could be taken, is here so sharp that it almost takes the eye itself. Revelation does both. It illuminates, and it can blind the one who stares at it with hostility. “We have made the signs clear” (2:118); “it is, for those who believe, a guidance and a healing, and for those who do not believe, there is deafness in their ears, and it is upon them blindness” (41:44). The same flash.

Then the famous walk: *kullamā aḍāʾa lahum mashaw fīhi wa idhā aẓlama ʿalayhim qāmū*. Whenever it lights up for them, they walk in it; when it darkens upon them, they stand. *Kullamā* is “every time,” a cycle, not a single night. This is the hypocrite’s religion as a weather report. When there is ease, spoils, a marriage, a victory at Badr, a verse of promise, they take a few steps with the believers. When there is a levy, a trench, a command that pinches, a verse that exposes, they freeze. Ibn ʿAbbās said: when Islam is in plenty they join; when a trial comes they stop. Al-Ṭabarī reports that they walk in the light of what they understand of the worldly benefit of Islam and halt at what they do not understand or do not like of its obligations. Ibn Kathīr ties the walk to their appearance in the mosque and the halt to their return to their satans. The image is unforgettable because it is so ordinary. Anyone who has crossed a field in a night storm knows this gait: a run in the flash, a statue in the black. It is not a path. It is a series of accidents of visibility. The people of 2:5 are *ʿalā hudan*, upon a road that does not depend on lightning. The people of 2:20 have no road. They have weather.

*Qāmū*, they stood, they halted, they froze. In other contexts *qāma* is a verb of dignity — establishing prayer, standing for justice. Here it is the standing of paralysis. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the hypocrites’ prayer as a standing of laziness: “When they stand for prayer they stand lazily, showing off to people, and they do not remember Allah except a little, wavering between that, neither to these nor to those” (4:142–143). Wavering is this walk. Neither to these nor to those is this halt. They are not with the believers when the sky is dark, and they are not openly with the disbelievers when the sky is bright. The Fātiḥah asked for a straight path; this verse shows the path that is not a path. Al-Qurṭubī notes that *mashaw fīhi* is walking *in* the light, not walking *by* a light they own. The illumination is never theirs. It is an event that happens *for them* (*lahum*) and then is withdrawn. They are consumers of other people’s weather.

The conditional that follows is a reminder of unused power: *wa law shāʾa Allāhu la-dhahaba bi-samʿihim wa abṣārihim*. If Allah (الله) had willed, He would have gone with their hearing and their sights — the same idiom as 2:17’s going with the light. He has not, yet. The fingers in the ears, the almost-snatched eyes, the sealed hearts of 2:7, are already a taking in part. The full taking remains within the *mashīʾah*, the will. This is both threat and unexplained respite. He could deafen them truly, blind them truly, and the parable would end. He leaves them the organs so that the proof remains, so that some of them may still, in a later flash, walk and not stop, so that the judgment is not that they could not hear but that they would not. “If Allah willed, He would have gathered them to the guidance” (6:35). “If We had willed, We would have given every soul its guidance” (32:13). The *law shāʾa* clauses of the Qur'an are the theology of a power that is not compelled to wait and that sometimes waits.

The sūrah’s first twenty verses then close with a sentence that could close the whole Qur'an: *inna Allāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr*. Indeed, Allah (الله) is over every thing All-Capable. *Qadīr* is from *q-d-r*, power, measure, decree. He is able, and He measures. The ability is not chaotic force; it is power with *qadar*. Every thing, *kulli shayʾ*, includes the hearts of 2:7, the increase of disease in 2:10, the taking of light in 2:17, the storm, the flash, the halt, the hearing, and the sight. It includes, for the believer who has been reading this opening as a mirror, the possibility of being made *mufliḥ* as in 2:5. The Prophet (ﷺ) taught the servant to ask from this power every morning and evening: “O Allah, I ask You for ability to do what You love,” and he taught, “When you ask, ask Allah, and when you seek help, seek help from Allah” (al-Tirmidhī), which is the Fātiḥah’s *iyyāka nastaʿīn* in a sentence. Capability, *qudrah*, is not a doctrine to frighten only. It is the reason asking is rational.

Thus the first panel of Sūrah al-Baqarah is complete. It began with letters whose meaning is a secret, then a Book without doubt, then three companies: the mindful who believe in the unseen and are successful; the disbelievers whose gates are sealed; the hypocrites whose speech is a trade in bankruptcy, whose fire dies, whose storm teaches them only to freeze. The last word is not the hypocrite’s halt. The last word is *qadīr*. In an age that believes itself to be the measure of all things — of truth, of weather, of the self — the verse returns measure to its Owner. The reader who has walked these twenty verses is meant to choose a gait. Walk upon guidance, as 2:5. Do not wait for lightning. And ask the One who could take hearing and sight to use them, instead, for return — the return 2:18 said some will never make, and that the living still can.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:21

> O humanity! Worship your Lord, Who created you and those before you, so that you may become mindful ˹of Him˺.

**Commentary:**

After twenty verses of portrait — the Book, the mindful, the sealed, the hypocrites — the sūrah turns from description to address, and the first word of that address is the widest word in the language of revelation: *yā ayyuhā al-nās*, “O humanity.” Not “O believers,” which will come later and often, and not “O Children of Israel,” which will come at 2:40, but *al-nās*, the people, the human kind, every descendant of Ādam. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr note that this is among the first direct commands of the *muṣḥaf* after the Fātiḥah’s petition, and that its audience is universal because its proof will be universal. The three companies have been shown. Now every reader, of whichever company, is called by the name he cannot refuse: human. The call is not to a tribe, a century, or a confession. It is to the creature as creature.

The command is *uʿbudū rabbakum*, “worship your Lord.” *Uʿbudū* is the imperative of *ʿibādah*, from the root *ʿ-b-d*, which yields the servant, the slave, the worshipper. In the language of the Arabs *ʿibādah* is the utmost humility joined to the utmost love; Ibn Taymiyyah’s famous definition — a name for everything Allah (الله) loves and is pleased with, of words and deeds, inward and outward — is the unfolding of this verb. The Fātiḥah taught the servant to say *iyyāka naʿbudu*, “You alone we worship.” This verse tells the whole species to enact that sentence. The object is *rabbakum*, your Lord, the same *Rabb* of 1:2, the Nurturer, Owner, and Sustainer. The possessive *your* is an argument: He is already yours as Lord; worship is not the invention of a relationship but the admission of one. You do not vote Him into lordship. You wake up inside it.

Then the reason, which is not a philosophical lemma but a biography of the body: *alladhī khalaqakum wa alladhīna min qablikum*, “Who created you and those before you.” *Khalaqa* is from *kh-l-q*, to create, to proportion, to bring into being from non-being. The Qur'an never tires of this proof because the creature never tires of forgetting it. “Does man not remember that We created him before, when he was nothing?” (19:67). “Were they created from nothing, or are they themselves the creators?” (52:35). The verse adds *wa alladhīna min qablikum* so that no generation may imagine itself a beginning. Your parents were created; their parents were created; the chain does not terminate in a tribe or a big bang that needs no Speaker. It terminates in the *Rabb*. Ibn ʿAbbās, as reported in the early tafsīr, treated this clause as the ground of the command: worship is owed to the One who originated you, not to the stones and rivals that did not. Al-Rāghib noted that *khalq* includes both bringing into existence and giving form; the Lord who is worshipped is the Lord who both started the human and shaped him, hearing, sight, and heart, as 16:78 says, “so that you might be grateful.”

The purpose clause is *laʿallakum tattaqūn*, “so that you may become mindful.” *Laʿalla* in the speech of Allah (الله) is not the *laʿalla* of human uncertainty. The mufassirūn have said it expresses a hoped-for outcome that, from the Lord’s side, is the wise purpose of the command. Worship is not an end that floats free of character. It is the road to *taqwā*, the same *taqwā* for which the Book of 2:2 is a guide. The circle closes. The *muttaqūn* were described; now the means of becoming one of them is named: worship the Lord who created you. Al-Qurṭubī writes that *taqwā* here includes both the fear that stops a person at the boundary of the forbidden and the covering that protects him from the Fire. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “The right of Allah upon the servants is that they worship Him and do not associate anything with Him, and the right of the servants upon Allah is that He will not punish whoever does not associate anything with Him” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). That hadith is 2:21 in prophetic prose: worship, without partner, as the creature’s first duty.

Theologically the verse is the first public statement of *tawḥīd al-ulūhiyyah* in al-Baqarah, built upon *tawḥīd al-rubūbiyyah*. Lordship is conceded even by many who will not worship: the pagans of Makkah admitted that Allah (الله) created the heavens and the earth (29:61). The verse refuses to let that admission remain theoretical. If He created you, He is the One to whom the forehead belongs. Ibn al-Qayyim observed that every call of the messengers began here: “Worship Allah; you have no deity other than Him” (7:59 and its sisters). Ādam (peace be upon him) will soon be placed on the earth as *khalīfah*; before that story is told, his children are told what the office is for. “I did not create jinn and mankind except to worship Me” (51:56). 2:21 is 51:56 as an imperative rather than as a report.

The address *yā ayyuhā al-nās* also rebukes two modern shrinkings of religion. One shrinking makes worship a private hobby of the “religious,” as if the rest of humanity were exempt by temperament. The verse does not say “O those inclined to spirituality.” Creation is the only membership required. The other shrinking makes worship a cultural identity, a heritage one inherits without kneeling. The verse does not say “remember your tradition.” It says *uʿbudū*, do the act. A person who has never placed his forehead on the ground has not yet answered this āyah, however many lectures he has heard about the Lord. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “The first deed for which the servant will be called to account on the Day of Resurrection is the prayer” (Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī), which is worship in its most repeated form. He also said, “Supplication is worship” (al-Tirmidhī, Abū Dāwūd), so that the poor, the sick, and the one who cannot stand still have a door.

For the reader who has just come through the parables of fire and storm, the verse is a mercy of simplicity. You need not remain a consumer of lightning. You are spoken to. You are told what to do, and why, and toward whom. Worship your Lord. He created you. He created those before you. The aim is that you become of the people for whom the Book is a guide. In an age that treats the self as self-made — the brand, the project, the “created myself” of the profile — this verse is a confiscation of the origin story. You were created. Those before you were created. The only reasonable next sentence is worship. The next verse will unfold the creation into earth, sky, rain, and fruit, so that the command is surrounded by gifts, and then it will forbid the setting up of equals. 2:21 is the door. 2:22 is the house inside which the door makes sense.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:22

> ˹He is the One˺ Who has made the earth a place of settlement for you and the sky a canopy; and sends down rain from the sky, causing fruits to grow as a provision for you. So do not knowingly set up equals to Allah ˹in worship˺.

**Commentary:**

The Lord who was to be worshipped because He created is now to be worshipped because He furnishes: *alladhī jaʿala lakumu al-arḍa firāshan wa al-samāʾa bināʾan wa anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-akhraja bihi mina al-thamarāti rizqan lakum fa-lā tajʿalū lillāhi andādan wa antum taʿlamūn*, “Who has made the earth a place of settlement for you and the sky a canopy; and sends down rain from the sky, causing fruits to grow as a provision for you. So do not knowingly set up equals to Allah in worship.” The verse is a single sentence of gifts ending in a prohibition. The grammar is the theology: because of all this, do not do that.

*Jaʿala* is to make, to appoint, to render a thing as something. The earth is appointed *firāsh*, a word from *f-r-sh*, to spread, to lay out a bed. The earth is not a trap and not a throne; it is a spread thing you can lie on, walk on, plant in, and trust not to throw you off. “He made the earth a carpet for you” is the sense. The sky is *bināʾ*, a building, a canopy, a raised construction without pillars that you can see. “He built the heaven” (79:27–28). “We made the sky a protected ceiling” (21:32). Ibn ʿAbbās and the early authorities, as al-Ṭabarī records, explained *firāsh* as a flattened, settled place, and *bināʾ* as a roof. The human being lives between a bed and a ceiling he did not nail. The pair is the pair of 20:53 and 40:64, and of 71:19–20: “Allah made the earth a spread for you, that you may follow its pathways.” Settlement, *qarār*, is a mercy. A planet that shook without cease, or a sky that leaked without measure, would not be a home. The Lord of 2:21 is the interior designer of 2:22.

Then the vertical gift: *wa anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan*, He sent down from the sky water. *Inzāl* again, the same root as revelation. Water descends as the Book descends. “And We sent down from the sky blessed water, and We caused to grow thereby gardens and grain of harvest” (50:9). “He is the One who sends the winds as good news before His mercy, until, when they have carried heavy clouds, We drive them to a dead land and send down thereby water, and bring forth thereby all the fruits” (7:57). *Fa-akhraja bihi mina al-thamarāti rizqan lakum*: He brought out by it, from the fruits, a provision for you. *Rizq* is more than food; it is the allotted provision, the portion. The fruits are named because they are the visible, colored, sweet end of a chain the human being does not run: vapor, cloud, drop, soil, root, flower, fruit. “Have you seen the water that you drink? Is it you who brought it down from the clouds, or are We the Bringer-down?” (56:68–69). Al-Rāghib noted that *thamarāt* covers all produce that is gathered; the verse is not a botany lesson but a table laid.

The *fāʾ* of *fa-lā tajʿalū* is the *fāʾ* of consequence. After the bed, the roof, the rain, and the fruit, the prohibition arrives: do not set up for Allah *andād*. *Nidd* (plural *andād*) is an equal, a rival, a like, one who is placed opposite another as a match. In the religious lexicon it is the partner worshipped or feared or loved as Allah (الله) is loved. “Yet there are some who take others as Allah’s equal — they love them as they should love Allah — but the true believers love Allah even more” (2:165). The Prophet (ﷺ) was asked which sin is greatest. He said, “To set up a rival to Allah while He created you” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). That hadith is this verse’s last clause given a human voice. The most grievous *shirk* is not a failure of cosmology; it is a failure of gratitude. He made the earth a bed, and you put an idol on it. He sent the rain, and you thanked a statue, a system, a self.

*Wa antum taʿlamūn*, “while you know,” is the sting. The mufassirūn, including Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, and Qatādah as cited by Ibn Kathīr, explained: you know that He created you, that the idols created nothing, that the rivals did not send the rain. The pagans of Makkah, when asked who created the heavens and the earth, said “Allah” (31:25). Their *shirk* was not atheism. It was the addition of equals in worship, intercession, and law after the confession of creation. “We only worship them that they may bring us nearer to Allah” (39:3). 2:22 closes that excuse. Knowledge of lordship, without exclusive worship, is knowledge that accuses. Al-Qurṭubī remarks that the verse is therefore a proof against those who say the Arabs did not know their Lord; they knew, and they associated. Ibn Taymiyyah made this the axis of the Makkan conflict: *tawḥīd al-rubūbiyyah* was largely granted; *tawḥīd al-ulūhiyyah* was the battle.

Theologically the verse teaches that the signs in creation are not optional poetry. They are premises of a legal conclusion: no *nidd*. The earth as bed is not an invitation to earth-worship; the sky as canopy is not an invitation to star-worship; the rain is not a nature-god. Each gift is a finger pointing past itself. “Do not prostrate to the sun or the moon, but prostrate to Allah who created them” (41:37). The modern forms of *nidd* are less often stone. They are the market that claims to provide, the state that claims to encompass, the desire that claims to command, the celebrity that claims love due to the Creator, the algorithm that claims to know what the servant should want. A person may recite “there is no god but Allah” and still live as if provision came from the paycheck alone, as if the sky were empty, as if the fruit were a product without a Sender. *Wa antum taʿlamūn* still applies. You know, at some depth, that you did not spread the earth.

The Prophet (ﷺ) used to look at the sky and recite verses of creation, and he taught the supplication upon seeing the new moon and upon rain, so that the gifts of 2:22 would not pass as weather. He said, “Allah is pleased with the servant who eats a bite and praises Him for it, or takes a drink and praises Him for it” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). That pleasure is the opposite of *andād*: the bite is traced to the Sender, not to a rival. The believer who recites this verse in a city of concrete, where the *firāsh* is buried under asphalt and the *bināʾ* is cut by towers, is not exempt. The earth is still spread beneath the asphalt. The sky is still a canopy above the towers. The fruit still came by water he did not send. The prohibition still stands. Do not set up equals, knowingly. The next two verses will take the same audience — people who know, people who doubt — and challenge them about the Book itself. If they cannot deny the rain, let them try to produce a sūrah. Creation was the first proof. Revelation will be the second.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:23

> And if you are in doubt about what We have revealed to Our servant, then produce a sûrah like it and call your helpers other than Allah, if what you say is true.

**Commentary:**

The gifts of earth and sky now give way to a challenge about speech: *wa in kuntum fī raybin mimmā nazzalnā ʿalā ʿabdinā fa-tū bi-sūratin min mithlihi wa dʿū shuhadāʾakum min dūni Allāhi in kuntum ṣādiqīn*, “And if you are in doubt about what We have revealed to Our servant, then produce a sūrah like it and call your helpers other than Allah, if what you say is true.” The *rayb* that 2:2 denied of the Book is now placed, hypothetically, in the chest of the hearer. *If* you are in that agitation of mistrust about what We sent down — not because the Book contains *rayb*, but because you have imported it — then do not merely talk. Produce.

*Nazzalnā* is the intensive of sending down, the form often used of the Qur'an in stages. *ʿAlā ʿabdinā*, “upon Our servant,” is a title of honor. The Prophet (ﷺ) is not named here as “Our messenger” or “Muḥammad,” but as *ʿabd*, servant, the same word from which *uʿbudū* of 2:21 was built. Ibn ʿAbbās and the mufassirūn have said that to be called *ʿabd* of Allah (الله) in a context of revelation is the highest of names, because it is the name of the night of the *Isrāʾ*: “Glory be to Him who took His servant by night” (17:1). The challenge is not issued about a genius’s poetry. It is issued about what was sent down upon a servant. The human being is not the source; he is the recipient. This already answers one kind of doubt: that Muḥammad (ﷺ) composed the Book. If he did, you who share his language, his market, and in some cases his tribe, can compose the like. The servant is one of you. The Speech is not.

*Fa-tū bi-sūratin min mithlihi*, then bring a sūrah of its like. *Sūrah* is a chapter, a fenced portion, from a root that includes the idea of an enclosure or a rank. The smallest unit of the challenge is not the whole Book, nor even ten sūrahs (as in an earlier formulation, 11:13), but a single sūrah. Al-Baqarah itself is long; al-Kawthar is three verses. The challenge, as the scholars of *iʿjāz* have noted, was reduced over time in the Qur'an’s own wording: the whole Book (17:88), then ten sūrahs (11:13), then one sūrah (10:38 and this verse), then, in the widest form, “let them produce a speech like it” (52:34). The reduction is not a retreat. It is a tightening of the noose. If you cannot bring three verses of this kind, your *rayb* is not scholarship. It is refusal.

*Wa dʿū shuhadāʾakum min dūni Allāh*, and call your witnesses, your helpers, your presenters of testimony, apart from Allah (الله). *Shuhadāʾ* here, as Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, and al-Ṭabarī explain, are the partners, the gods, the orators, the jinn, anyone you would summon as aid or as audience to attest that you have succeeded. “Other than Allah” because Allah (الله) will not help a lie against His own Speech. Sūrah al-Isrāʾ makes the summons even wider: “Say, if mankind and the jinn gathered to produce the like of this Qur'an, they could not produce the like of it, even if they were to each other assistants” (17:88). The challenge is public, collective, and open-ended. It is not a riddle solved in a court. It is a standing dare.

*In kuntum ṣādiqīn*, if you are truthful — truthful in the claim that this is human speech, or magic, or borrowed tales, or the product of a man who “was taught” (44:14). Truth, in this verse, would look like a sūrah. Fourteen centuries have not produced one that the Arabs who knew their own tongue accepted as a match. The attempted answers — the sentences of Musaylimah the Liar, the later pastiches — became, in the memory of the language, proofs of the challenge rather than replies to it. Al-Qurṭubī and the imams of *balāghah* described the inimitability as a meeting of unique composition, unique news of the unseen, unique legislation, and unique effect on the heart. One need not choose a single theory of *iʿjāz* to feel the verse. The servant recited, and the masters of the market of ʿUkāẓ could not sit down and write the like.

The Prophet (ﷺ) lived inside this challenge. Abū Jahl and the orators of Quraysh heard it. Al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah, a judge of poetry, is reported to have said that the speech was not the speech of humans or of jinn, that it had a sweetness and a freshness, that it was high and nothing was higher — and then he chose, against his own ear, to call it magic (74:24). That story, preserved by the mufassirūn at the opening of al-Muddaththir, is 2:23 from the other side: a man who could not produce the like, and who would not submit. Ibn Kathīr notes that the verse is a mercy to the sincere doubter as well as a humiliation to the obstinate. A doubt that is willing to test itself by production is a doubt that can end. A doubt that only talks is the *rayb* of 2:2’s opposite.

For the present age the challenge is often misunderstood as a boast about Arabic style that a non-Arab cannot feel. The verse does not require every reader to be a critic of *badīʿ*. It requires the claimant to match what he dismisses. If the Book is “only” a seventh-century text, the libraries of the world are open; bring a sūrah. If it is “only” psychology, produce a psychology that has held a civilization’s prayer, law, and memory with this compactness. The failure is not that no beautiful books have been written since. The failure is that none has stood in this place: recited as the speech of the Lord, unaltered, calling all humanity to worship, unmatched by those who knew the tongue best at the moment of its appearing. The next verse will close the door even on the attempt: you will not do it. 2:23 is the invitation to try. 2:24 is the announcement that the try is already known to fail, and that the real risk is not literary embarrassment but the Fire.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:24

> But if you are unable to do so—and you will never be able to do so—then fear the Fire fuelled with people and stones, which is prepared for the disbelievers.

**Commentary:**

The challenge is given a deadline of forever: *fa-in lam tafʿalū wa lan tafʿalū fa-ttaqū al-nāra allatī waqūduhā al-nāsu wa al-ḥijāratu uʿiddat lil-kāfirīn*, “But if you are unable to do so — and you will never be able to do so — then fear the Fire fuelled with people and stones, which is prepared for the disbelievers.” Two tenses of inability sit side by side. *In lam tafʿalū* is the condition of the present and the near future: if you have not done it, if you do not. *Wa lan tafʿalū* is the particle *lan* plus the imperfect, which in the grammar of Arabic is a negation of the future with emphasis: you will not, you will never. Ibn ʿAbbās and the mufassirūn treated *lan* here as news from the Unseen. Allah (الله) is not guessing that the Arabs will fail. He is informing them that the failure is already decreed as a fact of history. Fourteen centuries later the news has not needed an amendment. Al-Zamakhsharī, who as a Muʿtazilī made much of *lan* as intensification, and the Sunni rhetoricians after him, agreed on the force: this is not a taunt that might be luckily answered. It is a report.

The *fāʾ* of *fa-ttaqū* is again consequence. If you cannot answer the Book with a book, then answer it with *taqwā* — here, fear of the Fire. The same root *w-q-y* that made the *muttaqūn* of 2:2 and the *tattaqūn* of 2:21 now takes a direct object of terror: protect yourselves from the Fire. *Taqwā* of Allah (الله) and *taqwā* of the Fire are not two religions. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “Whoever fears sets out at night, and whoever sets out at night reaches the destination; the merchant of Allah is not poor; the commodity of Allah is dear; the commodity of Allah is Paradise” (al-Tirmidhī), and he used to seek refuge from the Fire in every prayer. “Protect yourselves from the Fire, even with half a date in charity” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The verse moves the hearer from a literary contest he will lose to a moral contest he might still win. You cannot produce a sūrah. You can still fear.

The Fire is described with a fuel: *waqūduhā al-nāsu wa al-ḥijārah*, its fuel is people and stones. *Waqūd* is that by which a fire is kindled and fed. The people are the disbelievers themselves; they are not only the inhabitants of the Fire but its firewood. “Then fear the Fire whose fuel is people and stones” is repeated at 66:6, addressed there to the believing household: protect yourselves and your families. Ibn Masʿūd, as reported by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, said the stones are stones of sulfur, more noxious in heat than ordinary rock. Other early authorities said they are the stone idols that were worshipped: the *andād* of 2:22, the rivals set up knowingly, now burning with their worshippers. “Indeed, you and what you worship besides Allah are firewood for Hell; you will surely arrive at it” (21:98). Both readings can stand together. The Fire is fed by bodies and by the false gods those bodies served. The humiliation is exact: the stone you thought would intercede is the stone that burns you.

*Uʿiddat lil-kāfirīn*, it has been prepared for the disbelievers. The verb is past. The Fire is not an improvisation of the Last Day. It is ready. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “When Allah created Paradise, He said to Jibrīl, ‘Go and look at it.’ He looked at it and said, ‘By Your might, no one will hear of it but will enter it.’ Then it was surrounded with hardships. He said, ‘Go and look at it.’ He said, ‘By Your might, I fear that no one will enter it.’ When He created the Fire, He said, ‘Go and look at it.’ He said, ‘By Your might, no one will hear of it but will avoid it.’ Then it was surrounded with desires. He said, ‘Go and look at it.’ He said, ‘By Your might, I fear that no one will be saved from it’” (Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī). Preparation is the Lord’s justice in advance of the crime, not a trap. “These two enemies have disputed about their Lord. As for those who disbelieve, garments of fire will be cut for them” (22:19). The garments are already cut.

Al-Qurṭubī and the theologians discuss whether *uʿiddat lil-kāfirīn* means it is prepared only for them, so that sinful believers are not its intended residents, or whether it means it is prepared as a recompense of *kufr* while not denying that some of the people of *tawḥīd* may be punished and then removed. Ahl al-Sunnah hold that the Fire is eternal for the disbelievers, and that whoever has in his heart a mustard seed of faith will not remain there forever, as the hadith of intercession and the hadith of the man who is taken out with no deed but the testimony declare (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). This verse names the Fire’s purpose and its people. It does not close the separate discussion of the sinful monotheist. The immediate audience of 2:23–24, those who doubt the revelation and will never match it, are being told that their literary failure is not the main loss. The main loss is the Fire prepared for those who cover the truth.

The pairing of inability and Fire is pastoral. Some hearts are moved by inimitability; some are moved by threat; the Qur'an uses both in two verses. Ibn al-Qayyim wrote that hope and fear are the two wings of the bird, and that a wing of proof without a wing of warning does not fly. 2:23 is the proof; 2:24 is the warning. The next verse will be the hope: good news of Gardens for those who believe and do good. The three verses together are the religion’s triangle — miracle, Fire, Paradise — set at the gate of the longest sūrah so that no one enters the laws of fasting and debt without knowing what is at stake.

For a reader today the sentence “you will never be able to do so” is either arrogance or prophecy. If the Book is human, the claim is arrogance and should have been falsified in the first generation, when the motive and the linguistic skill were at their peak. It was not. If the Book is from Allah (الله), the claim is a small piece of the Unseen, like the seal of 2:7 and the *qadīr* of 2:20. The practical fruit is *fa-ttaqū al-nār*. Fear the Fire. Do not wait until you have settled every literary theory. The fuel is people. People are not metaphors. The Prophet (ﷺ) described it until faces changed, and then he said, “Shall I not tell you of something that will extinguish it?” and he named *tawḥīd*, prayer, and charity in various teachings. The sūrah will legislate those extinguishers at length. Here it only says: you cannot write this Book. Fear what the Book warns of. Then listen, in the next verse, to what it promises.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:25

> Give good news ˹O Prophet˺ to those who believe and do good that they will have Gardens under which rivers flow. Whenever provided with fruit, they will say, “This is what we were given before,” for they will be served fruit that looks similar ˹but tastes different˺. They will have pure spouses, and they will be there forever.

**Commentary:**

The Fire of the previous verse is answered at once by a command of glad tidings: *wa bashshir alladhīna āmanū wa ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti anna lahum jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru*, “Give good news to those who believe and do good that they will have Gardens under which rivers flow.” *Bashshir* is addressed to the Prophet (ﷺ). His office is not only *indhār*, warning, as in 2:6; it is *tabshīr*, the announcing of what makes the heart glad. “We have sent you as a witness, a bringer of good news, and a warner” (33:45). After the challenge and the Fire, the servant who might have stood frozen under 2:24 is told that there is another prepared place, and that it has people: those who have *īmān* and *ʿamal ṣāliḥ*. The two are not rivals. Belief without good work is the claim of 2:8; work without belief is the husk of show. “Whoever does good, whether male or female, while being a believer, We will surely cause him to live a good life” (16:97). Al-Baqarah will later define righteousness at 2:177 as a joining of creed, charity, prayer, fidelity, and patience. This verse states the join in two verbs.

*Jannāt*, gardens, is plural. The root *j-n-n* is to conceal, to cover; a *jannah* is a garden whose trees conceal the soil, and *jinn* are the concealed, and the embryo is *janīn*. Paradise is named from abundance of cover and shade, the opposite of the exposed blaze. *Tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār*, rivers running from beneath them, is the Qur'an's most repeated picture of the Garden, appearing dozens of times. Water that in 2:22 descended as rain now flows as a permanent river under the feet. Ibn ʿAbbās and the mufassirūn described rivers of water, milk, wine, and honey, as 47:15 details: “a parable of the Garden promised to the righteous: in it are rivers of water unaltered, rivers of milk whose taste does not change, rivers of wine delicious to the drinkers, and rivers of purified honey.” The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “In Paradise there is a tree under whose shade a rider would travel for a hundred years and not cross it” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim), and he said that the Garden has what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and no human heart has conceived (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, from the hadith qudsī). 2:25 is the first opening of that unseen in this sūrah.

Then a detail that is almost homely: *kullamā ruziqū minhā min thamaratin rizqan qālū hādhā alladhī ruziqnā min qablu wa utū bihi mutashābihan*, whenever they are provided from it a provision of fruit, they say, “This is what we were provided before,” and they are given it resembling. The inhabitants of Paradise recognize the shape of a fruit — a pomegranate, a grape, a date — and name it by the name they knew in the world, and then the taste is other. Ibn ʿAbbās said: the appearance is similar; the flavor is not. Al-Ṭabarī reports that some of the *salaf* said they will think it the fruit of the world, then find it new. The word *mutashābih* is the same root as the *mutashābih* of the unclear verses (3:7), but here it is resemblance of form. The theological mercy of the detail is continuity. Paradise is not a erasure of the human being’s memory of apples. It is the apple raised to a degree the earth could not bear. “They will have whatever they wish therein, and with Us is more” (50:35). The more includes the surprise inside the familiar.

*Wa lahum fīhā azwājun muṭahharah*, and they will have therein spouses purified. *Azwāj* is pairs, spouses, the matching of one with another. *Muṭahharah* is the passive of purification: they are made pure, not merely well-behaved. The mufassirūn, including Ibn ʿAbbās, said purified from menstruation, from filth, from harm of character, from the hurts of the world. Al-Qurṭubī adds purification of heart and tongue. The Qur'an speaks of the spouses of the Garden in several tones — companions of modest gaze (37:48), the *ḥūr ʿīn* of the later sūrahs, and, as a comfort to the believers of this world, reunion with righteous wives (43:70). This verse does not specify further. It names purity as the essential quality. The Prophet (ﷺ) said that the first group to enter Paradise will have faces like the moon, and that they will not spit, nor blow their noses, nor relieve themselves, and that their spouses will be the *ḥūr al-ʿīn* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). Purity is the atmosphere of the place. What made the world heavy — decay, betrayal, the uncleanness of body and of speech — is lifted.

*Wa hum fīhā khālidūn*, and they are therein forever. *Khulūd* is remaining without end. The same word will close 2:39 of the Fire. Two eternities, two companies. The Qur'an will not let Paradise be a holiday from which one is sent home. “They will not taste death therein except the first death” (44:56). The Prophet (ﷺ) said that it will be called out: “You will be healthy and never sick, you will live and never die, you will be young and never age, you will be in bliss and never miserable” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). Forever is the answer to the fear that every joy of this world carries in its belly: this will end. In the Garden the joy does not end, and therefore it does not poison itself with dread.

The placement after 2:24 is the Qur'an's habitual justice of pair. It does not mention the Fire to finish in despair, and it does not mention the Garden to finish in presumption. Ibn al-Qayyim wrote that the heart is turned between *khawf* and *rajāʾ*, fear and hope, and that the recitation of these two verses in sequence is a training of that turning. The challenge of 2:23 told the mind it cannot match the Book; the Fire told the soul to protect itself; the Garden tells the soul what it is protecting itself *for*. A religion of only threat produces hiding; a religion of only gardens produces play. Al-Baqarah, at its gate, refuses both reductions.

For the believer now, “believe and do good” is the daily condition of a hope that is not cheap. Good news is not given to those who say *āmannā* as in 2:8. It is given to those whose faith has hands. The fruits that look similar and taste different are also a parable of this life’s worship: the prayer you pray tomorrow looks like the prayer you prayed today; the taste, if the heart is present, is not the same. The purified spouses and the forever are not an escape from marriage and time in this world; they are the perfection of companionship and the end of the clock that makes companionship anxious. Recite the verse as *bushrā*, as it was commanded to be given. Then do the two verbs that qualify you to hear it as yours: believe, and do good.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:26

> Surely Allah does not shy away from using the parable of a mosquito or what is even smaller. As for the believers, they know that it is the truth from their Lord. And as for the disbelievers, they argue, “What does Allah mean by such a parable?” Through this ˹test˺, He leaves many to stray, and guides many. And He leaves none to stray except the rebellious—

**Commentary:**

The sūrah has already used fire and storm as *mathal*. Now it defends the method itself, even when the image is small: *inna Allāha lā yastaḥyī an yaḍriba mathalan mā baʿūḍatan fa-mā fawqahā*, “Surely Allah does not shy away from using the parable of a mosquito or what is even smaller.” *Yastaḥyī* is from *ḥayāʾ*, shyness, shame, the shrinking from a thing because it is beneath one’s dignity. Human speakers shy from low examples when they wish to seem grand. Allah (الله) does not. The mosquito, *baʿūḍah*, is among the smallest of flying things that still have a name; *fa-mā fawqahā* is “what is above it” in smallness — what is even tinier — though some of the *salaf* read it as what is larger. Al-Ṭabarī prefers the first: the argument is that no creature is too slight to be a sign. “What does Allah mean by such a parable?” will be the objectors’ question. The answer is: He means what He means by the spider (29:41), the fly (22:73), the donkey (62:5), and the seed that sprouts seven ears (2:261). Truth does not become untrue by being housed in a small body.

The occasion of revelation, as Ibn Masʿūd, Ibn ʿAbbās, and others reported and as Ibn Kathīr records, is that when Allah (الله) struck the parables of the spider and the fly, the hypocrites and the pagans said, “What has this to do with Allah? A spider? A fly?” They treated the lowliness of the image as an insult to the Speaker. This verse answers that the Speaker is not insulted. *Ḥayāʾ* as a divine attribute, in the hadith “Your Lord is Modest and Generous; He is shy to let His servant raise his hands to Him and put them down empty” (Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī), is a shyness of generosity, not a shyness of status. He does not shy from truth, however small the creature that carries it. Al-Rāghib noted that *ḍarb al-mathal* is the striking of a likeness so that the hidden becomes open. A mosquito can open a door that a throne cannot.

Then the audience splits, as the sūrah has been splitting since 2:2: *fa-ammā alladhīna āmanū fa-yaʿlamūna annahu al-ḥaqqu min rabbihim*, as for those who believe, they know that it is the truth from their Lord. Knowledge here is not the knowledge of entomology. It is the knowledge that whatever Allah (الله) says is *ḥaqq*, reality that does not fail, from *rabbihim*, their Lord — the same Lord of 2:21. The believers of 2:5 were upon guidance from their Lord; here they recognize the Lord’s voice in a mosquito. *Wa ammā alladhīna kafarū fa-yaqūlūna mādhā arāda Allāhu bi-hādhā mathalan*, and as for those who disbelieve, they say, “What did Allah intend by this as a parable?” The question is not a student’s question. It is a sneer dressed as inquiry. The Qur'an quotes it so that the sneer will be remembered as part of the Book it tried to belittle.

*Yuḍillu bihi kathīran wa yahdī bihi kathīran*, He misleads by it many and guides by it many. The same *mathal* is a fork. This is the law already seen at 2:10 and 9:124–125: revelation increases and decreases according to the vessel. Ibn Taymiyyah and Ibn al-Qayyim returned often to this verse as the key of *hidāyah* and *iḍlāl*. Allah (الله) is the one who guides and the one who leaves to stray; the *bihi*, “by it,” is the parable as instrument. The parable does not contain two messages. It contains one, which becomes guidance in a believing chest and error in a rebellious one. “By it He leaves many to stray, and guides many” is therefore not a statement that the Book is ambiguous in itself. It is a statement that hearts are not equal. Al-Qurṭubī warns against the Qadarī reading that would make Allah (الله) unjust in misleading, and against the Jabrī reading that would make the servant a stone. The next clause settles the justice: *wa mā yuḍillu bihi illā al-fāsiqīn*, and He misleads by it none except the rebellious.

*Fāsiqīn* is from *f-s-q*, to go out, to exit. The date that leaves its skin is *fāsiqah* in the old tongue; the *fāsiq* is the one who has exited from obedience, from the covenant, from the enclosure of command. The next verse will define this exit in three acts. Here the word is enough to protect the verse from fatalism. Those who are led astray by the mosquito are those who had already stepped out. The guidance of the many is a grace; the straying of the many is a judgment upon *fisq* already chosen. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “No one enters Paradise who has in his heart a mustard seed of pride, and no one enters the Fire who has in his heart a mustard seed of faith” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, in meaning across reports), and he said that Allah (الله) is *ḥayiyy*, modest. The modest Lord who does not shy from a mosquito also does not stray a servant who wants the straight path. “Those who strive in Our cause, We will surely guide them to Our ways” (29:69).

The contemporary bite of the verse is against two temperaments. One is the temperament that cannot bear a religion with flies and spiders in it, that wants only sublime abstractions and is embarrassed by the concrete. The Qur'an will not be edited into a salon. The other is the temperament that uses every humble image as a reason to leave: “this cannot be from God; God would speak like a philosopher.” Both temperaments are in the question “What does Allah mean by such a parable?” The believer’s opposite is *yaʿlamūna annahu al-ḥaqq*. He does not need the mosquito to be majestic. He needs it to be from his Lord. In an age of scorn — scorn of the small, scorn of the old Book, scorn of the believer who takes a fly as a sign — 2:26 is permission to remain unembarrassed. Allah (الله) is not embarrassed. Why would His servant be? The next verse will show what the rebellious do when they have exited: they break the covenant, cut the ties, and spoil the land. The mosquito was only the test that revealed the exit.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:27

> those who violate Allah’s covenant after it has been affirmed, break whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained, and spread corruption in the land. It is they who are the ˹true˺ losers.

**Commentary:**

The *fāsiqūn* of the previous verse are now given a job description in three clauses: *alladhīna yanquḍūna ʿahda Allāhi min baʿdi mīthāqihi wa yaqṭaʿūna mā amara Allāhu bihi an yūṣala wa yufsidūna fī al-arḍi ulāʾika humu al-khāsirūn*, “those who violate Allah’s covenant after it has been affirmed, break whatever ties Allah has ordered to be maintained, and spread corruption in the land. It is they who are the true losers.” The relative pronoun links this verse to the last word of 2:26. Rebellion is not a mood. It is a sequence of cuttings.

*Yanquḍūna* is from *n-q-ḍ*, to undo a rope, to unravel a structure, to violate a pact. *ʿAhd Allāh*, the covenant of Allah (الله), is a phrase the mufassirūn have read at more than one level, and the levels stack rather than cancel. Ibn ʿAbbās and others said it is the covenant taken from the children of Ādam, “Am I not your Lord?” (7:172), the *mīthāq* of original testimony. Others said it is the covenant of what Allah (الله) has commanded and forbidden in revelation, the pact that every Book has carried. Others, looking at the Madinan scene, said it is the covenant of the People of the Book to believe in the unlettered Prophet (ﷺ) when he came, a covenant this sūrah will soon press upon the Children of Israel at 2:40–41. Al-Ṭabarī includes the general: every contract Allah (الله) has bound upon His servants. *Min baʿdi mīthāqihi*, after its confirmation, after it has been tied. *Mīthāq* is a covenant made firm, from *w-th-q*, to bind securely. The crime is not ignorance of a rumor. It is the undoing of a rope already knotted. “Those who break the covenant of Allah after its binding, and cut what Allah has commanded to be joined, and spread corruption in the land — those have the curse, and they have the worst home” (13:25). 2:27 and 13:25 are sister verses.

The second clause is *wa yaqṭaʿūna mā amara Allāhu bihi an yūṣala*, they cut what Allah (الله) has commanded to be joined. *Qaṭʿ* is cutting; *waṣl* is joining. The most famous object of this command is *ṣilat al-raḥim*, the joining of the ties of kinship. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “The womb is attached to the Throne, saying: Whoever joins me, Allah will join him, and whoever cuts me, Allah will cut him” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, in close wordings). He said, “The joiner of kinship is not the one who reciprocates. The joiner is the one who, when his kinship is cut, joins it” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī). But the mufassirūn did not confine the clause to family. Qatādah and others said it includes every joining Allah (الله) ordered: faith in all the prophets rather than some, joining of the heart to the Book, joining of the community of believers, joining of word to deed. Ibn Kathīr gathers these. To cut is the *fāsiq*’s method. He unravels the covenant, then he severs the bonds that the covenant had made sacred.

The third clause returns to a word the sūrah has already used of the hypocrites: *wa yufsidūna fī al-arḍ*, and they spread corruption in the land. 2:11–12 named the people who claim to be peacemakers and are corruptors. Here the same *ifsād* is the last of three marks of *fisq*. The order is inward to outward: first the pact with Allah (الله) is undone, then the human ties are cut, then the earth itself is spoiled. A land of cut families, broken treaties, and discarded revelation cannot remain *ṣāliḥ*. “Do not spread corruption in the land after it has been set right” (7:56). The earth was set right by *tawḥīd* and by joined wombs and by kept promises. The rebellious unmake all three.

The verdict is *ulāʾika humu al-khāsirūn*, those — they — are the losers. *Khusrān* is loss, the commercial opposite of the *ribḥ* that 2:16 denied the hypocrites and of the *falāḥ* that 2:5 gave the mindful. The definite and the pronoun are again *ḥaṣr*: they are the losers, they and not the people they may have cheated in the short run. “The losers are those who lose themselves and their families on the Day of Resurrection. Unquestionably, that is the clear loss” (39:15). Al-Qurṭubī notes that a man may gain a kingdom by cutting a tie and still be *khāsir*, because he has spent the covenant for a world that ends. Ibn al-Qayyim wrote that every sin is a loss of something of the heart, and that the three acts of this verse are the loss of religion, the loss of social mercy, and the loss of the earth’s right — a complete bankruptcy.

The Prophet (ﷺ) placed joining and cutting at the center of the coming accounting. “The merciful are shown mercy by the Most Merciful. Be merciful to those on the earth, and the One above the heaven will be merciful to you” (Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī). He said that the most grievous of the major sins include *shirk* and cutting the womb (in the well-known lists of *kabāʾir*). 2:27 is those lists in a single āyah of definition. Theologically it also explains how a parable of a mosquito can lead a person astray. The one who has already unbound the covenant will not receive a small sign as a small mercy. He will receive it as an insult, as 2:26 quoted, and the insult will confirm him in his exit. The one who still holds the rope will receive the mosquito as *ḥaqq* from his Lord.

For the present day the three clauses are a diagnostic that does not require a seventh-century costume. Covenants with Allah (الله) are unbound when prayer is discarded, when the lawful and forbidden are rewritten, when the testimony of “Am I not your Lord?” is drowned in self-creation. Ties commanded to be joined are cut when the family is treated as an optional lifestyle, when the *ummah* is only an online aesthetic, when the poor are unjoined from the wealth of the rich. Corruption in the land is the visible remainder: plundered earth, broken trust, blood that is cheap. The verse does not let the reader call these “complex modern problems” as if they had no moral name. Their name is *fisq*, and their end is *khusrān*. The losers of 2:27 stand opposite the successful of 2:5. Between them the sūrah now turns to a question that should make the unbinding impossible: how can you deny the One who gave you life?

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:28

> How can you deny Allah? You were lifeless and He gave you life, then He will cause you to die and again bring you to life, and then to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned.

**Commentary:**

The sūrah asks a question that is not a request for information: *kayfa takfurūna billāhi wa kuntum amwātan fa-aḥyākum thumma yumītukum thumma yuḥyīkum thumma ilayhi turjaʿūn*, “How can you deny Allah? You were lifeless and He gave you life, then He will cause you to die and again bring you to life, and then to Him you will all be returned.” *Kayfa* is “how,” the interrogative of manner, used here for *taʿajjub*, wonder that borders on rebuke. How — by what route, by what logic, by what right — do you cover the truth of Allah (الله) when your own biography is His signature? Al-Zamakhsharī and the rhetoricians treated the verse as an argument from the nearer sign: the self. “And in yourselves; do you not see?” (51:21). The rebellious of 2:27 cut covenants; this verse shows that the first covenant is written in the fact of being alive.

*Wa kuntum amwātan*, and you were dead things. *Amwāt* is the plural of *mayyit*. The mufassirūn, following Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid as recorded by al-Ṭabarī, explained this first death as the state of non-existence, or the state of the nutfah in the loins and the wombs — lifeless matter, not yet a person. “From what did He create him? From a drop He created him and proportioned him” (80:18–19). “Was there not upon man a period of time when he was not a thing remembered?” (76:1). *Fa-aḥyākum*, then He made you live. The *fāʾ* is of sequence without delay in the argument: from that lifelessness, life. The first resurrection, if the word may be used, is birth. Every denier is already a walking refutation of his denial, because he was nothing and now he argues.

*Thumma yumītukum*, then He causes you to die. *Thumma* is then, with a gap. Life is not a possession; it is a loan with a known, if unseen, due date. “Every soul will taste death” (3:185). The same Lord who started the breath stops it. The pagans of Makkah, and the materialists after them, could grant this much: people are born and people die. The verse does not stop at the agreed. *Thumma yuḥyīkum*, then He makes you live — a second time. This is the resurrection that 2:4 made a mark of the *muttaqūn* and that the Makkans denied: “Does he promise you that when you have died and become dust and bones you will be brought forth? Far-fetched, far-fetched is what you are promised” (23:35–36). The argument of 2:28 is the argument of 36:78–79: “He strikes for Us a parable and forgets his own creation. He says, ‘Who will give life to bones when they are decayed?’ Say, ‘He will give them life who produced them the first time.’” The first life is the proof of the second. He who did the harder thing in your own memory — making you from death — will do it again.

*Thumma ilayhi turjaʿūn*, then to Him you are returned. *Rujūʿ* is the return of 2:18, but here it is not the return to the path; it is the return to the Owner. The passive *turjaʿūn* is the passive of a people who do not chart this last journey. They are taken. “And that to your Lord is the finality” (53:42). The four stations — death, life, death, life — end not in a fifth earthly state but in a meeting. Ibn Kathīr outlines them as: dead in the loins of Ādam or in non-being; alive in this world; dead in the graves; alive for the rising; then return for the account. Some of the *salaf* counted the stages of 40:11, “You caused us to die twice and You gave us life twice,” in the same pattern. Al-Qurṭubī discusses the slight differences of counting and then returns to the force of the rebuke: a being who has already been moved from death to life has no philosophical right to declare the Mover dead or absent or partnered.

The Prophet (ﷺ) taught the servant to remember this sequence in the grave-side and in the night. “Remember often the destroyer of pleasures: death” (al-Tirmidhī, al-Nasāʾī). He stood at a grave and said, “I have not seen a sight but the grave is more terrible than it,” and he taught the funeral prayer that asks for the deceased the mercy of the One who gives life and death. He said, in the hadith of Jibrīl, that *īmān* includes the Last Day, which is this verse’s last two stations. The Fātiḥah named the Master of the Day of Judgment; 2:28 names the road to that Day as the road already under the hearer’s feet.

Theologically the verse is a pillar of *tawḥīd al-rubūbiyyah* used as a door to *tawḥīd al-ulūhiyyah*, as 2:21–22 were. If He alone gave life, He alone is to be worshipped; if He alone will raise, He alone is to be feared and hoped in. *Kufr* in this verse is not only the covering of His existence. It is the covering of His exclusive right, the living as if the sequence had no Speaker. Ibn al-Qayyim wrote that the servant is a traveler between two non-existences, with a brief spark between, and that intelligence is to treat the spark as a sowing. “This worldly life is only amusement and diversion; the home of the Hereafter is the life, if only they knew” (29:64).

For the present age the question *kayfa takfurūn* is addressed to a culture that knows more about the embryo than any previous culture and believes less in the One who fashions it. We can film the first life in the womb and still deny the second life in the grave. The verse does not need our cameras, but it is not contradicted by them. You were lifeless. He gave you life. The rest follows. Denial is not an achievement of science. It is a refusal to read one’s own beginning as a letter from one’s own end. The next verse will widen the camera from the self to the earth and the seven heavens, so that the One who gave you life is seen as the One who laid out everything in the earth for you, and who then turned to the sky.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:29

> He is the One Who created everything in the earth for you. Then He turned towards the heaven, forming it into seven heavens. And He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things.

**Commentary:**

The Giver of life is now the Giver of a world: *huwa alladhī khalaqa lakum mā fī al-arḍi jamīʿan thumma istawā ilā al-samāʾi fa-sawwāhunna sabʿa samāwātin wa huwa bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīm*, “He is the One Who created everything in the earth for you. Then He turned towards the heaven, forming it into seven heavens. And He has perfect knowledge of all things.” *Huwa alladhī* is the identifying clause of praise: He, and not another, is the One who did this. *Khalaqa lakum*, He created for you. The *lām* is the *lām* of benefit. The contents of the earth — its waters, beasts, grains, metals, winds, and paths — are appointed for the human being’s use, not as an owner in the absolute sense but as a trustee. “It is He who created for you all that is on the earth” is a sentence of honor and of trial. Honor, because Ādam’s children are not an accident on a hostile rock. Trial, because “then you will surely be asked that Day about pleasure” (102:8). Ibn ʿAbbās and the mufassirūn read *jamīʿan* as all that is in the earth, with the legal reservation that comes elsewhere: carrion, blood, swine, and what is dedicated to other than Allah (الله) are excepted (2:173). The default of earthly things is permissibility; prohibition is by later text. Al-Qurṭubī draws this as a principle of *fiqh* from the verse.

*Thumma istawā ilā al-samāʾ*, then He turned to the heaven. *Istawā* is a verb that will, in other verses, be followed by *ʿalā al-ʿarsh*, “upon the Throne” (7:54, 10:3, 20:5). Here it is followed by *ilā*, “toward,” and the mufassirūn have explained it as turning to, intending, setting Himself to the fashioning of the sky. The *salaf*’s rule, as Ibn Taymiyyah and Ibn al-Qayyim repeatedly stated, is to affirm what Allah (الله) affirmed of Himself without likening, without stripping, without asking how. *Istawā* is known; its how is unknown; belief in it is obligatory; asking after its modality is innovation. In this verse the sense is sequential in the report: after the earth’s contents, the heaven is taken in hand. “Then He turned to the heaven while it was smoke, and He said to it and to the earth, ‘Come, willingly or unwillingly.’ They said, ‘We come willingly.’ He completed them as seven heavens in two days, and inspired in each heaven its command” (41:11–12). 2:29 is that narrative in a shorter breath.

*Fa-sawwāhunna sabʿa samāwāt*, so He fashioned them, proportioned them, made them even, as seven heavens. *Taswiyah* is from *s-w-y*, to make level, to perfect the proportion of a thing. “He who created you, proportioned you, and balanced you” (82:7) is *taswiyah* of the human; here it is *taswiyah* of the skies. Seven is the number the Qur'an repeats: “He who created seven heavens in layers; you do not see in the creation of the Most Merciful any inconsistency” (67:3). “Allah is the One who created seven heavens, and of the earth the like of them; the command descends among them” (65:12). The Prophet (ﷺ) described, in the hadith of the *Isrāʾ* and *Miʿrāj*, his being taken through the heavens one after another, meeting a prophet in each, until the seventh (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The seven are not a poetic flourish. They are layers of a real building, the *bināʾ* of 2:22 now counted. What the seventh contains, and where the Throne sits above them, is from the Unseen; the servant affirms and does not map with a telescope what the telescope cannot reach.

*Wa huwa bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīm*, and He is, of every thing, Knowing. *ʿAlīm* is the intensive of knowledge. The verse that began with creation for you ends with knowledge of everything, so that no one imagines the Creator as a craftsman who walks away from his work. “He knows what enters the earth and what comes out of it, what descends from the heaven and what ascends in it” (34:2). “Not a leaf falls but He knows it” (6:59). The *qadīr* of 2:20 and the *ʿalīm* of 2:29 are sisters: power and knowledge, the two attributes without which lordship is a name without content. Ibn Kathīr notes that the closing is a warning as well as a praise. He who made the seven heavens knows the *fisq* of 2:27, the denial of 2:28, the mosquito-scorn of 2:26, and the secret of the chest. Nothing of the earth that was created *for you* is used outside His knowledge.

The Prophet (ﷺ) used to recite the praises of these heavens in the bowing and prostration, and he taught the looking at the sky as a thinking, not as an idle stare. “Those who remember Allah standing, sitting, and on their sides, and think about the creation of the heavens and the earth: ‘Our Lord, You did not create this in vain’” (3:191). 2:29 is material for that thinking. The earth’s contents for you; the seven heavens proportioned; the Knowledge that misses nothing. Theologically the verse also prepares the story that begins at 2:30. Before Ādam (peace be upon him) is placed as *khalīfah* on the earth, the earth is declared created for the children of Ādam, and the heavens are declared a finished work of the One who knows. The angels will ask about bloodshed; the Lord will answer that He knows what they do not. 2:29 has already said He knows everything. 2:30 will apply that knowledge to a dispute in the Higher Assembly.

For the reader today “created for you” has been twisted into a license to strip the earth. The verse is a gift, not a permission to *fasād*. The same sūrah that says the earth is for you has already forbidden spreading corruption in it (2:11, 2:27) and will say again, “Eat and drink, but do not be excessive” (7:31). Trustees eat; owners who imagine there is no *ʿAlīm* devour. The seven heavens, in an age that has photographed the near sky and found it larger than the ancestors guessed, are not threatened by photographs. What is seen is still created; what is unseen of the seven remains unseen. The verse does not ask the believer to deny a star. It asks him not to deny the Fashioner. He created what is in the earth for you. He turned to the heaven and made it seven. He knows every thing. The next word is *wa idh qāla rabbuka lil-malāʾikah* — and when your Lord said to the angels. The story of the human being, as the Qur'an tells it, begins not with the human being’s opinion of himself but with a conversation he was not in.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:30

> ˹Remember˺ when your Lord said to the angels, “I am going to place a successive ˹human˺ authority on earth.” They asked ˹Allah˺, “Will You place in it someone who will spread corruption there and shed blood while we glorify Your praises and proclaim Your holiness?” Allah responded, “I know what you do not know.”

**Commentary:**

The sūrah now opens the first great narrative of the Qur'an, and it opens it in heaven, before the human being has spoken: *wa idh qāla rabbuka lil-malāʾikati innī jāʿilun fī al-arḍi khalīfatan*, “And when your Lord said to the angels, ‘I am going to place a successive human authority on earth.’” *Idh* is “when,” and the mufassirūn treat it as a command to remember: recall that scene. *Rabbuka*, your Lord, addresses the Prophet (ﷺ) and, through him, every reciter; the Lord of the Fātiḥah is the Speaker in the Higher Assembly. The angels, *malāʾikah*, from a root of message and kingship, are the servants who do not disobey (66:6). They are told, not consulted as equals. *Jāʿil* is the active participle of appointing: I am in the act of placing. The thing placed is a *khalīfah*.

*Khalīfah* is from *kh-l-f*, to come after, to succeed, to stand in another’s place. The word has filled volumes. Ibn ʿAbbās, as al-Ṭabarī records, said a successor who will succeed one another, generation after generation, on the earth. Others said a vicegerent who will execute Allah’s (الله) command in the earth, judging by His law. Dāwūd (peace be upon him) is later called *khalīfah* in this second sense: “O David, We have made you a successor on the earth, so judge between people in truth” (38:26). Both senses are true of Ādam (peace be upon him) and his children: they succeed one another, and they are entrusted with a trust. The verse does not mean that Allah (الله) has vacated a throne and needs a replacement. The Lord does not die and is not absent. *Khalīfah* is honor by assignment, not a filling of a lack. “It is He who has made you successors on the earth” (6:165).

The angels’ reply is the first question in the Book: *a-tajʿalu fīhā man yufsidu fīhā wa yasfiku al-dimāʾa wa naḥnu nusabbiḥu bi-ḥamdika wa nuqaddisu lak*, “Will You place in it someone who will spread corruption there and shed blood, while we glorify Your praises and proclaim Your holiness?” The mufassirūn are nearly unanimous that this is not an objection of protest, as if the angels accused their Lord of error. It is a question of understanding, of *istifhām*, perhaps of concern for the holiness of the earth. How did they know there would be *fasād* and bloodshed? Some of the *salaf* said Allah (الله) had informed them that this would be the nature of this creature. Others said they compared him to the jinn who had been on the earth before and had shed blood, so that Iblīs’s later refusal has a prehistory. Others said they understood from the word *khalīfah* that a ruler among the created would punish and that punishment implies crime. Al-Ṭabarī leaves the door open. What is certain is that they named the two crimes this sūrah has already named: *fasād fī al-arḍ* (2:11, 2:27) and, now, the spilling of blood. The human story begins under the shadow of its worst possibility.

Their alternative is themselves: *wa naḥnu nusabbiḥu bi-ḥamdika wa nuqaddisu lak*. We glorify with Your praise and we sanctify for You. *Tasbīḥ* is to declare Allah (الله) free of every defect; *taqdīs* is to declare Him holy, far from every impurity. The angels offer a worship that is already perfect as worship. “They do not disobey Allah in what He commands them, and they do as they are commanded” (66:6). “They glorify night and day and do not slacken” (21:20). Why, then, a creature who will spoil and kill? The question is the question of every heart that has looked at history. The answer is the verse’s last sentence, and it is not a full disclosure: *qāla innī aʿlamu mā lā taʿlamūn*, He said, “I know what you do not know.”

That sentence is the theology of 2:29 applied to a conversation. He is *ʿalīm* of every thing; they are not. What He knows and they do not includes, as the next verses will show, that Ādam (peace be upon him) will be taught names they cannot tell; that among this blood-spilling species there will be prophets, truthful ones, martyrs, and the righteous of 4:69; that from this *khalīfah* will come the Messenger (ﷺ) to whom the angels will send *salām*; that the earth will be a place of *tawbah* as well as of *fasād*; that Iblīs’s pride is already in the room, concealed. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī both stress that the angels’ worship is not rejected. It is simply not the whole of what the Lord intends for the earth. There is a knowledge that worships by *tasbīḥ*, and there is a knowledge that worships by choosing good against a possible evil. The second is the human being’s honor and his danger.

The Prophet (ﷺ) said that the angels were created from light, the jinn from a smokeless fire, and Ādam from what was described to you (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). He said that the angels do not eat or drink and that they are offended by what offends the children of Ādam of foul smells, which is part of the reason for the *siwāk* and the cleanliness of the mosque. Their question in this verse is therefore the question of creatures of light about a creature of clay. The clay will fail in ways light does not fail. The clay will also rise, by knowledge and by repentance, in ways the angels will be told to acknowledge in the prostration of 2:34.

For the reader the verse is a warning against two arrogances. One is the arrogance of the human who hears *khalīfah* and thinks himself a little god, licensed to spill and to spoil because he was placed. The angels have already named that misuse. The other is the arrogance of the despairing, who looks at bloodshed and says the angels were right and the project should be cancelled. The Lord’s “I know what you do not know” forbids the cancellation. History is not a mistake. It is a trust whose outcome is known to the One who assigned it. The next verse will begin to show the angels what they did not know: a lesson in names, which is to say, a lesson in knowledge, which is the first mark by which clay is honored above a question.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:31

> He taught Adam the names of all things, then He presented them to the angels and said, “Tell Me the names of these, if what you say is true?”

**Commentary:**

The knowledge the angels were told they did not have is now put on a table: *wa ʿallama Ādama al-asmāʾa kullahā thumma ʿaraḍahum ʿalā al-malāʾikati fa-qāla anbiʾūnī bi-asmāʾi hāʾulāʾi in kuntum ṣādiqīn*, “He taught Adam the names of all things, then He presented them to the angels and said, ‘Tell Me the names of these, if what you say is true?’” Ādam (peace be upon him) is named for the first time. The word *Ādam*, the mufassirūn have said, is from the *adīm* of the earth, the surface of the soil from which he was taken; the Prophet (ﷺ) said, “Allah created Ādam from a handful taken from all the earth, so the children of Ādam come according to the earth: red, white, black, and in between, easy and difficult, good and foul” (Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī). The *khalīfah* is a handful of the planet he will be placed upon. Then he is taught.

*ʿAllama* is the intensive of teaching. The Teacher is Allah (الله). This is the first school, and it has one student and one subject: *al-asmāʾa kullahā*, the names, all of them. What names? The reports from Ibn ʿAbbās, as al-Ṭabarī gathers them, include: the names of all things, of every creature, of the utensils of human life, of the species, of the angels themselves, of his own descendants. Some of the later theologians said what was taught was the faculty of language, the power to name, not a finite list. Al-Rāghib noted that a name is a sign that makes a thing present to the mind; to be taught names is to be given a map of the world. The Qur'an elsewhere makes teaching the honor of the species: “Recite, and your Lord is the Most Generous, who taught by the pen, taught man what he did not know” (96:3–5). “He taught him speech” (55:4). Ādam’s superiority in this scene is not muscle and not a halo. It is *ʿilm* that he did not invent.

*Thumma ʿaraḍahum ʿalā al-malāʾikah*, then He presented them to the angels. The pronoun *hum* is the pronoun of rational beings or of a mixed group treated with that dignity; the mufassirūn said the named things were presented, or their forms, or the named among the angels and the children. The presentation is a test, not a humiliation for its own sake. *Anbiʾūnī bi-asmāʾi hāʾulāʾi*, tell Me the names of these. *Inbāʾ* is news, a report of what one knows. The angels who glorify and sanctify are asked to *inform*. Worship is not the same as this knowledge. They can fill the heavens with *tasbīḥ* and still not be able to name the things of the earth. The *khalīfah* is being qualified for the earth by earth-knowledge, the names of what he will live among.

*In kuntum ṣādiqīn*, if you are truthful. Truthful in what? In the implication of their question at 2:30: that they, the glorifiers, were the more fitting, or that this creature was only a spoiler and a spiller. Ibn Kathīr reads the clause as “if you are truthful in that I should not place a *khalīfah*,” or “if you know the names.” Al-Qurṭubī includes: if you are truthful in your suggestion that you are more suitable. The phrase is the same *in kuntum ṣādiqīn* as 2:23, the challenge to produce a sūrah. There the humans were challenged about the Book; here the angels are challenged about names. In both places truth is not a feeling. It is a demonstration.

Theologically the verse is the Qur'an's first philosophy of knowledge. Knowledge is taught, not merely discovered. The Teacher is the Lord. The human being’s honor is receptivity to that teaching. Ibn Taymiyyah wrote that Ādam’s *ʿilm* was a proof against two later errors: the error of those who scorn knowledge in the name of worship, as if the angels’ *tasbīḥ* were enough without the names; and the error of those who scorn worship in the name of knowledge, as if the names were a god. Ādam (peace be upon him) will still be commanded not to approach a tree. Knowledge does not cancel prohibition. It qualifies for trust.

The Prophet (ﷺ) was himself the recipient of taught names. He said, “I have been given the keys of speech,” and he said, “I have been given Jawāmiʿ al-kalim,” the comprehensive words (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). He taught the *ummah* the names of Allah (الله) — “Allah has ninety-nine names; whoever enumerates them will enter Paradise” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim) — so that the children of Ādam would not only name the things of the earth but name their Lord as He named Himself. The school of 2:31 is still open. Every child who learns the word for water, and every scholar who learns a ruling, is in a distant row of Ādam’s classroom, provided he remembers who the Teacher is.

The scene also answers the angels’ fear of blood with a different kind of power. A creature who can name can legislate, can remember, can pass a book from one generation to the next. The *fasād* they dreaded is real; the remedy they had not imagined is also real: a being who learns. “Allah will raise those who have believed among you and those who were given knowledge in degrees” (58:11). The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “Whoever travels a path seeking knowledge, Allah makes easy for him a path to Paradise” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim), and “The angels lower their wings for the seeker of knowledge, pleased with what he does” (Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī). The lowering of wings is a later echo of the lowering of the forehead in 2:34: knowledge, when it is received as Ādam received it, is a reason the angels honor clay.

For the present age the verse both honors and limits the sciences. It honors them: naming the world is Ādamic. Biology, language, law, and craft are not thefts from the angels. They are the *khalīfah* doing what he was taught to do. It limits them: the names were taught. A science that begins by denying the Teacher will still use the names and will still not know what the angels did not know — the purpose, the unseen, the *mā lā taʿlamūn* of 2:30. The angels will, in the next verse, give the only right answer of a creature asked beyond his knowledge: glory to You; we have no knowledge except what You have taught us. Ādam will then speak the names. The order is the ethic of every classroom that does not wish to become Iblīs’s classroom: admit the limit, then receive the gift, then speak.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:32

> They replied, “Glory be to You! We have no knowledge except what You have taught us. You are truly the All-Knowing, All-Wise.”

**Commentary:**

The angels answer as angels answer: *qālū subḥānaka lā ʿilma lanā illā mā ʿallamtanā innaka anta al-ʿalīmu al-ḥakīm*, “They replied, ‘Glory be to You! We have no knowledge except what You have taught us. You are truly the All-Knowing, All-Wise.’” Before they speak of themselves they speak of Him. *Subḥānaka* is the *tasbīḥ* they had already claimed in 2:30, now used as it should be used: not as a rival résumé against Ādam, but as a confession that the Lord is free of every defect, including the defect of having asked a question He could not answer, and including the defect of having chosen a *khalīfah* in error. *Tasbīḥ* at the opening of a reply is the etiquette of the Higher Assembly. The Prophet (ﷺ) taught the same etiquette to the lower assembly: when a matter is too great, say *subḥān Allāh*; when you are asked what you do not know, do not invent. “Whoever is asked about knowledge and conceals it will be bridled with a bridle of fire” (Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī) is about concealing what one knows; this verse is about not claiming what one does not.

*Lā ʿilma lanā illā mā ʿallamtanā*, we have no knowledge except what You have taught us. The restriction *illā* is the whole epistemology of servanthood. Knowledge is not a mist the creature gathers from nowhere. It is a gift with a Giver. The angels, who have filled the cosmos with worship, admit that their *ʿilm* is a subset of a lesson. What they were not taught — the names of 2:31 — they do not have. There is no shame in this sentence. There is *tawḥīd*. Ibn ʿAbbās and the early mufassirūn treated it as the angels’ repentance from the implication of their question, a return to the proper place. Al-Ṭabarī: they submitted and entrusted knowledge back to its Owner. Ibn Kathīr: this is the model for every scholar, that he say “I do not know” when he does not know. Mālik ibn Anas, it is reported, would say “I do not know” until he was a mountain of it; the angels said it before there was a Mālik.

*Innaka anta al-ʿalīmu al-ḥakīm*, You — You — are the All-Knowing, the All-Wise. The double pronoun is *ḥaṣr*: You, and no other, are these two. *Al-ʿAlīm* has just closed 2:29; the angels now use it as an address. *Al-Ḥakīm* is from *ḥ-k-m*, wisdom, the putting of a thing in its place, and also judgment, the ruling that does not err. The pairing is the pairing of many verses: knowledge without wisdom can be a flood; wisdom without knowledge can be a guess. In Allah (الله) they are both perfect. The angels had seen the future blood of 2:30; they had not seen the wisdom of placing a being who would bleed and also repent, who would kill and also receive books. *Al-Ḥakīm* is their admission that the placement is not a mistake waiting to be discovered. Ibn al-Qayyim wrote that *ḥikmah* is to give each thing its due; the due of clay was the *khilāfah*, and the due of light was the *tasbīḥ*, and the Lord of both does not confuse the dues.

Theologically the verse is a correction of the most ancient intellectual sin after pride: to speak beyond knowledge. Iblīs will, in two verses, refuse from *kibr*. The angels here refuse from humility — they refuse to name what they cannot name. Both are refusals; they are opposite in worth. The Qur'an will later command the children of Ādam, “Do not pursue what you have no knowledge of; the hearing, the sight, and the heart — all of those will be questioned” (17:36). 2:32 is that command in a heavenly scene. Al-Qurṭubī draws from it the legal adab of the mufti and the witness: do not say “Allah says” when Allah has not said; do not testify to what you have not been given. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “The one among you who is most bold in giving fatwā is the most bold toward the Fire,” in reports the scholars of *ḥadīth* have discussed, and he said, “I do not know what will be done with me or with you” in a worldly matter until Allah (الله) informed him. The Messenger (ﷺ) himself lived the angels’ sentence: no knowledge except what he was taught.

There is a further courtesy in *innaka anta*. The angels do not say “You know and we do not” as a sulk. They say *You* are the All-Knowing, the All-Wise, as praise. The gap between their knowledge and His is not a wound. It is a reason for *tasbīḥ*. The Prophet (ﷺ) taught, when the reciter reaches a verse of *tasbīḥ*, that he should glorify, and when he reaches a verse of supplication that he should ask (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, in the hadith of Ḥudhayfah’s night prayer). 2:32 is both: glorification, then a confession that is itself a *duʿāʾ* of the capable — keep us within what You have taught; do not let us speak the names we have not been given. The scholars of *ʿaqīdah* have used the verse against those who claim that the intellect, unaided, can map the unseen. The intellect of the angels is greater than ours; they still said *lā ʿilma lanā illā mā ʿallamtanā*.

For the present age, which drowns in information and starves for the sentence “we do not know,” the verse is almost medicine. A culture that must have an opinion on every unseen thing — the future of the species, the inner of other hearts, the ruling of every new event before the evidence is in — is a culture that has forgotten *subḥānaka*. The angels were wiser than the feed. They glorified, they limited their claim, they named their Lord with two Names that cover the gap. The believer who is asked a question that belongs to 2:30’s *mā lā taʿlamūn* should not fill the gap with noise. He should say what they said, and then, if there is a teaching, wait for it. The next verse will give Ādam (peace be upon him) permission to speak the names. The permission comes from the same Teacher. Knowledge, when it arrives, will still be *mā ʿallamtanā*. Ādam will not be a rival to *al-ʿAlīm*. He will be a student who has learned his lesson in front of his older, brighter classmates, to the glory of the One who taught him.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:33

> Allah said, “O Adam! Inform them of their names.” Then when Adam did, Allah said, “Did I not tell you that I know the secrets of the heavens and the earth, and I know what you reveal and what you conceal?”

**Commentary:**

The student is now told to stand and recite: *qāla yā Ādamu anbiʾhum bi-asmāʾihim*, “He said, ‘O Ādam, inform them of their names.’” The same verb *anbiʾ* that the angels could not fulfill is given to the handful of earth. *Yā Ādamu* is the first vocative of a human name in the Qur'an. It is a calling of honor. The *khalīfah* is not installed by a crown; he is installed by being able to tell the angels what they asked to be told and could not tell. *Fa-lammā anbaʾahum bi-asmāʾihim*, then when he had informed them of their names — the Qur'an does not list the names. The silence is part of the teaching. The point is not that the reader learn the list. The point is that Ādam (peace be upon him) had it, and that he gave it back as *inbāʾ*, as news, which means he spoke truly what he had been taught. He is not a discoverer in this scene. He is a transmitter. The first human speech in the presence of the angels is a recitation of a lesson.

Then the Lord turns to the angels with a question that is a reminder: *qāla a-lam aqul lakum innī aʿlamu ghayba al-samāwāti wa al-arḍi wa aʿlamu mā tubdūna wa mā kuntum taktumūn*, “Did I not tell you that I know the unseen of the heavens and the earth, and I know what you reveal and what you conceal?” *A-lam aqul lakum* looks back to 2:30: “I know what you do not know.” That general sentence is now specified in two directions. First, *ghayb al-samāwāt wa al-arḍ*, the unseen of the heavens and the earth — all that is hidden in the two realms the previous verses have been describing, the seven heavens and the earth created for you. “With Him are the keys of the unseen; no one knows them but He” (6:59). The names Ādam spoke were a sample of that unseen, brought into the open by teaching. Second, *mā tubdūna wa mā kuntum taktumūn*, what you make manifest and what you used to conceal.

What did the angels reveal, and what did they conceal? The revealed is easy: their question about bloodshed, their *tasbīḥ*, their confession of 2:32. The concealed is the subject of early reports. Ibn ʿAbbās, in a well-known interpretation recorded by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, said that what they concealed was Iblīs’s secret: he was among them, though not of their species, and he had hidden pride, a thought that “I am better than this creature,” which will become speech at 7:12. Other reports said the angels themselves concealed a wonder or a worry they did not utter. Al-Qurṭubī allows both: the Lord’s knowledge covers the unspoken of the obedient and the hidden of the soon-to-be rebellious. *Kuntum taktumūn* uses *kāna* plus the imperfect, a past of continuity: what you were concealing. The secret had duration. It was not a flicker. The One who taught Ādam the names did not need Ādam in order to know the secret. He knew it while it was being kept.

The verse is therefore the closing of a circle of *ʿilm*. 2:29, He knows every thing. 2:30, I know what you do not know. 2:31, He taught Ādam. 2:32, we have no knowledge except what You taught. 2:33, I know the unseen of the heavens and the earth, and I know the open and the hidden of you. Knowledge begins with Him, is gifted to whom He wills, is denied to whom He wills, and is never absent from Him. Ibn Taymiyyah used such verses against both the philosopher who makes the Lord ignorant of particulars and the mystic who claims a knowledge that bypasses revelation. The particulars of the earth’s names are known to Allah (الله); the particulars of a concealed thought are known to Allah (الله); the servant’s share is *mā ʿallamtanā*.

The Prophet (ﷺ) lived under this verse as a comfort and as a fear. Comfort, because “I know what you conceal” means that the unspoken faith of the weak, the tear that does not reach a cheek in public, the intention that could not become an act, are already with Allah (الله). “Their sides forsake their beds; they call upon their Lord in fear and hope” (32:16). Fear, because the same clause means that the unspoken pride, the hidden envy, the *khalwah* of 2:14, are also with Him. “He knows the treachery of the eyes and what the chests conceal” (40:19). The angels are sinless and still are told that their concealments are known; how much more the children of Ādam, whose concealments include *nifāq*.

Al-Rāzī and others noted that *ghayb al-samāwāt wa al-arḍ* is wider than the list of names. It includes the future of this *khalīfah*: the prophets in his loins, the Book that will be recited by his children, the Fire and the Garden of 2:24–25, the hypocrites of 2:8, the mosquito of 2:26. The angels saw blood; Allah (الله) saw also the *mufliḥūn*. The presentation of names is a sample of a knowledge that does not sample. “Not an atom’s weight in the earth or in the heaven escapes your Lord, nor what is smaller than that or greater, but that it is in a clear Book” (10:61). Ādam’s recitation is one line of that Book, spoken aloud so that light might learn why clay was chosen.

For the reader the scene is a school of adab. When you are taught, speak what you were taught, as Ādam did. When you are asked what you were not taught, glorify and decline, as the angels did. When you are tempted to think your secret is a secret, remember *mā kuntum taktumūn*. The *khalīfah*’s first public act is not a war and not a city. It is a recitation before a higher class, to the praise of the Teacher. The next verse will command those same angels to prostrate to this reciter — not in worship of clay, but in obedience to the Lord of clay and of light. Iblīs will refuse. The knowledge of 2:33 has already included that refusal. “I know what you conceal” was, among other things, a sentence about a fire-creature standing in the row of light, waiting to say no.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:34

> And ˹remember˺ when We said to the angels, “Prostrate before Adam,” so they all did—but not Iblîs, who refused and acted arrogantly, becoming unfaithful.

**Commentary:**

The honor of knowledge is now sealed with an honor of the body: *wa idh qulnā lil-malāʾikati usjudū li-Ādama fa-sajadū illā Iblīsa abā wa istakbara wa kāna mina al-kāfirīn*, “And when We said to the angels, ‘Prostrate before Ādam,’ so they all did — but not Iblīs, who refused and acted arrogantly, becoming unfaithful.” *Usjudū* is the imperative of *sujūd*, the placing of the forehead, the utmost bodily humility. The *lām* in *li-Ādama* is the *lām* of the occasion, not of worship. The mufassirūn, with a consensus the jurists have repeated for centuries, said this was a prostration of greeting and honor, a *sujūd taḥiyyah*, not a *sujūd ʿibādah*. Worship is for Allah (الله) alone. Ādam (peace be upon him) is the *qiblah* of the act, not its god. Yūsuf’s (peace be upon him) family later fall in prostration to him (12:100) in the same old sense of honor, a sense this *ummah* has since closed, for the Prophet (ﷺ) forbade that one human prostrate to another. In that heavenly hour the command was given, and the angels obeyed.

*Fa-sajadū*, so they prostrated. The *fāʾ* is of speed. There is no committee. The creatures of light put their foreheads down for a creature of clay because the Lord of both had spoken. “They said, ‘We have heard and obeyed’” is the angelic religion. *Illā Iblīs*, except Iblīs. The exception, as the grammarians and the theologians have discussed, is an exception of interruption, *istithnāʾ munqaṭiʿ*, because Iblīs is not an angel. “He was of the jinn, and he departed from the command of his Lord” (18:50). He was in the assembly, under the address, and he was not of the species. The Prophet (ﷺ) said the angels were created from light and the jinn from smokeless fire (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). Fire’s child is asked to bow to clay’s child, and the fire refuses.

His name, *Iblīs*, is from *iblās*, despair, the cutting off of hope, or it is a proper name. The Qur'an also calls him *al-shayṭān*, the one who is far, the one who burns with enmity. His story is told again at 7:11–18, 15:28–44, 17:61–65, 18:50, 20:116–117, 38:71–85, each time with a shade. Here it is given in three verbs: *abā*, he refused; *istakbara*, he waxed proud; *wa kāna mina al-kāfirīn*, and he was of the disbelievers. *Ibāʾ* is refusal of a known command. It is not confusion. It is *no*. *Istikbār* is the tenth form of *kibr*, pride, the seeking of greatness, the seeing of oneself as too large to descend. He will say, in the longer telling, “I am better than him; You created me from fire and created him from clay” (7:12). The comparison is the sin. Clay and fire are both created; the command is the measure, not the material. Ibn ʿAbbās said Iblīs was the first to use analogy against a text. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr both pause on this: *qiyās* against *naṣṣ*, reasoning against the spoken command, is his method, and it remains a satanic method whenever a servant prefers his own ranking of things to the ranking of his Lord.

*Wa kāna mina al-kāfirīn*, and he was of those who cover the truth. *Kāna* may mean he became, or that he was, in the knowledge of Allah (الله), of them. The mufassirūn have said his *kufr* was not ignorance of the Lord’s existence — he speaks to Allah (الله), asks for respite, swears by His might — but refusal of His right to command. It is *kufr ibāʾ* and *istikbār*, the disbelief of pride. Ibn Taymiyyah distinguished the *kufr* of denial from the *kufr* of arrogance; Iblīs is the imam of the second. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “No one who has a mustard seed of pride in his heart will enter Paradise.” A man said, “What if a man likes his garment to be nice and his sandals to be nice?” He said, “Allah is Beautiful and loves beauty. Pride is to reject the truth and to look down on people” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). Rejecting the truth and looking down on Ādam: 2:34 in a definition.

The scene is the origin of the human trial. The enemy is older than the first sin of Ādam. Before a tree is approached, a forehead has already refused to touch the ground. “Do not let Satan seduce you as he drove your parents out of the Garden, stripping them of their clothing to show them their private parts. He sees you, he and his tribe, from where you do not see them” (7:27). The enmity is not a metaphor for the psyche. It is a being who asked for time until the Day of Resurrection and was given it, who vowed to sit on the straight path and come at the children of Ādam from before, behind, right, and left (7:16–17). Al-Qurṭubī notes that the verse is a warning to this *ummah* about pride in lineage, in knowledge, in worship. Iblīs had worship that filled the earth, in some reports; one command revealed the *kibr* that the worship had not killed. A servant may pray and still be of his party if he refuses a single clear command because it injures his self-estimate.

For the reader the verse is the daily choice of forehead. The angels’ religion is *fa-sajadū*. Iblīs’s religion is *abā wa istakbara*. Every command that arrives — a prayer that interrupts work, a charity that interrupts accumulation, a truth that interrupts a reputation — is a small *usjudū*. The one who goes down is with the angels in that instant. The one who stands, ranking himself above the command, is with the exception. The next verses will show that Ādam (peace be upon him) will also disobey, but his disobedience will be of another type: a slipping, a deception, then a receiving of words and a repentance. Iblīs will not repent. That is the difference between a sin of desire that breaks the sinner and a sin of pride that hardens him. 2:34 is the hardening. 2:37 will be the breaking and the mending. Between them is the tree.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:35

> We cautioned, “O Adam! Live with your wife in Paradise and eat as freely as you please, but do not approach this tree, or else you will be wrongdoers.”

**Commentary:**

After the prostration comes a home: *wa qulnā yā Ādamu uskun anta wa zawjuka al-jannata wa kulā minhā raghadan ḥaythu shiʾtumā wa lā taqrabā hādhihi al-shajarata fa-takūnā mina al-ẓālimīn*, “We said, ‘O Ādam, live with your wife in the Garden and eat from it freely as you both please, but do not approach this tree, or else you will be of the wrongdoers.’” The Speaker is now “We,” the royal plural of majesty used of Allah (الله) throughout the narrative. *Uskun* is from *s-k-n*, to dwell, to be still, to settle. The *khalīfah* who will be placed on the earth is first given a dwelling that is not the earth. *Al-jannah* here, as the mass of the *salaf* held, is the Garden of the Unseen, the Paradise that will later be the reward of the *muttaqūn*, though some later theologians asked whether it was a garden of this world. Al-Ṭabarī prefers the first. Ādam (peace be upon him) and his wife are housed in bliss before they are housed in trial.

*Anta wa zawjuka*, you and your spouse. She is not named in this sūrah. The reports of the Prophet (ﷺ) and of the Companions name her Ḥawwāʾ, Eve, created from Ādam’s side, as in the hadith: “Treat women well, for the woman was created from a rib…” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The Qur'an in this verse makes her a partner of the address from the first: *uskun* is grammatically to Ādam, then the two of them are the subject of *kulā* and *lā taqrabā*. The first human community is a pair. Ibn Kathīr notes that Ādam’s loneliness was removed by her, and that the Garden is not given to him as a solitary mystic. It is given to a marriage. The *khalīfah*’s first companionship is not the angels and not Iblīs. It is a wife.

*Wa kulā minhā raghadan ḥaythu shiʾtumā*, and eat from it, both of you, in plenty, wherever you wish. *Raghad* is ease, abundance, the unworried plenty of a life without invoice. *Ḥaythu shiʾtumā* is wherever you will: the Garden is not a ration-queue. The permission is wide so that the prohibition will be narrow and clear. This is the pattern of the later law as well: the earth is created for you (2:29), eat of the good things (2:168), and then a few doors are shut. Ibn al-Qayyim observed that Ādam’s trial was not hunger. It was a limit inside fullness. The most dangerous tree is the tree in a garden of trees.

*Wa lā taqrabā hādhihi al-shajarah*, and do not approach this tree. The prohibition is not only “do not eat.” It is “do not come near.” The jurists have long heard in this the principle that the means of a forbidden thing are forbidden, that the fence is built out from the house. “Those are the limits of Allah, so do not approach them” (2:187). *Hādhihi*, this, points to a known tree. Which tree? The reports vary as the reports of the *salaf* vary: wheat, grape, fig, a tree unlike the trees of this world. Al-Ṭabarī, after listing them, says the knowledge of its species is of no benefit, and the ignorance of it does no harm. The Book withheld the name so that the children of Ādam would not worship a botany. What matters is that there was a *this*, a specific, and that it was forbidden by speech, not by poison. The tree was not evil. The approach was disobedience.

*Fa-takūnā mina al-ẓālimīn*, so you will become of the wrongdoers. *Ẓulm* is to put a thing in other than its place, to wrong, to darken. “It is those who believe and do not mix their faith with wrongdoing — those have security” (6:82), and the Prophet (ﷺ) explained that *ẓulm* there as *shirk* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī). Here the *ẓulm* is the wrong of crossing a limit of the Lord, a wrong against their own souls, as they will confess: “Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves” (7:23). The verse does not say “you will become disbelievers.” Ādam’s slip, by the consensus of Ahl al-Sunnah, is not *kufr*. It is a disobedience of a prophet before his earthly mission, or a lapse that prophets are protected from after prophethood, according to the two ways the scholars have spoken. What the verse says is *ẓulm*. They will have put themselves in the wrong place: outside the permission, near the tree.

The story as told in 7:19–22 and 20:120–121 fills the caution with a whisper. Iblīs will swear to them that he is a sincere adviser. He will name the tree the tree of eternity and of a kingdom that does not decay. He will approach through the desire not of appetite only but of forever — the same forever 2:25 promised by a lawful road. The forbidden tree is sold as a shortcut to *khulūd*. Al-Qurṭubī notes that this is still the devil’s marketing: he does not sell the sin as sin. He sells it as a door to something Allah (الله) has already promised by another door. 2:35 is the clear speech before the marketing. Live, eat, do not approach.

For the reader the Garden of Ādam is not a fairy tale about someone else. It is the map of every human life that has plenty and one forbidden thing. The plenty is *raghad*. The forbidden thing is *hādhihi al-shajarah*. The warning is *lā taqrabā*. Cultures that have more than any previous culture still live at this tree: a screen, a contract, a body that is not lawful, a word that should not be approached. The verse does not forbid eating. It forbids approaching what was named. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “The lawful is clear and the unlawful is clear, and between them are ambiguous matters; whoever guards against the ambiguous has protected his religion and his honor” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). *Lā taqrabā* is that hadith in a Garden. The next verse will tell what happened when they came near. The caution was not enough, because an enemy who had refused to prostrate had not yet refused to whisper.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:36

> But Satan deceived them—leading to their fall from the ˹blissful˺ state they were in, and We said, “Descend from the heavens ˹to the earth˺ as enemies to each other. You will find in the earth a residence and provision for your appointed stay.”

**Commentary:**

The caution is followed by the slip: *fa-azallahumā al-shayṭānu ʿanhā fa-akhrajahumā mimmā kānā fīhi*, “But Satan made them both slip from it, and he brought them out of what they had been in.” *Azallahumā* is from *z-l-l*, to slip, to lose one’s footing. The fourth form makes Satan the cause of the slip: he made them slip. The Qur'an does not say he forced them. A slip is a foot that still belongs to the walker. 7:20–22 tells the method: he whispered, he displayed their private parts to them as a future shame, he swore “I am to you both a sincere adviser,” and he caused them to fall by delusion. 20:120 says he whispered, “O Ādam, shall I show you the tree of eternity and a kingdom that does not decay?” The desire was not filth. It was forever and kingdom — goods that, in their lawful form, Allah (الله) had already written for the servants in 2:25. Iblīs’s genius is to sell the promised by means of the prohibited.

*ʿAnhā*, from it — from the Garden, or from the state, or from the tree, the pronoun having been read in all these ways. *Fa-akhrajahumā mimmā kānā fīhi*, so he brought them out of what they had been in. What they had been in was *raghad*, the plenty, the unashamed dwelling, the garments of the Garden that 7:27 says he wanted to strip. The first human couple’s first loss is not money. It is a state. Ibn ʿAbbās and the mufassirūn described their being stripped of the heavenly clothing and their covering themselves with the leaves of the Garden, as 7:22 says. Shame entered the world as a mercy and as a wound: a mercy because the children of Ādam would know nakedness as a sign that something had gone wrong; a wound because the ease of *uskun* was gone. Al-Ṭabarī emphasizes that the *akhraja* is ascribed to Satan as cause and will at once be ascribed to Allah’s (الله) command as decree. Both ascriptions are true in their places.

Then the divine sentence: *wa qulnā ihbiṭū baʿḍukum li-baʿḍin ʿaduwwun*, and We said, “Descend, some of you an enemy to others.” *Ihbiṭū* is a plural: go down. The addressees, as Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī report from the *salaf*, are Ādam, Ḥawwāʾ, and Iblīs — and in some counts the serpent of the reports — all sent down, not as one family but as opponents. *Baʿḍukum li-baʿḍin ʿaduwwun* is the enmity of 20:123 and of 7:24. The human pair and the devil are not colleagues in a fall. They are now on a field where one side whispers and the other must refuse. “Satan is an enemy to you, so take him as an enemy” (35:6). The earth will not be a second Garden. It will be a residence of conflict. The *khalīfah* of 2:30 arrives at his post already at war.

*Wa lakum fī al-arḍi mustaqarrun wa matāʿun ilā ḥīn*, and for you in the earth is a dwelling-place and a provision until a time. *Mustaqarr* is a place of settling, from the same *qarār* that made the earth a bed in 2:22. *Matāʿ* is enjoyment, goods that are used and wear out. *Ilā ḥīn* is until an appointed time: death, the Hour, the end of each person’s lease. The earth of 2:29, created for you, is now specified as a temporary *mustaqarr*. It is not the *jannah* they left. It is enough: a place to stay, a provision to use, a clock. Ibn al-Qayyim wrote that this sentence is the definition of the world. Whoever treats it as a Garden will be cheated; whoever treats it as *matāʿ ilā ḥīn* will travel light. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “Be in this world as a stranger or a traveler” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī), and he said, “The world is a prison for the believer and a paradise for the disbeliever” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). Ādam’s children inherit the *mustaqarr*, not the first *jannah*. The second *jannah* will be by the guidance of 2:38.

Theologically the verse holds together three truths that later sects have pulled apart. Ādam (peace be upon him) chose; Satan caused a slip; Allah (الله) decreed the descent. The Qadariyyah would drop the decree; the Jabriyyah would drop the choice; the verse writes all three in three clauses. Ahl al-Sunnah, as Ibn Taymiyyah formulated, say: the servant acts, really, and Allah (الله) creates the act, really, and the enemy whispers, really. The descent is not only a punishment. The mufassirūn have said it is also the beginning of the *khilāfah* that was announced before the tree. Ādam was always going to the earth. The tree changed the manner of the going — from a going of honor without the taste of a slip, to a going that would need *tawbah*. Some of the wise have said that a Ādam who had never slipped would not have been the father of a species that must return. The slip is not a good. The repentance that follows it will be a good that the angels of 2:30 had not listed when they listed blood.

For the reader the verse names the condition he is already in. He is on the earth. He has an enemy. He has a *mustaqarr* and a *matāʿ* with an expiry. The Garden is behind his father and, if he follows guidance, before him. The practical fruit is to stop treating Satan as a fable and the world as a home. “We said, descend” is the explanation of why ease is mixed, why the body dies, why the heart is whispered to at the door of every tree. The next verse will not leave Ādam in the shame of the leaves. Words will be received. The first man will be the first to repent, and the Names of the Lord will include, for the first time in this sūrah, *al-Tawwāb al-Raḥīm*.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:37

> Then Adam was inspired with words ˹of prayer˺ by his Lord, so He accepted his repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.

**Commentary:**

The slip is not the last word of the human being: *fa-talaqqā Ādamu min rabbihi kalimātin fa-tāba ʿalayhi innahu huwa al-tawwābu al-raḥīm*, “Then Ādam received from his Lord words, so He turned to him. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, the Most Merciful.” *Talaqqā* is to receive, to meet, to take what is coming. The subject is Ādam (peace be upon him); the gift is *kalimāt*, words; the source is *min rabbihi*, from his Lord — the same *Rabb* who placed him, taught him names, housed him, warned him, and sent him down. The first man does not invent the language of return. He is given it, as he was given the names. Ibn ʿAbbās, in the report that al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr prefer, said the words are those of 7:23: “Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves, and if You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we will surely be among the losers.” Other reports add praise, or the testimony of *tawḥīd*, or a longer petition. The meaning is one: Ādam was taught how to come back, and he came back with speech, not with a gesture of pride.

*Fa-tāba ʿalayhi*, so He turned upon him. *Tawbah* is a turning. When it is the servant’s, it is a turning from the sin to the obedience. When it is the Lord’s, it is a turning of mercy toward the servant, an acceptance, a returning of favor. The same verb serves both, because the two turnings meet. “Then He turned to them that they might turn” (9:118). Ādam’s turning is implied in the receiving of the words and in their use; Allah’s (الله) turning is stated. The *fāʾ* is of consequence without delay in the telling: words received, turning granted. There is no mention of a long freeze, no mention of intercession of another creature, no mention of a price Ādam paid to a third party. The Lord who was wronged by the approach to the tree is the Lord who teaches the apology and accepts it. This is the creed of *tawbah* in Islam, against every later theology that would put a barrier of priesthood or of inherited stain between the sinner and this verse.

*Innahu huwa al-tawwābu al-raḥīm*, indeed He, He, is the Oft-Returning, the Merciful. *Al-Tawwāb* is the intensive of *tawbah*: He who turns again and again, who accepts repeatedly, who does not tire of the returning servant. *Al-Raḥīm* is the Name of the Fātiḥah, the particular mercy. The pairing is the pairing of 2:54, 2:128, 2:160, and of many other closes. Ibn al-Qayyim wrote that *al-Tawwāb* is among the most beloved Names to the sinner who still has a pulse of *īmān*, because it means the door is a Name of Allah (الله), not a mood of Allah (الله). The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “Allah is more pleased with the repentance of His servant than one of you who, having lost his camel in a waterless desert, finds it again” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). He said, “Every son of Ādam sins, and the best of those who sin are those who repent” (al-Tirmidhī, Ibn Mājah). He said, “The one who repents from sin is like the one who has no sin” (Ibn Mājah). These hadiths are 2:37 unfolded over the children of the first repentant.

The contrast with Iblīs is the contrast the sūrah wants. Iblīs refused, grew proud, and was of the disbelievers (2:34). He asked for respite, not for pardon. Ādam slipped, received words, and was turned to. Both disobeyed a command. The difference is not the size of the act — a prostration refused is not smaller than a tree approached — but the response to the act. Pride makes a sin into a *kufr*; *tawbah* makes a sin into a road. Al-Qurṭubī and the theologians of Ahl al-Sunnah have been careful: Ādam is a prophet, and the prophets are protected from persistence in sin and from *kufr*; his act is described as *ẓulm* of the soul and as being deceived, and it is followed by election: “Then his Lord chose him, and turned to him, and guided” (20:122). The father of the species is not a model of the slip. He is a model of the return.

The words themselves, if they are 7:23, are a complete theology of the sinner. *Rabbana*: the relationship is still *Rabb* and servant; the slip did not make Ādam an orphan. *Ẓalamnā anfusanā*: the wrong is confessed, and it is located in the selves, not in the decree as an excuse, not in the spouse as a blame. The Qur'an does not say, here, that Ḥawwāʾ was the cause. They both slipped; they both, in 7:23, say *we*. *Wa in lam taghfir lanā wa tarḥamnā*: forgiveness and mercy are asked as the only rescue. *La-nakūnanna mina al-khāsirīn*: without that, they would be the losers of 2:27. The first prayer after the first sin is the opposite of Iblīs’s “I am better.” It is “we have wronged.”

For the reader who is already on the earth of 2:36, this verse is the reason not to despair and the reason not to persist. Despair is Iblīs’s second arrow, after the whisper. “Do not despair of the mercy of Allah; indeed Allah forgives all sins” (39:53). Persistence is the refusal to receive the words that are already on the page. The words are still being taught: every *istighfār*, every *tawbah*, every raising of the hands is *talaqqā* of Ādam’s lesson. The Prophet (ﷺ) used to seek forgiveness more than a hundred times in a sitting (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim), not because his slips were Ādam’s but because the Names *al-Tawwāb al-Raḥīm* deserve a servant who returns often. The next two verses will set the terms of the earthly stay: guidance will come; whoever follows it will not fear or grieve; whoever covers and denies will be of the Fire, forever. 2:37 is the door that makes those terms livable. A species that can receive words after a slip is a species that can follow guidance after a descent.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:38

> We said, “Descend all of you! Then when guidance comes to you from Me, whoever follows it, there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.

**Commentary:**

The descent is spoken a second time, now as the opening of a law for all history: *qulnā ihbiṭū minhā jamīʿan fa-immā yaʾtiyannakum minnī hudan fa-man tabiʿa hudāya fa-lā khawfun ʿalayhim wa lā hum yaḥzanūn*, “We said, ‘Descend from it, all of you. Then when guidance comes to you from Me, whoever follows it, there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.’” *Ihbiṭū minhā jamīʿan*, descend from it, all together. The repetition of *ihbiṭū* after 2:36 is not filler. The first descent named enmity and a temporary provision. This descent names the rescue. *Jamīʿan* gathers Ādam, his wife, their enemy, and, by extension, every child who will be born on the earth they are entering. No one stays in the first Garden by inertia. Everyone who lives, lives under the next clause.

*Fa-immā yaʾtiyannakum minnī hudan*, then if there should come to you, from Me, a guidance. *Immā* is *in* plus *mā*, a conditional made more open, and the *nūn* of emphasis is on the verb: it will surely come. The guidance is *minnī*, from Me, not from the earth, not from the tribe, not from the *khalīfah*’s unaided mind. This is the same *hudā* as 2:2 and 2:5, now promised at the gate of history. Ibn ʿAbbās and the mufassirūn said it means the prophets, the books, the revealed law. Ādam (peace be upon him) himself will be the first of those who bring it to his children; Nūḥ, Ibrāhīm, Mūsā, ʿĪsā, and Muḥammad (peace be upon them) will be of its later carriers. The Fātiḥah asked “Guide us”; 2:38 says the guidance will come from the One who was asked. Al-Ṭabarī notes that the coming is not left as a rumor. It is a divine commitment to the species.

*Fa-man tabiʿa hudāya*, then whoever follows My guidance. *Tabiʿa* is to follow, to walk behind, to take as imam. The guidance is not a relic to be kissed. It is a road to be walked. *Hudāya*, My guidance, the possessive of honor and of source. The one who follows is not named as a tribe. *Man* is whoever. The angels had asked about a species that would spill blood; the answer, delayed across seven verses, includes this: from that species, whoever follows. Ibn Kathīr reads the verse as the general law of salvation until the Hour. It will be restated at 2:62 for the believing Jews, Christians, and Sabians of their times, and at 20:123 in the parallel telling: “Whoever follows My guidance will not go astray, nor will he suffer.” Not going astray is the Fātiḥah’s path; not suffering is this verse’s pair of negations.

*Fa-lā khawfun ʿalayhim wa lā hum yaḥzanūn*, no fear upon them, nor shall they grieve. *Khawf* is fear of what is coming; *ḥuzn* is grief over what has gone. The two eat the human heart between them: anxiety of the future, sorrow of the past. The followers of the guidance are promised that, in the final settlement, neither will have a claim. “Unquestionably, the allies of Allah — no fear will there be concerning them, nor will they grieve” (10:62). “Those who say, ‘Our Lord is Allah,’ then remain steadfast, the angels descend upon them: ‘Do not fear and do not grieve, and receive good news of the Garden that you were promised’” (41:30). The verse does not promise that the earth of *mustaqarr* will be without frightening things or grievous things. Ādam will bury a son. The Prophet (ﷺ) will bury children and companions. The promise is about the end, and about a presence that already lightens the road: “Know that the helpers of Allah have no fear, nor do they grieve” as a state that begins, in seed, with *īmān*.

The Prophet (ﷺ) was the greatest arrival of this *hudā*. “There has come to you from Allah a light and a clear Book; by it Allah guides those who seek His pleasure to paths of peace” (5:15–16). He said, “I have left you upon a clear white path, its night like its day; none strays from it but one who is destroyed” (Ibn Mājah). To follow his guidance is to follow *hudāya* of this verse in the last and completing form. He taught, at graves and in consolations, the very words *lā khawfun ʿalayhim wa lā hum yaḥzanūn* as the description of the believer’s end. The funeral is not the cancellation of 2:38. It is one of the places it is meant to be heard.

Theologically the verse is the charter of *nubuwwah*. After the fall, Allah (الله) does not leave the *khalīfah* with only reason and the remaining light of *fiṭrah*. He sends *hudā*. Ibn Taymiyyah wrote that the *fiṭrah* is a receptivity, and that revelation is what it is receptive for; neither is a replacement of the other. Those who claim that Ādam’s children needed no prophets, after this verse, have not read the terms of the descent. Those who claim that following is without reason have not read *tabiʿa*, which is an act of a mind that recognizes the guide. The next verse will state the opposite term of the charter: those who cover and deny the signs will be companions of the Fire, remaining.

For the reader on the earth now, 2:38 is the reason the Book of 2:2 exists. Guidance has come from Him. The mosquito of 2:26, the challenge of 2:23, the stories that will fill the rest of the sūrah, are that *hudā*. Following it is the one condition of the only promise that answers both the enemy of 2:36 and the death of 2:28: no fear, no grief. Not because the world is safe, but because the end is. The successful of 2:5 are this verse’s followers under another name. The losers of 2:27 are those who will not be this verse’s *man tabiʿa*. The sūrah has taken twenty-eight verses to arrive at a sentence a child can memorize. The child should memorize it. The adult should follow it. The next verse will say what happens if he does not.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:39

> But those who disbelieve and deny Our signs will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.”

**Commentary:**

The charter of the descent has a second article, and it is as clear as the first: *wa alladhīna kafarū wa kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā ulāʾika aṣḥābu al-nāri hum fīhā khālidūn*, “But those who disbelieve and deny Our signs will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.” The *wa* is the *wa* of the other side of *fa-man tabiʿa*. Guidance comes; some follow; some cover and deny. *Kafarū* is the covering already defined at 2:6. *Kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā* is to call the signs a lie. *Āyāt* are signs, verses, the miracles of prophets, the sentences of the Book, the tokens in the self and on the horizon (41:53). To deny them is not to fail to understand a mosquito. It is to give the lie to what has arrived *minnī*, from Me, as 2:38 promised. The two verbs together are creed and response: they cover the truth in the heart, and they give the lie with the tongue and the life.

*Ulāʾika*, those — the far demonstrative of 2:5 and 2:16 — now points at this company. *Aṣḥāb al-nār*, companions of the Fire, people of it, residents whose companionship is with blaze rather than with the purified spouses of 2:25. The Fire was already named at 2:24 as fuelled with people and stones, prepared for the disbelievers. Here the preparation receives its population in the language of the Ādam-story. The first man’s children are not doomed by his slip. They are sorted by 2:38–39: followers of guidance, or deniers of signs. Ibn ʿAbbās, as the mufassirūn record, treated these two verses as the first *sharīʿah* of the earth: a promise and a threat that every prophet would repeat. Nūḥ (peace be upon him) will say it; Ibrāhīm will say it; Mūsā will say it; the Prophet (ﷺ) will say it. Al-Baqarah says it at the exit from the Garden so that no one may claim the terms were hidden.

*Hum fīhā khālidūn*, they are in it remaining forever. The same *khulūd* that was the joy of 2:25 is the grief of 2:39. Two gardens of time, two companies. Ahl al-Sunnah hold, against the Khawārij who made every major sinner eternal in the Fire and against the Murjiʾah who emptied works of threat, that eternity in the Fire is for those who die upon *kufr*, and that the people of *tawḥīd* who are punished are not *khālidūn* in this sense. The hadiths of intercession, of the going out of whoever has in his heart a mustard seed of faith, are the commentary (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). This verse’s people are those who *kafarū wa kadhdhabū*, a pairing the Qur'an uses of those who have refused the message at the root. “Those who disbelieve and deny Our signs, they are the companions of the Fire, remaining therein” is repeated in 2:217, 3:116, 5:10, 5:86, and elsewhere, a refrain of the Book.

The Prophet (ﷺ) described the Fire until the Companions wept, and then he described the Garden, as 2:24–25 had done. He said, “The least punished of the people of the Fire on the Day of Resurrection is a man who has two embers placed under his feet, by which his brain boils” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim), so that no one would think *khulūd* a metaphor of mild regret. He also said, “Whoever dies not having associated anything with Allah will enter Paradise, and whoever dies associating anything with Allah will enter the Fire” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). 2:39 is that second clause in the vocabulary of Ādam’s descent. The signs denied include, for this *ummah*, the Qur'an that began *Alif-Lām-Mīm* and the Messenger (ﷺ) who is *ʿabdinā* of 2:23. To deny them after they have come is the *takdhīb* this verse names.

Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr both note the mercy of the pairing. Allah (الله) does not mention the Fire at the beginning of the human story without having mentioned, in the verse before, the open door. The door is *tabiʿa hudāya*. Iblīs’s way is to make 2:39 look like the whole religion, so that the servant despairs, or to make 2:38 look like the whole religion without conditions, so that the servant presumes. The reciter who holds both verses holds the religion. Ibn al-Qayyim wrote that the heart should be like a bird: fear of 2:39 is one wing, hope of 2:38 is the other, and love of the One who spoke both is the head that steers.

The grammar of *aṣḥāb* is companionship. A man is the companion of what he sits with. The followers of guidance will have, in 2:25, gardens, rivers, fruit, and pure spouses; the deniers will have the Fire as their sitting-mate. “They will have no food except the thorn of *ḍarīʿ*, which neither nourishes nor avails against hunger” (88:6–7). The Prophet (ﷺ) asked his Companions, “Do you know what the Fire is like?” They said, “Allah and His Messenger know best.” He said, “Its blaze is seventy times the fire of this world” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, in meaning). *Khālidūn* means that this companionship does not break. The time *ilā ḥīn* of the earth’s *matāʿ* (2:36) does not apply there.

For the present age the words *kafarū wa kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā* describe more than a man who says “there is no God.” They describe the culture that treats every sign as raw material for a joke, a career, or a private spirituality without a Lord who speaks. The *āyāt* are still arriving: the Book is recited, the heavens are still seven in the report of 2:29, the self is still a sign of 2:28. Denial is work. It must be maintained against the rain, the fruit, the birth, the death, the mosquito, and the sūrah. The verse says the work has a wage, and the wage is remaining. The believer does not use 2:39 as a stone to throw only at others. He uses it as a fence around his own following. Have I covered a sign? Have I given the lie to a verse by a life that contradicts it? 2:8’s people said they believed and were not believers. 2:39’s people deny and remain. Between them 2:38 is still open. The next verse will turn from the father of the species to a particular people among his children, a people who received guidance in abundance and are now told to remember it: the Children of Israel.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:40

> O children of Israel! Remember My favours upon you. Fulfil your covenant and I will fulfil Mine, and stand in awe of Me ˹alone˺.

**Commentary:**

The universal story of Ādam now narrows to a people who carried a Book before this Book: *yā Banī Isrāʾīla udhkurū niʿmatiya allatī anʿamtu ʿalaykum wa awfū bi-ʿahdī ūfi bi-ʿahdikum wa iyyāya fa-rhabūn*, “O Children of Israel! Remember My favours upon you. Fulfil your covenant and I will fulfil Mine, and stand in awe of Me alone.” *Yā Banī Isrāʾīl* is the first address to a named nation in the sūrah. *Isrāʾīl* is Yaʿqūb (peace be upon him), Israel, the son of Isḥāq, the son of Ibrāhīm (peace be upon them). His children are the tribes who were given Mūsā (peace be upon him), the Torah, the long history of deliverance and of breaking that this sūrah will now recount from 2:40 to well past 2:100. The Jews of Madinah — Banū Qaynuqāʿ, Banū al-Naḍīr, Banū Qurayẓah, and those who sat with them — are the first hearers. The wording, as always, is larger than the first hearers. Every child of Israel until the Hour is called; and every child of Ādam who has received a favor is meant to overhear.

*Udhkurū niʿmatiya allatī anʿamtu ʿalaykum*, remember My favor which I bestowed upon you. *Dhikr* is remembrance, the opposite of the *nisyān* that will be charged against them in later verses. *Niʿmah* is favor, a gift that lands on the servant from above. The singular *niʿmatī* can mean the genus of favor, all of it gathered as one. What were the favors? The sūrah will list them: preference over the worlds of their time (2:47), rescue from Pharaoh (2:49), the parting of the sea (2:50), the pardoning of the calf (2:52), the Book and the criterion (2:53), the shade and the manna and the quails (2:57), the twelve springs (2:60). Ibn ʿAbbās and the mufassirūn said “remember” here is remember with thanks, with the limbs, with fulfillment, not with a memory that does not change a life. “If you are grateful, I will surely increase you” (14:7). Remembrance of favor is itself a covenant.

*Wa awfū bi-ʿahdī ūfi bi-ʿahdikum*, fulfill My covenant, I will fulfill your covenant. *Wafāʾ* is to fill a promise to the brim, to pay what is owed without shorting. *ʿAhdī*, My covenant, is the covenant Allah (الله) took from them in the Torah: to worship Him alone, to follow His law, and — as Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿAlī, and others said in reports carried by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr — to believe in the Prophet (ﷺ) whose description they had. “Those who follow the Messenger, the unlettered prophet, whom they find written with them in the Torah and the Gospel” (7:157). “Remember the favor of Allah upon you and His covenant with which He bound you when you said, ‘We hear and we obey’” (5:7). *Ūfi bi-ʿahdikum* is the reply of the Lord: if you pay, I pay. Their side of the paid covenant, in the Madinan context, would be support, belief, and the opening of what they knew; His side would be the Gardens of 2:25, the *falāḥ* of 2:5, the no-fear of 2:38. “Allah has purchased from the believers their lives and their properties in exchange for Paradise” (9:111). A covenant is a purchase with two cashiers. They had begun to short the payment — hiding, distorting, wishing to be paid without believing in what confirmed their own Book (2:41, 2:89). This verse is the invoice.

*Wa iyyāya fa-rhabūn*, and of Me, of Me then be in awe. The object *iyyāya* is placed first, the same *taqdīm* that made *iyyāka naʿbudu* a restriction in the Fātiḥah. Be in awe of Me, not of the tribe, not of the losing of a station in Madinah, not of the mockery of those who would say you followed an unlettered prophet. *Rahbah* is awe that contains fear, a dread that is not panic but reverence. The Qur'an uses *khashyah*, *khawf*, *taqwā*, *rahbah* as a family of words for the heart’s taking of Allah (الله) seriously. “And of Me, then, be in awe” will be paired at 2:41 with “and of Me, then, be mindful.” Al-Rāghib distinguished *rahbah* as a fear with shrinking and vigilance. Ibn al-Qayyim placed it among the stations of the travellers: the servant who remembers favors without awe becomes spoiled; the servant who has awe without remembrance of favors becomes crushed. 2:40 gives both in one breath.

The Prophet (ﷺ) dealt with the Children of Israel in Madinah as this verse deals with them: reminding, not inventing a hatred. He had the Jews at the beginning of the Madinan years as neighbors under compact. He recited this sūrah in their hearing. He said, “Do not exceed in praising me as the Christians exceeded in praising the son of Mary. I am only a servant, so say: the servant of Allah and His messenger” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī), which is a *wafāʾ* of his own to the covenant of *tawḥīd* that Israel was also given. He said, “The scholars of the Children of Israel split into seventy-two sects,” in reports that warn this *ummah* not to repeat their *qaṭʿ* of what was to be joined. The long address that begins here is therefore a mercy to two audiences: to Israel, that they might fulfill; to the Muslims, that they might not become a second Israel of forgotten favors.

For the present reader the verse is easily misused as a stone, and rightly used as a mirror. As a stone, it becomes a permission to despise a people as a people, which the next verses’ own justice will not support: they will distinguish those who were true from those who distorted, and 2:62 will still speak of reward for whoever among them believed. As a mirror, it asks every community that has been given a *niʿmah* — this *ummah* above all, given the last Book and the last Prophet (ﷺ) — whether it remembers, whether it fulfills, whether it fears Allah (الله) alone. “Allah has promised those of you who believe and do good that He will surely grant them succession in the land” (24:55): that too is a covenant with a payment. The favors upon the Muslims are not smaller than the favors upon Israel. The invoice is not lighter. Remember. Fulfill, and He will fulfill. And fear Me alone. The next verses will begin the list of favors, and the list of failures, in a detail that will occupy the heart of al-Baqarah. 2:40 is the heading of that ledger.
