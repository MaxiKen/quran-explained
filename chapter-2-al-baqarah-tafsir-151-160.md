# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 151–160

This is the passage that closes the community's constitution and then turns to the trial it will face. It opens by closing a loop: the messenger prayed for at verse 129 is here declared sent, reciting, purifying and teaching (151). Then the exchange that defines the relationship — remember Me and I will remember you; be thankful and do not cover over what you have been given (152). Then the two instruments the believer is given for the weight of life: patience and the prayer, with the promise that Allah is with the patient (153). Then the declaration that those killed in His way are not dead but alive, though you cannot perceive it (154). Then the announcement, certain and mitigated, that there will be testing — fear, hunger, and loss in wealth, lives and crops — with the command to give good news to the steadfast (155). Then their response when the blow falls, in the words the whole community has recited ever since (156), and the three things that come down upon them (157). Then the ruling on Safa and Marwa, with one of the most instructive occasions of revelation in the Qur'an (158). Then the curse on those who conceal what has been made clear (159) — and, in the same sentence, the door left open for them, on three conditions (160).

---

## Verse 151

**"Since We have sent you a messenger from among yourselves—reciting to you Our revelations, purifying you, teaching you the Book and wisdom, and teaching you what you never knew—"**

In simple terms: just as [We favoured you], We sent among you a messenger from yourselves, reciting Our signs to you, purifying you, and teaching you the Book, the wisdom, and what you did not know.

*Kama arsalna fikum rasulan minkum* — "as We sent among you a messenger from among you."

The crux is the opening *kama* — "as / just as" — and what it is comparing. The commentators gave three connections, and they are complementary rather than exclusive. The first connects it forward to the favours just mentioned: just as We completed Our favour upon you with the qiblah and made you a witness-community, so too We sent you a messenger. The second connects it backward to the previous communities: just as We sent messengers before you, so We have sent to you. And the third — the one that matters most for the structure of the surah — connects it to verse 129, where Abraham and Ishmael prayed *Rabbana wa-b'ath fihim rasulan minhum*.

**On that third reading, this verse closes a loop twenty-two verses long.** At 129 Abraham asked for a messenger from among his descendants who would recite, teach and purify. Here the Qur'an turns to the community and says: as you asked, We sent. The prayer of the patriarch and the address to his descendants' descendants meet in this verse.

*Minkum* — "from among you." The same word as *minhum* in Abraham's prayer, and the same point as at 14:4: "And We did not send any messenger except [speaking] in the language of his people." One of you — known to you, verifiable by you, whose lineage and character you had forty years to examine before he said anything at all.

*Yatlu 'alaykum ayatina wa yuzakkikum wa yu'allimukumu al-kitaba wa al-hikmata wa yu'allimukum ma lam takunu ta'lamun* — "reciting to you Our signs and purifying you and teaching you the Book and the wisdom, and teaching you what you did not know."

Note the two differences from the prayer at verse 129.

The first is the order. At 129 the sequence was recite, **teach**, **purify**. Here it is recite, **purify**, **teach**. The commentators discussed this. Some held it significant: purification is placed before instruction because purification is its condition — a vessel has to be clean before it can receive. Others observed that the conjunctive *wa* in such lists does not of itself indicate sequence, and that the two verses describe one work rather than two orders. Both readings are transmitted, and the second is the safer one; but the first is the one the tradition has acted on, and it is the reason the discipline of *tazkiyah* is treated as prior to the accumulation of knowledge.

The second is the addition: *wa yu'allimukum ma lam takunu ta'lamun* — "and teaching you what you did not know." In the prayer it was three functions. Here a fourth clause is appended, and it widens the scope from the Book and the wisdom to everything the community needed and did not possess. The parallel is 4:113: "And Allah has revealed to you the Book and wisdom and has taught you that which you did not know. And ever has the favour of Allah upon you been great."

For practice, three things.

The first is the baseline. *Ma lam takunu ta'lamun* — what you did not know. The verse states plainly, to the community, that before this it did not know. And it states it not as an insult but as the measure of a favour. There is a version of religious pride that cannot say "I did not know," and a version of cultural shame that hears the sentence as humiliation. Neither is the verse's register. The register is: you were in a condition, and you were taken out of it, by gift.

The second is the sequence, applied to anyone who teaches or parents. Instruction delivered to someone unprepared to receive it does not land. The tradition's insistence on character before curriculum, on *adab* before *'ilm*, is this verse's ordering. A person who wants to learn something should ask himself what in him needs cleansing before the learning can enter — and it is rarely a matter of more information.

And the third is *minkum*. The messenger was one of them. That is a principle for anyone who carries a message to a people: the carrier who belongs to the people is not an inferior instrument, and a stranger who does not belong is not a superior one. The strongest carrier of a thing to a community is one of that community who has actually become it.

## Verse 152

**"remember Me; I will remember you. And thank Me, and never be ungrateful."**

In simple terms: remember Me and I will remember you. Be thankful to Me, and do not cover over [what you have been given].

*Fa-dhkuruni adhkurkum* — "so remember Me; I will remember you."

Note the *fa* — "so" — which ties the verse to what came before. Because I sent you a messenger who recited, purified and taught what you did not know, therefore: remember Me. The remembrance is the response to the gift, not a free-floating exercise.

*Dhikr* is remembrance, mention, recollection — bringing someone to mind, and speaking of him.

And *adhkurkum* — "I will remember you." Allah does not forget, so His remembering cannot be the recovery of something lost. The commentators explained it as His care, His attention, His mention of the servant, and His mercy toward him.

And the commentary on this verse is one of the greatest hadith in the religion — a hadith qudsi recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Abu Hurayrah, in which the Prophet ﷺ said that Allah says:

"I am as My servant thinks of Me, and I am with him when he remembers Me. If he remembers Me within himself, I remember him within Myself. And if he remembers Me in an assembly, I remember him in an assembly better than it. And if he draws near to Me a hand's span, I draw near to him an arm's length. And if he draws near to Me an arm's length, I draw near to him a fathom. And if he comes to Me walking, I come to him running."

**And the thing to notice about that hadith — the thing most readers miss — is that the divine response exceeds the human act at every single stage.** Within yourself → within Myself. In an assembly → in an assembly better than it. A hand's span → an arm's length. An arm's length → a fathom. Walking → running.

Four pairs, and in each pair the response is larger than the approach. Not equal to it. Larger. That is the arithmetic of this verse, and it is the reason the tradition treats *dhikr* as the most accessible of all acts: the smallest genuine turning produces a response out of all proportion to its cost.

The related narration, recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim, describes the mechanism of the mention in the assembly above: when Allah loves a servant He calls Gabriel and says that He loves so-and-so, so Gabriel loves him, and then it is announced in the heavens, and he is granted acceptance on the earth.

*Wa-shkuru li* — "and give thanks to Me." And on *shukr*: in the Qur'an and in the works of the tradition, gratitude is not primarily a matter of words. The three-part description that the scholars of the heart gave is this — to acknowledge the blessing inwardly, to praise the Giver outwardly, and to use the blessing in His obedience. A person who says "alhamdulillah" over a faculty he uses to harm people has performed the middle third and failed the other two.

*Wa-la takfurun* — "and do not be ungrateful."

And here is one of the most important facts in the language of the religion. The word for ingratitude and the word for disbelief are **the same word**: *kufr*, from a root meaning to cover, to conceal, to bury. Covering a blessing and covering the truth are one movement with one name.

The Qur'an presses the identity hard elsewhere. 14:7: "If you are grateful, I will surely increase you [in favour]; but if you deny [*kafartum*], indeed, My punishment is severe." 27:40, in Sulayman's words: "This is from the favour of my Lord to test me whether I will be grateful or ungrateful [*ashkuru am akfuru*]." And 39:7: "If you disbelieve — indeed, Allah is Free from need of you. And He does not approve for His servants disbelief. And if you are grateful, He approves it for you."

For practice, this short verse gives three things.

The first is the reciprocity, and its asymmetry. Remember, and you are remembered — and the remembering that comes back is greater than the remembering that went out. Most people approach devotional practice with a sense of cost. The hadith reframes it entirely: every approach is met with a larger one.

The second is the first clause of the hadith, which is one of the most pastorally loaded sentences in existence: *I am as My servant thinks of Me.* A person who has quietly concluded that God is severe, uninterested, or unreachable has, by the terms of this hadith, described the God he will meet. That is not a claim that thinking makes things true regardless of the truth; it is a claim about which posture receives. And it means that despair is not modesty and presumption is not faith.

And the third is *kufr* as covering. Held alongside the command to give thanks, it produces a question that is worth asking regularly: what have I covered over? Not only what have I denied — what have I received, and quietly filed as though it were normal, or mine, or nothing? The opposite of gratitude in this verse is not complaint. It is concealment.

## Verse 153

**"O believers! Seek comfort in patience and prayer. Allah is truly with those who are patient."**

In simple terms: you who believe, seek help through steadfastness and the prayer. Allah is certainly with the steadfast.

*Ya ayyuha alladhina amanu ista'inu bi-al-sabri wa al-salati* — "O you who believe, seek help through patience and the prayer."

*Ista'inu* is form X of the root meaning help: to seek aid, to ask for assistance. And note the preposition: *bi* — **seek help by means of** patience and prayer. The two are named as instruments, not as virtues to be admired from a distance. The verse is addressed to people in need of help and it tells them what to reach for.

*Al-sabr* is from a root meaning to bind and to restrain: holding the self back from what it reaches for. The tradition divides it into three, and the division is derived from the Qur'an's own usage:

**Patience in obedience** — the steadfastness to keep doing the worship when it is heavy, boring, unseen or unrewarded by anyone.
**Patience in restraint** — holding the self back from what is forbidden, which is the hardest of the three for most people because the reward is invisible and the pleasure is immediate.
**Patience in affliction** — endurance of what befalls you without turning on God in complaint.

A person who is struggling can usually identify which of the three is failing, and the diagnosis is more useful than a general exhortation to "be patient."

**Now the structural point, which is important.** This verse has an almost identical twin at 2:45: "And seek help through patience and prayer; and indeed, it is difficult except for the humbly submissive [*al-khashi'in*]."

The surah gives the same command twice — once to the Children of Israel, and once to the believers. That is deliberate and it is one of the surah's recurring devices: the community is handed the instrument the previous community was handed, with the previous community's failure standing as the commentary. Verse 45 adds the note that it is *kabirah*, heavy, except for those who are inwardly submissive. Verse 153 replaces that caveat with the promise. The instrument is the same; what is said about it changes.

*Inna Allaha ma'a al-sabirin* — "indeed Allah is with the patient."

***Ma'iyyah*** — "with-ness" — and the theological crux is what it means.

The Qur'an uses *ma'a* of Allah in two distinct ways, and the distinction is load-bearing. There is the **general** with-ness, which is with all creation by His knowledge, sight, hearing and encompassing: "And He is with you wherever you are" (57:4); "There is no private conversation of three except that He is the fourth of them" (58:7). And there is the **particular** with-ness, which is with the believers by aid, support, protection and care: "Indeed, Allah is with those who fear Him and those who are doers of good" (16:128); "Do not grieve; indeed Allah is with us" (9:40, in the cave); "Indeed, I am with you both; I hear and I see" (20:46).

This verse is of the second kind. It is a promise of help, not a statement about location. And the tradition's position on the attribute has been consistent: it is affirmed as stated, without asking how, and it is understood as His knowledge and — for those described — His aid and support. It is not physical adjacency, and it is not indwelling. The Salaf affirmed it and did not enquire into its modality; later scholars largely read it as aid, with some reading it figuratively. Both readings are held, and neither dissolves the promise.

For practice, two things.

The first is that patience and prayer are named as the **means**, which means they are to be used rather than possessed. Most people treat patience as something they hope to have and prayer as something they hope to maintain. The verse treats both as tools you pick up when you need help. The practical consequence is specific: when something is heavy, the prescribed response is not to wait until you feel steadfast, and not to wait until the prayer feels meaningful. It is to pray, and to hold. In that order, or in either order — the verse names them together and the tradition has always read them as mutually sustaining, the prayer being what makes the steadfastness possible and the steadfastness being what keeps the prayer alive.

And the second is the promise, and its scope. *Ma'a al-sabirin* — with the patient. Not with the successful, not with the comfortable, not with the vindicated. The with-ness attaches to the person who is holding on. There is no promise in the Qur'an more directly useful to a person in the middle of something long.

## Verse 154

**"Never say that those martyred in the cause of Allah are dead—in fact, they are alive! But you do not perceive it."**

In simple terms: do not say, of those killed in Allah's way, "they are dead." Rather, they are alive — but you do not perceive it.

*Wa la taqulu li-man yuqtalu fi sabili Allahi amwatun* — "and do not say of those who are killed in the way of Allah: [they are] dead."

The transmitted context places this among the Muslims killed at Badr, the first battle, when the community was still small enough that every death was a hole in it. The prohibition is against the *statement* — *la taqulu* — because the statement carries a whole view of what death is.

*Fi sabili Allahi* — "in the way of Allah." And this phrase needs its definition, because it is the one most frequently detached from it.

The defining hadith is recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Abu Musa al-Ash'ari: a man came and asked about the man who fights for spoils, the man who fights to be remembered, and the man who fights to be seen — which of them is in the way of Allah? And the Prophet ﷺ said: **whoever fights so that the word of Allah is supreme, that is in the way of Allah.**

So the way of Allah is defined by the aim, and the aim is not courage, or loyalty, or even — by itself — the justice of the cause. It is that the word of Allah be supreme. A person can fight for his people, or his land, or his anger, and none of those is the way of Allah however legitimate they may otherwise be.

*Bal ahya'un* — "rather, they are alive."

*Bal* is a reversal, not a softening: not "they are not dead" but "rather, they are living." And the question the commentators addressed is what kind of life this is.

The position of Ahl al-Sunnah is that it is real. It is the life of the *barzakh*, the intermediate state between death and resurrection — real, not figurative; not the life of this world and not yet the life of the Hereafter; of a modality known to Allah. It is **not** a way of saying that their memory lives on, or that their cause outlasted them. The verse says *ahya'*, living, and the statement is about them.

The expansion is at 3:169–170: "And never think of those who have been killed in the cause of Allah as dead. Rather, they are alive with their Lord, receiving provision, rejoicing in what Allah has bestowed upon them of His bounty, and receiving good tidings about those [to be martyred] after them who have not yet joined them — that there will be no fear concerning them, nor will they grieve."

And the authoritative transmitted explanation is the hadith recorded by Muslim, in which Masruq said: we asked 'Abdullah [ibn Mas'ud] about this verse, and he said: we asked about it, and [the Prophet ﷺ] said: **"Their souls are in the crops of green birds, having lamps hung from the Throne, roaming wherever they wish in Paradise, then returning to those lamps."** And in the narration, their Lord looks upon them and asks whether they desire anything further.

*Wa lakin la tash'urun* — "but you do not perceive." *Tash'urun* is from the root of perception and awareness.

**And note where the verse locates the limitation: in us, not in them.** They are alive. You do not perceive it. The defect is in the faculty, not in the fact.

That is a general principle worth carrying, because the Qur'an states several things that the senses do not corroborate, and the reader's instinct is to adjust the statement until it matches what he can perceive. The verse forbids that move. When revelation asserts and perception does not register, the conclusion is about perception.

For practice, three things.

The first is the definition of *sabil Allah*, and it is a guardrail rather than a technicality. Martyrdom is not available for any cause a person considers sacred, and it is not conferred by the manner of a death. It is conferred by the aim, and the aim belongs to God to judge. Which is why — and this is the standing rule maintained throughout this commentary — **no individual may be declared a martyr by name**, however he died. The Prophet ﷺ named some; we do not have that knowledge about anyone else.

The second is the hadith that prevents the romanticisation of one form of death. Recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Abu Hurayrah: **the martyrs are five — the one who dies of plague, the one who dies of a stomach illness, the one who drowns, the one who is killed by a collapsing structure, and the one who is killed in the way of Allah.** Muslim's version adds further categories, including the one who dies defending his property and the one who dies defending his family.

The effect of that hadith is enormous and is often missed. It places the person who died of an illness in his bed in the same category as the person who died on the field. It means the community's ordinary dead — the ones who died badly, slowly, in hospital, of cancer, in a flood — are not in a lesser category than the ones who died dramatically. A religion that only honours the dramatic death has abandoned most of its dead.

And the third is the *la tash'urun*. Applied: when you are told something about the unseen that you cannot verify or even picture, the correct response is not to reinterpret it into something you can picture. The verse permits the reader to say "I do not perceive this" — it says exactly that — and it does not permit him to conclude from that non-perception that the thing is not so.

## Verse 155

**"We will certainly test you with a touch of fear and famine and loss of property, life, and crops. Give good news to those who patiently endure—"**

In simple terms: We will most certainly test you with something of fear and hunger, and a diminution of wealth, lives and crops. And give good news to the steadfast.

*Wa la-nabluwannakum* — "and We will most certainly test you." The emphasis is doubled — the particle *lam* with the emphatic *nun* — and the mood is an oath. This is not a possibility. It is announced as a certainty.

And note the root: *b-l-w*, the same root as *ibtala* at verse 124, where Allah tested Abraham. **Abraham was tested, and so will you be.** The surah has been describing the founder's trial at length and now tells the community that the same thing is coming for them, which is a form of preparation rather than a threat.

The necessity of trial is stated elsewhere in terms that leave no room for the idea that affliction means something has gone wrong. 29:2–3: "Do the people think that they will be left to say 'We believe' and they will not be tried? But We have certainly tried those before them." And within this very surah, 2:214: "Or do you think that you will enter Paradise while such [trial] has not yet come to you as came to those who passed on before you? They were touched by poverty and hardship and were shaken until [even their] messenger and those who believed with him said, 'When is the help of Allah?' Unquestionably, the help of Allah is near."

*Bi-shay'in mina al-khawfi wa al-ju'i* — "with **something** of fear and hunger."

**And here is one of the great instances of divine gentleness in the Qur'an.** The verse does not say "with fear and hunger." It says *bi-shay'in* — with **something**, a portion, a measure — *min*, of, fear and hunger. The commentators noted the indefinite and partitive form and read it as a mitigation: the trial is real, and it is also limited, and the limitation is stated in the same breath as the threat.

*Al-khawf* is fear — of enemies, of loss, of what is coming. *Al-ju'* is hunger — famine, scarcity, the failure of provision.

*Wa naqsin mina al-amwali wa al-anfusi wa al-thamarati* — "and a **diminution** of wealth, selves and crops."

*Naqs* is reduction, not elimination. Again *min* — a reduction of wealth, not its removal; a reduction in lives, not their annihilation; a reduction in crops, not the end of the harvest.

And the five categories between them cover the whole map of human vulnerability. **Fear** — the security of the person. **Hunger** — the sustenance of the body. **Wealth** — provision and standing. **Lives** — *al-anfus*, read variously as your own person (illness, death) or your people (the loss of those you love); both readings are transmitted and both are true. **Crops** — livelihood, the produce of your labour, the thing you planted.

*Wa bashshir al-sabirin* — "and give good news to the steadfast."

*Bashshir* — bring glad tidings. And note the structure: a verse that has just announced testing, fear, hunger, and loss in three further categories ends with a command to bring **good news**.

That is not a contradiction and it is worth pausing on. The trial is announced, and the immediate instruction to the Prophet ﷺ is to announce good news to the people undergoing it. The trial is not the last word, and the bearer of the message is not permitted to deliver it as though it were.

For practice, three things.

The first is the certainty. Trial is not a deviation from the plan; it is stated in the plan. The single most common way affliction damages faith is by being interpreted as evidence that God has withdrawn, or that one has done something wrong, or that the religion is not working. The verse removes that interpretation in advance: *la-nabluwannakum*. The question is not whether but how, and the how in this verse is described as *something* of fear and hunger and a *reduction* — which is to say, less than the full weight.

The second is the mitigation, and it is a model for how to speak to someone who is suffering. God announces the trial and simultaneously minimises its description. Not "you will lose everything" but "a reduction." Anyone who has ever tried to comfort an afflicted person has something to learn from the verse's own restraint: state the reality, and do not inflate it.

And the third is the command to give good news, turned into a ruling about how the afflicted are to be spoken to. The default posture of the religious person toward someone in trial is too often diagnosis — what did you do, what is the lesson, what must you repent of. The verse assigns a different task: *bashshir*. Bring good news. There is a place for everything else, but it is not the first place and it is not what this verse assigns.

## Verse 156

**"who, when faced with a disaster, say, “Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will ˹all˺ return.”"**

In simple terms: those who, when a calamity strikes them, say: we belong to Allah, and to Him we are returning.

*Alladhina idha asabathum musibatun qalu inna lillahi wa inna ilayhi raji'un* — "those who, when a calamity strikes them, say: indeed we belong to Allah, and indeed to Him we are returning."

*Musibah* is from a root meaning to hit the mark: something that strikes. The word covers the whole range, from the largest bereavement to the smallest loss, and the tradition has never restricted it.

And the saying itself — *inna lillahi wa inna ilayhi raji'un*, the *istirja'* — is among the most recited sentences in the religion, and it is worth taking apart, because its two halves do different work.

**The first half is a statement about ownership: we belong to Him.** What was taken was His. I was never the owner of it; I was the one holding it. A person who truly holds that the child, the marriage, the health, the money, the years were held on loan has already made most of the peace the verse is offering — and the sentence is the means of holding it rather than the proof of having held it.

**The second half is a statement about destination: to Him we are returning.** What has gone has gone ahead, and I am going to the same place. The separation is real and it is also temporary, and the direction of the one who left and the one who remains is the same.

**And the two together are what make it work.** Ownership alone could read as cold: it was His, He took it, that is that. Return alone could read as abstract: we all die eventually. Held together, the sentence says: the thing was His, and I am going to Him — so what has happened is a transfer within His ownership and a movement toward reunion. That is the whole of the consolation, and it is contained in eight words.

Note that they *say* it. *Qalu*. The verse does not describe them as feeling it or believing it silently. It describes speech. The practice is verbal, and the verbal practice is what trains the reflex.

The Sunnah supplies what follows the verse's words. Recorded by Muslim from Umm Salamah, who said: I heard the Messenger of Allah ﷺ say — **"There is no servant who is afflicted with a calamity and says, 'Indeed we belong to Allah and to Him we return. O Allah, reward me in my calamity and grant me something better in exchange for it,' except that Allah rewards him in his calamity and grants him something better in exchange."** She said: when Abu Salamah died, I said it — and Allah granted me in exchange the Messenger of Allah ﷺ.

Note carefully: the verse's words are *inna lillahi wa inna ilayhi raji'un*. The petition that follows — *Allahumma ajirni fi musibati wa-ukhluf li khayran minha* — comes from the hadith, not from the verse. Both are prescribed; they are just not from the same source, and it is worth knowing which is which.

The other governing hadith is recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim: **no fatigue, nor illness, nor grief, nor sorrow, nor harm, nor distress befalls a Muslim — not even a thorn that pricks him — except that Allah thereby expiates some of his sins.**

For practice, two things.

The first is that the scale of the *musibah* is not specified, and the thorn hadith settles the question. The small losses count. A broken object, a missed appointment, a minor injury, a small humiliation — the *istirja'* is for these too. And the reason for saying it at the small ones is not that they are equivalent to the large ones but that the reflex is built at the small ones. The person who has said *inna lillahi* over a hundred small losses has the sentence ready when the large one arrives, and the person who has reserved it for bereavements has to construct it from nothing at the moment he is least able to.

And the second is the content of the sentence as an actual claim, which is worth being honest about. A person can say *inna lillahi* while inwardly maintaining that the thing was his and that it was taken unjustly. The sentence is not a charm; it is a claim, and saying it is the act of taking up the claim. The tradition's counsel is to say it and then to let the saying do its work over time rather than to measure its sincerity at the moment of saying it, which is exactly when it cannot be measured.

## Verse 157

**"They are the ones who will receive Allah’s blessings and mercy. And it is they who are ˹rightly˺ guided."**

In simple terms: upon those are blessings from their Lord and mercy. And those are the guided ones.

*Ula'ika 'alayhim salawatun min rabbihim wa rahmatun* — "those — upon them are blessings from their Lord, and mercy."

***Salawat*** is the plural of *salah*, and the crux is what it means when it is from Allah. The commentators gave it as praise, commendation, and forgiveness — and the usage elsewhere supports the combined sense. When *salah* is from Allah it is His praise of the servant and His mercy upon him; when it is from the angels it is their supplication for forgiveness; when it is from the believers it is du'a. The well-known hadith recorded by Muslim — that whoever sends one *salah* upon the Prophet ﷺ, Allah sends ten upon him — works on the same word with the same doubling that governs verse 152's reciprocity.

And note the preposition: *'alayhim* — **upon** them. The blessings are described as descending, coming down onto the person.

*Wa rahmatun* — and mercy. That is the second of the three gifts.

*Wa ula'ika humu al-muhtadun* — "and those are the guided."

And the third gift is the one most easily skimmed past. The steadfast are described not as rewarded, or compensated, or even honoured, but as **guided**.

That inverts the order most people assume. The ordinary assumption is that guidance produces patience: you become guided, and then you are able to bear things. The verse says that the people who bear things well are *thereby* the guided ones. Patience is not the fruit of guidance only; it is a form of guidance, and it is named as the mark by which the guided are identified.

For practice, this verse is a reward-verse and the reward is worth looking at carefully, because of what it attaches to.

The three gifts — blessings, mercy, and the name of being guided — are attached to the **bearing**, not to the removal. The trial is not described as ending. What is promised is what comes down upon the person *while* he is in it and because of how he is in it.

That matters because most people's idea of the reward for patience is that the difficulty finally stops. Sometimes it does and sometimes it does not, and the verse makes no promise about it. What it promises is that the person in the middle of it is under something descending, and is being counted as guided. For anyone in a long trial with no end in sight — a chronic illness, a marriage, a debt, a grief that has simply become the shape of life — this is the more useful promise, because it is available now.

And there is a second application in the word *muhtadun*. If the steadfast are the guided, then a person unsure about his own state has a place to look that is not his feelings: how am I bearing what has been given me? Not "do I feel guided" but "am I holding." The verse makes endurance legible as a sign rather than merely as a trial to be survived.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 158

**"Indeed, ˹the hills of˺ Ṣafa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah. So whoever performs the pilgrimage or minor pilgrimage, let them walk between ˹the two hills˺. And whoever does good willingly, Allah is truly Appreciative, All-Knowing."**

In simple terms: Safa and Marwa are among the rites of Allah. So whoever performs the pilgrimage or the lesser pilgrimage — there is no blame on him in walking between them. And whoever does good voluntarily, Allah is Appreciative, All-Knowing.

*Inna al-Safa wa al-Marwata min sha'a'iri Allahi* — "indeed Safa and Marwa are among the rites of Allah."

Al-Safa and al-Marwah are the two rises near the Ka'bah in Makkah, between which the pilgrim walks seven times — the *sa'y*.

*Sha'a'ir* is the plural of *sha'irah*: the rites, the marks, the visible signs by which the religion is recognisable. The Qur'an uses the word for the rites of the pilgrimage (5:2: "do not violate the rites of Allah") and for the sacrificial animals (22:36), and the governing statement is 22:32: "And whoever honours the rites of Allah — indeed, it is from the piety of hearts." So the *sha'a'ir* are external acts whose value is internal, and honouring them is a function of *taqwa* rather than of performance.

*Fa-man hajja al-bayta aw i'tamara fa-la junaha 'alayhi an yattawwafa bihima* — "so whoever performs the pilgrimage to the House or the lesser pilgrimage, there is no blame on him in going between them."

**And here is the crux, and one of the best-attested and most instructive occasions of revelation in the whole Qur'an.**

Why would a required or recommended act be phrased as the removal of blame? *La junaha* — "there is no sin upon him" — is the language used where a scruple needs removing. Something was making the Muslims hesitate.

The transmitted occasion, recorded by al-Bukhari from 'Asim ibn Sulayman, who said: I asked Anas ibn Malik about Safa and Marwa, and he said — **"We used to consider them among the matters of the pre-Islamic period. When Islam came, we refrained from them, and Allah revealed: 'Indeed, Safa and Marwa are among the rites of Allah.'"** Related narrations are transmitted from 'A'ishah and from 'Urwah, and the tafsir literature records the detail that idols had been placed on the two hills — Isaf on Safa and Na'ilah on Marwah — around which the pre-Islamic Arabs used to circumambulate.

So the Companions' hesitation was not superstition. It was scruple: these two places are associated with idolatry, and we do not want to do what the idolaters did.

**And the answer is the principle, and it is one of the most important in the religion: what has been corrupted is reclaimed, not abolished.**

The act was not abolished and the association was not accepted. The two hills were declared to belong to Allah — *min sha'a'ir Allah* — and the act of walking between them was returned to its true owner with the idols removed from it.

And note that this is the third time the surah has done this. At 2:125 the Ka'bah, surrounded by idols, was not abandoned — it was purified: *an tahhira baytiya*. At 2:141–144 the qiblah was not left where it was out of deference to the previous community, nor was the change a repudiation of them — the community was simply turned toward the House. And here at 158 the two hills with idols on them are not abandoned — they are declared to be among the rites of Allah.

The three together establish a rule that resolves one of the most common and destructive disputes in religious life: **when a legitimate practice has been corrupted by association, the answer is neither to abandon it nor to keep the corruption.** It is to sever the association and keep the practice. The reformer who abolishes everything that has been touched by error and the traditionalist who preserves the error along with the practice are both wrong in the same way — they have both accepted that the corruption determines the practice's fate.

The fiqh question is whether the *sa'y* is obligatory. The schools differed, and the disagreement should be stated rather than flattened. The majority across the schools hold it to be a pillar or a necessary element of the pilgrimage, such that omitting it invalidates the Hajj or requires a compensatory sacrifice. Abu Hanifah held it to be necessary but not a pillar — omitting it requires the sacrifice but the pilgrimage remains valid. And a minority of early authorities, reasoning from the literal wording *la junaha*, held it to be recommended rather than required. The majority answer to that last reading is that the verse was revealed to remove a scruple rather than to set the ruling, and the ruling is established by the Sunnah — the Prophet ﷺ performed the *sa'y* and said, as noted at verse 128, "Take your rites from me."

*Wa man tatawwa'a khayran* — "and whoever does good voluntarily." *Tatawwa'a* is form V of the root from which *nafilah* derives: to do more than is required, willingly, without obligation.

*Fa-inna Allaha shakirun 'alim* — "then indeed Allah is Appreciative, All-Knowing."

***Shakir*** — and this is the striking word. Allah is described as the one who is grateful.

The commentators explained it as: the one who accepts a small deed and rewards it greatly, who acknowledges the work of the worker and does not waste it. The root runs through the Qur'an's descriptions of Him: 35:30, "Indeed, He is Forgiving and Appreciative"; 35:34, "Indeed, our Lord is Forgiving and Appreciative."

**And note the reciprocity across this passage.** At verse 152 the servant is commanded: *wa-shkuru li* — be grateful to Me. And here, six verses later, Allah is described as *shakir* — Appreciative. Gratitude runs in both directions in this relationship. The servant gives thanks, and the Lord gives thanks.

*'Alim* — All-Knowing — closes it, and it is the necessary pair to *shakir*: appreciation without knowledge would miss the act, and knowledge without appreciation would record it without rewarding it. He knows what was done and He responds to it.

For practice, three things.

The first is the reclaiming principle, which is the verse's great gift and which applies far beyond Safa and Marwa. Any practice, institution, habit or symbol that has been corrupted by false association is a candidate for purification rather than abolition. The practical test the verse suggests is: does the thing belong to Allah by origin, or was it always the other thing? If by origin it is His, the corruption is an accretion and accretions can be removed.

The second is the scruple of the Companions, which should be honoured rather than dismissed. They refrained from something good because of its associations, and they were not rebuked for the refraining — they were given the clarity they needed. A sensitive conscience that hesitates is not the problem that an insensitive one is.

And the third is *shakir*. A person who does extra good — the voluntary fast, the extra charity, the unseen service — is dealing with a Lord who is described in this verse as Appreciative. The deed is known and it is answered. And *'alim* means that the parts no one saw were seen.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 159

**"Those who hide the clear proofs and guidance that We have revealed— after We made it clear for humanity in the Book—will be condemned by Allah and ˹all˺ those who condemn."**

In simple terms: those who conceal the clear proofs and the guidance that We sent down, after We made it clear to people in the Book — those are cursed by Allah and by all who curse.

*Inna alladhina yaktumuna ma anzalna mina al-bayyinati wa al-huda min ba'di ma bayyannahu lil-nasi fi al-kitabi* — "indeed those who conceal what We sent down of clear proofs and guidance, after We made it clear to the people in the Book."

*Yaktumuna* is to conceal, to hide, to suppress — the same verb used at 2:42 and 2:146 and 2:140.

*Al-bayyinat* is the clear proofs, the evidences. *Al-huda* is the guidance.

**And the aggravating circumstance is the point: *min ba'di ma bayyannahu* — after We made it clear.** This is not obscurity being suppressed. It is clarity being suppressed. The thing was made plain, publicly, in the Book — *fi al-kitab*, and *lil-nas*, to the people — and then it was hidden.

That is why 2:42 pairs the prohibition with knowledge: "And do not mix the truth with falsehood or conceal the truth while you know [*wa antum ta'lamun*]." And 3:187 states the obligation positively: "And when Allah took a covenant from those who were given the Scripture: 'You must make it clear to the people and not conceal it.'"

*Ula'ika yal'anuhumu Allahu wa yal'anuhumu al-la'inun* — "those — Allah curses them, and the cursers curse them."

*La'n* is from a root meaning to drive away and to distance. From Allah, the curse is expulsion and distance from His mercy.

***Al-la'inun*** — "the cursers" — and who are they? The readings: the angels and the believers; or all of creation; or every creature that curses on that Day. The majority take it as the angels and the believers, with some extending it to all creatures. The point in any case is the comprehensiveness: the concealment is such that everything that is capable of cursing it does.

And the severity is directed at **knowledge**, which is the thing to notice. The gravest curse in this passage attaches not to ignorance but to the suppression of what is known.

The hadith that belongs here is recorded by Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah from Abu Hurayrah: **"Whoever is asked about knowledge and conceals it, Allah will bridle him with a bridle of fire on the Day of Resurrection."** Its grading is discussed among the specialists, and it is cited here for the principle, which is this verse's own.

For practice, two things.

The first is the obvious and necessary one: this verse is a warning to everyone who knows something of the religion and does not convey it. That includes — and in practice mostly means — people who are not scholars. The parent who does not tell his child what he knows. The person who stays silent when a falsehood about the religion is being repeated in front of him. The one who has read and understood and cannot be bothered. The verse's severity is not reserved for a class.

And the second is the guardrail that must be stated whenever this verse is taught. The verse is addressed to those who conceal the revelation **knowingly, after it was made clear** — which means it cannot be applied to a person who genuinely does not know, and it cannot be used as a weapon. No individual is to be declared accursed. The standing rule maintained throughout this commentary holds: Allah judges the concealment, and our business is the conveying.

And there is a third thing which is not a guardrail but a necessity, and it is the next verse: **159 and 160 are one sentence.** The exception is in the same breath as the curse. Anyone who quotes 159 alone has quoted half of a verse.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 160

**"As for those who repent, mend their ways, and let the truth be known, they are the ones to whom I will turn ˹in forgiveness˺, for I am the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful."**

In simple terms: except those who repented, and mended, and made clear — those: I turn to them in acceptance. And I am the Accepter of Repentance, the Most Merciful.

*Illa alladhina tabu wa aslahu wa bayyanu* — "except those who repented, and mended, and made clear."

*Illa* is the exception, and it attaches directly to the curse of the previous verse. The two are one sentence, and the structure is the whole message: severity stated in full, and the door opened in the same breath.

**And there are three conditions, and the third is the one that does the work.**

**1. *Tabu* — they repented.** They turned back from the concealment. This is the inward turn: the recognition, the regret, the stopping.

**2. *Aslahu* — they mended.** Form IV of the root *s-l-h*: to set right, to repair, to make sound. This is the outward repair: what the concealment damaged, they repaired. The repentance is not complete at the level of feeling.

**3. *Bayyanu* — they made clear.** Form II of *b-y-n*: to declare, to make plain, to make public. **This is the condition that matches the sin and reverses it.**

The sin was concealment. Therefore the repentance is disclosure. A person cannot privately repent of a public suppression. What was hidden must now be declared, by the same person, to the same people, in the same register. Private sorrow over a public concealment is not this repentance.

And that is a principle with an enormous reach, and it generalises well beyond the original addressees. The general rule in the fiqh of repentance is that the repair corresponds to the damage: what was wrongfully taken is returned; what was damaged is restored; and where the sin was public, the return is public. So the scholar who taught error must correct it, publicly and in the same forum. The person who spread a falsehood must retract it to the people he told it to. The witness who withheld must give testimony. The institution that concealed must disclose. None of that is optional garnish on a repentance; it *is* the repentance, and its absence is the difference between regret and turning.

*Fa-ula'ika atubu 'alayhim* — "so those — I turn to them in acceptance." The same verb as at 2:37 for Adam and 2:54 for the calf-worshippers.

*Wa ana al-tawwabu al-rahim* — "and I am the Accepter of Repentance, the Most Merciful." The same closing pair as 2:37, 2:54 and 2:128. The surah has now used it four times, and in every instance it is on the lips of, or addressed to, someone returning.

For practice, this verse is one of the most important in the Qur'an for how it structures the relationship between the severity of a text and the availability of return.

**Severity and mercy are stated in one sentence, and the mercy has the last word.** Verse 159 is among the most severe statements in the Qur'an. Verse 160 is among the most merciful. They are grammatically one. The Qur'an does not state the threat and then, elsewhere, soften it; it puts the softening inside the threat, which is a different and far more deliberate thing — it means no one reading the severity can read it as final.

The standing cross-references confirm it. 25:68–70 places repentance after even the gravest sins, naming them. 39:53: "Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of Allah. Indeed, Allah forgives all sins."

And the practical applications follow directly.

To anyone who has concealed: the door is open, and the terms are stated, and the terms include the disclosure. That is the hard part and it is the point. Most people who have suppressed something would rather do almost anything — give charity, pray more, feel terrible for years — than say the thing out loud to the people they hid it from. The verse names the saying as the condition.

And to anyone reading verse 159 with fear: the exception is addressed to you and it is not qualified. *Illa alladhina tabu* — except those who repented. No category is excluded from the exception, and the One describing Himself as *al-Tawwab* is the same One who pronounced the curse in the previous clause.

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

Two movements, and the seam between them is visible.

**The community's formation and its trial (151–157).** The messenger prayed for at 129 is declared sent, reciting, purifying and teaching — closing a loop opened twenty-two verses earlier (151). The exchange that defines the relationship is stated: remember Me and I will remember you, and the response is larger than the approach at every stage (152). Two instruments are handed over — patience and the prayer — with the promise of with-ness attached to the steadfast (153). Those killed in His way are declared alive, with the limitation placed in our perception rather than in their state, and martyrdom expanded far beyond the battlefield (154). The trial is announced as certain and as mitigated: something of fear and hunger, a reduction in wealth, lives and crops — and the bearer of the message is commanded to bring good news (155). The steadfast respond with the eight words that carry the whole theology of loss (156). And upon them descend blessings, mercy, and the name of being guided (157).

**The rites and the responsibility of knowledge (158–160).** Safa and Marwa are declared rites of Allah, with the occasion showing the Companions refraining from a good act because of its idolatrous associations — and the answer establishing that what has been corrupted is reclaimed rather than abolished (158). Then the curse on those who conceal what was made clear (159) — and, in the same sentence, the way back, on three conditions, of which the third is the reverse of the sin (160).

Three threads hold the batch together. First, **the response exceeds the act**: remembrance met with greater remembrance (152), with-ness given to the steadfast (153), life given where death was assumed (154), good news commanded in the middle of the trial (155), three gifts descending (157), and a deed met by One who is described as Appreciative (158). Second, **what has been corrupted is reclaimed, not abolished** — the Ka'bah at 125, the qiblah at 141–144, Safa and Marwa at 158, and the concealer at 160. And third, **severity and mercy are one sentence** — the curse and the exception are grammatically inseparable, which is the pattern the whole surah has maintained.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given. This is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and supplying precise references from memory would mean inventing them. Where the commentators and jurists differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved: what *kama* in verse 151 connects to; whether the ordering of purification and instruction is significant; who *al-la'inun* are; whether the *sa'y* is a pillar, a necessary element, or recommended; and what *salawat* from Allah means.

Transmitted reports have been flagged as reports throughout: the connection of verse 154 to Badr, the idols Isaf and Na'ilah, and the occasion of verse 158. The hadith on concealing knowledge has been cited with its grading noted as discussed.

Four boundaries have been observed. On verse 154: martyrdom has been defined by the hadith's criterion rather than by the manner of death, the categories have been expanded to include the ordinary dead so that no form of death is romanticised at the expense of another, and the standing rule has been restated that no individual may be declared a martyr by name. On verse 158: the reclaiming principle has been stated against both the reformer who abolishes and the traditionalist who preserves the corruption, since the verse declines both. On verse 159: the warning has been generalised to every person with knowledge while the guardrail has been maintained that no individual is declared accursed. And on verse 160: the three conditions have been given in full, with the third — disclosure — named as what makes the repentance real, and the grammatical unity of 159 and 160 insisted on so that the severity can never be read as final.
