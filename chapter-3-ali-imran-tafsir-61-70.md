# Surah Ali 'Imran (The Family of 'Imran, 3) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 61–70

These ten verses contain both the harshest verse in the surah's engagement with Christianity and the most conciliatory one, and they sit four verses apart.

Verse 61 is the mubāhalah: bring your children, your women, yourselves, and let us call down God's curse on whoever is lying. There is no more severe form of argument available in the Qur'an, and it is issued after the case has been made in full.

Verse 64 is the "common word": O People of the Book, come to a term that is fair between us and you — that we worship none but God, associate nothing with Him, and take no one among us as a lord besides Him. It is the single most quoted verse in modern Muslim–Christian dialogue, and the 2007 open letter to the churches of the world took its name from it.

Both are in the same passage, and the tension between them is not an accident. The surah's method throughout has been to honour Jesus exhaustively and then draw one line. Here it does the same to the argument itself: it escalates to the ultimate challenge in order to show that the matter is settled, and then it immediately opens the widest door available — an invitation to genuine common ground, refused only at the cost of being told: bear witness that we have submitted, and leave it there.

The passage then shifts subject. From verse 65 the argument moves from Jesus to Abraham, because both the Jews and the Christians of Madinah claimed Abraham, and so did the Muslims, through Ishmael. The surah settles the question of who owns him, and its answer — that the claim runs through following rather than descent — is one of the most consequential things it says about religious identity.

One thread to watch, because it runs through the whole ten verses: **bearing witness.** The disciples asked to be counted among the witnesses (3:53); God tells the believers to say "bear witness that we have submitted" (3:64); and the passage closes by asking why the People of the Book reject the signs of God *while they themselves bear witness* (3:70). Witnessing is this surah's central category for what a believer is.

## Verse 61

**"Now, whoever disputes with you ˹O Prophet˺ concerning Jesus after full knowledge has come to you, say, “Come! Let us gather our children and your children, our women and your women, ourselves and yourselves— then let us sincerely invoke Allah’s curse upon the liars.”"**

In simple terms: if anyone argues with you about Jesus now, after the truth has been made clear to you, then say: let us each bring our families — our sons, our women, ourselves — and let us pray earnestly and ask God to curse whichever of us is lying.

**Start with the condition, because everything depends on it.** *Fa-man ḥājjaka fīhi min ba'di mā jā'aka min al-'ilm* — "whoever disputes with you concerning him **after what has come to you of knowledge**."

The mubāhalah is not a first move. It is not a debating tactic to reach for when the argument gets difficult. It comes after the knowledge has arrived — after fifty-nine verses of it, in this case. The verse builds the precondition into its own grammar: *min ba'di*, "after."

That is a rule with teeth, and the jurists took it from exactly here. The challenge may only be issued once the evidence has been established and the opponent persists anyway. Anything else is not mubāhalah; it is a curse.

**"Disputes with you."** *Ḥājjaka*, from *ḥ-j-j*, which means to argue by bringing proof — the same root as *ḥujjah* and *ḥajj*. This is not a brawl. The word assumes both sides are producing arguments.

**"Say: Come!"** *Fa-qul ta'ālaw.* The imperative *ta'ālaw* comes from *'alā*, to rise, to come up, and it is the ordinary Arabic invitation: come here, come forward. Not a challenge shouted from a distance but an invitation to come close.

**"Let us gather our children and your children, our women and your women, ourselves and yourselves."** *Nad'u abnā'anā wa-abnā'akum wa-nisā'anā wa-nisā'akum wa-anfusanā wa-anfusakum.*

Note the three pairs and their order: sons, then women, then selves. The circle widens outward from the centre until nothing is held back. The whole community, and the most precious people in it, are placed on the table.

**Why?** This is the question worth stopping on, because the answer is what makes the verse work. The cursing of the liar would fall on the liar and those with him. So by bringing his family, the Prophet ﷺ is not adding stakes for drama. He is performing the certainty. He is saying, in the only way that cannot be faked: **I believe this so completely that I will put my grandchildren in the path of the curse.**

That is why the Najrān delegation, on the reports, folded. Not because they were cowards, but because no one stakes his family on a bluff.

**"Then let us sincerely invoke."** *Thumma nabtahil.* The root *b-h-l* carries the sense of letting oneself go in supplication — to pray with everything you have, to abase yourself and implore. It is a rare word in the Qur'an, used here and in only a handful of other places, and it describes the quality of the prayer rather than its form. Not a recitation. An abandonment of oneself in asking.

**"And invoke Allah's curse upon the liars."** *Fa-naj'al la'nata Allāhi 'alā al-kādhibīn.* *La'nah* is expulsion — to be cut off from mercy. Note that the imprecation is not "upon you." It is "upon **the liars**," whoever they turn out to be. Both sides say the same words. Each is asking God to curse himself if he is lying.

That is the structural fairness of it, and it is why this is not an ordeal or a trial by combat. It is a joint appeal to a judge both parties acknowledge. It could only ever be offered to someone who believes in God — which is precisely why it was offered to the Christians of Najrān and never to the idolaters of Makkah.

**The occasion.** The reports connect this verse to the delegation from Najrān, in Yemen: a party said to number around sixty riders including fourteen of their leading men, who came to Madinah, debated the Prophet ﷺ about Jesus, and would not accept what he said. The challenge was issued, and they declined.

**A caution I want to be explicit about, because it applies to everything that follows in this verse.** These reports come through sīrah transmission — principally Ibn Isḥāq — and related channels, not through rigorously authenticated hadith chains. The details vary considerably between sources: some say they refused immediately, some that they asked for three days and consulted among themselves before declining; the terms of the settlement they agreed instead are given differently in different reports (a figure of two thousand garments, paid in two instalments, appears in some versions). The broad outline — a delegation, a debate, a challenge, a refusal, a treaty — is widely accepted. The details should be read as reported, not established, and I am not going to build anything on them.

**Who did the Prophet bring?** Here the reports are much stronger, and this is a point on which Sunni and Shi'i sources agree.

The narration of Sa'd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ, recorded in **Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim**, states that when this verse was revealed, the Messenger of God ﷺ called 'Alī, Fāṭimah, Ḥasan and Ḥusayn, and said: "O God, these are my family." Narrations to the same effect are recorded in Jāmi' al-Tirmidhī and elsewhere, and the identification is given by commentators across the spectrum — al-Zamakhsharī, Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, al-Bayḍāwī, Ibn Kathīr and others among Sunni authorities, and al-Ṭabarsī, al-Ṭūsī and Ṭabāṭabā'ī among Shi'i ones.

The mapping is: *abnā'anā*, "our sons" — al-Ḥasan and al-Ḥusayn. *Nisā'anā*, "our women" — Fāṭimah. *Anfusanā*, "ourselves" — the Prophet ﷺ and 'Alī. Note that *anfusanā* is **plural**, so it naturally covers the Prophet and at least one other, and the reports name 'Alī.

**Now the part that must be handled carefully.** This verse is a central proof-text in the Sunni–Shi'i disagreement over succession and spiritual authority, and it would be dishonest to write a tafsir of it without saying so.

The Shi'i reading: by naming 'Alī as his *nafs* — his self — in a verse where the stakes are God's curse upon the liar, the Prophet ﷺ placed 'Alī in a position of parity with himself in representing the truth of Islam. Ṭabāṭabā'ī argues that the participation of these four, *to the exclusion of all other Muslims*, requires their partnership with the Prophet in his claims, since the curse targets only liars — and that this is a basis for the doctrine of the Imamate.

The Sunni reading: the event is accepted and 'Alī's immense virtue in it is affirmed without reservation. But it is taken as establishing his rank and nearness to the Prophet, not his designation as successor. Ibn Taymiyyah, for example, accepts the hadith as authentic and explains that the Prophet took them because they were his closest family.

**Where the ground is shared, and where it is not.** Both sides agree on the event, the four participants, and 'Alī's extraordinary merit. Sunnis hold 'Alī as the fourth of the Rightly-Guided Caliphs, the Prophet's cousin and son-in-law, and among the most virtuous of all the Companions — that is not in dispute. What is in dispute is whether this verse, read as the Shi'i read it, establishes the Imamate. It is a fourteen-hundred-year disagreement about a verse whose immediate context is a debate with Yemeni Christians about the nature of Jesus, and I am not going to adjudicate it here. What I will say is that the verse's function **in this surah** is to demonstrate the Prophet's certainty, and that its use in the succession dispute is a later and separate employment of it — legitimate to argue, and contested.

**Did the mubāhalah take place?** No. On all reports, the delegation withdrew.

Maududi's reading of why is worth having, and it is a strong one: the purpose of proposing the procedure was to expose that their position was not held with conviction. They had no answer to the arguments, and they knew it, and when faced with a test that required them to stake their families on their beliefs, they declined — and that refusal was itself the demonstration. I would hold that as his reading rather than as the verse's plain statement, but it fits the shape of the thing.

**The fiqh of mubāhalah.** This matters practically, because the practice is claimed today more often than it is understood.

The established position of the jurists is that mubāhalah is legislated and permissible, and that it is **not** restricted to the Prophet ﷺ. It is reported that Ibn 'Abbās invited someone to mubāhalah over a question of inheritance, with no Companion objecting — and Ibn al-Qayyim records this in *Zād al-Ma'ād* as evidence that the ruling is general. The instance cited from Bukhārī and Muslim on the authority of Abū Hurayrah and others, relating to a man who refused to accept a ruling, contains the Companions' words: "Do not do it! By God, if he is a prophet and we invoke God's curse with him, neither we nor our descendants will survive."

But the scholars set conditions, and they are severe. Following the formulation of al-Dawwānī as reported by Ibn al-Qayyim, and restated by later jurists, mubāhalah is permissible only:

1. in a **serious religious matter**, not a worldly dispute and not a personal quarrel;
2. **after the evidence has been established** and the proof has reached the opponent;
3. where the opponent **persists in obstinacy** that cannot be removed by ordinary argument and sincere advice;
4. where there is an **expected benefit** — such as the removal of an injustice;
5. and never as a rhetorical flourish or a way of backing up an opinion.

Ibn Ḥajar is reported to have remarked that in his experience a person who calls to mubāhalah while lying does not survive the year, and to have recounted an instance of it. I report that as his observation, and I would caution against treating it as a mechanism. It is not a test you can run, and it is not a promise in the text.

**And here is the contemporary application that actually matters.** Almost every instance of "mubāhalah" you will encounter today — sectarians cursing one another in comment threads, preachers inviting rival preachers to it over subsidiary issues, people invoking God's curse on opponents in disputes that were never serious religious matters in the first place — fails conditions one through five. The verse permits a last resort after the truth has been fully established and refused. It does not permit a way of winning an argument.

**What the verse means for someone reading it alone.** Two things.

The first is about the order of operations. The challenge comes after the knowledge. In every serious disagreement you will ever have, there is a stage at which more argument is useless and the only honest move is to stop. The surah's own sequence is: argue fully (59), declare the truth (60), stake everything (61), and then walk back to an open door (64). Note that even the mubāhalah is not the end of the conversation.

The second is about certainty. The Prophet ﷺ could stake his grandchildren on this because he knew where the knowledge came from. Most of us, in most disagreements, do not have that. The verse is not a licence to be certain; it is a description of what certainty looks like when it is warranted — and how rare that is.

## Verse 62

**"Certainly, this is the true narrative, and there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Allah. And indeed, Allah ˹alone˺ is the Almighty, All-Wise."**

In simple terms: this is the true story. There is no god but God. And God — He alone — is the Almighty, the All-Wise.

**"This is the true narrative."** *Inna hādhā la-huwa al-qaṣaṣu al-ḥaqq.*

The word *qaṣaṣ* comes from *qaṣṣa*, to follow a track, to trace footsteps. A *qiṣṣah* — a story — is literally the following of tracks. So when the Qur'an calls this *al-qaṣaṣu al-ḥaqq*, it is saying something more pointed than "this is a true story." It is saying: **this account follows the real tracks.** It is not a story shaped to a purpose. It traces what actually happened.

And note what is being sealed. This verse closes the whole Jesus narrative that began back at verse 42 with the angels addressing Mary — and, on the wider reading, that began at verse 33 with the election of the family of 'Imrān. Everything between here and there is being declared *qaṣaṣ ḥaqq*.

That includes the parts a reader might have been inclined to treat as edifying legend: the lot-casting for Mary's care, Zakariyyā's silence, the cradle speech, the miracles, the plot, the raising. All of it, together, is declared the true account.

**Two notes on the supplied translation, both of them helpful additions rather than distortions.** First, "no god **worthy of worship** except Allah" — the Arabic is *mā min ilāhin illā Allāh*, "there is no god but God." The bracketed gloss is doing real work, because *ilāh* means "that which is worshipped," and the Islamic declaration is not a claim about how many deities *exist* but about who may be worshipped. The addition is correct and important.

Second, "**and indeed, Allah alone** is" — the Arabic is *wa-inna Allāha la-huwa al-'azīzu al-ḥakīm*. The *la-* is an emphatic particle and *huwa* is an emphatic separating pronoun; together they produce an exclusivity that English has to add a word for. "God **alone** is" is a fair rendering of a construction that in Arabic is doing the work of an argument.

**"The Almighty."** *Al-'Azīz.* The One who is not overcome, whose will is not resisted, who is unassailable.

Why this name here, at the end of the Jesus narrative? Because it answers every thread that has been left hanging. The plot at verse 54 — the *'Azīz* is not out-planned. The deliverance at verse 55 — the *'Azīz* is able to raise whom He wills. The mubāhalah at verse 61 — the *'Azīz* is the one who would answer it. Nothing in this passage has happened outside His power.

**"The All-Wise."** *Al-Ḥakīm.* The One whose every act and every word lands in its right place, and nothing is arbitrary.

And this name is the one that explains the shape of the whole passage. The surah spent twenty-five verses honouring Jesus before drawing a single distinction. It quoted him preaching its own theology. It refused to let the argument become contemptuous. That restraint is *ḥikmah* — and the verse tells you that the restraint was not diplomacy but wisdom.

**The two together.** Might without wisdom would be terrifying; wisdom without might would be ineffective. The verse closes the argument by naming the One in whom both meet, and that is why the surah can be so severe in verse 61 and so open in verse 64 without contradicting itself.

## Verse 63

**"If they turn away, then surely Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of the corruptors."**

In simple terms: and if they turn away, God knows well who the corrupters are.

**This is the answer to a refused mubāhalah.** Read the sequence: verse 61 issues the ultimate challenge. Verse 62 seals the account. Verse 63 says: and if they turn away — and the Arabic *tawallaw* means to turn one's back, to turn aside — then God has knowledge of the corrupters.

Notice what the verse does **not** say. It does not say "then fight them." It does not say "then they are doomed and you are avenged." It says: **God knows.**

**"The corruptors."** *Al-mufsidīn*, from *f-s-d* — those who spread corruption, who spoil, who put things out of their right order.

There is a choice of word here worth noticing. Verse 61 called the losing side "the liars" (*al-kādhibīn*). This verse calls them "the corrupters." The shift is deliberate. Refusing the truth after it has been made clear is not only a false position; it is an *active* one. It corrupts. It spoils the ground for other people. A person who rejects what he knows to be true and then teaches others to do the same is not merely mistaken — he is spreading *fasād*.

**A necessary caution, and it is the same caution this surah has required of us before.** The verse says God has knowledge of the corrupters. It does not hand that knowledge to us, and it does not authorise us to go about identifying them. We have already met the rule at verse 55: judgment between the parties belongs to God. The verse is a statement about His knowledge and a comfort to the Prophet ﷺ, not a licence for us.

**The deeper principle: conveyance, not conversion.** This is the verse's most useful content for a reader today, and it is one of the most repeated principles in the Qur'an.

The pattern appears over and over: "if they turn away, your duty is only clear conveyance" (16:82, 24:54, 29:18, 64:12). At 3:20 the surah put it to the Prophet ﷺ directly: "if they submit, they are guided; but if they turn away, then upon you is only the conveyance." Here the same idea is expressed by handing the matter to God's knowledge.

What that removes is a burden most religious people carry without noticing. The measuring of oneself by other people's responses. The sense that a failed conversation is a personal failure. The slow drift from conveying to pressuring, because pressure feels more effective.

The verse says: you convey. God knows who is corrupting. Those are different jobs and you only have one of them.

**Bring it into ordinary life.** Anyone who has argued with a relative about religion, or tried to explain something important to a friend who did not want to hear it, knows the specific exhaustion of this. You make the case. It is a good case. It does not land.

The verse gives you permission to stop — not in resentment, not with a final flourish, but by handing the person to God. There is a version of "leaving it with God" that is actually sulking, and the Qur'an does not license that either; notice that verse 64 immediately reopens the door with an invitation. The sequence is: state the truth, stake it if you must, and when it is refused, keep the door open and hand the outcome over. All three, in that order.

**And one more thing.** The verse is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ in the second person — "if **they** turn away." It is a verse about other people's refusal. It says nothing about what happens if *you* are the one who turns away. But the surah has already covered that, at verse 20, and it will return to it. The reader who uses this verse only as comfort about other people has read half of it.

## Verse 64

**"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O People of the Book! Let us come to common terms: that we will worship none but Allah, associate none with Him, nor take one another as lords instead of Allah.” But if they turn away, then say, “Bear witness that we have submitted ˹to Allah alone˺.”"**

In simple terms: say — O People of the Book, let us come to a word that is fair and level between us and you: that we worship none but God, that we associate nothing with Him, and that we do not take some of us as lords over others besides God. And if they turn away, say: then bear witness that we have submitted to God.

**This is the most important verse in the Qur'an for interfaith relations, and it deserves the closest reading I can give it.**

**"A word that is level between us and you."** *Kalimatin sawā'in baynanā wa-baynakum.*

Start with *sawā'*, because it carries the whole method. *Sawā'* means level, even, equal — equidistant from two points. It is not "a compromise," and it is not "the lowest common denominator." It is **a term that leans neither way.**

That is a very specific and very demanding thing to offer. The verse is not saying "let us find something we can both agree on." It is saying: let us strip away what each side has added and see whether there is a proposition that neither of us has tilted in his own favour. And then it states what that proposition is.

**Three clauses, and note their shape:**

1. *Allā na'buda illā Allāha* — that we worship none but God.
2. *Wa-lā nushrika bihi shay'an* — and that we associate nothing with Him.
3. *Wa-lā yattakhidha ba'ḍunā ba'dan arbāban min dūni Allāh* — and that we do not take one another as lords besides God.

The first two are about God. The third is about **us** — about how the first two play out in human relationships. Worship, then association, then authority. And the third is the one that gets lived, tested, and broken.

Also note: the three clauses are an explanatory apposition. They *are* the "word." The common ground is not a vague shared sentiment; it is a proposition with content, and it is stated.

**Now the crux — the third clause. "Nor take one another as lords besides God."** *Wa-lā yattakhidha ba'ḍunā ba'dan arbāban min dūni Allāh.*

**Important: this is not a polemic aimed only at Christians.** The phrasing is *ba'ḍunā ba'dan* — "some of us, others of us." It is reciprocal, and it is addressed to a community that includes the speaker. We take **one another** as lords. The clause binds Muslims at least as much as anyone.

What does it mean to take a human being as a *rabb* — a lord? Two readings, and both are held by the commentators, and both are correct.

**Reading one: deifying a human being.** The immediate addressees are the People of the Book, and the passage has just spent thirty verses arguing that a man — Jesus — was ascribed lordship that belongs to God alone. On this reading the clause says plainly: you have taken one of us as a lord besides God. It has the whole context behind it.

**Reading two: obeying religious authorities into disobedience of God.** This is established by a hadith that unlocks the entire concept, and it is worth quoting in full because it is one of the most important narrations in the whole religion.

'Adī ibn Ḥātim — a chief of the tribe of Ṭayyi', and a Christian before he accepted Islam — came to the Prophet ﷺ wearing a gold cross. The Prophet ﷺ was reciting from Sūrat Barā'ah: "They took their rabbis and their monks as lords besides God" (9:31).

'Adī said: "But they did not worship them."

And the Prophet ﷺ said: "Did they not forbid for you what God made lawful, and you treated it as forbidden; and permit for you what God made unlawful, and you treated it as lawful?"

'Adī said: Yes.

The Prophet ﷺ said: "**That is how you worshipped them.**"

This narration is recorded in Jāmi' al-Tirmidhī, in the book of tafsīr, and it is the definitive explanation of what "worship" means in Islam. **Worship is not primarily prostration. It is ultimate obedience in legislation.** Whoever is followed into permitting what God forbade or forbidding what God permitted has been taken as a lord — whether or not anyone bowed to him.

**Both readings are intended.** The immediate context points to the first; the Prophet's own explanation of the parallel verse points to the second; and the wording is general enough to hold both. The commentators hold them together, and so should we.

**Now apply it inward, because that is where the clause bites.** The verse says *ba'ḍunā ba'dan*. Some of us, over others of us.

It means the sheikh whose word is taken over the text even when the text is clear. It means the movement whose positions you accept without asking for evidence. It means the scholar you follow into what God has made unlawful because he is on your side. It means, in many places today, the state, the party, or the online community that functions as the final authority on what is permitted and forbidden.

And it means something quieter and more common: the inherited custom — the thing your family, your culture, your class do — treated as though it had the force of revelation.

**But I want to draw a line here, because this verse is also abused.** Following a qualified scholar is not taking him as a lord. The Prophet's definition to 'Adī was specific: it is following someone **into what God has forbidden or permitted against His ruling**. That is different from *taqlīd* — following a scholar in a matter where you do not have the tools to weigh the evidence yourself, and where he is transmitting a ruling grounded in the sources. The verse is not an argument against scholarship or against learning from scholars. It is an argument against obeying any human being against the clear instruction of God. Confusing the two produces people who are sure of everything and grounded in nothing.

**"But if they turn away, then say: Bear witness that we have submitted."** *Fa-in tawallaw fa-qūlū ishhadū bi-annā muslimūn.*

Note the detail. The verse opens with *qul*, "say," in the singular — addressed to the Prophet ﷺ. It closes with *qūlū*, "say," in the **plural** — addressed to the community. The declaration is not his alone. Everyone who has submitted makes it together.

And note what they are told to say. Not "bear witness that you are wrong." Not "bear witness that we have won." But: **bear witness that we have submitted.** The response to refusal is a calm statement of where we stand, offered as testimony, and then left there.

**This is the third response to refusal in four verses, and the progression is worth studying:**

- Verse 61: invoke God's curse on the liars.
- Verse 63: God has knowledge of the corrupters.
- Verse 64: bear witness that we have submitted.

Severe, then resigned, then open. The surah does not settle on a single register, and the refusal does not produce a single response. What it produces is: the truth stated, the outcome handed to God, and the door left open. That is the whole Qur'anic posture toward religious disagreement in three moves.

**The letter to Heraclius.** The version of the Prophet's ﷺ letter to the Byzantine emperor preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī — in the long narration of Ibn 'Abbās concerning Abū Sufyān — contains this verse. The invitation to the ruler of the Roman world was made with *kalimatin sawā'in*. That is worth knowing: the verse was not kept for private argument. It was sent to Caesar.

**Why this verse matters now.** It gives Muslims a model for interfaith engagement that is neither capitulation nor combat, and it is worth stating precisely so it is not softened:

1. **Find the real agreement.** Not a manufactured one — the genuine article. Worship God alone. Associate nothing with Him. Take no human as lord.
2. **State it plainly.** The common ground is *tawḥīd*, and it is not a soft proposition. It is the hardest thing in the verse.
3. **If it is refused, declare your own position and stop.** Testify, and leave the outcome with God.

What it rules out is the two failures that dominate religious conversation today: the failure that dissolves every difference into a bland sameness, and the failure that turns every difference into a war. The verse refuses both. It insists on a real agreement, and it declines to coerce anyone into it.

## Verse 65

**"O People of the Book! Why do you argue about Abraham, while the Torah and the Gospel were not revealed until long after him? Do you not understand?"**

In simple terms: O People of the Book, why do you argue about Abraham? The Torah and the Gospel were only sent down long after his time. Do you not use your reason?

**The subject changes.** For sixty-four verses the argument has been about Jesus. From here to verse 68 it is about Abraham. The reason is not hard to find: in Madinah, all three communities claimed him. The Jews claimed him as the father of Isaac and the bearer of the covenant. The Christians claimed him as the man whose faith preceded the law and was fulfilled in Christ — a claim made explicitly in the New Testament. And the Muslims claimed him as the father of Ishmael and the builder of the Ka'bah.

The surah settles the question, and it does it with an argument that is disarmingly simple.

**"The Torah and the Gospel were not revealed until long after him."** *Wa-mā unzilat al-tawrātu wa-al-injīlu illā min ba'dihi.*

The argument is chronological. Judaism as a religion constituted by revealed law begins with Moses. Christianity begins with Jesus. Abraham precedes both by many centuries. So the categories "Jew" and "Christian" — as names for the religions defined by those two revelations — cannot be applied to him. To argue about which of the two he belonged to is to argue about something that did not exist.

This is a historical claim, and it is not a controversial one. Both Judaism and Christianity locate Abraham before Moses and before Jesus. The Qur'an is simply pointing out what follows from that.

**"Why do you argue?"** *Lima tuḥājjūna* — the same verb *ḥājja* we saw at verse 61 and verse 66. Arguing by producing proof. The verse does not forbid argument; it asks why the argument is being had about this.

**"Do you not understand?"** *A-fa-lā ta'qilūn.* The appeal is to *'aql* — to reasoning, to the faculty that connects cause and effect. The surah has been appealing to that faculty since its opening verses: *ulū al-albāb*, those with understanding hearts (3:7); *ulī al-abṣār*, those with insight (3:13). The Qur'an's characteristic move is not to override the intellect but to ask why it is not being used.

**And note what the verse does not deny.** It does not deny that Abraham is the ancestor of the Israelites through Isaac, or of the Arabs through Ishmael. Descent is not in question. What is in question is whether he belonged to either of the religions that were constituted later. The Qur'an says no — and then, at verse 67, says what he actually was.

**A practical observation about the argument form.** This is a *tu quoque* avoided. The Qur'an does not say "you are wrong about Abraham because you are wrong about everything." It says: check your dates. The argument is made on ground both sides accept, and it is settled by a fact rather than by an authority.

There is a lesson there for anyone who argues about anything. The most effective arguments are usually not the cleverest; they are the ones that rest on something the other person already grants. Here it is a piece of chronology nobody disputes, and it decides the question.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 66

**"Here you are! You disputed about what you have ˹little˺ knowledge of, but why do you now argue about what you have no knowledge of? Allah knows and you do not know."**

In simple terms: look at yourselves — you argued about things you had some knowledge of, so why do you argue now about things you have no knowledge of? God knows, and you do not.

**"Here you are!"** *Hā-antum hā'ulā'i.* This is a striking Arabic construction and worth a moment. It is an attention-calling particle, then a pronoun ("you"), then a demonstrative ("these ones"). The effect is something close to "There you are, then!" or "Just look at yourselves." It expresses astonishment at a piece of conduct, and it is a shape of words the Qur'an uses when it wants the reader to step outside himself and see what he is doing.

**Two grades of knowledge.** This is the heart of the verse.

The first: *fī-mā lakum bihi 'ilmun* — about that of which you have knowledge. They had argued about their own scriptures and their own law. That was a domain they at least had access to.

**A note on the supplied translation:** the word "little" is in half-brackets, which means it has been added. The Arabic says simply "that in which you have knowledge." The gloss reflects a reading — held by a number of commentators — that even their knowledge of their own scripture was partial, because parts of it had been obscured. It is a defensible reading and it is honestly flagged, but the literal text states knowledge, not partial knowledge, and the contrast the verse is drawing works either way.

The second: *fī-mā laysa lakum bihi 'ilm* — about that of which you have **no** knowledge. Abraham's religion. What God actually wills. The unseen. The end of things.

**And the rule the verse establishes is one of the great ethical rules of the Qur'an: do not argue about what you have no knowledge of.** It is not a minor etiquette point. It is repeated, and it is backed by a sanction:

- 17:36: "Do not pursue that of which you have no knowledge. Indeed, the hearing and the sight and the heart — all of those will be questioned." Note what this does: it makes unverified speech a matter of **accountability for the faculties**, not merely of manners. You will be asked what you did with your ears and your eyes.
- 22:3 and 31:6 describe the person "who disputes concerning God without knowledge" and follows every rebellious devil.
- 53:28: "They have no knowledge of it. They follow nothing but assumption — and assumption avails nothing against the truth."

**"Allah knows and you do not know."** *Wa-Allāhu ya'lamu wa-antum lā ta'lamūn.*

This closing clause is not a shrug. It is the establishment of a category. There is a class of matters that belong to God: what Abraham's religion actually was in its detail, what is in the hearts of people, what the final judgment is. To argue confidently inside that category is not boldness; it is a category error.

**And note the mercy in it.** The verse does not condemn not knowing. It condemns arguing **as though you knew**. Ignorance acknowledged is not the sin here. Ignorance performing as knowledge is.

**Now bring this into our own lives, because this verse is more useful to a modern reader than almost anything else in this batch.** We live in an information environment that rewards exactly the behaviour this verse prohibits.

Consider what it looks like in practice. You read a headline. You now have an opinion about a war, an economy, a scientific finding, a court case. You have no knowledge of it — not in the sense this verse means — but you will argue about it at length, and people will take you seriously because you argued confidently.

Or: a ruling circulates. Someone says "Islam says X." You repeat it. You have never seen the evidence, do not know whether it is a majority position or a minority one, and do not know what the counter-argument is. The verse asks: on what basis are you speaking?

Or, most sharply: you state what someone else **meant**. You know their words and you know your interpretation, and you treat them as the same thing. That is the precise error this verse is aimed at — and it is the single most common failure in both religious and political argument.

The practical discipline the verse suggests is small and concrete: **grade your own certainty.** Keep three categories — what you know, what you partly know, what you do not know — and speak in the register appropriate to each. This is not intellectual squeamishness. It is the difference between testimony and guesswork, and the verse says only one of them is permitted.

**And there is a second, quieter application.** The verse also releases you. There is a great deal you are not required to have an opinion about. The relief of not needing to adjudicate everything is real, and the Qur'an offers it here explicitly: God knows, and you do not. That is not a failure. It is the correct state of a created mind.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 67

**"Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian; he submitted in all uprightness and was not a polytheist."**

In simple terms: Abraham was not a Jew and not a Christian. He was a *ḥanīf*, one who submitted to God — and he was not one of those who associate partners with God.

**A note on the supplied translation first, because it matters here.** "He submitted in all uprightness" renders *kāna ḥanīfan musliman* — "he was a ḥanīf, a muslim." In the Arabic those are two **technical terms**, and they are the entire burden of the verse. The English has turned them into a verb phrase, which reads well but loses the two words the verse is built on. So let me take them one at a time.

***Ḥanīf.*** This is a genuinely difficult word, and honest scholarship has to say that its precise origin is not settled.

What can be said with confidence:

The Arabic root *ḥ-n-f* means to incline, to turn aside — and *aḥnaf*, in the lexicons, describes a person whose foot turns inward.

In the Qur'an the word always describes Abraham and those on his religion, and always in contrast to the polytheists: 2:135, 3:67, 3:95, 4:125, 6:79, 6:161, 16:120, 16:123. It also appears in a more general sense at 10:105, 22:31, 30:30 and 98:5. It occurs twelve times.

In pre-Islamic Arabia the word appears to have denoted someone who was circumcised and performed the pilgrimage — the surviving traces of Abraham's religion — and even idol-worshippers claimed it. Ibn Isḥāq's *Sīrah* describes men who rejected the idols and went seeking "the *ḥanīfiyyah*, the religion of Abraham," the most famous of them Zayd ibn 'Amr ibn Nufayl, who asked Jewish and Christian scholars in turn and was told by each that the true religion was Abraham's, and that he was neither a Jew nor a Christian.

**And the word is probably a loan.** Syriac *ḥanpā* means heathen or pagan, and Arabic Christian authors use *ḥanīf* in that same sense. Al-Mas'ūdī noted the word as Syriac in origin. Modern scholarship has pressed this further: François de Blois argued that the Qur'anic sense is closer to "a believing gentile" — a monotheist who is not under Mosaic law. That is a serious scholarly hypothesis, and I present it as one rather than as established fact; the philology is contested.

**What is not contested is the sense the word carries in context**, whichever etymology you prefer: one who turns away from everything false and inclines wholly to the One God. Pristine, unaffiliated monotheism. Ibn Kathīr's gloss on this verse — that Abraham was far removed from idolatry and firmly established upon true faith — captures it.

***Muslim.*** Again, in the **descriptive** sense: one who has submitted. This is the same word the disciples used of themselves at 3:52 — "bear witness that we have submitted" — and it is not the name of a community founded in the seventh century.

And here is an important precision, because this is exactly the place where readers go wrong in both directions. Saying "Abraham was a Muslim" does **not** mean he pronounced the shahādah, or followed the Sharī'ah of Muhammad, or prayed five times a day in the form we do. The scholars distinguish *islām* in its general sense — the stance of submission to God that belongs to every prophet and every true follower — from the specific revealed law that God sent to a particular messenger. Abraham was *muslim* in the first sense. The verse is making a claim about his posture before God, not about his fiqh.

**A Christian or Jewish reader will reasonably object here** that this is a retrojection — that calling Abraham a "muslim" is naming him with a later term. That is a fair challenge, and it should be met rather than waved away. The Qur'an's answer, in effect, is the one it gave at 3:52 and 3:19: *islām* is not, in the first instance, the name of a religion. It is the name of an act — submission — and the claim being made is that this act is what God has asked of everyone, and that the various named religions are later developments around it. It is a claim, and it is disputed, and it is more coherent than it first sounds.

**"And he was not a polytheist."** *Wa-mā kāna min al-mushrikīn.*

Why is this third clause here? It looks like padding, and it is not.

Because there was a **third** claimant to Abraham in Madinah. The Quraysh and the Arab tribes also claimed him — as the one who raised the foundations of the Ka'bah, as the father of Ishmael and the ancestor of the Arabs, as the possessor of a religion whose remaining rites they still performed. Verse 65 refuses the Jewish and Christian claims. This clause refuses the polytheist one.

All three are refused in a single verse. Abraham belongs to none of the parties who are arguing over him, and he belongs to whoever follows him.

**The theological claim underneath it.** The religion of God is one, and it is older than every community that claims to possess it. Islam's self-understanding, on this verse, is restoration rather than innovation — a return to what Abraham was before anyone added anything. See 3:95 ("follow the religion of Abraham, a ḥanīf, and he was not of the polytheists") and 22:78 ("He named you 'those who submit' — before, and in this").

**One common misunderstanding to clear.** The verse does not deny Abraham's fatherhood. It denies his membership in the later named religions. Those are different claims, and conflating them has produced a great deal of unnecessary heat.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 68

**"Indeed, those who have the best claim to Abraham are his followers, this Prophet, and the believers. And Allah is the Guardian of those who believe."**

In simple terms: the people with the strongest claim to Abraham are those who followed him, and this prophet, and those who believe. And God is the protector of the believers.

**"The best claim."** *Awlā*, from the root *w-l-y*, which carries proximity, nearness, being next to something. The word means closest, most entitled, having the prior claim.

**Three groups are named:**

1. *Alladhīna ittaba'ūhu* — those who followed him. Note the past tense: those who followed Abraham in his own time.
2. *Wa-hādhā al-nabiyyu* — and this prophet. The demonstrative *hādhā*, "this," points at Muhammad ﷺ, present and in front of them.
3. *Wa-alladhīna āmanū* — and those who believe. Present tense, ongoing: the believers of every time after.

Read together, the three form a chain across history: Abraham's own followers, then the final messenger, then everyone who believes after him.

**A note on the grammar.** How exactly *hādhā al-nabiyyu* attaches to what precedes it is discussed by the grammarians. It stands in the nominative case, which supports reading it as conjoined to *alladhīna ittaba'ūhu* — "those who followed him, **and** this prophet" — making the Prophet a named member of the group with the closest claim. It can also be read as the beginning of a fresh clause: "and this prophet [likewise]."

The parsing is discussed; **the meaning is not in dispute.** On either reading, the Prophet ﷺ is named among those with the best claim to Abraham, and the verse is making a claim about him that the Madinan audience would have understood perfectly well.

**And now the point of the verse, which is the one that matters.** *The claim to Abraham runs through following, not descent.*

This is a radical statement in the mouth of a seventh-century Arab. In a tribal society, nearness is blood. Your claim on a great ancestor is genealogical, and it cannot be acquired. The verse says: none of that is the criterion. The criterion is *ittibā'* — following.

The Qur'an makes the same point elsewhere, and it is consistent. At 2:124, when Abraham asks whether the covenant of leadership will extend to his descendants, the answer is: "My covenant does not include the wrongdoers." At 60:4 Abraham and those with him are an example "for those who hope for God and the Last Day" — anyone, by implication.

**A caution, because this verse gets read as a licence.** Saying that the claim runs through following and not blood does **not** deny the descent of the Israelites from Abraham, which the Qur'an affirms repeatedly (see 2:47, 2:122, 40:53, 45:16). What it denies is that descent **constitutes** the claim. Being descended from someone is not the same as belonging to him.

**"And Allah is the Guardian of those who believe."** *Wa-Allāhu waliyyu al-mu'minīn.*

Notice the root. *Walī* — guardian, protector, close friend — is from the same *w-l-y* as *awlā*, the word the verse opened with. The wordplay is deliberate and it is the verse's closing argument: **nearness to Abraham is not blood; nearness is God's to give.** Those who are *awlā* to Abraham are those to whom God is *walī*.

The verse opens and closes on the same root, and the effect is to relocate the whole question. You do not inherit a claim. You are given one.

**What this means in practice.** Every community that has a founder eventually fights over who owns him. Christians, Muslims, Jews over Abraham; Muslims themselves over Muhammad ﷺ; Buddhists, Marxists, Freudians — it is a universal pattern. The verse's answer is transferable and it is unusually clean: **the claim belongs to whoever follows, and following is visible.** Not to whoever has the loudest genealogy, the oldest institution, or the largest number.

There is a searching version of this for the individual believer too. Claiming a tradition is easy. Following it is the thing that constitutes the claim, and it is observable. The verse quietly removes the possibility of coasting on an inherited identity.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 69

**"Some of the People of the Book wish to mislead you ˹believers˺. They mislead none but themselves, yet they fail to perceive it."**

In simple terms: a party among the People of the Book would love to lead you astray. But they lead no one astray except themselves — and they do not even realise it.

**"A faction."** *Ṭā'ifatun min ahli al-kitāb.* Not "the People of the Book." **Some** of them.

This qualifier is not incidental and it is not politeness. The surah has been scrupulous about it throughout, and it is one of its most important moral features. At 3:75 it is "among the People of the Book is one who, if you entrust him with a great amount, returns it to you, and among them is one who, if you entrust him with a single coin, will not return it." At 3:78: "among them is a group who distort the scripture with their tongues." At 3:113: "they are not all alike — among the People of the Book is an upright community."

And at 3:199: "among the People of the Book is he who believes in God."

Every charge in this surah is levelled at a **party**, never at a whole people. That precision is a religious requirement, not a stylistic nicety, and it is routinely destroyed by readers on all sides who want the Qur'an to be saying something more sweeping than it says.

**"Wish."** *Waddat* — she longed, she wished ardently. This is desire, not policy. They want this badly.

**"That they could mislead you."** *Law yuḍillūnakum.* The object of the wish is stated with *law*, the particle of hypothetical desire: would that they might make you stray.

**And then the reversal, which is the entire point of the verse: "They mislead none but themselves."** *Wa-mā yuḍillūna illā anfusahum.*

The mechanism here is worth spelling out, because it is a genuine claim about how misguidance works and not merely a taunt.

Misguidance, in the Qur'an's account, is not something one person does to another. It is something a person **undergoes**. God guides and God leaves astray; a human being can invite, conceal, distort, and pressure, but he cannot plant error in another person's heart. So the one who sets out to mislead has already had to do the damage to himself: he has stopped being honest, stopped testing his own claims, started treating truth as an instrument. That is the injury, and it is done before a word is spoken to anyone else.

**The verse is quoting itself.** Compare 2:9–16, on the hypocrites: "they seek to deceive God and those who believe, and they deceive none but themselves, **and they perceive not**" — *wa-mā yakhda'ūna illā anfusahum wa-mā yash'urūn*. The closing clause is nearly word for word. The surah is deliberately placing the Madinan opponents in the same category as the hypocrites of al-Baqarah: people whose problem is not ignorance but a failure of perception about their own state.

**"Yet they fail to perceive it."** *Wa-mā yash'urūn.* *Shu'ūr* is awareness — a more immediate, lower-order kind of knowing than *'ilm*. They are not merely wrong. They are unaware that they are wrong, and that unawareness is the condition being described.

**The spiritual psychology of this is precise and it is worth sitting with.** The verse describes self-blinding as a consequence of action. You set out to deceive; you must first stop checking yourself; the stopping becomes a habit; and the habit becomes the condition you cannot see out of. The attempt is the injury.

**Now bring it into the present, because there is no verse in this batch that maps more directly onto contemporary life.** The verse describes the propagandist, the misinformation operator, the person who circulates material he knows is false because it damages someone he wants damaged.

And its claim is counter-intuitive and, I think, true: **the person damaged first is the one doing it.** Not that attempts to mislead are harmless — they cause enormous real harm, and the Qur'an prosecutes slander and concealment very seriously. But at the level of the soul, the operator's loss is primary and structural. A person who lives by fabrication loses the capacity to distinguish what is true, and he loses it without noticing, and that is exactly the *mā yash'urūn* at the end of the verse.

There is a version of this available to anyone in ordinary life. The small decision to repeat something you suspect is false because it is useful. The exaggeration that helps your case. Each one requires a small act of not-looking, and the acts accumulate into a person who cannot tell.

**And the comfort in the verse, which is real.** For a community that is being targeted — and the Madinan community was — the verse says: the plot cannot reach the level at which it aims. You can be harmed, slandered, pressured. You cannot be misguided by someone else. That is between you and God.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 70

**"O People of the Book! Why do you reject the signs of Allah while you bear witness ˹to their truth˺?"**

In simple terms: O People of the Book — why do you reject the signs of God when you yourselves testify to them?

**The question form again.** *Lima* — why? The surah keeps asking this rather than simply pronouncing. Verse 65 asked "why do you argue about Abraham?" This verse asks "why do you reject the signs of God?" The interrogatory form is itself an argument: it assumes the person has a reason and demands that he produce it.

**"You reject."** *Takfurūna*, from *k-f-r*, whose root sense is to cover or conceal. The word is not mainly about intellectual disbelief. It is about **covering something you know**. That matters for what comes next.

**"The signs of Allah."** *Āyāti Allāh* — both the recited verses of the Qur'an and the evidences in the world. Both are meant here.

**And now the sting: "while you bear witness."** *Wa-antum tashhadūn.*

This is a circumstantial clause — the Arabic *ḥāl*, which describes the state you are in while doing the thing. And the state named here is not ignorance. It is **testifying**.

The verse does not say "while you are unaware." It says: while you yourselves are witnesses.

**What do they witness? Two readings, both held by the commentators.**

The first, and the majority one: they witness to the truth of Muhammad's prophethood, because they find him described in their own scriptures. This is the claim the Qur'an makes repeatedly — 2:146: "those to whom We gave the scripture recognise him as they recognise their own sons"; 2:89; 6:20. On this reading the verse is charging them with rejecting what they themselves acknowledge they know.

The second: they witness the signs themselves — they see the Qur'an and its evidences and reject them anyway.

Both readings converge on the same thing, and it is a serious charge: **rejecting what you yourself acknowledge is not a failure of the intellect. It is a failure of the will.** The Qur'an's harshest language is consistently reserved for that gap, and for the related sin of concealing what you know — see 2:42, "do not mix the truth with falsehood and conceal the truth while you know," and 3:71, which follows immediately in the next batch.

**The arc of verses 69–70.** Verse 69: they wish to mislead you, mislead only themselves, and do not perceive it. Verse 70: you reject while you witness. Both verses are about a gap between knowing and acting — in one case unperceived, in the other explicit. And the pairing tells you that the gap is the real subject here, not the community being addressed.

**Now the application, and I want to be direct about it.** The single most profitable thing a reader can do with this verse is apply it to himself, because the condition it describes is not unusual. It is one of the most common states of a religious person.

It is the state of the person who knows a thing is wrong and does it anyway. Who knows he should give and does not. Who knows the argument for the position he holds is weak and holds it because his people hold it. Who has read the evidence and chosen against it because the alternative costs him too much.

That is *kafara bi-āyāti Allāhi wa-huwa yashhadu* — rejecting while witnessing. It is not the worst state a person can be in, because it comes with knowledge, and knowledge can be acted on. But it is the state the Qur'an speaks to most sharply, because the person in it cannot plead ignorance.

**A word about the bracketed addition in the supplied translation.** "While you bear witness **to their truth**" — the Arabic is simply *wa-antum tashhadūn*, "while you bear witness," with the object unstated. The gloss makes explicit what the context implies: what they witness to is the truth of what they reject. It is a fair and clarifying addition, and it is flagged.

**And a final observation on the flow.** This verse closes the ten with a question that is not answered. The surah has made its case, issued its challenge, offered its common ground, reasoned about Abraham, and diagnosed the failure. What comes next — from verse 71 — is the exposure of a specific method: mixing truth with falsehood and concealing it deliberately. The argument moves from the condition to the tactic.

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

There is a shape to these ten verses, and it is worth seeing whole.

**The passage escalates and then de-escalates, and the two moves are deliberate.** Verse 61 is the most severe thing the Qur'an ever proposes in an argument: stake your family on the truth and call down the curse. Verse 62 seals the account. Verse 63 hands the refusal to God. Verse 64 offers the widest door available — a common word, honestly stated — and when that is refused, the instruction is not to curse but to **testify**: bear witness that we have submitted.

Three responses to refusal in four verses: curse, entrust, testify. The surah does not settle into a single register, and the reader who takes verse 61 as the summary of Islam's approach to religious disagreement has stopped four verses too early.

**The mubāhalah is a last resort, and the verse says so inside itself.** *Min ba'di mā jā'aka min al-'ilm* — after the knowledge has come to you. Every condition the jurists later set on the practice is drawn from that phrase. It is worth repeating because the practice is claimed today far more often than it is warranted.

**The common word is real and it is demanding.** *Kalimatin sawā'in* is not a compromise and not a sentiment. It is a proposition that leans neither way, and its content is *tawḥīd*: worship God alone, associate nothing with Him, and take no human being as a lord — including, and especially, the religious authorities on our own side. The hadith of 'Adī ibn Ḥātim is the key to that third clause: worship is ultimately obedience in legislation, and the clause is as much a warning to Muslims as to anyone.

**Abraham belongs to whoever follows him.** The argument from chronology at verse 65 is simple and unanswerable; the identification of *ḥanīf* and *muslim* at verse 67 refuses all three claimants; and verse 68 relocates the claim entirely, from blood to following, closing on the wordplay between *awlā* and *walī*. Nearness to Abraham is not inherited. It is given.

**And the surah's precision holds throughout.** *Ṭā'ifatun min ahli al-kitāb* — a party among them, never the whole. The Qur'an's charges are always particular, and readers who generalise them are not following the text.

**The thread running under everything is witnessing.** The disciples asked to be enrolled among the witnesses (3:53). God tells the community to say, when the invitation is refused, "bear witness that we have submitted" (3:64). And the passage closes by asking why the People of the Book reject the signs of God *while they themselves bear witness* (3:70). To be a believer in this surah's vocabulary is to be a witness — someone whose life is evidence in a case that God will eventually decide.

**And the practical heart of the batch is verse 66.** Do not argue about what you have no knowledge of. Grade your certainty into what you know, what you partly know, and what you do not know, and speak in the right register. In an environment that rewards confident ignorance, that single rule would change almost everything about how we talk to each other — and 17:36 reminds us that it is not only good manners. It is something we will be asked about.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given, because this is a synthesized commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and inventing precise references would be worse than omitting them.

Four places in this batch required particular care.

The first is **3:61**. I have distinguished what is established — the narration in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim that the Prophet ﷺ brought 'Alī, Fāṭimah, Ḥasan and Ḥusayn and said "these are my family" — from what is disputed, which is whether the verse establishes the Imamate. I have stated the Sunni and Shi'i readings, named the shared ground, and declined to adjudicate a fourteen-century disagreement that belongs to another discipline. I have also flagged that the reports of the Najrān delegation come through sīrah transmission with varying details and should be read as reported, not established.

The second is **3:64**. I have given both readings of *arbāban* — deification, and obedience to religious authorities into disobedience of God — and given the hadith of 'Adī ibn Ḥātim in full, because it is the definitive explanation of what "worship" means here. I have applied the clause inward, since the wording is reciprocal, and drawn the line that protects legitimate scholarship from being conflated with taking a scholar as a lord.

The third is **3:67**. The word *ḥanīf* is genuinely contested. I have set out the Arabic root, the Qur'anic usage, the pre-Islamic usage, the Syriac loan hypothesis and the modern scholarship, and said plainly that the etymology is not settled while the contextual sense — one who turns from everything false to the One God — is not in doubt. I have also made the precision about *muslim* as a descriptive rather than confessional term, and explained why the third clause ("not a polytheist") is not padding.

The fourth is the **conditions on mubāhalah**. I have stated them as the jurists state them — serious religious matter, evidence established, obstinacy, expected benefit, never as rhetoric — and noted that almost every contemporary resort to the practice fails them. The reported observation of Ibn Ḥajar that those who invoke it falsely do not long survive I have recorded as his observation and explicitly declined to present as a mechanism or a promise.

Where the supplied translation adds or chooses, I have flagged it: "worthy of worship" and "alone" in 3:62, "little" in 3:66, "to their truth" in 3:70, and the rendering of *kāna ḥanīfan musliman* as "he submitted in all uprightness" in 3:67, which reads well but converts two technical terms into a verb phrase. In none of these is the translation wrong; in each it has chosen, and the reader is entitled to know what was chosen from.
