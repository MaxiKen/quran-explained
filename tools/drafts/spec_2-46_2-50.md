### 2:46
> those who are certain that they will meet their Lord and to Him they will return
~ The previous verse describes patience and prayer as heavy except for the humble; this verse identifies the people who meet that description. “Those who are certain that they will meet their Lord and to Him they will return” begins with certainty, not a passing guess. The word translated as “certain” is used here for certainty and conviction. “Meet their Lord” points to the Day of Judgement. “To Him they will return” completes the account of the meeting, and they will be held accountable for their actions. This expectation is not an escape from present duties. It is connected to the ease of performing acts of obedience and refraining from prohibitions. The humble are described not merely by outward modesty or a bowed head, but by genuine humility and fear of God in the heart. Its signs can appear in conduct, but external modesty alone does not create it. The verse thus explains why patience and prayer can be sought as help: certainty of meeting God gives obedience its direction, while humility keeps that certainty joined to worship. The return is not merely a change of place: it names the final destination of every person and the answerability that follows. That belief gives obedience a lasting purpose even when worldly loss makes it feel difficult. A future meeting also changes how present difficulty is weighed: worldly loss is temporary, while returning to God is certain. Present hardship is not the final measure of a life. It sustains worship through difficulty.

### 2:47
> O Children of Israel! Remember ˹all˺ the favours I granted you and how I honoured you above the others
~ “O Children of Israel!” identifies the community that the passage now addresses. The call names the listeners before turning to their history. “Remember ˹all˺ the favours I granted you and how I honoured you above the others” is one continuous command, so the remembered gifts and the community’s honour belong together. “Remember” asks for more than recalling dates: it asks the people to recognize the giver and let gratitude shape their conduct. “All the favours” reaches beyond a single rescue. It includes guidance through scripture and messengers, protection and victory, forgiveness, help in danger, and provision. By gathering these gifts under “all,” the verse directs the audience to consider their full history rather than selecting only the honours that flatter them. The phrase “how I honoured you above the others” then gives the special role its setting. It was connected to particular gifts and a trust: the people received God’s message and were charged with carrying His commands to others. Their honour is real, but it is not a free-standing claim that makes conduct unnecessary. The word “how” turns attention to the manner of that honour—what was given and what the people were expected to do with it. The comparison with “the others” is limited to the time and gifts in view; it is not an unrestricted ranking across every people and every age. The account can recognize a community’s past distinction without making that distinction a guarantee for every descendant. The comparison also explains why the verse joins revelation with protection and provision: the gifts were not only private comforts, but part of a public trust. Receiving guidance carried a duty toward others, so privilege and service belong to the same account. The people are asked to remember not only that they were honoured, but how that honour shaped their task. The phrase also brings visible rescue and guidance into the community’s shared memory. The blessing came first to their forefathers, yet later generations could share its effects and are addressed through that history. This link does not make the generations interchangeable: the ancestors received earlier gifts, while listeners now have to respond. The reminder begins with the community’s history, but its command is aimed at those hearing it in the present. The blessings persisted across generations, yet the decisions of each generation are its own. Remembering asks the audience to acknowledge what was received and put its guidance into practice now; gratitude is more than saying thank you, because it means responding to what was entrusted to them. That response is personal, even when the favour was communal. The verse joins memory and responsibility: the past is recalled not to secure an exemption, but to guide the present response. An inherited story can be a reason to be thankful, but it cannot supply another person’s faith or deeds. For that reason, remembering should lead to gratitude and obedience, not self-conceit or an assumption that ancestry guarantees salvation. The next verse makes the individual consequence explicit: on the Day of Judgement no soul can simply take another’s place. This warning also keeps the word “honoured” from being read as a promise that every member of a community is beyond judgement. The honour describes what was granted in history; the command to remember asks the present audience how it will respond. Gifts and accountability therefore stand together, and the line from the forefathers to their descendants carries benefits without transferring responsibility.

### 2:48
> Guard yourselves against the Day on which no soul will be of help to another. No intercession will be accepted, no ransom taken, and no help will be given
~ “Guard yourselves against the Day on which no soul will be of help to another” begins with a command for the present and names the Day of Judgement as its reason. To guard against it means responding while action is still possible, not expecting someone else to rescue us after the final account begins. “No intercession will be accepted, no ransom taken, and no help will be given” then names three forms of help a person might expect: an appeal by another, a payment, or the support of a powerful friend. No soul can settle another person’s account or take its place. Intercession is not denied in every case, but it cannot be demanded or guaranteed; it is accepted only by God’s permission. The word “accepted” matters: the verse does not set up a negotiation between equal parties. A person cannot compel intercession or treat it as an entitlement; any such aid remains subject to God’s permission. Nor can kinship or inherited status turn an appeal into a right. Nor can prayers or fasts be transferred from one person to make up another’s obligations. “No ransom taken” excludes wealth as a way to buy release: even a vast payment cannot undo a person’s record. “No help will be given” completes the list, closing off the hope that some other ally can override the judgement. These are not claims that friends never help in ordinary life. The contrast is between assistance that can matter in this world and the final account where no one controls another person’s outcome. The warning follows the reminder of the favours in the preceding verse. Those gifts do not turn into a family guarantee; each person remains responsible for faith and conduct. The order of the clauses matters: first the verse names the Day, then it rules out another’s intervention, payment, and any remaining help. That sequence makes clear what “Guard yourselves” asks of the listeners now: follow God’s directions and avoid what He has prohibited. The point is not to despair of every aid; intercession remains under God’s permission. The point is to refuse the assumption that wealth, status, kinship, or a powerful friend can substitute for one’s own response before judgement. The words “no soul will be of help to another” and “no intercession will be accepted” must be read together: no person controls the outcome, even though intercession can occur only with God’s permission. The warning is therefore not a denial of every possible aid, but a call to prepare before the Day arrives, when no person can take another’s place. The image of the earth filled with gold makes the point concrete: even immeasurable wealth cannot purchase release. In this way, the verse brings the memory of earlier favour into an urgent personal question: what is each listener doing with the guidance already received?

### 2:49
> ˹Remember˺ how We delivered you from the people of Pharaoh, who afflicted you with dreadful torment, slaughtering your sons and keeping your women. That was a severe test from your Lord
~ “˹Remember˺ how We delivered you from the people of Pharaoh, who afflicted you with dreadful torment, slaughtering your sons and keeping your women” begins the recollection with the suffering that made rescue necessary. The first word, “Remember,” brings an event from the forefathers’ past into the present duties of their descendants. “We delivered you” is addressed to later generations through what their ancestors endured: they can share the benefit of the rescue, but their inheritance does not replace their own response. “The people of Pharaoh” identifies the power responsible for the oppression. The account is not simply about an escape from Egypt; it is about a ruler and his people using their authority to humiliate and harm an entire community. “Dreadful torment” is then made concrete. “Slaughtering your sons” points to the killing of newborn boys, while “keeping your women” does not describe a kindness: the women were left alive for service, and the policy was driven by Pharaoh’s self-interest. The killing is connected to Pharaoh’s fear that a child born among the Israelites would end his rule. One account also recalls a dream that Pharaoh understood as a warning, followed by orders to kill the boys and assign the people degrading work. The final words, “That was a severe test from your Lord,” can refer to the calamity of the slaughter or to the blessing of being delivered from Pharaoh. In the first reading, patience is tested by suffering; in the second, gratitude is tested by rescue. Those are not morally equivalent events: the verse names oppression as cruelty and deliverance as favour. Calling both a test keeps the whole history together without confusing the harm with the mercy that ended it. The phrase “from your Lord” also directs attention to the one who brought the rescue, rather than treating survival as a reason for pride. The account now moves from the violence of Pharaoh’s rule to the means of escape: the next verse describes the sea being parted and the pursuers drowned. The present audience is therefore asked to remember both the suffering its forefathers faced and the deliverance that followed, and to respond to that history with patience in hardship and gratitude for favour. The repeated command to remember does not erase the past or turn oppression into a gift; it makes the rescue and the people’s responsibility visible together. The two verbs “slaughtering” and “keeping” place distinct harms side by side: one group was marked for death, while the other was held under the ruler’s control. The women’s survival did not mean freedom; it was part of the way Pharaoh used the community. This makes the word “delivered” larger than a simple move from place to place: the people had to be freed from the threat of death as well as the humiliation of forced work. The order of phrases traces the harm from Pharaoh’s rule to its effect on families, and only then asks how the whole episode was a test. The next verse completes the sequence by showing the pursuers destroyed, so the history moves from oppression to escape and then to safety. Remembering this return from danger is part of the favour being recalled. The two readings also explain why “dreadful torment” comes before “severe test”: first the human harm is named plainly, and only then is the response or rescue discussed. The language does not present the victims’ suffering as a benefit; it presents deliverance as mercy within a painful history. For descendants who did not witness the event, remembering is a way to inherit responsibility, not a claim to automatic reward.

### 2:50
> And ˹remember˺ when We parted the sea, rescued you, and drowned Pharaoh’s people before your very eyes
~ “And ˹remember˺ when We parted the sea, rescued you” connects this event to the preceding account: deliverance from Pharaoh’s oppression did not end when the Israelites left Egypt. The people were pursued, and the sea stood between them and safety. “We parted the sea” names the way forward; “rescued you” states what that opening made possible. The verse then turns to the pursuers: “and drowned Pharaoh’s people before your very eyes” describes the fate of the army that followed into the sea and emphasizes that the people saw the outcome themselves. The whole movement matters: the way opened for them, they crossed, and the danger behind them was removed. The same event was escape for one people and ruin for the army that pursued them. The scene is not only an account of geography or military defeat. The phrase “rescued you” places the people at the center of what the opening made possible, while the ending asks them to recognize the fate of the pursuers. The story invites remembrance of both the saved and the lost. Remembering the parting alone would leave the threat unfinished; remembering only the drowning would hide why the path opened. Both outcomes are part of one deliverance, bringing relief to the Israelites and humiliation to their enemy. It also links the two verses: the rescue named after Pharaoh’s torment is now shown in detail, from the moment the sea divided to the end of the pursuit. The people are asked to remember not simply a narrow passage through water, but the complete reversal of their danger. This explains why the enemy’s fate appears beside the people’s escape rather than the verse ending when the sea opens. The rescue is complete only when the army can no longer pursue them. “Remember” points to the result as well as the route: the people are to recall who made a way and how the threat ended. A later observance carries this rescue into new generations through worship, gratitude, and shared remembrance beyond the eyewitnesses of the event.
