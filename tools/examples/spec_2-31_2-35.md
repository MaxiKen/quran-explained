### 2:31
> He taught Adam the names of all things, then He presented them to the angels and said, “Tell Me the names of these, if what you say is true?”
+auto 42

~ The verse takes the test out of the angels' hands and puts it in Adam's, and it does so in four moves that have to be kept in order. First, "He taught Adam the names of all things", with the teaching before any claim of rank, so the advantage is described as something given and not something earned, and the names are his by the same route the gnat in the earlier parable is a sign, by being told. Then "then He presented them to the angels and said", which is the turn: the parade is for the angels, not for information. "Tell Me the names of these" is a command addressed to them, and the sources notice that the wording is an invitation to admit what they had said. "if what you say is true" closes the test with the condition, and it is the clause the commentators work with here, since the demand is only for a recital of the names and they have already been told the reason they were asked.

### 2:32
> They replied, “Glory be to You! We have no knowledge except what You have taught us. You are truly the All-Knowing, All-Wise.”
+auto 38

~ The reply is the whole lesson, and the order it comes in is why it is quoted in every discussion of etiquette afterwards. "They replied, Glory be to You" puts the exalting before anything is told about themselves, and the sources read that as the proper shape of an answer when a person has been shown to be short of knowledge: speak first about God, not about the case. "We have no knowledge except what You have taught us" then concedes the point the test was built to make, and the wording is careful, since they do not claim that knowledge is bad but that none of it is theirs except what has been given. "You are truly the All-Knowing, All-Wise" ends with a description of God rather than an apology for themselves, and the emphatic construction in that clause is what one commentary stops on. The verse before had exposed a claim to know, and this is the opposite posture, drawn from the angels themselves, set down for any hearer who is asked something beyond them.

### 2:33
> Allah said, “O Adam! Inform them of their names.” Then when Adam did, Allah said, “Did I not tell you that I know the secrets of the heavens and the earth, and I know what you reveal and what you conceal?”
+auto 46

~ The verse has three clauses and each one changes who is speaking, so the guidance is in following that. "Allah said, O Adam" is the address, and the sources point out that Adam is named while the angels are still the ones being tested, so the teaching is shown to be a trust rather than a superiority. "Inform them of their names" tells him to speak to the angels, which is the reversal of the previous verse, since they had been told to recite and could not. "Then when Adam did, Allah said" passes over the recital itself in four words, and the commentaries are aware of how quickly it is done, the point being the obedience and not the list. "Did I not tell you that I know the secrets of the heavens and the earth" then returns to the angels, with the question addressed to them about what they had said earlier, and "and I know what you reveal and what you conceal" then covers the part of their case that no parade of names could touch, so the verse closes on what was in their hearts rather than on what they had said out loud. The commentaries use the sequence to make two points at once, that knowledge is given and not inherited, and that the one who receives it is answerable for it, which is the same order the parable of the gnat had set, a small thing taught and a claim examined. The names themselves are left unspecified in the verse, and the sources list what they were taken to cover, so nothing here turns on settling which of the things in the parade was meant. The verse after this turns from knowledge to command, and the prostration that is refused.

### 2:34
> And ˹remember˺ when We said to the angels, “Prostrate before Adam,” so they all did—but not Iblîs, who refused and acted arrogantly, becoming unfaithful
+auto 48

~ The command, the obedience and the refusal are in one sentence here, and the weight of the verse falls on the clause that separates them. "And remember when We said to the angels" is again a reminder rather than a first telling, and the address includes them as a body. "Prostrate before Adam" states the act and its direction in three words, and the sources dwell on that direction, since the prostration is to Adam and not of worship to him, a distinction the legal discussions need. "so they all did" is given without exception among the angels and without any description of reluctance, and the commentators notice that the text keeps the angels alone here, though other beings stand in the same account. "but not Iblîs" is the hinge, and the verse does not name a reason yet. "who refused and acted arrogantly, becoming unfaithful" then gives the three steps in one clause, the refusal, the arrogance, and the unfaithfulness it ends in. The order is stopped at, since the refusal comes first and the pride after it, and the sources take the last word of the clause as the outcome rather than the motive, so the verse does not have to say what he believed. What he was asked to do is treated as an honour laid on Adam and a test of the hearers at the same time, and the excuses given in the verses that follow, a difference of material and a claim of precedence, are what the legal and spiritual discussions work with here, one of them ruling on the direction of a prostration and the other on what pride does to a person who knows the command.

### 2:35
> We cautioned, “O Adam! Live with your wife in Paradise and eat as freely as you please, but do not approach this tree, or else you will be wrongdoers.”
+auto 46

~ Everything in this verse is permission except one clause, and the order matters because the exception is what the next verses turn on. "We cautioned, O Adam! Live with your wife in Paradise and eat as freely as you please" opens with warning rather than with the gift, and the two halves are quoted together here because the permission is the shape the warning sits inside, and the sources note the word for caution here, since what follows sounds like a favour. "Live with your wife in Paradise and eat as freely as you please" gives the dwelling as shared, the wife named in the same breath, and the eating without reckoning; the commentators take "as freely as you please" to cover whatever they wanted, in any part of the Garden, and the permission to eat is treated as the general rule from which the prohibition is an exception. "but do not approach this tree" is then not a second command but the edge of the first, and the wording, approach rather than eat, is stopped at more than once. "or else you will be wrongdoers" ends without naming a punishment at all, which is how the discussion of what the prohibition actually lays on them begins, since the consequence is stated as a condition of the act and not as a sentence. The dwelling with his wife is taken by the sources as the first of the permissions in this passage and the pair are named together on purpose, and the verse after this records the slip.
