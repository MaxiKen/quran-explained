## Verse 81

Verse 81 says: *˹Remember˺ when Allah made a covenant with the prophets, ˹saying,˺ “Now that I have given you the Book and wisdom, if there comes to you a messenger confirming what you have, you must believe in him and support him.” He added, “Do you affirm this covenant and accept this commitment?” They said, “Yes, we do.” Allah said, “Then bear witness, and I too am a Witness.”*

In simple terms, the verse reminds the People of the Book of the covenant Allah made with the prophets: after giving them the Book and wisdom, if a messenger came confirming what they had, they were to believe in him and support him; the prophets affirmed this and Allah called them to bear witness to it.

The verse returns to the theme of the surah's opening — the covenant of the prophets — and places it at the head of the warning passage that closes the second major section of Surah Ali 'Imran. Ibn Kathir frames the section: "Allah states that He took a pledge from every Prophet whom He sent from Adam until 'Isa, that when Allah gives them the Book and the Hikmah, thus acquiring whatever high grades they deserve, then a Messenger came afterwards, they would believe in and support him. Even though Allah has given the Prophets the knowledge and the prophethood, this fact should not make them refrain from following and supporting the Prophet who comes after them."

"when Allah made a covenant with the prophets" — *wa idh akhadha Allahu mithaqa al-nabiyyin* — when Allah took the covenant, the mithaq, of the prophets. The covenant is the same word used for the covenant of the Children of Israel (2:40, 2:83-84) and for the covenant taken from the believers (5:7). The address is to the prophets themselves, and through them, as Al-Jalalayn stresses, to their communities of descendants: "their communities of descendants follow them in what is incumbent upon them." The covenant is thus the charter of the succession of the prophets and their peoples.

"Now that I have given you the Book and wisdom" — *lama ataytukum min kitabin wa hikmah* — since I have given you of the Book and wisdom. The *lam* can be read as an oath-introducing particle or as the cause: "for what I have given you" — the point being that the gifts of the Book and the wisdom are the very reason the prophets must acknowledge the later messenger. The grandeur of the gift does not exempt them from the response to the next revelation; it obliges them to recognize the confirmation of what they were given. Ibn Kathir stresses this: the knowledge and prophethood already given should not make a prophet refrain from following and supporting the one who comes after.

"if there comes to you a messenger confirming what you have, you must believe in him and support him" — the covenant's condition and its two obligations: *la tu'minunna bihi wa la tansurunnah* — you shall surely believe in him and surely help him. The confirming messenger is the one who comes with what agrees with the revelation already received — and in the surah's context, this is Muhammad: the Prophet whose description the People of the Book found in their scriptures, who confirmed the Tawrah and the Injil, who made no distinction among the prophets. Ibn Kathir: "'Ali bin Abi Talib and his cousin 'Abdullah bin 'Abbas said, 'Allah never sent a Prophet but after taking his pledge that if Muhammad were sent in his lifetime, he would believe in and support him.' Allah commanded each Prophet to take a pledge from his nation that if Muhammad were sent in their time, they would believe in and support him." The two obligations are belief and support — the inner acknowledgment and the active assistance, the faith and the help, which together make the acknowledgment real.

"He added, 'Do you affirm this covenant and accept this commitment?'" — *qala a'aqartum wa akhazhtum 'ala dhalikum isri* — do you affirm, and have you taken upon that My *isr*? The word *isr* is the crux of the verse. Ibn 'Abbas, Mujahid, Ar-Rabi', Qatadah, and As-Suddi: "My covenant." Muhammad bin Ishaq: "the responsibility of My covenant that you took — the ratified pledge that you gave Me." The *isr* carries the sense of a burden, a load, an obligation assumed — the same root as the *isr* lifted from the Children of Israel (7:157, 2:286): the heavy commitment accepted. The covenant is not a polite agreement; it is a load carried.

"They said, 'Yes, we do.'" — *qalu aqrarna* — we have affirmed, we have confirmed. The affirmation is the prophetic answer to the divine question. Al-Jalalayn: "They said 'We affirm.'" Ibn 'Abbas's treatment adds the dimension of the transmission: the prophets affirmed, each explained this to his nation, and each made the members of his nation witnesses for each other, just as each prophet bore witness to it. The covenant is not only between Allah and the prophets; it is between Allah, the prophets, and their peoples — the affirmation of the prophet obliges the people, and the witness of the prophet is the witness against the people.

"Allah said, 'Then bear witness, and I too am a Witness.'" — *fashhadu wa ana ma'akum mina al-shahidin* — then bear witness, and I am with you among the witnesses. Al-Jalalayn: "Bear witness to this before your own souls and those of your followers, and I shall be with you among the witnesses before you and them." The witness is the transfer of the testimony: Allah takes the covenant, the prophets affirm it, and the testimony is recorded — Allah as the primary Witness and the prophets as the witnesses to their peoples. The verse closes the chain of the proof: the truth of the final messenger was not an innovation but the content of the ancient covenant, witnessed by the prophets and guaranteed by Allah.

Ibn Kathir draws the consequences of the covenant: Muhammad is the Final Prophet until the Day of Resurrection, the greatest Imam, who if he existed in any time period deserves to be obeyed rather than all other prophets; this is why he led the prophets in prayer during the night of Isra' when they gathered in Bayt al-Maqdis; he is the intercessor on the Day of Gathering, the holder of the Maqam al-Mahmud (cf. 17:79), a responsibility the mighty prophets and messengers will decline to assume. The covenant of 3:81 is therefore the thread that runs through the whole prophetic history: every prophet prepared his people for the one who would come after, and the final one gathers the witness of them all.

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's statement of the deep unity of the prophetic message and the primacy of the truth over the authority of the past. The prophets were not given the Book to become the final authorities against later revelation; they were given it to become the witnesses to later revelation. The scholar who has received the Book and the wisdom is the one most obligated to recognize the confirmation when it comes; the grandeur of the gift is the grandeur of the responsibility. The verse also teaches the believer the meaning of the covenant in his own life: the testimony of the prophets, the affirmation of the community, and the return of the witness to Allah — and the burden of knowledge is the burden of the acknowledgment.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 82

Verse 82 says: *Whoever turns back after this, they will be the rebellious.*

In simple terms, the verse warns: after this covenant, whoever turns away will be among the defiant, the rebellious.

The verse is the penalty clause of the covenant of 3:81. Ibn Kathir: "'then whoever turns away after this' — from fulfilling this pledge and covenant — 'they are the rebellious.'" The word *fasiqun* — the defiantly disobedient, the rebellious, the ones who deviate from the command — is the Qur'an's standard term for those who break the covenant of Allah (cf. 2:26-27, 5:47, 57:26-27). The covenant's breach is not a mere disagreement; it is a moral deviation, a departure from the path.

"Whoever turns back after this" — *fa man tawalla ba'da dhalik* — whoever turns away after that. The *tawalla* is the turning of the back, the deliberate withdrawal — the same verb used in 3:63 for those who turn away from the true narrative and in 3:64 for those who refuse the common word. The qualification "after this" is essential: the verse does not condemn the one who has not heard, but the one who has heard the covenant, affirmed it, and turned away from its consequence. The turning is the rejection of the acknowledged truth — the same pattern the surah has been exposing in the People of the Book.

"they will be the rebellious" — *fa ula'ika humu al-fasiqun* — then those are the fasiqun. The grammatical form — "they are the rebellious" — is the verdict of identity: not merely "they are rebellious" but "they are the rebellious ones," the category of the deviants. Ibn Kathir's context: the exegetes of the verse place it among the warnings of the surah's second section — the covenant, the rebellion, and the consequences that follow in the next verses (the seeking of another religion, the disbelief after the clear proofs, the condemnation, the punishment).

The verse's place in the surah's structure: 3:81-85 form a single argument. First the covenant (81); then the verdict on those who break it (82); then the impossibility of a religion other than the religion of Allah, to which all creation submits (83); then the creed of the believers in all the revelations (84); and then the rejection of any way other than Islam (85). The covenant of 81 is the foundation; the rebellion of 82 is the breach; and the verses that follow define the religious order the breach rejects.

For the reader, the verse establishes the seriousness of the religious commitment. The covenant is made with the prophets and their peoples; the affirmation is a commitment; and the withdrawal after the affirmation is a moral deviation, a *fisq* — not because the truth changed but because the affirmer betrayed it. The believer is taught that the acknowledgment of the covenant is a burden and that the turning from it is a rebellion — the *fisq* is the internal contradiction of the one who witnessed the truth and abandoned it. The verse also teaches the standard of the final judgment: what matters is not the identity of the covenanter but the faithfulness of the covenant — and the one who turns after the affirmation is the rebellious one, whatever his past honor.

The verse's brevity is its force. The Qur'an does not expand the penalty here into a detailed punishment; it names the category: *fasiqun*. The verse is the divine signature of the covenant's seriousness, and it works in the surah exactly as the similar formula in 2:26-27 works — the covenant with the believers, the breaking of it, and the *fasiqun* as the category of the breakers. The *fisq* of 3:82 is the breach of the mithaq — the same word used of the breaking of the covenant of the Children of Israel (2:27) and of the covenant of the prophets themselves. The surah's unifying thread: the series of the covenants — the one with the Children of Israel at the beginning of Surah 2, the one with the prophets here, the one with the believers in 3:81's completion — all framed by the same verdict: the turners-away are the rebellious.

The verse also has a historical resonance with the People of the Book's own scholars. The ones who bore the Book and wisdom, who were the descendants of the covenanting prophets, who were themselves the keepers of the prophetic descriptions — they were the ones whose turning after the covenant was the most consequential: the witnesses of the testimony who refused the testimony's completion. Ibn Kathir's context places the verse against the Jews of Madinah who had the description of the Prophet in their Books and rejected him — the *fisq* of the knowing, not of the ignorant. The verse's warning is therefore not a distant matter of the ancient covenant but a direct matter of the present controversy.

For the modern reader, the verse is the caution against the reverence of the past that becomes the obstacle to the truth. The scholars and the communities of the earlier revelation received the Book and the wisdom; they were the guardians of the description of the final Messenger; and the covenant required them to recognize the confirmation. The turning after the affirmation — the refusal of the one whose coming their own Books announced — was the *fisq*. The believer of today is being taught the same lesson in his own context: the authority of the past, the tradition, the community's inheritance — all of it is a trust that points to the truth, and the one who receives the truth and turns from it after he has affirmed it is the rebellious one. The duty of the knowledge is the duty of the acknowledgment; the acknowledgment is the covenant's content; and the turning after the acknowledgment is the breach.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 83

Verse 83 says: *Do they desire a way other than Allah’s—knowing that all those in the heavens and the earth submit to His Will, willingly or unwillingly, and to Him they will ˹all˺ be returned?*

In simple terms, the verse asks the rhetorical question: do they seek a religion other than Allah's, while everything in the heavens and the earth submits to Him, willingly or unwillingly, and all of it will return to Him?

The verse is the argument of the impossibility of an alternative religion. Ibn Kathir titles the passage "The Only Valid Religion To Allah is Islam" and frames it: "Allah rebukes those who prefer a religion other than the religion that He sent His Books and Messengers with, which is the worship of Allah Alone without partners." The rhetorical question of the verse is the Qur'an's way of declaring the absurdity of the alternative: the same reality that submits to Allah, and the same future that returns to Allah, leave no room for a religion of another authority.

"Do they desire a way other than Allah's" — *afaghyra dini Allahi yabghun* — do they seek other than the religion of Allah? The Al-Jalalayn notes the disavowing hamza: "the hamza at the beginning of the verse denotes a disavowal." The question is not an inquiry; it is the rebuke of the choice. The *din* — the religion, the way, the allegiance — is Allah's: the worship, the submission, the law of the One who created. The seeker of another *din* is seeking the contradiction of the reality.

"knowing that all those in the heavens and the earth submit to His Will" — *wa lahu aslama man fi al-samawati wa al-ard* — and to Him submitted whoever is in the heavens and the earth. Ibn Kathir's distinction is the heart of the verse: "the faithful believer submits to Allah in heart and body, while the disbeliever unwillingly submits to Him in body only, since he is under Allah's power, irresistible control and mighty kingship that cannot be repelled or resisted." The submission of all creation is not the voluntary choice of every creature; it is the ontological fact of the creation: everything, willing or not, is under the divine order — the angels in obedience, the beasts in the law of their nature, the disbeliever in the power from which he cannot escape. The believer's submission is the voluntary alignment with the order; the denier's submission is the involuntary participation in it.

"willingly or unwillingly" — *taw'an wa karhan* — voluntarily or by compulsion. Al-Jalalayn: the unwilling submission is "by the sword and by seeing what a refusal results in" — in the context of the Qur'anic account of the nations that fought the prophets, the refusal of the voluntary obedience is followed by the involuntary submission. Mujahid's parallel (cited by Ibn Kathir): the verse is similar to 39:38 — "if you ask them, Who created the heavens and the earth, surely they will say: Allah" — the disbelievers acknowledge the Creator in their own affirmation while refusing the worship. Ibn 'Abbas: "When He took the covenant from them" — the primal acknowledgment of the covenant binds them beyond their refusal.

"and to Him they will ˹all˺ be returned" — *wa ilayhi yurja'un* — and to Him they will be returned. The return is the Day of Return, the resurrection and the reckoning. Al-Jalalayn adds the variant reading *turja'un* — "you shall be returned" — the direct address to the disbelievers. The verse's argument is complete and circular: the reality that submits and the future that returns leave no third option; the seeker of another religion is the seeker of the impossible — the one who wishes to belong to a reality that has no other master.

Ibn Kathir's parallel verses: 13:15 — "And unto Allah falls in prostration whoever is in the heavens and the earth, willingly or unwillingly"; 16:48–50 — "Have they not observed the things Allah has created: how their shadows incline to the right and the left, making prostration unto Allah, and they are lowly? And to Allah prostrate all that is in the heavens and all that is in the earth, of the moving creatures and the angels, and they are not proud." The submission of the shadows and the creatures is the visible witness to the invisible submission of all things: the *din* of Allah is the order of the creation, and the religion of the believers is the voluntary participation in that order.

For the reader, the verse is the cosmological argument for the unity of the religion. The oneness of Allah is not a theological preference; it is the structure of the universe — the submission of all things, the return of all things, the sovereignty over all things. The believer's *din* is the alignment with the reality; the seeker of another *din* is the seeker of a fiction within the reality. The verse also teaches the distinction between the voluntary and the involuntary submission: the disbeliever is not outside the divine order but in rebellion within it; and the believer is not merely a creature who submits by necessity but a servant who submits by choice. The verse's question — *do they desire a way other than Allah's?* — remains the question for every generation: the seeker of the alternative must answer what alternative there is, when everything submits and everything returns.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 84

Verse 84 says: *Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants; and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and other prophets from their Lord—we make no distinction between any of them, and to Him we ˹fully˺ submit.”*

In simple terms, the verse gives the creed of the believers: they believe in Allah, in their own revelation, and in all the revelations given to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob and his descendants, and to Moses, Jesus, and the other prophets — making no distinction among them and submitting to Allah alone.

The verse is the positive creed that answers the challenge of the People of the Book and the negation of 3:83. Ibn Kathir: "Say: 'We believe in Allah and in what has been sent down to us' — the Qur'an — 'and what was sent down to Ibrahim, Ismai'l, Ishaq, Ya'qub' — the scriptures and revelation — 'and the Asbat' — the twelve tribes who originated from the twelve children of Israel (Ya'qub) — 'and what was given to Musa, 'Isa' — the Tawrah and the Injil — 'and the Prophets from their Lord' — and this encompasses all of Allah's Prophets." The creed is the summary of the Islamic understanding of revelation: one source, many prophets, one message, no distinction.

"We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us" — the creed begins with the immediate revelation: the Qur'an, given to Muhammad. The believer's faith is anchored in the revelation of the present, the Book in hand. Ibn Kathir makes this the ground of the whole assertion: the Muslims believe in what was revealed to them, and this is the standard by which the earlier revelations are recognized — not the acceptance of the earlier books over the later, but the confirmation of the earlier by the later.

"and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants" — the list of the patriarchs: Ibrahim, Ismai'l, Ishaq, Ya'qub, and the Asbat — the twelve tribes. The list is the Qur'an's genealogy of the revelation: the patriarchs of the line of the promise, the fathers of the twelve tribes, the bearers of the covenant before the prophets of the law. The mention of Ishmael is decisive in the surah's context: the son of the first Arab line, the father of the Ka'bah's builders, the prophet whose descendants are the believers of the final revelation. Ibn Kathir: "the Asbat are the twelve tribes who originated from the twelve children of Israel (Ya'qub)."

"and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and other prophets from their Lord" — the two great scriptures of the earlier communities — the Tawrah and the Injil — and, beyond them, all the prophets, each given what was given from their Lord. The verse's formula is comprehensive: it names the patriarchs, the tribes, the two law-bearing prophets, and then "the Prophets from their Lord" — the universal category. Ibn Kathir: "this encompasses all of Allah's Prophets." The creed refuses the sectarian narrowing: the Muslims believe in *every* prophet Allah sent and *every* book He revealed, and never disbelieve in any of them.

"we make no distinction between any of them" — *la nufarriqu bayna ahadin minhum* — we do not distinguish between any one of them. The clause is the creed's sharpest edge: the Muslims believe in all the prophets without preferring one over another as the object of faith. Ibn Kathir: "We believe in all of them." The Jews distinguished between the prophets (believing in some, rejecting others like 'Isa and Muhammad); the Christians distinguished in another way (elevating 'Isa and rejecting the final Messenger); the Muslims' creed is the unity of the prophetic message: all of them, from Adam to Muhammad, messengers of the one Allah, and faith in each is faith in all.

"and to Him we ˹fully˺ submit" — *wa nahnu lahu muslimun* — and to Him we are surrendered, Muslims. The creed ends with the act: the submission to Allah alone. The *islam* of the believers is the conclusion of the unity of the prophets: if all the prophets came with the one religion of the submission to the One, then the believers of the final revelation are the *muslimun* — the submitters — and their creed is the creed of all the prophets. Ibn Kathir: "Therefore, faithful Muslims believe in every Prophet whom Allah has sent and in every Book He revealed, and never disbelieve in any of them."

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's inclusive creed, and it is at the same time the sharpest boundary of the faith. The believers believe in Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, the tribes, Moses, Jesus, and all the prophets — no distinction; and they submit to Allah alone. The verse is the answer to the religious pluralism of the surah's debate: the truth of the earlier prophets is affirmed completely, but the affirmation is the affirmation of the doctrine that they all transmitted — the worship of Allah alone — and the believers' submission is the completion of theirs. The verse also teaches the believer the etiquette of faith toward the prophets: the reverence, the affirmation, the love of all of them — and the worship of none of them. The one who reads the verse is being taught: belief in the prophets is not the belief in the sectarian versions of them but in the authentic message of them — the message of the One.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 85

Verse 85 says: *Whoever seeks a way other than Islam, it will never be accepted from them, and in the Hereafter they will be among the losers.*

In simple terms, the verse declares: whoever seeks a religion other than Islam, it will not be accepted, and they will be among the losers in the Hereafter.

The verse is the conclusion of the argument of 3:83–84. Ibn Kathir: "whoever seeks other than what Allah has legislated, it will not be accepted from him, and in the Hereafter he will be one of the losers." The verse's structure is the double verdict: the non-acceptance in the present and the loss in the future. The *din* of Allah is the worship of the One; the seeker of another way is the seeker of the rejected.

"Whoever seeks a way other than Islam" — *wa man yabtaghi ghayra al-islami dinan* — and whoever seeks other than al-Islam as a religion. The word *islam* here is the verbal noun of the submission: the surrender, the religion of the submission to Allah — the religion of all the prophets, the religion of the *muslimun* of 3:84. Al-Jalalayn gives the occasion: "the following was revealed regarding those who apostatized and became disbelievers." Ibn 'Abbas (Tanwir): "he will be among the duped because he will miss Paradise and all that is in it and dwell in hell with all that is in it." The verse is addressed in the first instance to the rejecters of the final revelation among the People of the Book and the apostates from the faith, but its principle is universal: the *islam* as the religion of the submission is the only religion that corresponds to the reality of 3:83.

"it will never be accepted from them" — *falan yuqbala minhu* — it will never be accepted from him. The rejection is categorical: *lan* — never. The acceptance of the religion is not measured by sincerity alone but by the correspondence with the truth: the sincere worship of a false object is not accepted, any more than the sincere action against the command is accepted. Ibn Kathir cites the Prophet's hadith: "Whoever commits an action that does not conform to our matter (religion), then it is rejected" (authentic). The verse is the Qur'an's statement of the criterion of the accepted religion: it must be the religion Allah sent, not merely the religion the seeker prefers.

"and in the Hereafter they will be among the losers" — *wa huwa fi al-akhirati mina al-khasirin* — and he, in the Hereafter, is of the losers. The *khasirun* — the losers — are the ones whose trade is loss (cf. 2:27, 3:64's context, 39:15): they have spent the life in the worship of the false, and the balance is the loss. Ibn Kathir: the loser's end is "the Fire made everlasting for him." The double verdict — the non-acceptance and the loss — is the Qur'an's severity at its exact point: the religion of the submission is the only way, and the way of the other is the way of the loss.

The theological context of the verse requires care and balance. The *islam* of the verse is not the historical label of the later community alone but the submission to Allah — the religion of the prophets, the religion of the *hanif* Abraham, the religion of the worship of the One. The verse's categorical statement is the Qur'an's answer to the claim that sincerity saves regardless of the truth: the truth is the criterion, and the truth is the submission. At the same time, the verse is not a license for the arrogance of the believers: the *islam* is the submission of the heart and the body, and the admission to it is by the mercy of Allah, not by the label of the community. The verse's force is the exclusivity of the truth; its grace is the openness of the submission.

For the reader, the verse is the theological foundation of the exclusivity of the religion of the submission, and one of the most discussed verses of the Qur'an in interfaith dialogue. The reader is taught: the criterion of the accepted religion is the correspondence with the truth revealed, and the seeker of the alternative — the worship of other than the One, the autonomy of the human legislation against the divine — is the seeker of the rejected. The verse is also the believer's warning: the *islam* is not a label but a submission; the one who bears the name without the surrender is as much at risk as the one who never bore it. And the verse's double verdict — the non-acceptance and the loss — is the seriousness of the religious choice: it is not a matter of preference but of truth, and the truth is the way of the submission.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 86

Verse 86 says: *How will Allah guide a people who chose to disbelieve after they had believed, acknowledged the Messenger to be true, and received clear proofs? For Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people.*

In simple terms, the verse asks the impossible question: how can Allah guide a people who deliberately disbelieved after believing, after acknowledging the Messenger, and after receiving clear proofs? Allah does not guide the wrongdoers.

The verse is the Qur'an's most pointed statement on the condition of guidance. Ibn Kathir's section frames it: "Allah threatens and warns those who revert to disbelief after they believed and who thereafter insist on disbelief until death." The verse's question — *kayfa yahdi Allahu qawman kafaru ba'da imanihim* — how will Allah guide those who disbelieved after their belief — is not a question of the divine inability but of the human closure: the guidance is not withheld arbitrarily; it is closed by the choice of the wrongdoers.

"who chose to disbelieve after they had believed" — the first element of the condition: the belief and the abandonment. The people of the verse are not the ignorant or the untaught; they are the ones who had believed, then disbelieved. The sequence is the apostasy of the People of the Book in the surah's context — the ones who had known the prophets, acknowledged the description of the final Messenger, and then rejected him. Ibn Kathir: the verses expose those who "revert to disbelief after they believed."

"acknowledged the Messenger to be true" — *wa shahidu anna al-rasula haqq* — and they bore witness that the Messenger is true. The acknowledgment is the testimony of the People of the Book: they had witnessed, borne witness, that the Messenger is the truth — the description in their Books, the signs of his coming, the confirmation of the prophets. The verse's progression is deliberate: the belief, the witness, and the clear proofs — all present, and the choice against them. Ibn Kathir: "You know for certain that Allah's Ayat are true and authentic" — the acknowledging witnesses were the hardest to mislead and the most guilty of the turning.

"and received clear proofs" — *wa ja'athum al-bayyinat* — and clear proofs came to them. The *bayyinat* — the manifest signs, the clear evidences — complete the condition: not only the belief and the witness but the proofs in their hands: the miracles, the scriptures, the prophecies fulfilled. The verse's accumulation is the certification of the guilt: three conditions — belief, testimony, and proof — all present, and the choice against them. The *kufr* of the verse is not the absence of the evidence but the rejection of it.

"For Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people" — *wa Allahu la yahdi al-qawma al-zalimin* — and Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people. The verse's conclusion is the divine justice, not the divine caprice: the guidance is the opening of the heart to the truth, and the wrongdoer — the one who has chosen the *kufr* after the belief, the witness, and the proof — has closed the heart to the guidance. Ibn Kathir: the verse's parallel is the *zulm* of the wrongdoing: the wrong is the wrong of the choice, and the closure of the guidance is the consequence of the wrong. As in 2:7, 2:88, 4:155, the Quran teaches that the wrongdoing hearts are sealed: not because Allah desires their damnation but because the persistent wrong closes the passage of the guidance — the one who rejects the truth repeatedly hardens against it, and the guidance that requires the openness cannot pass through the hardness.

The theological point of the verse is the balance of the divine guidance and the human choice. The Qur'an does not say that Allah cannot guide the wrongdoers; it says Allah does not guide them — the *la* of the negative but not of the incapacity. The guidance is conditioned by the receptivity: the one who turns to Allah is guided (2:282's promise, 13:27, 42:13), and the one who persists in the wrong is left in the wrong. Ibn Kathir's framing is the Qur'an's own: the ones who return to disbelief and insist on it until death are the ones of whom no repentance is accepted (91) and no ransom is received (92) — the sequence of the verses 86–91 is the exposition of the closure.

For the reader, the verse is a deep lesson on the nature of the proof and the fate of the evidence. The believer is taught: faith is not the mere assent to the proofs but the openness of the heart to them — and the proofs themselves can be seen and rejected; the ones who have believed, witnessed, and received the clear proofs are the ones whose rejection is the gravest. The verse is the quiet warning of the surah: the believers, received the like of the conditions; the ones who persist in the wrong — the wrong of the heart against the proofs — are the ones of whom the guidance is closed. And the verse's hope is embedded in its own grammar: the guidance is open while the heart is open, and the believer's task is the preservation of the openness — the sincerity, the humility, and the constant reception of the proofs.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 87

Verse 87 says: *Their reward is that they will be condemned by Allah, the angels, and all of humanity.*

In simple terms, the verse states the recompense: their reward is the curse of Allah, the angels, and all of humanity.

The verse is the first clause of the verdict on the deniers of 3:86. The word *jaza'* — reward, recompense — is used here in the Qur'an's characteristic way: the reward of the evil is the evil itself, the *la'na* — the curse. Ibn Kathir's treatment of 3:86–91 covers the whole: the verdict is the curse of Allah, the angels, and the people — the triple condemnation.

"Their reward is that they will be condemned by Allah" — *ula'ika jaza'uhum an 'alayhim la'nata Allah* — their recompense is that upon them is the curse of Allah. The *la'na* — the curse, the exclusion from the mercy, the banishment — is the opposite of the *baraqa* — the blessing. The curse of Allah is the removal of the grace, the withdrawal of the guidance's light, the abandonment of the wrongdoer to the wrong. The verse's grammar — *jaza'* — is the justice of the recompense: the ones who rejected the guidance are left outside the guidance's blessing.

"the angels" — *wa al-mala'ikah* — and the angels: the carriers of the mercy and the executors of the judgment. The angels curse the ones who oppose the truth they serve: the guardians of Paradise and Hell, the carriers of the revelation they rejected, the witnesses of the deeds they denied. Ibn Kathir: the cursing is the consequence of the wrong: the rejection of the divine word brings the rejection of the divine witness — the angels who conveyed the word bear witness against those who refused it.

"and all of humanity" — *wa al-nas ajma'in* — and all of mankind together. The third element of the curse is the most striking: the condemnation of all humanity. The *la'na* of the collective is not a hatred among people but the testimony of creation: the ones who rejected the truth, the Messenger, and the proofs are condemned by the very humanity they wronged — the ones they sought to mislead, the religion they obstructed, the name of the truth they corrupted. The curse of humanity is the testimony of the wrong against the warners (cf. 7:38, 40:52, 11:18 — the curse of the onlookers and the communities).

The triple curse — Allah, the angels, and the people — is the Qur'an's escalation of the verdict. The parallel in 2:159–162 shapes the picture: "Those who conceal what We sent down of the clear proofs and guidance, after We made it clear to the people in the Book — Allah curses them, and the cursers curse them" — the concealment of the truth is the first curse, and the ones who conceal it are joined by the cursing of all. The verse of 3:87 is the same verdict on the ones who witnessed and rejected: the curse of Allah, the angels, and the people.

For the reader, the verse is a rigorous teaching on the social and cosmic reality of the wrong. The believer is taught: the wrong against the truth is not a private matter; it is registered in the three registers of the creation — the divine judgment, the angelic witness, and the human testimony. The curse of humanity is the most sobering element: the one who corrupts the truth for the people is cursed by the people whose truth he corrupted. And the verse's picture is also the picture of the opposite: the believer who conveys the truth receives the blessing of Allah, the angels, and the people (cf. 3:45 — the blessing of the one who is honored in this world and the Hereafter) — the recompense is the mirror of the deed.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 88

Verse 88 says: *They will be in Hell forever. Their punishment will not be lightened, nor will they be delayed ˹from it˺.*

In simple terms, the verse describes the state of the cursed: they will remain in Hell eternally; the punishment will not be lightened, and they will not be granted any respite.

The verse is the description of the abode of the condemned of 3:87. Ibn Kathir: the ones who are cursed by Allah, the angels, and the people are the ones who remain in the Fire — the *khulud* — the permanence. The verse's three elements: the duration (eternal), the intensity (not lightened), and the time (not deferred).

"They will be in Hell forever" — *khalidin fiha* — they will abide therein forever. The word *khulud* — the permanence, the remaining — is the Qur'an's term for the eternal abiding of the deniers (cf. 2:81, 4:169, 22:23's positive use, 33:65). The verse's *khalidin fiha* is the negative counterpart of the believers' *khalidin fiha* in the Gardens: the abiding is the abiding, and the difference is the abode. Ibn Kathir's context: the one who dies in the *kufr* remains in the Fire — the *khulud* of the evil.

"Their punishment will not be lightened" — *la yukhaffafu 'anhum al-'adhab* — the punishment will not be lightened for them. The *takhfif* — the lightening, the reduction — is the hope of the punished: the partial relief, the temporary easing. The verse denies it: the punishment is not reduced, not mitigated, not proportioned to the endurance. Ibn Kathir: the denied lightening is the denial of the mercy's intercession — the one who rejected the guidance in the life receives the punishment without relief in the Hereafter.

"nor will they be delayed ˹from it˺" — *wa la hum yunzarun* — and they will not be given respite, deferred, looked upon with delay. The *nazar* — the looking, the waiting, the respite — is the other hope: the delay, the extension, the chance. The verse denies it: no respite, no postponement, no interval. The two negations are the denial of the two hopes of the punished — the relief and the delay — and the abiding is the abiding without either.

The verse's place in the Qur'an's teaching on the eternal punishment requires the balanced treatment the surah itself gives. The *khulud* of the deniers is the counterpart of the *khulud* of the believers: the abiding in either is the consequence of the orientation of the life. The ones of 3:86–88 are the worst class — the ones who believed, witnessed, received the proofs, and rejected — and the severity of the abiding corresponds to the severity of the wrong. The Qur'an also teaches in the same breath the openness of the repentance (3:89 immediately follows) — the mercy is open until the end, and the abiding is the fate of the ones who close it.

For the reader, the verse is the sober reminder of the gravity of the choice. The believer is taught: the choices of the life are not the choices of the moment but the orientations of the eternity — and the abiding is the consequence of the orientation. The verse is not presented as a threat to terrify but as the reality to comprehend: the punishment of the persistent denier is not lightened, not delayed, because the wrong is not a temporary lapse but the closure of the heart against the truth. At the same time, the verse's placement before 3:89 is the divine method: the severity of the warning is immediately followed by the openness of the mercy — the *tawbah*, the repentance, the path of the return, which is the believer's hope and the surah's invitation.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 89

Verse 89 says: *As for those who repent afterwards and mend their ways, then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.*

In simple terms, the verse opens the door that 3:88 closed: those who repent after that and reform will find Allah All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

The verse is the mercy clause of the verdict. Ibn Kathir's sequence: the ones who disbelieved after belief and persisted (86–88) are met with the curse and the abiding; and the ones who repent after that are met with the forgiveness and the mercy. The verse's *thumma* — then, afterwards — is the temporal marker of the mercy: the repentance after the wrong, the reform after the fall, the return after the departure.

"As for those who repent afterwards" — *illa alladhina tabu min ba'di dhalik* — except those who repent after that. The *tawbah* — the return — is the turn of the heart from the wrong to the right: the turning of the back to the *kufr* and the face to the *iman*; the repentance is not the mere sorrow of the discovery but the direction of the life's change. Ibn Kathir: the verse's exception — the ones who repent after the disbelief — are outside the verdict: the curse is lifted, the abiding is escaped, the punishment is replaced by the mercy. The verse's *illa* — except — is the loop of the divine mercy: the categorical verdict of 3:86–88 is not the last word; the exception is the return.

"and mend their ways" — *wa aslahu* — and they reform, set right. The *islah* — the reform — is the companion of the *tawbah*: the repentance must be followed by the reform of the deeds — the correction of the path, the restoration of the worship, the amendment of the wrong. Ibn Kathir's framing: the *tawbah* and the *islah* together are the condition of the acceptance (cf. 4:17, 4:146, 6:54 — the repentance and the reform as the pair). The verse's *wa aslahu* is not a redundancy; it is the criterion: the repentance of the tongue without the reform of the conduct is the repentance of the pretense.

"then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful" — *fa inna Allaha ghafurun rahim* — then surely Allah is Forgiving, Merciful. The verse's *fa inna* — then surely — is the guarantee: the forgiveness is not the uncertain hope but the divine promise for the repentant. The *ghufran* — the forgiveness — covers the wrong; the *rahmah* — the mercy — fills the return. The pair *ghafurun rahim* is the Qur'an's most frequent formula of the divine mercy after the repentance (2:199, 2:218, 4:100, 4:129, 24:5, 49:12).

The verse is the balance point of the section. The surah has spoken of the covenant (81), the rebellion (82), the exclusivity of the religion (83–85), the rejection of the acknowledged truth (86), the curse (87), and the abiding (88) — and here the door opens: the repentance after all of it. The sequence is the Qur'an's method of the *targheeb wa al-tarhib* — the promise and the warning: the severity of the consequence is matched by the openness of the return. Ibn Kathir: the one who repents after the disbelief, who reforms, finds the forgiveness — the mercy is not closed by the severity but opened by the return.

For the reader, the verse is the heart of the Qur'an's mercy in the passage. The believer is taught: the wrongs, even the gravest — the rejection of the acknowledged truth — are not beyond the return; the *tawbah* is the open door and the *islah* is the path through it. The verse is also the believer's guard against two errors: the despair of the wrong (no, the door is open) and the presumption of the wrong (no, the door requires the real return — the repentance and the reform). The verse's teaching is the perfection of the divine justice and mercy: the justice of the verdict is not the closure of the mercy, and the mercy of the return is not the nullification of the justice.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 90

Verse 90 says: *Indeed, those who disbelieve after having believed then increase in disbelief, their repentance will never be accepted. It is they who are astray.*

In simple terms, the verse warns of the worst class: those who disbelieve after believing and then keep increasing in disbelief — their repentance will never be accepted; they are the truly lost.

The verse is the second exceptional clause of the section, and it is the exception to the exception: 3:89 opened the mercy to the repentant; 3:90 closes it for the ones who disbelieve after belief and then increase in disbelief. Ibn Kathir's section: "Neither Repentance of the Disbeliever Upon Death, Nor His Ransoming Himself on the Day of Resurrection Shall Be Accepted." Al-Jalalayn on the occasion: "The following was revealed regarding the Jews — surely those who disbelieve in Jesus after they have believed in Moses and then increase in unbelief in Muhammad — their repentance shall not be accepted when they are drawing their last gasps of life or when they have died as disbelievers."

"those who disbelieve after having believed" — the first escalation: the belief and the departure — the ones who would come to the *kufr* after the *iman*. The verse's class is the reversers: the ones who had the faith and abandoned it. Al-Bazzar records the report of Ibn 'Abbas: "some people embraced Islam, reverted to disbelief, became Muslims again, then reverted from Islam" — the verse was revealed about them (the chain satisfactory). The verse's picture is the shuttling of the faith: the in-and-out, the on-and-off, the faith that is not settled and the disbelief that grows.

"then increase in disbelief" — *thumma izdadu kufran* — then they increased in disbelief. The *izdiyad* — the increase, the growth — is the second escalation: not merely the departure but the accumulation — the deepening of the *kufr*, the growth of the hostility, the addition of the wrong to the wrong. Maududi's note: "They were not content with just rejecting the call to the Truth but stood in vehement opposition and hostility to it and spared no efforts in obstructing people from following the way of God. They created doubts, spread misgivings, sowed the seeds of distrust and engaged in the worst conspiracies and machinations in order to frustrate the mission of the Prophet." The increase is the opposite of the *islam* — the growth of the rebellion.

"their repentance will never be accepted" — *lan tuqbala tawbatuhum* — never will their repentance be accepted. The *lan* — never — is the categorical denial, and the qualification is essential: the repentance that is not accepted is the repentance at the last breath, the repentance of the deathbed of the persistent denier. Ibn Kathir: the meaning is the repentance upon death of the ones who persisted — the parallel of 4:18: "of no effect is the repentance of those who continue to do evil deeds until death faces one of them." The denial is not the denial of the *tawbah* of the living but of the *tawbah* of the dying who have persisted: the repentance of the wrongdoer at the brink is not the return of the heart but the panic of the deadline — and the Qur'an's justice refuses the panic as the equivalent of the return.

"It is they who are astray" — *wa ula'ika humu al-dallun* — and those are the ones who are astray, the lost. The *dalal* — the straying, the loss of the way — is the verdict of identity: the ones who went wrong after the guidance, who increased in the wrong, who repented only in the panic — they are the *dallun*. Ibn Kathir: "to those who abandon the path of truth for the path of wickedness." The verse's final clause is the same form as 3:82's *fasiqun* — the verdict of the identity of the class.

The theological depth of the verse: the Qur'an does not here teach that the living apostate's repentance is unreachable — the *tawbah* of the living is always open (4:17, 39:53, 25:70) — but that the persistent intensification of the *kufr*, followed by the death in it, closes the repentance: the one who spends the life increasing in the rejection of the truth and arrives at the death in the rejection cannot be met by the acceptance of the last-moment repentance. Ibn Kathir's distinction: the verse's *lan tuqbala tawbatuhum* is the repentance "when drawing their last gasps of life or when they have died as disbelievers." The open door of 3:89 is for the repentant who returns; the closed door of 3:90 is for the one who never returns and dies in the growth of the rejection.

For the reader, the verse is the ultimate warning of the section and the ultimate contrast to 3:89. The two verses are the two paths of the human response: the repentance and the reform — the return that finds the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful; or the persistence and the growth — the rejection that arrives at the last breath and finds the acceptance closed. The believer is taught to remove the negotiation with the deadline: the *tawbah* is not the emergency of the last breath but the return of the life; the *islam* is not the label but the submission. And the verse's final verdict — *they are the ones who are astray* — is the identity of the wrong: not the ones who never knew the way, but the ones who knew it, departed, and grew in the departure — the *dallun* of the reversed guidance.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 91

Verse 91 says: *Indeed, if each of those who disbelieve then die as disbelievers were to offer a ransom of enough gold to fill the whole world, it would never be accepted from them. It is they who will suffer a painful punishment, and they will have no helpers.*

In simple terms, the verse states the ultimate futility: if those who disbelieve and die as disbelievers offered the whole world filled with gold as a ransom, it would not be accepted; they will face a painful punishment with no one to help them.

The verse is the culmination of the warning that began at 3:86. Ibn Kathir: "The implication of this Ayah is that the disbeliever shall never avoid the torment of Allah, even if he spent the earth's fill of gold, or if he ransoms himself with the earth's fill of gold — all of its mountains, hills, sand, dust, valleys, forests, land and sea." The verse's imagery is deliberately extreme: the whole earth filled with gold as a ransom — the measure of the wealth and the futility of the purchase.

"if each of those who disbelieve then die as disbelievers were to offer a ransom of enough gold to fill the whole world" — the condition is the death in the *kufr*: *man kafaru wa matu wa hum kuffar* — who disbelieve and die while they are disbelievers. The ransom — *mila'a al-ard dhaban* — the fill of the earth in gold — is the maximum of the world's value, offered against the punishment. Ibn Kathir's parallels: 2:123 — "nor shall compensation be accepted from him, nor shall intercession be of use to him"; 14:31 — "on which there will be neither mutual bargaining nor befriending"; 5:36 — "if they had all that is in the earth, and as much again therewith to ransom themselves thereby from the torment on the Day of Resurrection, it would never be accepted of them."

"it would never be accepted from them" — *falan yuqbala min ahadihim* — never will it be accepted from one of them. The *lan* — never — is the categorical: no amount, no ransom, no compensation. Ibn Kathir: "Those who die while disbelievers shall have no good deed ever accepted from them, even if they spent the earth's fill of gold in what was perceived to be an act of obedience." The verse's teaching is the exchange of the credit: the good deeds of the disbeliever are not credited as the acts of obedience because the direction of the life is not the obedience; and the ransom of the world cannot purchase the acceptance of a life directed away from the truth.

The hadith of 'Abdullah bin Jud'an, recorded by Ibn Kathir: the Prophet was asked about him — the generous host, the helper of the indebted, the feeder of the poor — will all that benefit him? He said: "No, for not even one day during his life did he pronounce, 'O my Lord! Forgive my sins on the Day of Judgment.'" The hadith is the sharp edge of the verse: the generosity without the faith is the generosity of the world, and the life without the acknowledgment of the Lord is the life without the credit. The verse does not deny the worth of human goodness; it locates the worth — the goodness is credited to the one who has the orientation of the faith, and the orientation of the life is the determinant of the account.

The hadith of Anas, recorded by Ibn Kathir: the man of Paradise is asked about his dwelling and wishes only to return and be killed in the way of Allah ten times, because of the honor of martyrdom; and the man of the Fire is asked if he would ransom himself with the earth's fill of gold, says yes, and is told: "You have lied. I asked you to do what is less and easier than that, but you did not do it" — and he is returned to the Fire. The verse's picture is the picture of the accounting: the offer of the world's gold is the reply of the moment; the refusal of the small and easy command was the reply of the life.

"It is they who will suffer a painful punishment, and they will have no helpers" — *la'ulahum 'adhabun alimun wa ma lahum min nasirin* — for them is a painful punishment and they have no helpers. The *nasir* — the helper, the supporter, the intercessor — is the last hope of the punished: the one who might intervene, the friend who might interpose. The verse denies it: no helpers. Ibn Kathir: "they shall not have anyone who will save them from the torment of Allah or rescue them from His painful punishment."

For the reader, the verse is the deepest warning of the section on the futility of the wrong and the irreversibility of the death. The believer is taught: the life is the opportunity, and the death is the closure; the wealth, the generosity, the good deeds of the disbeliever cannot be used to ransom the life directed away from the truth, because the acceptance is not the purchase of a moment but the credit of a direction. The verse is not a dismissal of the human goodness outside the faith but the location of its worth: the goodness is the fruit of the faith, and the fruit cannot be credited to the tree that is not of the faith. And the verse's final clause — *no helpers* — teaches the believer the ultimate dependence: not on the wealth, not on the friends, not on the intercessors — but on the life of the submission, which is the only thing that cannot be ransomed by anything else.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 92

Verse 92 says: *You will never achieve righteousness until you donate some of what you cherish. And whatever you give is certainly well known to Allah.*

In simple terms, the verse sets the standard of true righteousness: you will never attain it until you give in charity from what you love most. And whatever you give, Allah is fully aware of it.

The verse turns from the warning to the positive command and is the pivot of the section: after the futility of the ransom (91), the Qur'an gives the path of the actual achievement — the *birr* — the righteousness, and the condition of it: the giving of what is cherished. Ibn Kathir titles the passage "Al-Birr is Spending from the Best of One's Wealth." Waki' reported that 'Amr bin Maymun said: "By no means shall you attain Al-Birr" is in reference to attaining Paradise.

"You will never achieve righteousness" — *lan tanalu al-birr* — you will never attain the *birr*. The *lan* — never — is the categorical: the attainment of the *birr* — the righteousness, the goodness, the piety of the complete kind — is not the natural achievement of the life. The verse's *lan* is not the negating of the possibility but the hardening of the condition: the *birr* is not attainable without the giving, and the giving is not attainable without the love. The *birr* of the verse is the righteousness of the whole: the religion's deepest estate — the virtue that encompasses the faith, the goodness, and the devotion.

"until you donate some of what you cherish" — *hatta tunfiqu mimma tuhibbun* — until you spend of what you love, of what you cherish. The word *nifaq* — the spending, the giving, the expenditure — is the Qur'an's term for the *infiq* in the way of Allah; and the qualifier *mimma tuhibbun* — of what you love — is the standard: not the surplus, not the spare, but the cherished. Ibn Kathir: the verse is parallel to 2:177 — "and gives his wealth, in spite of love for it" — and 76:8 — "and they give food, in spite of their love for it." The giving of the *tuhibbun* is the giving of the heart: the expenditure of the thing one loves for the cause of the One who loves.

The hadith of Abu Talhah, recorded by Ibn Kathir in the Two Sahihs: Abu Talhah had more property than any other among the Ansar in al-Madinah, and the most beloved of his property to him was the garden of Bayruha', in front of the Messenger's mosque; the Messenger would go and drink of its fresh water. When the verse was revealed, Abu Talhah said: "O Allah's Messenger! Allah says, 'By no means shall you attain Al-Birr unless you spend of that which you love.' No doubt, Bayruha' garden is the most beloved of all my property to me. So I want to give it in charity in Allah's cause, and I expect its reward and compensation from Allah." The Messenger said: "Well-done! It is profitable property, it is profitable property. I have heard what you have said, and I think it would be proper if you gave it to your kith and kin." Abu Talhah distributed the garden among his relatives and cousins. The hadith is the perfect model of the verse: the most beloved property, given to Allah's cause, distributed to the kin — the spending of the love and the direction to the nearest of the need.

The hadith of 'Umar: "O Messenger of Allah! I never gained possession of a piece of property more precious to me than my share in Khaybar. Therefore, what do you command me to do with it?" The Prophet said: "Retain the land to give its fruits in Allah's cause" — the founding statement of the *waqf*: the endowment, the retention of the origin and the dedication of the produce. The two hadiths are the two forms of the *nifaq* of the beloved: the outright gift and the permanent endowment — and both are the spending of the *tuhibbun*.

Maududi's note is the deepest commentary: the purpose of the verse is to remove the misconception of the Jews concerning "righteousness." Their notion was the outward formal conformity to an elaborate legal code, accumulated by the casuistry and hair-splitting legalism of their jurists; they evaluated all daily actions, especially the trivial, in terms of conformity to that code. Beneath the veneer of formal piety lay narrow-mindedness, greed, covetousness, meanness, concealment of the truth, and readiness to barter with it. The real spirit of righteousness consists in the love of God — a love which makes man value the good pleasure of God above all worldly acquisitions. If the love of anything seizes a man's mind to such an extent that he is unable to sacrifice it for the sake of the love of God, then that thing has virtually become an idol, and until he smashes it the door to righteousness will remain closed to him. Formal legalism is no more than glossy paint over a piece of hollow, worm-eaten wood: it may deceive human beings by the lustre of the paint, but not God.

"And whatever you give is certainly well known to Allah" — *wa ma tunfiqu min shay'in fa inna Allaha bihi 'alim* — and whatever you spend of anything, then surely Allah is Knowing of it. The verse's close is the grace of the accounting: the spending is not lost, not overlooked, not unregistered — the All-Knowing knows it. Ibn Kathir: "Allah knows it well." The verse pairs the severity of the condition (the giving of the beloved) with the sweetness of the register (the knowledge of the All-Knowing): the sacrifice is not the anonymity of the loss but the deposit of the divine knowledge.

For the reader, the verse is one of the most penetrating tests of the faith. The believer is taught: the *birr* is not the formal conformity but the love that sacrifices; the wealth is the test because the wealth is the love; and the giving of what is cherished is the breaking of the idol of the possession. The verse's standard is the interiority: the one who gives the surplus is generous but not necessarily righteous; the one who gives the cherished is righteous because he has broken the attachment. The verse is also the believer's guard against the legalism: the formal piety without the sacrifice is the paint over the worm-eaten wood; and the verse's promise — Allah knows it — is the answer to the secrecy and the despair of the giving.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 93

Verse 93 says: *All food was lawful for the children of Israel, except what Israel made unlawful for himself before the Torah was revealed. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Bring the Torah and read it, if your claims are true.”*

In simple terms, the verse answers the Jewish objection about food: all food was lawful for the Children of Israel except what Israel (Ya'qub) made unlawful for himself before the Torah — and the challenge: bring the Torah and read it, if your claims are true.

The verse is the rebuttal of the Jewish objections to the Prophet's legislation. Ibn Kathir: "The Questions that the Jews Asked Our Prophet" — the section opens with the Jews' challenge, and the verse answers the objection of the food prohibitions. Al-Jalalayn records the occasion: "When the Jews said to the Prophet, 'You claim that you follow the creed of Abraham, but Abraham did not eat camel's meat nor drink its milk,' the following was revealed."

"All food was lawful for the children of Israel" — *kullu al-ta'ami kana hillan li bani Isra'il* — all food was lawful for the Children of Israel. The verse's *kull* — all — is the categorical: the food was lawful, the dietary law had not been revealed. Ibn Kathir: "before the Tawrah was revealed, all types of foods were allowed, except what Isra'il prohibited for himself." The verse's argument: the restriction the Jews attributed to the primordial law was in fact the personal vow of the patriarch — the self-prohibition, not the divine prohibition.

"except what Israel made unlawful for himself" — *illa ma harrama Isra'ilu 'ala nafsihi* — except what Isra'il made unlawful upon himself. The *Isra'il* here is Ya'qub: the father of the tribes, the patriarch of the Children of Israel. Al-Jalalayn: "Israel (Jacob) forbade for himself namely camels when he was afflicted with sciatica ('irq al-nasa); he made a vow that if he were cured he would not eat of it again, and so it was forbidden to him." The prohibition was the vow of the personal devotion: the self-denial of the beloved food for the sake of Allah, in the hope of the cure. Ibn Kathir adds the two objectives of the verse's mention: first, he forbade himself the most delightful things for Allah's sake — a practice allowed in his period of law, and thus suited to follow 3:92 (spend of what you love); second, the proof that abrogation already occurred within the Tawrah itself.

"before the Torah was revealed" — *min qabli an tunazzala al-Tawrah* — before the Tawrah was sent down. The chronological point is the crux: the *tahrim* — the prohibition — preceded the Tawrah, and the Torah came after; the Torah's prohibitions were the legislation of the later law, not the primordial state. Ibn Kathir's examples of the abrogation within the Tawrah: when Nuh departed from the ark, Allah allowed him to eat the meat of all types of animals; afterwards Isra'il forbade the meat and milk of camels, and his children imitated the practice; the Tawrah later prohibited this type of food and added more prohibitions. Allah allowed Adam to marry his daughters to his sons, later forbidden. The law of Ibrahim allowed the man to take female servants as companions along with his wife, as Ibrahim did with Hajar while married to Sarah — later prohibited in the Tawrah. It was previously allowed to take two sisters as wives at the same time, as Ya'qub did — later prohibited. "All these examples are in the Tawrah and constitute a Naskh (abrogation) of the Law. Therefore, let the Jews consider what Allah legislated for 'Isa and if such legislation falls under the category of abrogation or not."

"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'Bring the Torah and read it, if your claims are true'" — *qul fa'tu bil-Tawrati fatluha in kuntum sadiqin* — say, bring the Torah and recite it, if you are truthful. The challenge is the appeal to the source: the Jews claimed the prohibition as the divine law; the challenge is the production of the text — the Torah itself, to be read, to be examined, to be tested against the claim. Al-Jalalayn: "they were stupefied and did not bring it." The source that would have settled the dispute was the source they would not produce — because the Torah did not support the claim of the primordial prohibition. Ibn Kathir: "the Tawrah affirms what we are stating here."

Ibn Kathir's hadith of the four questions (Imam Ahmad): a group of Jews came to the Prophet and said, "Talk to us about some things we will ask you and which only a Prophet would know." He asked for their pledge, similar to the pledge Ya'qub took from his children, that if he told them the truth they would follow him in Islam. They agreed and asked the four matters: the food Isra'il prohibited; the sexual discharge of man and woman and its role in the sex of the offspring; the condition of the unlettered Prophet during sleep; and his Wali among the angels. The Prophet answered: Isra'il became very ill; when the illness was prolonged he vowed that if Allah cured him he would prohibit the best types of drink and food — the best food to him was camel meat and the best drink camel milk. The man's discharge is thick and white, the woman's yellow and thin; if either dominates, the offspring takes its sex and resemblance by Allah's leave. The eyes of the unlettered Prophet sleep, but his heart does not sleep. And his Wali among the angels is Jibril — Allah never sent a Prophet but Jibril was his Wali. They said, "We then shun you. Had you a Wali other than Jibril, we would have followed you" — and Allah revealed 2:97: "Say: Whoever is an enemy to Jibril…"

Maududi's notes: the Jewish rabbis, finding no grounds for criticizing the fundamentals, raised objections about the details of the religious law — the first objection was that the Prophet had declared lawful a number of things reckoned unlawful since the time of the ancient prophets. If "Israel" means the Children of Israel, the verse means that before the Torah they treated things as prohibited by custom and usage alone; if "Israel" is Ya'qub, the more common reading, he avoided certain foods due to temperamental dislike or an ailment, and his descendants wrongly understood it as religiously prohibited. The camel and rabbit prohibition was not part of the original Torah but an interpolation by Jewish doctors.

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's model of the argument from the source. The believer is taught: the claim must be tested against the evidence — the Torah was the source the Jews claimed, and the Torah was the source that refuted them. The verse is also the teaching on the personal vow and the divine law: the self-denial of the patriarch — the giving up of the beloved for Allah — is the *birr* of 3:92 in the prophetic line, and the self-denial became the customary-law of the descendants who mistook the vow for the command. The believer is taught the distinction: the personal discipline is not the legislation of the community; the divine law is not the accumulation of the custom; and the reading of the source is the test of the claim — the *fatluha* of the challenge.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 94

Verse 94 says: *Then whoever still fabricates lies about Allah, they will be the ˹true˺ wrongdoers.*

In simple terms, the verse warns: after this clear evidence, whoever still invents falsehood against Allah will be the outright wrongdoers.

The verse is the verdict on the ones who persist in the falsehood after the challenge of 3:93. Ibn Kathir: "'Then after that, whosoever shall invent a lie against Allah, then these it is that are the wrongdoers' — in reference to those who lie about Allah and claim that He made the Sabbath and the Tawrah eternal. They are those who claim that Allah did not send another Prophet calling to Allah with the proofs and evidences, although evidence indicates that abrogation, as we have described, occurred before in the Tawrah."

"Then whoever still fabricates lies about Allah" — *faman iftara 'ala Allahi al-kadhiba min ba'di dhalik* — then whoever forges the lie against Allah after that. The *ifitra'* — the forging, the invention, the fabrication — is the strongest word for the falsehood: not the error, not the mistake, but the manufacture of the lie. Ibn Kathir: the lie is the claim that the Torah and the Sabbath are eternal, that no further Prophet would come, that the abrogation never occurred. The verse's *min ba'di dhalik* — after that — is the key: after the challenge of the Torah and its reading, after the evidence of the abrogation within the Torah itself, the persistence in the claim is no longer the error of the obscure but the fabrication of the knowing.

"they will be the ˹true˺ wrongdoers" — *fa ula'ika humu al-zalimun* — then those are the wrongdoers. The *zulm* — the wrong, the injustice, the transgression — is the verdict of the identity: the wrongdoers are the ones who place the falsehood in the place of the truth. Ibn Kathir's conclusion: the fabrication of the lie after the evidence is the *zulm* — the wrong against Allah (the false attribution), against the truth (the concealment), against the people (the misguidance).

The verse's position in the argument: 3:93 challenged the Jews to produce the Torah; the Torah, read properly, contains the evidence of the abrogation — the changes in the prohibition, the chronology of the law. The ones who persist in the claim of the eternal law are fabricating against Allah: they know the source, they know the evidence, and they claim the opposite. The *zulm* of the verse is the knowing wrong — the same *zulm* of 3:86's "Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people."

For the reader, the verse is the warning against the fabrication in the name of the religion. The believer is taught: the falsehood about Allah is not only the direct lie — the invented saying — but also the claim of the permanence of what Allah changed, the denial of the abrogation of what He abrogated, the assertion of the eternality of the human interpretation over the divine command. The verse's standard is the evidence: the claim is tested by the source, and the claim that survives the evidence only as the falsehood is the fabrication — the *ifitra'* — and the fabricator is the wrongdoer. And the verse at the same time teaches the reader the humility of the religious claims: the law of Allah has a history — the revelation, the development, the abrogation — and the claim of the static eternal law may be the very *ifitra'* the verse condemns.

The verse's connection with the surah's larger argument is precise. The whole dispute with the Jews over the abrogation of the law is the dispute over whether Allah can change His legislation: whether the final law of Muhammad could supersede the law of the Tawrah. The Jews' position — the eternal Sabbath, the permanent Tawrah, no further prophet — was, in Ibn Kathir's analysis, the *ifitra'* of the verse: the claim that Allah's legislation was static, that He had no further messages, no final Messenger. The verse's verdict identifies the position precisely: it was not the conservative reverence for the old law but the fabrication of a claim Allah never made — the claim that the divine will was exhausted in the past, and that the future belonged to no one.

The classical treatment also notes the *zulm* — the wrongness — of the fabricators is the wrong of the knowers: the ones who had the evidence of the abrogation within their own Torah, who saw the changes in the law from Adam to Noah to Abraham to Jacob to Moses, and who then asserted the eternity of the final state. The *zulm* is not the error of the unknowing but the wrong of the concealing — the same wrong of 3:70-71, the same *kitman* of the truth they knew. The verse thus completes the surah's portrait of the People of the Book at this point: they conceal the truth (71), they refuse the common word (64), they distort the tongue (78), and they fabricate the claim of the eternal law (94) — and the verdict on the fabricators is the wrongdoers.

For the modern reader, the verse is the standard for evaluating the religious claims of every generation: the claim that Allah's message is complete in the past and no further guidance can come, the claim that the law is static and cannot be superseded, the claim of the infallibility of the human understanding of the eternal — all of these are the *ifitra'* whenever they are asserted against the clear evidence of the revelation's own development. The believer is taught the principle: the truth is what Allah says, not what the tradition asserts against it; the evidence of the source is the test; and the persistence in the claim after the evidence is the fabrication of the knowing — the wrongdoers.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 95

Verse 95 says: *Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Allah has declared the truth. So follow the Way of Abraham, the upright—who was not a polytheist.”*

In simple terms, the verse gives the answer to the whole dispute: Allah has spoken the truth; so follow the religion of Abraham, the upright one, who was not of the polytheists.

The verse is the positive seal of the argument that began at 3:93. Ibn Kathir: "'Say: Allah has spoken the truth' — meaning, O Muhammad, say that Allah has said the truth in what He conveyed and legislated in the Qur'an — 'follow the religion of Ibrahim the Hanif, and he was not of the idolators' — therefore, follow the religion of Ibrahim that Allah legislated in the Qur'an. Indeed, this is the truth, there is no doubt in it, and the perfect way, and no Prophet has brought a more complete, clear, plain and perfect way than he did."

"Allah has declared the truth" — *qul sadaqa Allahu* — say, Allah has told the truth. The declaration is the believer's answer to the disputed claims: the truth is the truth of Allah's saying, not the truth of the community's claim. Ibn Kathir: the *sidq* of the verse — the truthfulness — is the declaration that Allah has spoken truly in the Qur'an: the legislation, the narrative, the correction of the Jewish claims. The verse's *qul* — say — is the command of the declaration: the Prophet is to say it, and the believers after him are to say it: the truth is Allah's, and the Qur'an is the declaration of it.

"So follow the Way of Abraham, the upright" — *fattabi'u millata Ibrahima hanifan* — then follow the creed of Ibrahim, a hanif. The commandment is the direction: the *millah* — the creed, the way of religion — of Ibrahim. The *millah* is not the label but the way: the submission of the patriarch, the worship of the One, the *hanifiyyah* — the uprightness. Ibn Kathir's parallels: 6:161 — "Say: Truly, my Lord has guided me to a straight path, a right religion, the religion of Ibrahim, the Hanif, and he was not of the idolators"; 16:123 — "Then, We have sent the revelation to you: Follow the religion of Ibrahim, the Hanif, and he was not of the idolaters." The way of Abraham is the way of the Qur'an: the worship of Allah alone, the rejection of the partners, the submission of the whole.

"who was not a polytheist" — *wa ma kana mina al-mushrikin* — and he was not of the polytheists. The negative completes the positive: the *hanif* is the one who turns from the *shirk*; the *millah* of Abraham is the exclusion of the partners. Ibn Kathir: "shunning Shirk and living in Iman" — the *hanifiyyah* is the turning from the falsehood to the truth, and Abraham's way is the way of the two movements: the abandonment and the submission.

The verse's theological function in the section: the Jews claimed Abraham, the Christians claimed Abraham (3:65–68), and now the believers are commanded to follow the way of Abraham — not as the sectarian annexation but as the religion of the submission. Ibn Kathir: the *millah* of Ibrahim that Allah legislated in the Qur'an — the perfect way, the most complete, clear, plain, and perfect. The verse's *fattabi'u* — then follow — is the imperative of the following: not the claim of the descent but the walk of the way; the *hanif* is the one who follows the path.

For the reader, the verse is the culmination of the surah's treatment of Abraham and the declaration of the religion of the *hanifiyyah*. The believer is taught: the truth is the declaration of Allah, and the way of the truth is the way of Abraham — the uprightness, the turning from the partners, the submission to the One. The verse's command is not the command to the ancestor-worship or the sectarian claim; it is the command to the following: the *millah* of Abraham is the way of the Qur'an, and the believer follows it in the worship of Allah alone, in the rejection of the associates, in the uprightness of the life. And the verse's declaration — *Allah has spoken the truth* — is the believer's creed of the whole Qur'an: the truth is not the preference, not the tradition, not the community — the truth is the saying of Allah, and the way is the way of the upright one.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 96

Verse 96 says: *Surely the first House ˹of worship˺ established for humanity is the one at Bakkah—a blessed sanctuary and a guide for ˹all˺ people.*

In simple terms, the verse states that the first House of worship established for people is the one at Bakkah — a blessed place and a source of guidance for all people.

The verse answers the second Jewish objection: the change of the qibla from Jerusalem to the Ka'bah. Al-Jalalayn records the occasion: "When they said, 'Our direction of prayer (qibla) came before yours,' the following was revealed." The answer is the priority of the Ka'bah: not the priority of the claim but the priority of the history — the first House of worship established for humanity was at Bakkah. Ibn Kathir: "Allah said, 'Verily, the first House appointed for mankind' — for all people, for their acts of worship and religious rituals. They go around the House in Tawaf, pray in its vicinity and remain in its area in I'tikaf. 'was that at Bakkah' — meaning, the Ka'bah that was built by Ibrahim Al-Khalil, whose religion the Jews and Christians claim they follow. However, they do not perform Hajj to the house that Ibrahim built by Allah's command, and to which he invited the people to perform Hajj."

"the first House ˹of worship˺ established for humanity" — *inna awwala baytin wudi'a lil-nas* — indeed the first house placed for the people. The verse's *awwal* — first — is the claim of the priority: the first house of the worship of the One God. Al-Jalalayn's tradition: "it was built by the angels before the creation of Adam, and after it the Aqsa in Jerusalem was built, a period of forty years separating them, as reported in the hadith of the Two Sahihs" — and in the hadith: "The first thing to appear on the surface of the water at the creation of the heavens and the earth was a white foam underneath which the earth was unrolled." The verse's *bayt* — house — is the first establishment of the worship, the house of the tawhid in the earth.

"is the one at Bakkah" — *lalladhi bi Bakkata* — is that at Bakkah. Bakkah is one of the names of Makkah. Ibn Kathir: "Bakkah means 'it brings Buka' (crying, weeping) to the tyrants and arrogant — meaning they cry and become humble in its vicinity. It was also said that Makkah was called Bakkah because people do Buka next to it, meaning they gather around it. There are many names for Makkah, such as Bakkah, Al-Bayt Al-'Atiq (the Ancient House), Al-Bayt Al-Haram (the Sacred House), Al-Balad Al-Amin (the City of Safety) and Al-Ma'mun (Security). Makkah's names include Umm Rahm (Mother of Mercy), Umm Al-Qura (Mother of the Towns), Salah, as well as others." Al-Jalalayn: "Bakka, a variant of Makka, so called because it 'crushes' (tabukku) the necks of tyrants."

"a blessed sanctuary and a guide for ˹all˺ people" — *mubarakan wa hudan lil-'alamin* — blessed and a guidance for the worlds. The *barakah* — the blessing — is the presence of the divine grace at the house; the *huda* — the guidance — is the orientation of the worship: the qibla of the believers, the direction of the *salat*, the center of the pilgrimage. Ibn Kathir cites the hadith of Abu Dharr (Imam Ahmad, the Two Sahihs): "Which Masjid was the first to be built on the surface of the earth?" — "Al-Masjid Al-Haram (in Makkah)." — "Which was built next?" — "Al-Masjid Al-Aqsa (in Jerusalem)." — "What was the period of time between building the two?" — "Forty years." — "Wherever you may be, and the prayer becomes due, perform the prayer there, for the whole earth was made a Masjid."

Maududi's note: the second objection was the change of the qibla — answered in Surah 2 (142 ff.). The Bible itself testifies that Jerusalem was built by Solomon more than four and a half centuries after Moses (1 Kings 6:1), and that it was during his time that the worshippers of the One God began to pray towards it (1 Kings 8:29–30). It is established by traditions from numerous sources, undisputed throughout Arabia, that the Ka'bah was constructed by Abraham, who lived some eight or nine centuries before Moses. The Ka'bah was older than the Temple of Jerusalem beyond dispute.

The theological and historical point of the verse: the qibla is not the arbitrary choice of the new religion but the return to the origin. The first house of the worship of the One, the house of Adam's descendants, the house of Abraham and Ishmael — the *bayt* of the primordial monotheism — is the direction of the believers of the final revelation. The verse's answer to the Jews: your house — Jerusalem — was built centuries after the Ka'bah; the priority is ours, and the guidance of the house is the guidance of all the worlds — the *hudan lil-'alamin*.

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's statement of the cosmic and historical centrality of the Ka'bah. The believer is taught: the direction of the prayer is the direction of the history of the worship; the first house is the house of the tawhid; the qibla is the orientation of the one direction — the worship of Allah alone, from the first house to the last. The verse's *mubarakan* — blessed — is the believer's sense of the sanctity: the house is not a monument of the past but the living center of the worship — the *tawaf*, the *salat*, the *i'tikaf* of every generation. And the verse's *hudan lil-'alamin* — guidance for all people — is the universality of the house: not the sanctuary of a tribe but the first house of humanity, the qibla of the worlds.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 97

Verse 97 says: *In it are clear signs and the standing-place of Abraham. Whoever enters it should be safe. Pilgrimage to this House is an obligation by Allah upon whoever is able among the people. And whoever disbelieves, then surely Allah is not in need of ˹any of His˺ creation.*

In simple terms, the verse details the marks of the House: it contains clear signs, including the standing-place of Abraham; whoever enters is safe; Hajj to it is a duty owed to Allah by whoever is able; and whoever refuses is in want of nothing from Allah — He needs nothing.

The verse completes the account of the Ka'bah with the signs, the security, the obligation, and the divine independence. Ibn Kathir's treatment covers the four elements in sequence: the visible signs (the Maqam), the sanctuary's security, the obligation of the Hajj, and the kufr of the denier.

"In it are clear signs" — *fihi ayatun bayyinat* — in it are manifest signs. Ibn Kathir: "clear signs that Ibrahim built the Ka'bah and that Allah has honored and blessed it." Al-'Awfi reports Ibn 'Abbas: "Such as the Maqam and Al-Mash'ar Al-Haram." Mujahid: "The impression of Ibrahim's feet remains on the Maqam as a clear sign" — reported similarly by 'Umar bin 'Abdul-'Aziz, Al-Hasan, Qatadah, As-Suddi, Muqatil bin Hayyan, and others. The signs of the House are the visible proofs of the divine history: the sanctuary of the primordial worship, the traces of the patriarch, the place of the *tawaf* — the *ayat* for the believers.

"and the standing-place of Abraham" — *maqamu Ibrahima* — the station of Ibrahim. Ibn Kathir: "When the building (the Ka'bah) was raised, Ibrahim stood on the Maqam so that he could raise the walls higher, while his son Isma'il was handing the stones to him. We should mention that the Maqam used to be situated right next to the House. Later, and during his reign, 'Umar bin Al-Khattab moved the Maqam farther to the east, so that those who go around the House in Tawaf are able to perform it easily, without disturbing those who pray next to the Maqam after finishing their Tawaf. Allah commanded us to pray next to the Maqam: 'And take you (people) the Maqam (station) of Ibrahim as a place of prayer' (2:125)." The *maqam* is the visible trace of the prophet's act: the stone of the standing, the imprint of the feet, the station of the prayer.

"Whoever enters it should be safe" — *man dakhalahu kana aminan* — whoever enters it is secure. Ibn Kathir: "the Haram of Makkah is a safe refuge for those in a state of fear. There in its vicinity, they will be safe, just as was the case during the time of Jahiliyyah." Al-Hasan Al-Basri: "A man would commit murder, then wear a piece of wool around his neck and enter the Haram. And even when the son of the murdered person would meet him, he would not make a move against him, until he left the sanctuary." The parallels: 29:67 — "Have they not seen that We have made (Makkah) a secure sanctuary, while men are being snatched away from all around them?"; 106:3–4 — "So let them worship the Lord of this House, Who has fed them against hunger and has made them safe from fear." The sanctuary is the divine protection: the *aman* of the house is the sign of the protection of Allah.

The prohibitions of the Haram, per Ibn Kathir: it is not allowed to hunt in the Haram, drive game out of its den, cut the trees, or pick the grass — as the hadiths testify. The Two Sahihs record the hadith of the conquest: "Beware! Allah made this town (Makkah) a sanctuary when He created the heavens and earth, and it is sacred by Allah's decree until the Day of Resurrection. Fighting in Makkah was not permitted for anyone before me, and it was made legal for me for only a few hours or so on that day. It is not allowed to uproot its thorny shrubs, hunt its game, pick up its lost objects except by announcing it, or to uproot its trees" — with the exception of lemongrass. Abu Shurayh's hadith: "Allah, not the people, made Makkah a sanctuary. Therefore, anybody who has belief in Allah and the Last Day should neither shed blood in it nor cut down its trees… Allah allowed me only for a few hours on that day, and today its sanctity is as valid as it was before." Jabir: "None of you is allowed to carry a weapon in Makkah" (Muslim). And the hadith of 'Abdullah bin 'Adi: "By Allah! You are the best of Allah's land and the most beloved land to Allah. Had it not been for the fact that I was driven out of you, I would not have left you" (Ahmad; at-Tirmidhi hasan sahih).

"Pilgrimage to this House is an obligation by Allah upon whoever is able among the people" — *wa lillahi 'ala al-nasi hijju al-bayti mani istata'a ilayhi sabila* — and Hajj to the House is a duty upon the people for Allah, whoever is able to find a way. The verse establishes the obligation of the Hajj. Ibn Kathir: "This Ayah established the obligation of performing Hajj. There are many hadiths that mention it as one of the pillars and fundamentals of Islam, and this is agreed upon by the Muslims. According to texts and the consensus of the scholars, it is only obligatory for the adult Muslim to perform it once during his lifetime." The hadith of Abu Hurayrah: "O people! Hajj has been enjoined on you, therefore, perform Hajj." A man asked, "Is it every year, O Allah's Messenger?" The Prophet remained silent until the man repeated three times, then said: "Had I said yes, it would have become an obligation and you would not have been able to fulfill it. Leave me as I leave you, those before you were destroyed because of their many questions and disputing with their Prophets. If I command you with something, perform it as much as you can. If I forbid something for you, then refrain from it" (Muslim). The meaning of the ability: Ibn 'Umar — "Having provision and a means of transportation"; Al-Hakim's Anas — "Having sufficient provision and a means of transportation"; Ibn 'Abbas — "Whoever intends to perform Hajj, let him rush to perform it" (Ahmad; Abu Dawud).

"And whoever disbelieves, then surely Allah is not in need of ˹any of His˺ creation" — *wa man kafara fa inna Allaha ghaniyyun 'an al-'alamin* — and whoever disbelieves, then indeed Allah is independent of the worlds. Ibn Kathir: "Ibn 'Abbas, Mujahid and several others commented on this Ayah: 'Whoever denies the necessity of Hajj becomes a disbeliever, and Allah is far richer than to need him.'" Al-Isma'ili records 'Umar: "Whoever can afford Hajj but did not perform it, there is no difference in his case if he dies while a Jew or Christian" (authentic chain). The verse's *ghaniyy* — the rich, the self-sufficient, the independent — is the divine attribute answering the refusal: the one who denies the obligation denies the right of the One who needs nothing — and the *ghina* of Allah is the measure of the loss of the denier.

For the reader, the verse is the foundation of the Hajj teaching and the doctrine of the sanctuary. The believer is taught: the House carries the signs of the divine history — the *maqam* of the patriarch, the security of the sanctuary, the obligation of the pilgrimage; the Hajj is the once-in-a-lifetime duty of the capable, and the denial of it is the *kufr* of the denial of the right of Allah. The verse's *ghaniyy* — Allah is in need of nothing — is the believer's deepest orientation: the worship is not the service of a needy deity but the response to the self-sufficient One; the obligation is the honor of the believer, not the need of the Lord.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 98

Verse 98 says: *Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O People of the Book! Why do you deny the revelations of Allah, when Allah is a Witness to what you do?”*

In simple terms, the verse challenges the People of the Book again: why do you reject Allah's revelations while Allah Himself witnesses everything you do?

The verse returns to the address of the People of the Book and is the first of the two questions of the closing appeal. Ibn Kathir's section on 3:98–100 frames them within the warning "Warning Muslims Against Imitating People of the Scriptures" — but the direct address at 3:98 is the question to the People of the Book themselves. The question is the same challenge of 3:70 — *lima takfuruna bi ayat Allah* — why do you disbelieve in the Ayat of Allah — now with the added witness: *wa Allahu shahidun 'ala ma ta'malun* — while Allah is a Witness to what you do.

"O People of the Book! Why do you deny the revelations of Allah" — *ya ahl al-kitabi lima takfuruna bi ayat Allah* — O People of the Book, why do you disbelieve in the revelations of Allah. The *ayat* — the signs, the verses, the revelations — are the ones they had confirmed: the signs of the earlier prophets, the descriptions of the final Messenger in their Books, the clear proofs of the Qur'an. The *kufr* of the verse is the rejection of the known: the *ayat* of Allah were not foreign to the People of the Book; they were the content of their own witness, the inheritance of their own tradition.

"when Allah is a Witness to what you do" — *wa Allahu shahidun 'ala ma ta'malun* — and Allah is a Witness over what you do. The witness of Allah is the third register of the verse: the deeds are not hidden, the denials are not private, the rejections are not unobserved — the Witness is present. Ibn Kathir's framing: the *shahadah* of Allah — the witnessing — is the guarantee of the account: the one who denies the Ayat while Allah witnesses the denial is the one whose denial is recorded. The verse's *shahid* is the counterpart of 3:81's *shahidin* — the witness: Allah witnessed the covenant of the prophets; Allah witnesses the deeds of the People of the Book; and the witness is the same — the divine attention to the human choice.

The verse's place: 3:98–99 are the closing appeal to the People of the Book before the final warning to the believers (3:100–101). The two questions are the surah's last word to the community of the earlier revelations: why do you reject (98)? why do you obstruct (99)? Both are questions of the known truth — the *kufr* and the *sadd* — and both are under the divine witness.

For the reader, the verse is the reminder of the presence of the witness. The believer is taught: the deeds are done before the Witness; the denial of the truth is not the private act of the conscience but the recorded act before the divine attention. The verse's *shahid* — the Witness — is the believer's security and the denier's record: the one who acts in the knowledge of the witness acts in the truth; the one who acts in the denial of the witness acts in the denial of the record's reality. And the verse is the apologist's method in miniature: the question of the inconsistency — you who claim the Book, why do you reject its Ayat? — the question that exposes the contradiction of the witness and the denial.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 99

Verse 99 says: *Say, “O People of the Book! Why do you turn the believers away from the Way of Allah—striving to make it ˹appear˺ crooked, while you are witnesses ˹to its truth˺? And Allah is never unaware of what you do.”*

In simple terms, the verse asks the second question: why do you block the believers from Allah's way, trying to make it look crooked — while you themselves are witnesses to its truth? Allah is never unaware of what you do.

The verse escalates from the denial of 3:98 to the obstruction. Ibn Kathir: the People of the Book are accused of turning the believers away from the Way of Allah — the *sadd* — the blocking, the barring — and of trying to make the Way appear crooked — the *bugh* of the deviation — while they were the very witnesses of the truth. The accusation combines the two wrongs: the rejection of the truth (98) and the obstruction of the others from it (99).

"why do you turn the believers away from the Way of Allah" — *lima tasudduna 'an sabili Allahi man amana* — why do you bar from the way of Allah those who believe. The *sadd* — the barring, the blocking, the turning away — is the act of the obstruction: the prevention of the believers from the Way, the hindrance of the seekers. Ibn Kathir's parallels: the same charge in 7:86, 11:19, 16:88, 22:25 — the *sadd* of the way of Allah and the *zulm* of the barrers. The act is the obverse of the *da'wah*: the one who cannot convince the believers attempts to divert them.

"striving to make it ˹appear˺ crooked" — *tabghunaha 'iwajan* — seeking to make it crooked. The *'iwaj* — the crookedness, the deviation — is the distortion of the straight path: the attempt to make the *sirat al-mustaqim* appear as the path of the error, the religion of the truth as the path of the crooked. The People of the Book did not merely reject; they sought to render the path crooked in the eyes of others — the *bugh* of the distortion. Ibn Kathir: the description of the Prophet in their Books was hidden; the sign of his coming was concealed; and the straight path was presented as the crooked.

"while you are witnesses ˹to its truth˺" — *wa antum shuhada'* — while you are witnesses. The clause is the crux: the witnesses of the truth are the ones who know the straightness of the path; and the witnesses who know the truth are the ones who seek to make it appear crooked — the contradiction of the knowledge and the act. Ibn Kathir: the *shuhada'* — the witnesses — are the ones who testified to the truth of the path, who found the description of the final Messenger in their Books, who acknowledged the way in their hearts, and who then obstructed it. The verse's accusation is the same shape as 3:70-71: the rejection and the concealment of the known truth.

"And Allah is never unaware of what you do" — *wa ma Allahu bighafilin 'amma ta'malun* — and Allah is not heedless of what you do. The verse's close is the denial of the divine negligence: the *ghaflah* — the heedlessness, the unawareness — is not an attribute of Allah; the works are not lost, the obstructions are not unregistered, the crooked-making is not unseen. Ibn Kathir: the same assurance to the believers — the wrongs of the People of the Book are known to Allah, and the account is with Him. The verse's *la ghafilin* is the guarantee of the believers' security: the ones who are obstructed, diverted, and slandered are not left without the record — the Witness and the Knower sees.

For the reader, the verse is the warning against the obstruction of the way and the comfort of the faithful. The believer is taught: the Way of Allah is straight; the obstructionists of every age have sought to make it appear crooked — the distortion, the slander, the misrepresentation — and the witnesses of the truth are the ones who know better. The verse's *wa antum shuhada'* is the exposure: the ones who know the truth and distort it are the ones whose knowledge is the witness against their act. And the verse's *la ghafilin* is the believer's rest: the wrongs are seen, the deceptions are known, and the account is with the One who is never heedless. The believers' task is the steadfastness in the straight path; the obstructionists' charge is the crookedness they themselves attempt.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 100

Verse 100 says: *O believers! If you were to yield to a group of those who were given the Scripture, they would turn you back from belief to disbelief.*

In simple terms, the verse warns the believers: if you obey a group of the People of the Book, they will turn you back from belief to disbelief.

The verse is the first of the surah's direct warnings to the believers at the close of the second section. Ibn Kathir titles the passage "Warning Muslims Against Imitating People of the Scriptures": "Allah warns His believing servants against obeying the People of the Book, who envy the believers for the favor that Allah gave them by sending His Messenger." Al-Jalalayn records the occasion: "The following was revealed when the Jews passed by the Aws and the Khazraj and were infuriated by their comradeship and set about reminding them of their mutual hostility in the days before Islam, such that they caused them to quarrel and the two tribes were on the verge of fighting one another."

"O believers!" — *ya ayyuha alladhina amanu* — O you who believe. The address is the believers — the ones who have the faith, who have submitted, who are being warned. The verse's addressees are the strong in the religion of the community: the ones who received the revelation, the ones who had the Messenger among them — and the warning is for them: the faith is not immune to the influence of the others.

"if you were to yield to a group of those who were given the Scripture" — *in tuti'u farigan mina alladhina utu al-kitaba* — if you obey a group of those who were given the Book. The *ita'ah* — the obedience — is the key: not the mere contact or the discussion but the *ita'ah* — the yielding, the heeding, the following of the instruction. The *fariq* — the group, the party — is the specific faction among the People of the Book: the envious, the conspiring, the obstructing group of 3:69–74 and 3:99. Ibn Kathir: the warning is against the ones "who envy the believers for the favor that Allah gave them by sending His Messenger" — the envy of 2:109: "Many of the People of the Scripture wish that they could turn you away as disbelievers after you have believed, out of their own envy."

"they would turn you back from belief to disbelief" — *yaruddukum ba'da imanikum kafirin* — they would turn you back, after your belief, to disbelievers. The *radd* — the returning, the turning back — is the reversal: the believers turned back to the *kufr* after the *iman*. The verse's power is the conditional: *in* — if — the possibility is not the inevitability but the danger: the believers are not to be turned if they do not yield; the warning is the condition of the obedience. Ibn 'Abbas (Tanwir): "they will make you disbelievers in Allah and Muhammad after your belief."

The verse's context — the occasion of Aws and Khazraj — is the historical depth of the warning: the ancient hostility of the two tribes, simmering beneath the brotherhood of Islam, was the instrument the Jews used — the reminder of the pre-Islam enmity, the stirring of the old quarrels, the verge of the fight. The *ita'ah* — the yielding to the promptings of the envious — would have undone the *ukhhuwwah* — the brotherhood — of the believers. The verse's warning is the warning against the *fitnah* — the discord, the stirring of the old divisions by the external agents.

The hadith of the most amazing faith, recorded by Ibn Kathir on 3:101: the Prophet asked, "Who among the faithful believers do you consider has the most amazing faith?" They said, "The angels." He said, "Why would they not believe, since they are with their Lord?" They mentioned the Prophets, and he said, "Why would they not believe while the revelation is sent down to them?" They said, "Then, we are." He said, "Why would not you believe when I am among you?" They asked, "Who has the most amazing faith?" He said, "A people who will come after you and who will find only books that they will believe in." The hadith is the surah's answer to the warning: the faith of the later believers — without the Prophet among them, without the direct revelation — is the most amazing faith; and the warning of 3:100 is the caution to those who have the Prophet among them: the ones who see the evidence should be the most secure in the faith, and the yielding to the envious is the risk of the faith that has the best conditions.

For the reader, the verse is the lasting warning of the surah on the influence of the external agents. The believer is taught: the faith is not the automatic security of the community; the ones who have the best conditions — the revelation, the Messenger, the witness — can be turned if they yield to the envious of the truth. The warning is the *ita'ah* — the obedience: not the contact, not the dialogue, not the learning, but the subservience to the instruction of the envious. The believer's defense is the discernment: the knowledge of the truth, the vigilance against the stirring of the old divisions, and the independence of the faith from the promptings of the external. And the verse's answer is the hadith of the later people: the faith that persists through the books alone is the most amazing; and the faith that has the new revelation among it should be the strongest against the reversal — the *iman* of the ones who see and the *iman* of the ones who believe — both anchored in the truth, both guarded against the yield.
