# Sūrah Al-Ikhlāṣ (Chapter 112) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir

## Introduction to the Sūrah

Four verses, and the whole of the creed is in them. The sūrah takes its name from no word inside it, which is a rare thing in the Book, and it is called Sincerity because that is both what it demands and what it produces. A person who holds what these four sentences say has cleared his worship of every partner, every likeness, every lineage and every comparison that people have attached to God, and is left with one Lord whom nothing resembles. With Sūrat al-Kāfirūn it forms a pair: that sūrah denies every false object of worship, and this one affirms the true one, so the two together are a complete statement of divine unity, and the community has taught them to its children first for that reason.

The question that brought the sūrah down was a demand for a lineage. The idolaters asked the Prophet to trace the ancestry of his Lord, as Ubayy ibn Kaʿb reports, and the answer came down in these words (Aḥmad, al-Tirmidhī). The same demand is reported from a delegation of Jews who pressed it further and asked who created the Creator, and a third report describes ʿĀmir ibn al-Ṭufayl and Arbad ibn Rabīʿah asking whether God is made of gold, silver, iron or wood; al-Ṭabarānī records the question from Quraysh on the authority of Ibn Masʿūd. Every version of the question assumes that God can be located, described, or traced through something else. The sūrah does not answer by describing God in relation to anything; it says what He is in Himself, and the question dies where it stands.

Its rank is stated in the Book's own measure. Al-Bukhārī records from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī that the Prophet said of this sūrah that it is a third of the Qurʾān, and that a man who recited it in his prayer was told, "By the One in whose hand is my soul, it is equal to a third of the Qurʾān." The reason the early scholars gave for the comparison is that the Qurʾān's message turns on three things — the oneness of God, the truth of prophethood, and the return to God for reckoning — and this sūrah carries the first of the three entire and unmixed, with no law, no story and no warning inside it to dilute it. The Prophet's attachment to it appears again in the report of a man who recited it in every unit of prayer and, when asked why, said that he loved it because it is the description of the Most Compassionate; the Prophet told him that his love of it would bring him into Paradise (al-Bukhārī). ʿĀʾishah reports that the Prophet would recite it with the two sūrahs of refuge into his palms at night and pass his hands over his body (al-Bukhārī).

The sūrah is a reply, and it is also a duty: it opens with the command to say, so the creed is not kept as a private thought but confessed in words. It then moves in four steps — the One who has no second, the Master whom all needs seek while He seeks nothing, the denial of lineage in both directions, and the denial of any equal at all — and it closes by returning to the word with which it opened. A person who says it in prayer says the whole of what Islam says about God, in a few short sentences that a child can carry and that no argument has yet exhausted.

## Verse 112:1

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “He is Allah—One ˹and Indivisible˺

**THE CREED IS PUT INTO THE MOUTH**

The sūrah opens with a command rather than a statement: Say. The report that carries its occasion of revelation explains the command: Ubayy ibn Kaʿb said that the idolaters asked the Prophet to trace the ancestry of his Lord, and this sūrah came down as the reply (Aḥmad, al-Tirmidhī). The people who asked for a description are given a sentence to repeat, and the answer to their question is not a picture of God but a confession about Him. This is how the Book answers a demand for a lineage: it places the words in the mouth of the one who is asked, and makes the answer a duty of speech that the community has repeated in every prayer since. The command also places the saying above the sayer, since the Prophet is told to say it; the words are therefore not his description of God but God's own account of Himself, delivered to be transmitted.

**A PRONOUN, THEN A NAME**

The sentence begins with the pronoun, "He is Allah", before anything is predicated of Him. The question had been about a being the questioners were disputing, and the answer first points at Him and marks Him off, and only then names Him. The grammarians of the language read the pronoun as the pronoun of the affair, standing at the head of the sentence to announce the weight of what follows it. Then comes Allah, the name that cannot be applied to anything else, either truly or by way of metaphor, while other names of God may be used of creatures in a limited sense. The verse therefore fixes its subject twice over — once by pointing, once by naming — before it says a single thing about Him.

**ONE, IN A SENSE NOTHING ELSE IS**

Then the predicate: One. The word is aḥad, and the early scholars drew a line between it and the ordinary Arabic word for one, wāḥid. Wāḥid counts; a thing can be one and still have a second in its kind, and a person can be one of a pair. Aḥad excludes the second altogether: it is unity into which no plurality, no sharing and no division can enter, and for that reason the scholars said the word cannot be affirmed of any being but God. The reading reported from Ibn Masʿūd, al-Wāḥid in place of Aḥad, shows the sense the first community heard in it, and the two words are close in meaning even where they are not equal in force. The word denies a peer, a rival, a helper and a match in one movement.

The denial reaches further than the idols of the first audience. If God is One in this sense, then no other being shares His divinity, nothing is a part of Him, and nothing stands beside Him in a partnership of rule; the claim that God is one of three falls under this word, and so does every claim that a creature shares in what belongs to God alone. The Book states the same truth in a sentence that gathers hearing and seeing into it: (42:11 — **“There is nothing like Him, for He ˹alone˺ is the All-Hearing, All-Seeing”**). And where the claim is that He has a son, the reply in another sūrah is a question about the claim's own terms: (6:101 — **“How could He have children when He has no mate?”**).

## Verse 112:2

> Allah—the Sustainer ˹needed by all˺

**A WORD THE REPORTS CIRCLE AROUND**

The second sentence is a single Arabic word, al-Ṣamad, and the early authorities gave it several meanings which, taken together, show the sides of one idea. Ibn ʿAbbās, in the report of al-Ḍaḥḥāk, said it is the Master to whom people turn in their needs. Mujāhid, al-Ḥasan, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, ʿIkrima and al-Ḍaḥḥāk said it is the one who has no hollow — the Arabic muṣmat, a solid with no inside that needs filling. Al-Shaʿbī said it is the one who does not eat or drink. Qatādah and al-Ḥasan said it is the one who remains after His creation has passed away, and ʿIkrima said it is the one above whom there is none. Abū Hurayrah said it is the one free of need of everyone while everyone is in need of Him, and Muqātil said it is the one who is complete, with no defect in Him. The root in the language carries two of these at once: to repair to a thing in a moment of need, and to be solid through and through.

Weighing these reports, the soundest reading is the one the language itself gives: the master who is resorted to in the affairs and needs that press on people, because that is what an Arab speaker understood by the word before any interpretation reached it. A report is also raised to the Prophet, that the Ṣamad is the one who has no hollow, and al-Tirmidhī mentions it as a narration cut off before the Companion; the honest qualification stands with it, that if the report is sound it is the most deserving of the readings to be followed. A community that keeps both readings keeps the sense that matters: the one to whom the whole creation turns, and the one who is complete in Himself, with nothing missing that anything could supply.

**NO NEED IN HIM, ALL NEED TOWARD HIM**

What the word builds is a doctrine of dependence with both directions pointed correctly. The creation is made of need, the Creator is free of it, and the naming of Him as the One sought means that the seeking itself is part of what creatures are. Ubayy ibn Kaʿb drew the next verse out of this one when he said that the Ṣamad is the one who does not beget and was not begotten, because nothing is born that does not die, and nothing dies that is not inherited, while God neither dies nor is inherited. The Qurʾān states the relationship plainly in a verse addressed to all humanity: (35:15 — **“It is you who stand in need of Allah, but Allah ˹alone˺ is the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy”**). The sentence a person recites in prayer is therefore not a title of honour but a direction of travel: every need is turned toward the one who has none.

## Verse 112:3

> He has never had offspring, nor was He born

**THE LINEAGE IS CUT AT BOTH ENDS**

The questioners wanted a family tree, so the answer closes both directions of it: He has never had offspring, nor was He born. The first half denies a line below Him and the second denies a line above Him, and between them every chain of descent is broken. The report from Ibn ʿAbbās names the claims current at the time: God did not beget as Maryam bore, and was not begotten as ʿĪsā and ʿUzayr were born. The Christian and the Jewish claims that particular men are sons of God are refused at the root, and the Arab claim that the angels are daughters of God falls with them.

The form of the negation is worth noticing. The verse does not say only that He has no son at present; it denies that begetting can be spoken of Him at all, and the same for being born. The two are impossible together for a reason that can be stated plainly: begetting would mean a part separating off from Him, and that would make Him a composite, which contradicts both His unity and His self-sufficiency; and a child would have to be of the father's kind, while nothing shares His kind, because He is the One who must exist and everything else exists by His giving it existence. The negation of offspring is placed first because it was the live error of the audience, and the negation of being born follows because a claim about where God came from is the same error seen from the other side.

**WHAT BEGETTING WOULD MEAN FOR GOD**

The reasoning the early scholars used can be followed by anyone. Whatever is born has a beginning, whatever has a beginning was nothing before it began, and whatever dies is left behind. The one who is Ṣamad, complete and sought by all, cannot be any of those things. The Qurʾān states the same truth elsewhere in the form of praise rather than refutation: (25:2 — **“˹Allah is˺ the One to Whom belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth, Who has never had ˹any˺ offspring”**). Where the claim is made directly, the Book does not argue at the level of its details but rejects its whole premise: (19:92 — **“It does not befit ˹the majesty of˺ the Most Compassionate to have children”**). The sentence is short, and the door it shuts is every door.

A report shows how naturally these words were used in supplication. A man was praying and said, "O Allah, I ask You by my testifying that there is no god but You, the One, the Self-Sufficient, who does not beget and was not begotten, and there is none comparable to Him"; the Prophet said that he had called upon God by His Greatest Name, the name by which, when He is asked, He gives (Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī, al-Nasāʾī). The four sentences of the sūrah are gathered in that one petition, which is what makes them the ordinary language of a believer's need.

## Verse 112:4

> And there is none comparable to Him.”

**NO EQUAL, IN ANY MEANING OF EQUALITY**

The sūrah closes on the word kufuw, and the reports show how wide it is. Ibn ʿAbbās, in the report of ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah, said the verse means that nothing is like Him. Mujāhid read it as a denial of any spouse, since an equal in the language can be the partner of one's house. Others read it as the denial of a match, a peer, or a second of any kind. The word covers all of them, because equality has many forms: equality of essence, of name, of attribute, of act, and equality in the kind of claim that may be made about a thing. Every form of it is refused, and the denial is absolute: not one comparable being exists.

The readers of the Qurʾān differed on how the word is pronounced. The majority of the readers of the cities read it with the letter before its end voweled, as the pages of the Qurʾān have it today, while Ḥamzah and Ismāʿīl read it with that letter silent and the glottal stop pronounced. Both are sound Arabic and both were received. The judgement of the early discipline on the two is that they are two known readings and two well-known dialects, and whichever one a reciter recites, he is correct; the meaning does not turn on the difference.

**THE WORD THAT RETURNS TO THE OPENING**

The verse ends with aḥad, the same word the sūrah opened with. It opens by declaring that God is One and closes by stating that not one thing is comparable to Him, so the last word of the sūrah seals the first. Between the two, everything else has been cleared away: the second, the partner, the son, the parent, the rival, the likeness. What remains for the person reciting it is a creed with nothing in it but God, which is what the sūrah's name promises, and it is the reason the Prophet is reported to have said that its love brings a person into Paradise.

The community's practice shows what the words are for. ʿĀʾishah reports that the Prophet would recite this sūrah with the two sūrahs of refuge, blow into his palms, and wipe his head and face and whatever his hands reached of his body, three times, before he slept (al-Bukhārī). It is recited in every prayer, taught to children before they can read, and carried by a Muslim in illness and in fear. The words are few and the claim is total: that there is one God, that He needs nothing, that nothing came from Him as a child comes from a father, and that nothing in existence stands beside Him as an equal. The Book makes the last of these a mark of praise in more than one place, and one of them gathers all four sentences of the sūrah into a single verse: (17:111 — **“And say, “All praise is for Allah, Who has never had ˹any˺ offspring; nor does He have a partner in ˹governing˺ the kingdom; nor is He pathetic, needing a protector. And revere Him immensely.”**).
