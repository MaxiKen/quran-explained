# Sūrah Al-Fatihah (Chapter 1) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir

## Introduction to the Sūrah

This sūrah is the opening of the Book and the prayer that the community repeats more often than any other passage of the Qur'an. It is recited in every unit of every prayer, aloud or quietly, by the worshipper who has memorised nothing else and by the one who has memorised the whole Book. It is also the first text a child learns, and the last text a dying man is helped to say. The sūrah carries seven verses. And the whole of the religion is present in them in a compressed form: the praise of God, the naming of His mercy, the acknowledgment of the Day of Judgment, the pledge of worship, and the request for guidance, in that order. What the reader holds in his hand is therefore a summary that opens out into the rest of the Qur'an, and the chapters that follow are its elaboration.

Its names show what the community has seen in it. It is called the Fātiḥah, the opener. This is because the Book begins with it and because the prayer begins with it; it is called the Mother of the Book and the Mother of the Qur'an. This is because the rest of the Book is built upon what it establishes and returns to it as a house returns to its foundation, It is also called the seven oft-repeated verses, a name that comes from the Book itself, where God says to His Prophet: (15:87 — **"We have certainly granted you the seven often-repeated verses and the great Quran"**). Abū Hurayrah reported that the Messenger of God said that the praise of God at its head is the Mother of the Qur'an and the seven oft-repeated verses, a report that al-Tirmidhī carried and graded sound. The names are not decoration; each one marks a use the sūrah has among the believers.

The sūrah is also called the prayer itself, and the reason is that the prayer is not valid without it. A report carried by Muslim has Abū Hurayrah say that the Messenger of God said that God had said: *"I have divided the prayer between Me and My servant into two halves, and My servant will have what he asks."* When the servant says, ***“All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds,”*** God says, "My servant has praised Me". When he says, ***“the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful,”*** God says, "My servant has extolled Me". When he says, ***“Master of the Day of Judgment,”*** God says, "My servant has glorified Me", and when he says, ***“You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help,”*** God says, *"This is between Me and My servant, and My servant will have what he asks."* The sūrah is therefore a conversation rather than a recitation. And the division the report describes explains why the reader moves from speaking about God in the first verses to speaking to Him in the rest.

The order of the sūrah is the order of a man being taught what to say. The first three verses praise God as the Lord of all worlds, the Compassionate, the Merciful. And the Master of the Day of Judgment; the fourth turns the address from report to speech, so that the reader stops describing God and begins addressing Him. And the last three are the request itself, beginning with the pledge of worship and ending with the prayer for guidance along the path of those who were favoured. What stands between the praise and the request is the sentence of worship, and everything before it prepares the worshipper to say it honestly. A reader who notices the arrangement will see why the sūrah is learned early and understood late: the words are simple, and the order in which they place the soul is not.

The chapter before the reader takes this sūrah verse by verse, in the order in which it is recited. Each verse is explained with what has been transmitted about it, the language it turns on. And the place it holds in the movement from praise to request, and the reading follows the eleven works this book is written from without relaying any of them. What the reader is asked to bring is the disposition of a person who has come to be taught rather than to be confirmed in what he already knows. The sūrah is short enough to memorise in a week and deep enough to occupy a lifetime, and the commentary that follows treats every one of its words as carrying its share of that depth.

## Verse 1:1

> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**THE OPENING THAT FITS EVERY ACT**

The sūrah opens with ***“In the Name of Allah”***, and the first thing the sentence does is leave its own verb unsaid. Nothing in those words announces what is being begun — a prayer, a meal, a journey, a sale, a letter — because the verb that would announce it is understood and not written. That omission is not carelessness. Because the wording stays open, the phrase can stand at the head of every beginning a human being has, from the prayer that opens his day to the work that closes it, and it belongs to none of them exclusively. A sentence reading "I begin my prayer" would have been tied to the prayer alone; the bare name belongs to whatever the servant is about to do. The habit is therefore small enough to keep in every circumstance and wide enough to cover the whole of a life, which is exactly what a servant needs when he cannot know in advance which of his acts will matter most.

The Book itself places the phrase at the head of a written message, where Solomon sends it to the queen of Sheba: (27:30 — **“It is from Solomon, and it reads: ‘In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”**). A king writing to a foreign throne opens not with his own name but with the name of God, and the letter goes on to invite a kingdom to submit to what that name demands. The command that began the revelation takes the same direction. This is because the first words given to the Prophet joined reading to the name of the One who made him able to read: (96:1 — **“in the name of your Lord”**). The practice of the community followed from both, so that a sentence first heard at the head of a scripture became the ordinary furniture of daily speech. It was spoken over contracts, meals and journeys by people who had learned it before they learned to read.

**WHAT IT MEANS TO NAME THE ONE YOU STAND BEFORE**

A name is what a thing is known by, and so the phrase is a declaration of who is being addressed before anything is asked of Him. The wording carries no petition, and that bareness is what makes it a declaration rather than a formula: the servant states whose he is, and then acts. It is worth remembering what the sentence replaced, because the people who first heard it lived among a community that opened its affairs with the names of its idols. And A traveller could tell whose errand a man was on by the name he spoke first. The change was therefore not an addition of piety to commerce but a transfer of ownership: the same beginning, spoken over a different name, moved the act into the service of a different master. The smallest habit of the day thus became a daily answer to the largest question a person is asked.

The early community understood the opening as a marker of the recitation itself, and the reports it kept about the point are precise. Anas ibn Mālik reported that he had prayed behind the Prophet and behind the first three caliphs, and that they began their recitation with the praise of God without mentioning the name aloud. Meanwhile, Abū Hurayrah recited it when he led the prayer and said that his prayer was the closest of them all to the prayer of the Messenger of God. The two reports show that the sentence was never treated as a matter of indifference: it was discussed precisely because it stood at the head of the Book. And the community wanted to know whether it belonged to the recitation or to the pause before it. What neither report questions is the practice of beginning with the name, and that practice is what the verse teaches whether the words are said aloud or kept in the heart.

**THE TWO NAMES THAT STAND TOGETHER**

The second half of the sentence names the One being invoked by two titles from a single root: ***“the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”***. The two words are built on the same three letters. And the doubling is deliberate, since the first name describes mercy as a quality of the One who is merciful and the second describes mercy reaching the one who is shown it. The Book uses the first name for the mercy that covers the whole creation in this life, where every creature is fed and sheltered whether or not it acknowledges the giver. And the second for the mercy that carries the believers through to the end. One sūrah opens with the single name on its own, as though to make the reader wait for everything that follows: (55:1 — **“The Most Compassionate”**). The pairing at the head of the Book therefore announces the two movements of mercy at once, the general and the particular, before anything has been asked.

The order of the two names teaches something about how the servant should approach. The general mercy is named first because it is the ground he is standing on before he has done anything to deserve it. And the particular mercy is named second because it is what he will need for the road. Another verse carries the two names as the conclusion of a statement about God's oneness, so that a reader who has understood the opening finds the same pair closing the argument elsewhere in the Book: (2:163 — **“the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”**). What the repetition shows is that the names are not ornaments attached to the front of the sūrah but the frame in which everything else is said. And A request made inside that frame is made by someone who already knows he is speaking to mercy rather than to a stranger. The reader who keeps the frame in view will find that the rest of the sūrah is easier to say honestly.

**A WORKMAN WHO MARKS WHAT HE MAKES**

Think of a craftsman who stamps his workshop's mark into every piece he finishes, whether it is a door for a palace or a stool for a kitchen. The mark does not make the piece better, and a customer may never look at it, yet the mark decides whose the work is and who answers for it. The verse gives the believer a mark of the same kind to set on his day, and it costs him one sentence. A man who begins his work with the name of God has already answered the question of whom he works for, and the answer steadies him when the work becomes difficult or the gain becomes tempting. Anyone who has watched a colleague do wrong work he did not intend will recognise how much the first sentence of the day can decide, and how little it costs to say it today.

## Verse 1:2

> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds

**THE SENTENCE THAT TEACHES THE SERVANT WHAT TO SAY**

The second verse gives the worshipper the words of praise: ***“All praise is for Allah”***. The report that explains how the prayer works places this sentence at the point where the servant begins to speak and God answers him. The proof is a hadith carried by Muslim, in which Abū Hurayrah reports that the Messenger of God said that God had said: *"I have divided the prayer between Me and My servant into two halves, and My servant will have what he asks."* When the servant says the words of this verse, God says, *"My servant has praised Me."* The arrangement is worth pausing over. This is because it means the first thing the servant does in his prayer is not to ask for anything but to give God what is due to Him. And the asking comes later, after the ground has been established. A request that arrives before praise asks a favour from a stranger; the same request after praise is made by someone who has already said whose house he is standing in.

The word translated here as praise is wider than the word for thanks, and the difference shapes the sentence. Praise is spoken of a thing for what it is, whether or not a benefit has reached the speaker, while thanks is spoken for a benefit received; and praise is therefore the larger of the two. This is because a person praises God for His perfection before he has counted a single gift. Some of the early readers said that the opening of the sūrah is praise because God praised Himself in it before any creature praised Him. That is why the sentence is a statement of fact rather than an exclamation. That reading explains the order of the sūrah: the reader is not told to feel thankful and then given words for it; he is told what is true of God, and his own praise is the echo of it.

**THE PRAISE IS ALL, AND BELONGS TO ONE**

The sentence begins with the whole of praise and gives it to a single owner. Since everything that is praised anywhere is praised for some excellence. And every excellence is a gift that traces back to the One who made it, the praise of the world returns to Him even when the mouths that speak it aim elsewhere. A reader who takes the sentence seriously stops dividing his gratitude among the means he can see — the doctor, the employer, the friend who happened to be there — not because those people are undeserving. But because the sentence has already named the source of what they carried. What the verse does is not to forbid thanks to people but to settle where the praise of the heart is finally addressed. And the Book gives the words again in a form that covers the whole of a person's life: (6:162 — **“my prayer, my worship, my life, and my death are all for Allah—Lord of all worlds”**). What the servant says in the prayer, he is being taught to say about his whole existence, since the sentence extends the same praise from the tongue to the life that carries it. That is the sense in which praise belongs to God alone: not that other people receive nothing, but that nothing they carry reached the servant except through the One the verse names.

Because the word is "all", the sentence also leaves no room for the complaint that murmurs underneath a hard life. Praise spoken only when things go well is a report on circumstances rather than a statement about God, and it collapses at the first serious loss. The community learned early that the sentence is meant to be said in the middle of the trouble and not only after it, since the One being praised is the same One in both conditions. Anyone who has forced himself to say "all praise is for Allah" on a black day knows that the words do not deny the pain; they place it inside a larger fact. That is why the sūrah opens the prayer with this sentence rather than with a request. This is because the servant is being trained to speak from the truth about God rather than from the state of his own affairs.

**THE LORD OF EVERYTHING THAT EXISTS**

The second half of the verse names the One who is praised: ***“Lord of all worlds”***. The word rendered as Lord carries with it ownership together with the care of what is owned, so the title says both that everything belongs to Him and that everything is managed by Him. The word rendered as worlds has been read in more than one way among the early authorities. Ibn ʿAbbās explained it as everything that has a spirit and walks upon the earth, together with the dwellers of the heavens. He also reported it as meaning the jinn and human beings, and as meaning all created things with their Provider. The readings do not conflict, since each of them names a portion of the same truth. And the least of them already places every reader inside the sentence: the world that a man knows, the creatures he never sees. And the nations that have come and gone are all under the same Lord. Elsewhere the Book uses the same title to rebuke any claim that something else shares the rule: (26:192 — **“the Lord of all worlds”**).

Something in the verse answers a fear that lies close to every human being. To call God the Lord of all worlds is to say that the whole of creation, with its distances and its hidden regions, is neither abandoned nor unaccounted for. No thread of a person's life is too small to be inside the title. It also answers the temptation to imagine that the great matters belong to God while the small ones belong to others — the household, the wage, the journey, the neighbour. Since the title is measured by "all", it leaves nothing outside. And the servant who acknowledges it has already admitted that the care of his own small world is part of a rule that extends over everything that exists.

**A GUEST THANKING THE WRONG MAN**

Think of a guest at a wedding who thanks the porter at the gate for the feast and never turns to the host who paid for it. His thanks are real. And the porter may even deserve a kind word, yet the gratitude has been misdirected, and a guest who does it in every house will live his whole life with the wrong name on his lips. The verse corrects the direction of praise before the reader has learned to speak, which is why it comes second in the sūrah and before the requests. Anyone who has gone through a year of provision and cannot say where it came from will recognise how far a person can drift while feeling grateful. The sentence puts the name of the host back at the head of the table, and everything the reader asks afterwards is addressed to the One standing there. That is the correction the verse performs for a reader today, when the means of provision are so visible that a person can spend his whole life thanking the pipes and never the spring.

## Verse 1:3

> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**MERCY NAMED AGAIN BEFORE ANYTHING IS ASKED**

The verse repeats what the reader has just said: ***“the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”***. A reader who has followed the opening may wonder why the two names return so soon after the praise, and the answer lies in what stands between them. The title that has just been spoken is "Lord of all worlds". And A title of that kind asserts both ownership and rule over everything a person can see and everything he cannot. The two names come back immediately afterwards so that the reader's first thought about such a Lord is mercy rather than dread. The prayer's own report shows the sentence working at that exact point. This is because the same hadith in Muslim's collection carries the word that when the servant says these words God answers, *"My servant has extolled Me."* The repetition is therefore not an ornament but the piece of the sūrah that steadies the servant before he is asked to speak about judgment.

**THE NAME THAT THE LANGUAGE RESERVED**

The first of the two names is built on a pattern that the Arabic of the Arabs used only for the One who is beyond measure in the quality it describes. That is why they would say "the Compassionate of this world and the next" and never give the word to a creature in the same absolute sense. Its meaning is mercy that is already upon everything: the rain that falls on the farm of the man who denies God as well as on the farm of the man who prays, the air both breathe, the food both eat. The Book states the reach of that mercy in a sentence addressed to Moses: (7:156 — **“My mercy encompasses everything”**). Because the mercy is prior to anything the creature does, it cannot be earned or lost by performance; it is the ground on which the performance happens. And the sūrah places it before the reader has done a single thing in the prayer beyond praise.

**THE MERCY THAT FOLLOWS THE SERVANT**

The second name is formed from the same root on a pattern that describes what a person does rather than what he is. So the pair carries both the mercy that is and the mercy that acts. Read in the places where God speaks about His servants, that second name is the one attached to the acts of mercy the servant himself needs. Those acts are the mercy that answers a supplication, the mercy that lifts a punishment, and the mercy that reopens the door after a sin. The Book's invitation to the despairing speaks with that name in view: (39:53 — **“Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy”**). Ibn ʿAbbās is credited with the reading that the first name covers every creature in this life, while the second belongs specially to the believers. That order matches the experience of the reader: he lives by one mercy before he understands anything, and he is carried by the other once he understands. Both names sit at the very front of the sūrah, before the requests, so that no one asks for guidance from a Lord who must first be persuaded to be kind. The order means that the mercy is not a reward the servant earns by asking well, since it is already spoken over him before he has asked at all.

**A LANDOWNER WHO WRITES A KIND CLAUSE**

Think of a tenant who discovers that the entire valley, the farms, the water and the orchards, belongs to one owner, and who reads the lease expecting the worst. He turns the page and finds a clause granting him the right to stay, and to be provided for while he works. The title that frightened him and the clause that calmed him belong to the same owner, and the clause is the reason the title does not break him. The verse does exactly this with the reader's fear: the ownership is stated, and then the mercy is stated twice, so that the servant can hold both facts at once. Anyone who has heard of a coming decision from someone powerful, and then heard that the same person is known to be kind, will recognise what these two names are doing at the head of the prayer. The reader is being taught today, before he asks for anything, that the One he stands before is of that character. The lesson is worth carrying out of the prayer. A person who has once seen the ownership and the kindness together in the same sentence will stop treating the difficulties of his life as evidence that the owner has turned against him. He will keep asking for what he needs from the hand that has already been generous before he asked.

## Verse 1:4

> Master of the Day of Judgment

**THE OWNERSHIP THAT IS CLAIMED FOR ONE DAY**

The fourth verse adds a title that is not given at any other point in the sūrah: ***“Master of the Day of Judgment”***. Two readings of the first word have come down from the earliest reciters, one naming the owner and the other naming the king. And both are sound, since they differ in one letter and each adds a shade the other does not carry. The owner says that everything on that day is his property to dispose of, and the king says that the disposal is an exercise of authority rather than of purchase. What both readings assert is that the day belongs to Him in a way that the present life does not appear to. Here, power is scattered among kings and employers and families; there, every hand that seemed to hold something will open. The prayer's report again marks the sentence, since the servant's words here are answered with *"My servant has glorified Me."*

**A DAY NAMED AFTER WHAT IS PAID BACK**

The day is named in the verse after repayment rather than after destruction, and the name is worth learning because it explains the mood of the whole prayer. The word carries the sense of settling a debt, giving a wage, and answering for a trust, so a reader who says the name is announcing that he expects an accounting and not merely a spectacle. The Book sends the same expectation through its description of the general return: (2:281 — **“Be mindful of the Day when you will ˹all˺ be returned to Allah,”**). And the sentence that follows it in that place finishes the thought by saying that no soul will be wronged by a single measure. The early authorities explained the day in the same way, as the day on which the creation is repaid for what it earned. Ibn ʿAbbās is credited with describing it as the day of reckoning and requital. And Qatādah with the reading that God will judge the creation by His justice on it. The name is therefore a promise of exactness, which is a comfort to the wronged and a warning to the wrongdoer.

**WHY THE ACCOUNTING STANDS BEFORE THE REQUEST**

The sūrah places this title before the request for guidance, and the order teaches something that the reader would otherwise have to learn later. A person who asks to be guided before he has admitted that he will be judged is asking for a road while denying that it leads anywhere. Once he has said that the day belongs to God, his request for the straight path becomes a request to arrive on the right side of the account. His worship becomes the conduct of a man who knows he must answer for it. The title also settles the question of who will do the judging, since the one who owns the day is the one who sets its standard. A reader who has ever prepared for an examination he had forgotten was scheduled knows the difference between that haste and the calm of a man who has written the date down and planned around it.

**A COURT DATE WRITTEN INTO THE CALENDAR**

Think of a farmer who learns that the dispute over his land will be heard on a fixed date, and who tears up every plan that assumed the matter might never be settled. The date does not tell him who will win; it tells him that the question will be answered, and it changes what he does with every month between now and then. The verse gives the reader a date of exactly that kind, without a year in it, so that the whole of his life is lived in the interval. Anyone who has put off an obligation for years and then found the deadline standing in front of him will understand why the sūrah puts the accounting before the request. What the title does today is to make every small choice answerable to a court that is already written into the calendar, and to make the asking that follows a serious thing rather than a wish. The farmer in the comparison is not made anxious by the date; he is made careful by it. And the difference between those two reactions is the difference between a man who has understood the title and a man who has only heard it. Because the day belongs to God and not to a panel of strangers, the carefulness it produces is confidence rather than dread.

## Verse 1:5

> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help

**THE HALF OF THE PRAYER WHERE THE SERVANT TURNS**

Here the sūrah changes its footing. Until this sentence the worshipper has been speaking about God in the third person, and from here he speaks to Him directly, so the prayer turns from a report into an address. The turn is deliberate and it matches the report of the divided prayer that Muslim carries, where the servant's words at this point are answered with *"This is between Me and My servant, and My servant will have what he asks."* A man who has described his Lord as the master of the day of judgment now stands in front of Him and says ***“You ˹alone˺ we worship”***. The effect of the change is that the praise and the request are joined: it is the same Lord who owns the day and receives the worship and gives the guidance that is asked for a few words later. Anyone who has listened to a formal letter read aloud and then heard the writer address him by name will recognise the shift.

**THE PRONOUN THAT STANDS IN FRONT**

The wording puts the word "You" before the act of worship, and in the language this is how a writer makes something exclusive: the sentence means that worship is directed to Him and to no one else. It does not say that the servant worships Him among others. That is why the verse is the clearest statement of the oneness of God in a sūrah that begins with praise and ends with a request. This is because the whole structure of the religion stands on this fronted word. The same construction is used again in the second half for the request for help, so that the two things a human being does most often — serving something and leaning on something — are both fastened to one place. The Book rebukes the opposite arrangement in a passage where a man is told that his clinging to something other than God is a hold on a thing already breaking: (31:22 — **“Whoever fully submits themselves to Allah and is a good-doer, they have certainly grasped the firmest hand-hold.”**).

**THE DIVISION OF WORSHIP AND HELP**

The verse holds two halves together that are often separated in practice. The first half is what the servant owes, and the second is what he needs, and the wording carries both in one breath: ***“and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help”***. A person who serves but asks no help will break under the service, and a person who asks help but does not serve has made God a servant of his needs instead of his Lord. The prayer keeps them together in one sentence so that the servant says both before he asks for anything in particular. And the hasanat of the sentence is that it makes his worship and his dependence a single act. The Book teaches the same pair when it instructs the believers to carry the difficulty of the road with the two instruments of patience and worship: (2:45 — **“And seek help through patience and prayer.”**). A reader who has watched a colleague try to carry a heavy load alone, and another who tries to give orders without touching the load, has seen both halves of this sentence broken apart. The order of the halves matters as well. This is because the worship is stated before the request for help, which means the servant places his Lord first and his need second rather than arranging the sentence around his own difficulty.

**THE COMMUNITY SPEAKS IN THE SINGLE WORSIPPER**

The wording of the prayer is plural from beginning to end, so the worshipper says "we" and not "I" even when he is standing in his own room with nobody beside him. That plural places him beside every other person saying the same sentence at the same hour, and it also places him beside the community of those who have said it in the past and will say it in the future. The request that follows is then a request for all of them together: guidance for the household, the neighbourhood and the people who are not present to ask for it themselves. That is why the sentence is the point at which a private prayer becomes a public one, and why the religion has always treated the community's unity as something spoken aloud rather than merely intended. A reader who has been alone with his fears for months may find that the plural is itself a kind of relief, since it places companions around him in the very sentence he says.

**A MAN WHO PLOUGHS AND ASKS FOR THE CANAL**

Think of a farmer who spends the morning ploughing his field himself, and the afternoon asking the water-keeper to open the canal to it. The ploughing is his work and it is real; the water is not in his hands and no amount of diligence at the plough can bring it to the field. A farmer who did only the first would watch his crop die, and one who did only the second would have no field prepared for the water. The verse folds both into one sentence, and it does so before the servant has asked for anything specific, so that he begins his prayers as a man who works and who asks. Anyone who has built something with his hands and then had to wait on something beyond his control knows how truthful that arrangement is. And How easily today's confidence in one's own effort hides the second half of it.

## Verse 1:6

> Guide us along the Straight Path

**THE REQUEST THAT THE WHOLE SŪRAH HAS BEEN PREPARING**

With the praise complete and the worship declared, the servant finally asks, and what he asks for is not food, safety or victory but a road: ***“Guide us along the Straight Path”***. The request is placed at the end of the sūrah because it is the thing the earlier verses were building the right to ask for. A man who has not acknowledged his Lord, who has not named His mercy, and who has not admitted the coming judgment has no reason to want a particular road. Once those things have been said, the road becomes the only thing worth asking for. The Book's own description of the guided makes the same point from the other side. It says of the people who follow the Book that they are on a guidance from their Lord, and that they are the ones who will succeed: (2:5 — **“It is they who are ˹truly˺ guided by their Lord, and it is they who will be successful”**). What the verse asks is therefore not a new status but the continuation of the one thing that matters.

**THE GUIDANCE ASKED FOR BY PEOPLE ALREADY ON THE ROAD**

The request is spoken by believers, which has puzzled some readers, since a person who is already on the road might be thought to have no need of the prayer. The early authorities answered that the guidance asked for is of three kinds: the guidance that brought the servant to faith, the guidance he needs at every hour to keep to it. And the guidance that will carry him to the end. Ibn ʿAbbās is credited with reading the request as a prayer to be made firm in holding fast to the religion. And A report traced to him says that the angel Gabriel instructed the Prophet to say exactly these words, so that the request for guidance is itself part of what was revealed to be asked. A man does not stay upright because he stood up once; he stays upright because he is held upright, and the request acknowledges that. The Book commands the same prayer in another place where the servant is told to keep asking for increase in what he has been given, which shows that guidance is treated as a growing thing rather than a possession. Anyone who has kept a promise for years knows that the beginning is the easy part, and that the road needs to be asked for again every morning.

**WHAT THE ROAD IS, AND WHO WAITS ON IT**

The word rendered as path names a road that is wide and open, and the language carries the sense of a way that is clear enough to be travelled rather than a track hidden among rocks. The request is not for a private inspiration but for the way that others have walked and that the Book has marked, which is why the sūrah ends by describing the people who walked it. The Book also tells the reader that the road is watched, since it reports the enemy's own words about standing in ambush upon it: (7:16 — **“on Your Straight Path”**). The reader is therefore asking for a road that is both marked and contested, and the request for guidance is at the same time a request for protection on it. A traveller who knows there are robbers on the route does not turn back; he asks for company and keeps to the road the caravan uses.

**A ROAD THAT MUST BE ASKED FOR EVERY MILE**

Think of a driver travelling through country where the signs are few and the turns are easy to miss. He does not learn the route once and then stop looking, and a passenger who falls asleep at the wheel is not saved by having known the road an hour ago. The verse gives the reader the driver's prayer rather than the traveller's memory: the road is asked for at every prayer, in every unit, all life long. Anyone who has drifted from a practice he once kept, without deciding to leave it, will recognise how little it takes to lose a road, and how much it takes to hold one. That is why the request is taught to be repeated today and tomorrow rather than settled once, and why the sūrah ends with it rather than with a statement of faith.

**THE COMPANY AT THE END OF THE ROAD**

The request names the road but not yet the company, and the verse that follows fills that in by describing the people who travelled it and the two groups who did not. The order is worth noticing: the servant first asks to be shown the way, and then he is told whose footsteps to look for on it. The Book uses the same method elsewhere, where it puts the people of truth, the martyrs and the righteous beside the obedient. The reader is shown what the guided road produces in the lives of those who walk it: (4:69 — **“those blessed by Allah: the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous”**). What the reader is given at the end of the sūrah is therefore both a road and a company, and the two are asked for together in this sentence and the one after it.

## Verse 1:7

> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray

**THE ROAD IS SHOWN BY THE PEOPLE WHO WALKED IT**

The request for a road is answered by a description of travellers, since the verse names ***“the Path of those You have blessed”*** rather than describing the road in itself. That is a deliberate way of teaching, because a road can be described in words that a traveller misreads, while a company of people can be looked at directly. The Book defines the people in another sūrah, where the guided are named as the prophets and those who followed them: (19:58 — **“the prophets who Allah has blessed from among the descendants of Adam”**). The reader is therefore not left with an abstract line to follow but with a family to travel beside. And the measure of his own path becomes the company it places him in rather than his private confidence that he is moving forward. Anyone who has trusted a route because it looked straight on the map. And only later learned where it ended, will recognise the correction: the road and the people who walk it are checked together, and this verse puts them in that order.

**THE FOUR KINDS OF PEOPLE INCLUDED IN THE BLESSING**

The blessed are not a vague crowd, and the Book names the company in a verse that the early authorities read alongside this one. The description is given where the reward of obedience is stated: (4:69 — **“those blessed by Allah: the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous”**). The earliest commentators read the two verses together. The reader who asks whom he is following is given a list rather than a mood: the prophets who carried the message, the truthful who confirmed it, the witnesses who sealed it with their lives, and the righteous who lived it quietly. Ibn ʿAbbās is credited with reading the blessing here as guidance itself, the favour of being shown the religion and being held in it. The list is wide enough that a farmer, a trader or a student can find a place in it, and narrow enough that nobody enters it by accident.

**THE FAILURES THE PRAYER NAMES APART**

The verse then names the people the reader asks to be kept away from: ***“not those You are displeased with”***. The two descriptions that follow name different failures. The first group is named by what they brought on themselves: (5:60 — **“It is those who earned Allah’s condemnation and displeasure”**). A report carried by al-Tirmidhī has ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim ask the Prophet about these two groups and receive the answer that those who earned the displeasure are the ones who knew the truth and turned away from it. Meanwhile, those who are astray are the ones who missed it without knowledge. The distinction is worth learning because it names the two dangers the reader faces in his own life: refusing what he knows to be right, and walking confidently without ever asking what is right. A person who refuses the truth he knows is not the same person as one who never met it, and the treatment differs, since the first needs to submit and the second needs to be taught. The prayer puts both in front of the worshipper so that he watches for the failure that is actually his rather than the one he finds easier to condemn. The Book records the same pair of failures in its account of a people who were given the Scripture and turned away from it: (2:61 — **“they invited the displeasure of Allah”**).

**A PRAYER THAT ENDS WITH THE READER STANDING**

The last words of the verse leave the reader in the middle of the road: ***“or those who are astray”***. The Book uses the same image for those who travel without understanding, and the comparison it draws is harsh: (25:44 — **“they are astray from the ˹Right˺ Way”**). Qatādah is credited with the reading that the ones who are astray are the people who wandered from the truth through ignorance. And Al-Suddī with the reading that they are the ones whose desires carried them off the road. What the ending does is to send the worshipper out of the sūrah with his attention on his own direction rather than on the failures of others. This is because the prayer is spoken in the plural about the community and not as an accusation against anyone. That is also why the sūrah does not end with a lesson but with a request, because the reader is unfinished and will need the same guidance tomorrow. These days a reader can be surrounded by advice from every direction and still be walking with nobody who has arrived, and this last line makes him ask for company on the road instead of trusting the noise around him. Think of a traveller who joins a caravan through country he has never crossed: he keeps his eyes on the camels ahead of him rather than on the horizon he cannot read, and he arrives because the caravan arrives. The sūrah that began with the name of God ends with a request that God keep the reader with the people God has kept. And the worshipper says "Amen" to seal the request he has just made.
