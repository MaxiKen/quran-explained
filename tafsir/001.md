# Sūrah Al-Fatihah (Chapter 1) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir

## Introduction to the Sūrah

Al-Fātiḥah, "The Opening," stands first in the codex, though it was not the first passage revealed. Ibn Kathir reports that Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatādah and Abū al-ʿĀliyah all placed its revelation in Makkah, and that is the position of the great majority. Al-Alūsī records the one early voice against them: Mujāhid held that it was sent down in Madinah, a view the scholars who came after him counted a lapse. The strongest argument against the Madinan view is practical. The prayer was made obligatory in Makkah, and the prayer is not valid without this sūrah, so the sūrah must have been in the hands of the Muslims before Madinah.

The sūrah's names describe its work. Ibn Kathir lists al-Fātiḥah, "the Opener of the Book"; Umm al-Kitāb and Umm al-Qurʾān, "the Mother of the Book" and "the Mother of the Qur'an," because the Book begins with it and because it gathers the Book's message; and al-Sabʿ al-Mathānī, "the Seven Oft-Repeated," a name the Qur'an itself uses for it, since no prayer is complete without it: (15:87 — *“We have certainly granted you the seven often-repeated verses and the great Quran”*). Al-Bukhārī explains the mother-name simply, that the Qur'an opens with it and the prayer opens by reciting it. Ibn Jarīr adds the Arabic behind it: the Arabs call any comprehensive matter an umm, the skin around the brain umm ar-raʾs, the banner that gathers the ranks an umm, and Makkah Umm al-Qurā as the head of the settlements. Further names reached Ibn Kathir: al-Hamd and al-Ṣalāh, because it is praise and because prayer is named after it; al-Shifāʾ and al-Ruqyah, the cure and the healing recitation.

Its rank is fixed by reports. At-Tirmidhī, who graded the report ṣaḥīḥ, records Abū Hurayrah hearing the Prophet ﷺ call it the Mother of the Qur'an, the Mother of the Book, the seven repeated verses and the glorious Qur'an. Aḥmad records Abū Saʿīd ibn al-Muʿallā being told he would be taught the greatest sūrah in the Qur'an, and finding it was this one. Aḥmad also records Ubayy ibn Kaʿb being told that nothing like it had been sent down in the Torah, the Gospel, the Psalms or the Qur'an, and At-Tirmidhī, recording the same hadith, graded it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ. Muslim and An-Nasāʾī record Ibn ʿAbbās describing a door in heaven opened for the Prophet ﷺ alone, from which an angel brought two lights no prophet had been given before: the Opening of the Book and the close of Sūrah al-Baqarah. Its use as a healing recitation is in the two Ṣaḥīḥs, where Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī treated a stung tribal chief with it and the Prophet ﷺ asked the man how he had known it was a healing recitation, then told the group to divide the sheep and give him a share of them.

Two rulings keep it in the reader's life daily. Muslim records Abū Hurayrah reporting the Prophet ﷺ saying three times that whoever prays without Umm al-Qur'an has prayed incompletely; asked what a person standing behind the imam should do, Abū Hurayrah answered that he should recite it quietly, and quoted the ḥadīth qudsī behind the ruling, "I have divided the prayer between Myself and My servant into two halves, and My servant shall have what he asks for." The two Ṣaḥīḥs record ʿUbādah ibn al-Ṣāmit reporting the same requirement in a starker form, "There is no prayer for whoever does not recite the Opening of the Book."

The sūrah has one clear turn. Verses 1:1 to 1:4 speak about God in the third person: His Name, His praise, His mercy, His mastery of the Day of Judgement. Verse 1:5 turns and speaks to Him, and the last two verses are petitions He taught the servant to make. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān notes that the verse at the turn carries both movements at once, since it is praise to say "You alone we worship" and a request to add "and You alone we ask for help." The ḥadīth qudsī in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim describes the sūrah as divided into two halves between Allah and His servant, and the division falls at that same verse. The whole sūrah is spoken in the plural, so the one who prays asks on behalf of every believer, and the last line of Maudūdī's Tazkir al-Qurʾān on it is that this chapter is Islam in miniature while the rest of the Qur'an enlarges the picture.

## Verse 1:1

> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**A Line at the Head of Every Sūrah**

The basmalah opens 113 of the Qur'an's 114 chapters. Sūrah al-Tawbah alone begins without it. It appears inside the text once, in the letter Sulaymān sent to the Queen of Sheba: (27:30 — *“It is from Solomon, and it reads: ‘In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”*). The first word of revelation ever given to the Prophet ﷺ was the same command: (96:1 — *“Read, ˹O Prophet,˺ in the Name of your Lord”*). Maʿārif al-Qurʾān observes how deliberately this replaced the older habit of the Arabs of the Jāhiliyyah, who began their affairs with the names of their idols. The Muslim opens the day, the meal, the journey and the prayer with the Name of the One who made all of it possible.

**A Verse or a Divider**

Every Muslim agrees on two things: the line is part of Sūrah al-Naml, and it is written at the head of every chapter except al-Tawbah. What they differ on is whether it counts as a verse of each chapter or only marks where one chapter ends and the next begins. Ibn Kathir sets out the roster. Those who counted it a verse at the start of every chapter except the ninth include the Companions Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn ʿUmar, Ibn al-Zubayr, Abū Hurayrah and ʿAlī, and among the next generation ʿAṭāʾ, Ṭāwūs, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Makhūl and al-Zuhrī; among the jurists, ʿAbdullāh ibn al-Mubārak, al-Shāfiʿī, one report from Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal, Isḥāq ibn Rāhwayh and Abū ʿUbayd. Against them, Mālik and Abū Ḥanīfah held that it is not a verse of al-Fātiḥah or of any other chapter, while Dāwūd held it was a separate verse placed at each opening but not counted as part of the sūrah. Al-Ṭabarī took the stricter side and reasoned from the text itself: if the basmalah were a verse of al-Fātiḥah, the opening and the third verse would carry identical wording with nothing between them, and no two adjacent verses of the Qur'an repeat themselves in that way.

The disagreement has a practical edge. Those who counted the basmalah in the sūrah recited it aloud, and al-Shāfiʿī was among them, as were Abū Hurayrah, Ibn ʿUmar, Ibn ʿAbbās and Muʿāwiyah before him. Those who did not count it recited it quietly or left it out. Everyone agreed the chapter has seven verses, so the count of the sūrah never turns on the answer.

**Beginning in His Name**

The words themselves carry the lesson. Al-Mukhtaṣar explains that Allāh is the name of the divine Essence, and the two names that follow, al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm, both come from raḥmah, mercy, so that a servant who opens with them has already called on mercy. Al-Saʿdī adds that Allāh is the One who is worshipped, the One to whom worship belongs by right because of the perfections He possesses. Ibn Kathir notes that the view is supported by a report Ad-Dāraquṭnī recorded from Abū Hurayrah, and that similar statements are attributed to ʿAlī, Ibn ʿAbbās and others. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān adds that the Prophet ﷺ had used a shorter formula before these words were revealed, and that the full basmalah became the practice of his community from then on. Ibn Kathir also records the view that Allāh is the Greatest Name, since every other name appears as an attribute beside it.

## Verse 1:2

> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds

**All Praise, and to Whom It Belongs**

Ibn Jarīr al-Ṭabarī defines the sentence as the declaration that every kind of thanks belongs to Allah alone, not to any being worshipped beside Him and not to any creature. The favours behind the thanks are beyond counting, and they include the very tools by which a person thanks Him: the body that carries out His commands, the provision that keeps it alive, the guidance that shows it where to walk. Ibn Kathir preserves a report from ʿUmar that he asked ʿAlī about this word in particular, since the community already knew the meanings of "there is no god but Allah," "glory be to Allah" and "Allah is greatest." ʿAlī answered that al-ḥamdu lillāh is a statement Allah loved for Himself and was pleased with for Himself, and that He likes to hear it repeated. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported by Ibn Abī Ḥātim as saying that when the servant says it, Allah answers, "My servant has praised Me."

The definition is definite, and the grammarians noted why. Ibn Kathir explains that the alif and lām at the front of al-ḥamd gather every form of praise, so the sentence claims for Allah not one kind of thanks but all of them.

**Praise and Thanks Are Not the Same**

Ḥamd and shukr overlap, and the scholars separated them carefully. Ibn Kathir reports the distinction that ḥamd is broader, praise for what someone is as well as for what he has done, while shukr answers a favour received. Al-Zamakhsharī in al-Kashshāf puts the two words side by side as brothers and notes that thanks, unlike praise, is paid with the heart, the tongue and the limbs together.

The Prophet ﷺ encouraged the sentence as a habit of the tongue. Aḥmad records al-Aswad ibn Sarīʿ saying that he had collected words of praise for his Lord and asking permission to recite them, and the answer was, "Your Lord loves al-ḥamd." At-Tirmidhī, An-Nasāʾī and Ibn Mājah record Jābir ibn ʿAbdullāh reporting that the best remembrance is "there is no god but Allah" and the best supplication is al-ḥamdu lillāh; At-Tirmidhī graded that report ḥasan gharīb. Ibn Mājah records Anas ibn Mālik reporting that no servant is given a blessing and says al-ḥamdu lillāh without the praise being better than the gift.

**Lord of All the Worlds**

Rabb means owner and master, the one with full authority over what he owns, and it carries the further sense of the one who raises a thing stage by stage until it reaches its purpose. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān notes that the word is used of Allah alone, and that any other use needs a qualifier attached, as in "the master of the house."

Al-ʿālamīn is the plural of ʿālam, everything other than Allah. Ibn Kathir gathers the opinions. Al-Farrāʾ and Abū ʿUbayd restricted ʿālam to beings with minds, the jinn, humankind, the angels and the devils, excluding the animals; Zayd ibn Aslam and Abū Muḥaysin included everything created with a soul; Qatādah said every kind of creature is an ʿālam; and al-Qurṭubī concluded that the word covers everything Allah created in this world and the next. The phrase therefore places the reader inside a creation too large to survey, and the sūrah's third verse explains what holds it all together.

## Verse 1:3

> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**Mercy After Majesty**

The two names return here after "Lord of all worlds," and al-Qurṭubī explains the sequence as a deliberate pairing of fear and hope. Lordship over every world inspires awe; the two mercy names restore confidence, and al-Qurṭubī cites the Qur'anic pair that keeps the two together: (15:49 — *“Inform My servants ˹O Prophet˺ that I am truly the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”*) followed at once by the warning of punishment. He supports the point with a report in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim from Abū Hurayrah, that if a believer knew the extent of Allah's punishment no one would hope for Paradise, and if an unbeliever knew the extent of His mercy no one would despair of it. The sūrah opens the servant's speech with both truths at once.

**Compassionate to All, Merciful to the Believers**

Al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm share one root and describe two movements of the same mercy. Al-Mukhtaṣar explains that both derive from raḥmah and establish how wide that mercy is: it surrounds everything and embraces every living being, and the believers receive the greatest share of it. Al-Saʿdī puts it in the classical form, that the two names show a mercy vast enough to cover every creature and written in its fullness for those who follow the messengers. The Qur'an states the breadth directly: (7:156 — *“But My mercy encompasses everything”*). The commentators drew from this that al-Raḥmān describes mercy toward creation as such, and al-Raḥīm the mercy that carries a believer through to the end. Al-Jalālayn defines mercy simply as willing what is good for the one who deserves it.

Al-Saʿdī adds a point of creed. The two names are not decorations. They affirm a real attribute of mercy belonging to Allah, from which every blessing in creation flows, exactly as His names of knowledge and power affirm real knowledge and real power.

**Why the Repetition**

The two names are already in the basmalah, and al-Ṭabarī draws attention to the repetition as proof in a dispute. If the basmalah were a verse of al-Fātiḥah, he argues, the same words would stand twice in neighbouring lines with nothing between them, which does not happen anywhere else in the Book. Those who counted the basmalah as a verse answered that the two occurrences have different functions: the first opens the recitation, and the second belongs to the praise itself. Al-Bayḍāwī notes that the repetition also sets up the transition that follows, from describing God to addressing Him.

## Verse 1:4

> Master of the Day of Judgment

**Master of the Day of Judgment**

The word is read two ways, mālik and malik, and both readings are traced to the Prophet ﷺ. Al-Bayḍāwī records that ʿĀṣim, al-Kisāʾī and Yaʿqūb read mālik, "owner," while the rest of the reciters read malik, "king," and he notes that the second of these was chosen by the scholars of the two sacred cities. Owner and king are not alternatives but two aspects of the same authority: mālik disposes of what he owns as he wishes, malik commands and forbids those under him. The sūrah names the day on which both are exercised without a partner.

**Why That Day Alone**

Allah is master of every day. The verse names the Day of Judgement because on that day the ownership of everyone else comes to an end. Ibn Kathir explains that no one will then claim anything, no one will speak without permission, and the Qur'an shows that silence: (78:38 — *“None will talk, except those granted permission by the Most Compassionate”*). Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk reports from Ibn ʿAbbās the plain sense of it, that no one on that day owns what he used to own in the world.

Maʿārif al-Qurʾān states the wider point that human mastery is limited at both ends. A man's ownership has a before, when it did not exist, and an after, when it will not; it reaches the living but not the dead, the visible but not the hidden. Only Allah's mastery is complete, in this world and the next, so naming the Day does not narrow His rule. The Qur'an puts the question of that Day into a single line: (40:16 — *“Who does all authority belong to this Day? To Allah—the One, the Supreme”*). The two Ṣaḥīḥs record the Prophet ﷺ describing the scene behind the verse, that Allah will grasp the earth and fold the heavens in His right hand and call out, "I am the King. Where are the kings of the earth? Where are the tyrants? Where are the arrogant?"

**A Day for Reckoning**

Dīn here means recompense, the settling of accounts. Ibn Kathir supports it from the Qur'an's own usage, where dīn describes the full payment of what is due, and from the words of the disbelievers in Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt asking whether they will really be brought to judgement. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb left the community a sentence on the same theme, recorded by Ibn Kathir: hold yourselves accountable before you are held accountable, and weigh your deeds before they are weighed. The two Ṣaḥīḥs also record the warning that the most despised name before Allah is that of a man who calls himself king of kings, while no owner exists except Allah. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān draws the practical conclusion from the verse's place in the sūrah: the world is the field of action and not the place of payment, and this is why the servant is taught to name the Day before he asks for anything at all.

## Verse 1:5

> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help

**The Turn to Direct Address**

Until this verse the sūrah speaks about God in the third person; here it speaks to Him. Al-Qurṭubī notes the change and the effect it creates, since the worshipper moves from praise to intimate address in one step. Al-Bayḍāwī describes the same movement as a passage from proof to sight, and from absence to presence, so that what the servant knew about God he now says to His face. The classical commentators observe what this gives the believer: no priest stands between the worshipper and his Lord, and the address belongs to everyone who prays it.

**You Alone We Worship**

Arabic allows a sentence to be built in more than one order, and this verse puts the object first. Al-Zamakhsharī explains the placement as a device of exclusivity: the meaning is that worship is reserved for You alone and for no one else. Al-Qurṭubī records the Arab habit of putting the most important word first, with the story of a man who insulted another and then said, "You are the one I mean," and received the answer, "And it is you I turn away from."

Worship itself is defined by the classical lexicographers as humility and obedience together. Al-Ṭabarī notes that the root lies in the old Arabic picture of a path trodden down by feet until it is smooth, which is why the same word describes a road and a servant. The servant's confession therefore has a content: he is owned and obedient. The Qur'an names the purpose for which he was made: (51:56 — *“I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me”*).

**And You Alone We Ask for Help**

Worship is mentioned before the request for help, and the commentators explain why. A man does not ask a king for assistance before he has acknowledged that the king rules. Al-Ṭabarī defines the second half of the verse as the servant asking for help to worship his Lord, to obey Him, and to manage everything that concerns him, from no one besides Him. The Qur'an supplies the reason such help is needed at every hour: (47:38 — *“For Allah is the Self-Sufficient, whereas you stand in need ˹of Him˺”*).

Muslim records the ḥadīth qudsī in which this verse is the centre of the sūrah. Allah says that the prayer is divided between Him and His servant, and that when the servant reaches these words, He answers, "This is between Me and My servant, and My servant shall have what he asks for." The first half of the sūrah, in other words, has been the servant's praise, and this verse is where the two sides of the conversation meet.

## Verse 1:6

> Guide us along the Straight Path

**What Guidance Means**

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī, quoted at length in Maʿārif al-Qurʾān, defines guidance as leading someone toward his destination gently and kindly, and notes that in its fullest sense it comes from Allah alone. It has degrees. The first is shared by everything created, since every creature was given its nature and then directed to what that nature requires: (20:50 — *“Our Lord is the One Who has given everything its ˹distinctive˺ form, then guided ˹it˺”*). The guidance asked for here is of a higher kind, the leading of a rational being to what pleases God.

This answers the obvious question, why the believer asks for guidance when he already believes. Ibn Kathir gives the answer that the servant needs guidance every hour of his life, both to remain firm and to advance. The Qur'an commands the believers to believe, a command that makes sense as a call to steadiness rather than to a first step. It also teaches the prayer that protects what has already been given: (3:8 — *“Our Lord! Do not let our hearts deviate after you have guided us”*).

**The Straight Path**

Al-Ṭabarī reports that the community agreed on the meaning of ṣirāṭ mustaqīm: the clear road that has no branches. He supports it with the poetry of Jarīr ibn ʿAṭiyyah, who described a leader standing on a path that stays straight while other paths bend. He then notes how the Arabs used the same image for conduct, calling an honest man straight and a dishonest man crooked, and concludes that the path the Qur'an names is Islam.

Ibn Kathir adds a prophetic image of the road. Aḥmad records in his Musnad the report of an-Nawwās ibn Samʿān, that the Prophet ﷺ described a straight path with two walls on either side, doors standing open in the walls and curtains over the doors, a caller at the mouth of the path urging people to enter and keep straight, and another caller warning anyone who reaches for a door. The Prophet ﷺ then named each part of the picture: the path is Islam, the two walls are the limits set by Allah, the open doors are what He has forbidden, the caller at the path is the Book of Allah, and the caller above it is Allah's admonition placed in the heart of every Muslim.

The verse is short and the request is heavy, which is why the sūrah places it at the head of the servant's petition. Everything asked afterward depends on the road he is asking to be kept on.

## Verse 1:7

> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray

**The Path of Those You Have Blessed**

The verse names the road by its walkers. The Qur'an gives the roster in one line: (4:69 — *“And whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be in the company of those blessed by Allah”*), followed by the four ranks, the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs and the righteous. Al-Ṭabarī records the early attempts to define the blessed: Ibn ʿAbbās listed the angels, the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs and the righteous, a report whose chain he himself grades weak; Rabīʿ ibn Anas said the prophets; Wakīʿ said the believers, and Ibn Jurayj heard from Ibn ʿAbbās that they are the believers. Al-Ṭabarī prefers the wider sense, and his reason is textual, since the verse describes people whom God Himself has favoured and the favour is not restricted to one rank.

Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Bayḍāwī both point to the grammar of the verse, where "the path of those You have blessed" stands as an apposition to "the straight path." The effect is a definition: the straight path is, by God's own naming, the path the blessed walked. A claim to be on the right road can then be tested against a known procession, from the prophets down through the truthful and the martyrs of every generation.

**Those Who Earned Anger, Those Who Went Astray**

The three roads in the verse are drawn sharply. Al-Jalālayn and al-Mukhtaṣar read the first group as those who knew the truth and did not follow it, and the second as those who neglected to seek it; the classical identification behind both is the Jews and the Christians. Al-Mukhtaṣar ties the first to the Israelites, who had the covenant in front of them, and the second to the Christians, whose failing was in the search rather than in the knowledge. Ibn Kathir records the report in Aḥmad's Musnad in which ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim heard the Prophet ﷺ explain the two groups in exactly these terms.

Ibn Kathir also keeps the picture honest. Both descriptions apply to both communities, he says, and he cites the Qur'an for each emphasis: (5:60 — *“those who earned Allah’s condemnation and displeasure”*) describes the Jews who were cursed and made targets of wrath, while (5:77 — *“nor follow the vain desires of those who went astray before ˹you˺”*) describes the Christians who wandered and led others to wander. The verse is a warning against reading it as a tribal label; it marks two diseases of the will, one that refuses what it knows and one that stops looking.

**The Servant's Portion**

The petition ends with a hadith that returns to the theme of the sūrah's opening. Muslim records the Prophet ﷺ saying that when the servant recites the last verses, Allah answers, "This is for My servant, and My servant shall have what he asks for." The recitation then closes with Āmīn, which Ibn Kathir explains as the servant's request that his invocation be accepted, and which At-Tirmidhī glossed as a prayer not to be disappointed in hope.

Ibn Kathir collects the evidence for it. Aḥmad, Abū Dāwūd and At-Tirmidhī record Wāʾil ibn Ḥujr hearing the Prophet ﷺ recite the end of the sūrah and then say Āmīn, lengthening his voice, and At-Tirmidhī graded the report ḥasan. The two Ṣaḥīḥs record the Prophet ﷺ saying that when the imam says Āmīn, the congregation should say it, because whoever's Āmīn falls together with the Āmīn of the angels has his past sins forgiven. The sūrah of praise and petition thus closes with a word that asks God to make all of it true.
