# Sūrah Al-Fatihah (Chapter 1) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir

## Introduction to the Sūrah

This sūrah is the opening of the Book, and the community has never known it by any other place than the head of the muṣḥaf. The reports give it several names, each taken from a function it performs. It is the Opening of the Book (Fātiḥat al-Kitāb) because the Book begins with it, and the Mother of the Qur'an (Umm al-Qur'an) and the Mother of the Book because the rest of the Book is built upon it, as a settlement is built out from its centre. It is the Seven Oft-Repeated (al-Sabʿ al-Mathānī) because it holds seven verses which are recited again and again in every unit of prayer, and the Qur'an names the gift in a verse addressed to the Prophet: (15:87 — **“the seven often-repeated verses”**). It is also called the Praise (al-Ḥamd), the name it takes from its own second sentence, and the Prayer (al-Ṣalāh), because the Prophet said of it, as Abū Hurayrah reports, that a prayer in which the Opening of the Book is not recited is a deficient prayer (al-Bukhārī, Muslim). A report carried by Muslim gives the sūrah a higher honour still: Abū Hurayrah reports that the Prophet said that God says, “I have divided the prayer between Myself and My servant into two halves, and My servant shall have what he asks,” and the report then follows the recitation sentence by sentence, with the answer of God placed beside each one.

The reports do not leave the sūrah's rank to be inferred from its names alone. Abū Saʿīd ibn al-Muʿallā reports that the Prophet took him by the hand and promised to teach him the greatest sūrah in the Qur'an before he left the mosque; when Abū Saʿīd recited the Fātiḥah, the Prophet identified it as the Seven Oft-Repeated and the great Qur'an he had been given (al-Bukhārī). Ibn ʿAbbās reports a second distinction. While Gabriel was with the Prophet, a door in heaven opened that had not opened before, and an angel descended with glad tidings of two lights granted to no earlier prophet: the Opening of the Book and the closing verses of Sūrah al-Baqarah (Muslim). Its recitation was also used as a remedy. Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī reports that a Companion recited it over a stricken tribal chief, who recovered; when the party told the Prophet, he approved what had been done and asked, *"How did you know that it is a remedy?"* (al-Bukhārī). These reports explain why the sūrah belongs at once to public worship, private supplication and healing. The Book describes its own recitation in the same joined terms: (17:82 — **“We send down the Quran as a healing and mercy for the believers, but it only increases the wrongdoers in loss”**). The benefit is therefore not a charm detached from faith. It comes through words understood as prayer, praise and reliance on God.

The early scholars took up the question of where the sūrah was revealed, and the greater number of them held it to be Makkan. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatādah and most of the Companions are reported to have held that position, and the case is supported by the verse that names the seven oft-repeated verses, which belongs to the Makkan revelation by the agreement of the scholars. Mujāhid held it to be Madinan, alone among the early authorities, and later writers counted the view a slip on his part rather than a position with a case behind it. Some held that it was revealed twice, once at Makkah when the prayer was made obligatory and once at Madinah when the direction of prayer was changed, so that the community would know that its place in the prayer had not changed with the direction. Whatever the date of its coming down, its rank in the religion was settled from the first days: it is recited in every unit of every prayer, it stands at the head of the written Book, and the reports about its recitation are carried in the two collections that the community has received with the fullest acceptance.

The arrangement of the sūrah runs in three movements. The first movement speaks about God in the third person: praise belongs to Him, He is Lord of all the worlds, He is the Most Compassionate and the Most Merciful, and He is Master of the Day of Judgement. The second movement, the middle verse, turns the address around: the servant now speaks to God directly and says that it is Him alone he worships and Him alone he asks for help. The third movement is the petition that the two earlier movements have been preparing: guidance to a straight path, described in the terms of the people who walk it. The turn at the middle verse never turns back, and the sūrah does not return to speaking about God after the servant has begun to speak to Him. Its wording also moves from the widest horizon to the narrowest: from the Lord of all the worlds, through mercy and the Day of Judgement, to one person asking for one road. The arrangement is not an accident of composition. Because the servant is taught to open with praise before he asks, the sūrah models the order of supplication itself, and because the praise is put in his mouth before the request, he learns that the one to whom he will turn has already been described to him.

What the sūrah carries as a whole is the creed of the religion in seven short verses, and the request that gives that creed its direction. It states that God is one, that He is the Lord of everything, that mercy is His settled attribute and judgement is His settled work, and it places the servant before Him as a worshipper who has nothing to rely on but help from the one he worships. Then it gathers the whole religion into a single request, which is guidance, because a person who has been given everything else still needs the road, and knows it. The last verse describes that road by the people who travel it rather than by its features, and names the two deviations that have drawn people off it. One of the earliest Muslims described the opening sentence of this sūrah as a statement that God loved for Himself, was pleased with for Himself, and loves to have repeated, and the community has repeated it in every prayer since. The sūrah teaches the reader what to say, in what order to say it, and what to ask for when everything has been said.

## Verse 1:1

> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**A SENTENCE WITH ITS VERB LEFT UNSAID**

The sūrah opens with the words ***“In the Name of Allah”***, and the sentence leaves its own verb unspoken. Nothing in it states what is being begun, whether a recitation, a meal, a journey, or a contract. The verb is understood rather than written, and it takes its colour from the act that follows. That openness is deliberate. If the verb were written, the sentence would belong to one act and would have to be reworded for every other one, and a person would need a different formula for each part of his day. Because the wording carries no single act, the sentence can stand at the head of every act. A phrase reading *I begin my prayer* would belong to the prayer alone. A phrase reading *I begin my trade* would belong to the market. The bare name belongs to both, and to the ordinary hours between them. The Book places the same words at the head of a written message from one king to another: (27:30 — **“It is from Solomon, and it reads: ‘In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”**). It places them again at the head of the first revelation given to the Prophet, joined to the command to read: (96:1 — **“in the name of your Lord”**). The practice of the community followed the wording. A sentence first heard at the head of a scripture became the ordinary furniture of speech among the believers. They say it over meals and journeys, and they learned to say it before they learned to write.

**THE TWO NAMES AND THE MERCY THEY CARRY**

The sentence is completed by two titles taken from one root: ***“the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”***. The first of the two is a name the Arabs reserved for God alone. They would speak of the mercy of the Most Compassionate covering this world and the next, and they did not give that word to a creature in that absolute sense. The second is formed on a pattern that describes the one who shows mercy, and it may be used of a person. The Book therefore uses it of the Prophet in a sentence about his treatment of the believers: (9:128 — **“gracious and merciful to the believers”**). The two titles announce a mercy that is already present and a mercy that keeps arriving. The first name describes what mercy is in itself; the second describes mercy in its work toward the one who receives it. Both are needed here, because the sentence is being spoken by a servant and not by a student of grammar. The Book states the reach of the first in a sentence addressed to Moses: (7:156 — **“My mercy encompasses everything”**). An early reading says that the first name covers every creature in this life, since every living thing is fed and sheltered whether or not it acknowledges the giver. The second belongs especially to the believers, and it carries them to the end. The pairing also answers a fear that rises in a person who has just named God as the Lord of everything. The titles stand between the ownership and the servant like a hand held out before the first demand is made. One sūrah opens with the single name on its own, as though the reader were meant to wait for the rest: (55:1 — **“The Most Compassionate”**).

**HOW THE FIRST COMMUNITY SAID IT**

The practice of the earliest Muslims around this sentence was watched closely and reported, which shows that it was never treated as a small matter of preference. Anas ibn Mālik, who prayed behind the Prophet and behind the caliphs after him, reported that they began the recitation with the praise of God and did not mention the name aloud, and the report is carried by Muslim. Abū Hurayrah recited the name aloud when he led the prayer in Madinah, and he said afterwards that his prayer was the closest of them all to the prayer of the Messenger of God; that report too is carried by Muslim. A further report, carried by Abū Dāwūd from Ibn ʿAbbās, says that the Prophet did not know a sūrah had ended until the name was revealed, which is how the community learned where one sūrah finished and the next began. On that basis the schools of law took their two positions. One counts the sentence as the first verse of this sūrah. The other counts it as a verse revealed to separate the sūrahs rather than as part of them. Neither position questions the practice of beginning with the name. The difference between them turns on how the seven verses are counted, not on whether the words are said. The reader is therefore not asked to take a side in order to obey the verse. He is asked to say the name, and the saying is what the verse settles.

**A PRACTICE TAUGHT AT THE TABLE AND IN THE HOME**

The omission of a particular verb allows the name to accompany many lawful acts, and the Prophetic practice shows how that breadth becomes discipline rather than a vague slogan. ʿUmar ibn Abī Salamah reports that, when he ate as a child under the Prophet's care, the Prophet told him, *"Mention Allah's name, eat with your right hand, and eat from what is nearest to you"* (Muslim). Ibn ʿAbbās reports that the Prophet also taught a married couple to mention God's name and ask protection before intimacy, tying the same opening words to the formation of a household (al-Bukhārī, Muslim). The Qur'an gives the name a legal consequence in another setting: (6:121 — **“Do not eat of what is not slaughtered in Allah’s Name.”**). These uses explain each other. The name is not a verbal decoration placed on an act after its moral character has been decided, and it cannot make a forbidden act lawful. It declares that a permitted act is undertaken by God's leave, under His command and with dependence upon Him. Eating, family life and slaughter differ greatly, yet each involves receiving or taking life, and each is therefore placed under the name of the giver of life. This is why the unspoken verb matters. The form can mean that a person begins by the name and seeks help by the name at once. The servant supplies the act, while the wording supplies its direction and limit.

**THE MARK A PERSON SETS ON THE DAY'S WORK**

Think of a craftsman who stamps the mark of his workshop into every piece he finishes, whether the piece is a door for a palace or a stool for a kitchen. The mark does not make the work better, and a buyer may never look at it. Yet it decides whose the piece is and who answers for it. The sentence in this verse gives the servant a mark of the same kind, and it costs him one breath. A person who begins his work with the name of God has already answered the question of whom he works for. The answer steadies him when the work grows difficult or when the gain grows tempting. A task done under a name is done under an eye. Anyone who has watched a colleague do work he did not intend will recognise how much the first sentence of a day decides, and how little it costs. These days the hours are crowded with beginnings, from messages and purchases to journeys and meetings. The verse teaches the servant to put the name at the head of those beginnings rather than at the edges of his life, so that the whole of the day stands inside one short sentence.

## Verse 1:2

> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds

**A PRAISE WIDER THAN THANKSGIVING**

***“All praise is for Allah”*** opens the sūrah's second sentence, and the wording carries more than the English word suggests. Praise is the extolling of one who is worthy of it, and it is spoken of qualities before it is spoken of gifts. The Book uses both words, and the difference between them teaches the reader something about the God it names. The verse itself is built on that difference, and the reports explain it: Ibn ʿAbbās said that the sentence is the statement of appreciation, and that when the servant says it God answers that His servant has praised Him. The point is stated in the plainest form by the teachers of the language. Praise is given for what the one praised is in himself and for what he does; thanks are given for what he has given. Because praise reaches further than thanks, the Book does not say *all thanks is for Allah* in this place, and it does not leave the sentence to a time of good fortune alone. That is why a person is told to say that all praise is for Allah, and not that some of it is. The definite article in the Arabic does the same work as the word *all* in the translation. It gathers every kind of praise, from every voice, at every time.

**THE LORD OF EVERY WORLD**

The second half of the verse gives God the title ***“Lord of all worlds”***, and each part of that title deserves its own explanation. The word *rabb* in Arabic carries three ideas at once: a master who is obeyed, an owner who possesses what he holds, and one who cultivates what he owns until it reaches its proper state. The last of the three is the most surprising in this place, because it describes the care of a farmer for a field and the care of a parent for a child, and the verse applies it to God without any other qualification. It is for this reason that some said the title points to the sustaining of every creature through every stage of its life, and others said it points to the authority of the one who orders all affairs. Ibn ʿAbbās explained the phrase as the Lord of everything that has a spirit and walks the earth, and also of the dwellers of the heavens, and he added that it means the Creator, the Provider, and the one who changes all things from one state into another. The Book sets the title on the same pattern in more than forty places, and it also puts it in the mouths of the favoured: (26:192 — **“This is certainly a revelation from the Lord of all worlds”**). Its widest use in the Qur'an comes in a sentence reporting what the people of Paradise say, where the title is joined to praise and rises above every particular creature: (10:10 — **“their closing prayer will be, “All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds”**).

The plural ***“worlds”*** prevents the title from being reduced to human rule alone. Qatādah read every class of created being as a world, while other early explanations named humankind, jinn, angels and the creatures of land and sea. The readings differ in enumeration but agree in scope: nothing created stands outside the lordship in this sentence. The Qur'an itself explains the title when Pharaoh asks Moses, (26:23 — **“Pharaoh asked, “And what is ‘the Lord of all worlds’?””**), and Moses answers, (26:24 — **“˹He is˺ the Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between, if only you had sure faith.””**). The answer moves from a title to creation because the ordered worlds are the evidence of their Lord. Their variety does not divide His care. The differences of human speech are likewise counted among His signs: (30:22 — **“And one of His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth, and the diversity of your languages”**). Lordship therefore includes creating each world, preserving it, moving it from one condition to another and bringing it to the end appointed for it. The corresponding condition of every creature is need, however strong it appears inside its own small world: (35:15 — **“O humanity! It is you who stand in need of Allah, but Allah ˹alone˺ is the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy”**).

**THE SENTENCE AS GOD HEARS IT**

The reports add a layer to this sentence that no private reading of it can reach. Abū Hurayrah reports that the Prophet said that God says, *"I have divided the prayer between Myself and My servant into two halves, and My servant shall have what he asks"*, and that when the servant says *"All praise is for Allah, Lord of all worlds"*, God says, *"My servant has praised Me"* (Muslim). The report makes the recitation a conversation rather than a declaration. A person says the words in the direction of his own mouth, and the answer is already spoken before the sentence is finished. The same report explains why the sūrah is called the prayer in the first place, since its words are what God and the servant exchange in every unit. ʿAlī, the fourth of the caliphs, described the sentence as a statement God loved for Himself, was pleased with for Himself, and loves to have repeated. The sentence is therefore not reserved for the prayer mat. It is the language of a person who has understood where his food came from.

**PRAISE AS REMEMBRANCE AND SUPPLICATION**

The reports place ***“All praise is for Allah”*** among both remembrance and prayer. Jābir ibn ʿAbdullāh reports that the Prophet said, *"The best remembrance is that there is no god but Allah, and the best supplication is praise of Allah"* (al-Tirmidhī). Praise can be supplication without naming a particular need because it acknowledges the giver, confesses dependence and approaches Him through an attribute He loves. A demand may seek one gift while overlooking every gift already present; praise gathers the gifts and the giver before any demand is made. This is also why praise after food is not payment for the food. The servant could not enumerate the bodily powers, provision, safety and guidance gathered in a single meal, as the Qur'an states: (16:18 — **“If you tried to count Allah’s blessings, you would never be able to number them. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”**). Praise answers that inability with truthful completeness: every praise belongs to God even when the speaker cannot list every reason. Gratitude then has a promised consequence, (14:7 — **“If you are grateful, I will certainly give you more.”**), but the verse's order keeps increase from becoming the sole motive. God is praised first because He is worthy, and His gifts are then received as effects of that worthiness.

## Verse 1:3

> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**MERCY REPEATED AFTER POWER**

The two names appear at the head of the sūrah and then again in this verse: ***“the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”***. The repetition is the first thing the reader notices, and the reports explain why the words return. The verse before this one has set down the lordship of God over everything, and that is a heavy thing to measure. The verse therefore follows the title of power with the names of mercy, so that fear of the Master is met by hope in Him. Mercy in this sentence is placed where a person would have put judgement, and every reader who has done wrong is meant to notice that. The two names are not a single name written twice. The first describes mercy in itself, without limit or condition, and it is the wider of the two; the second describes mercy that arrives at the one who receives it. Ibn ʿAbbās gave both names the sense of gentleness. Another early reading says that mercy in the first name reaches the whole creation while mercy in the second belongs to those who are guided. The reading is therefore that the verse announces an arrangement: everything that lives is held inside the first name, and the second name carries a person through to the end.

**THE ORDER IN WHICH THE NAMES STAND**

The placement of the two names is not an ornament of style, and its purpose is stated in the Book itself. The one who makes a world and orders it also holds its people to account, and the Qur'an puts the two facts side by side. A verse of the same rhythm announces both: (15:49 — **“Inform My servants ˹O Prophet˺ that I am truly the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”**), and the next verse completes the sentence: (15:50 — **“and that My torment is indeed the most painful”**). Another verse gathers the two attributes in one breath while describing the gifts of this life: (6:165 — **“Surely your Lord is swift in punishment, but He is certainly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”**). A person who hears only lordship will either despair or grow careless, and a person who hears only mercy will grow careless in a worse way. The reports carry a sentence of the Prophet that holds the two together and steadies a person between them. On Abū Hurayrah's authority, the Prophet said, *"If the believer knew what punishment Allah has, none would hope for His Paradise, and if the disbeliever knew what mercy Allah has, none would despair of His mercy"* (Muslim). The verse repeats the two names because the reader needs both, and because neither one alone was ever meant to be recited.

The Qur'an speaks of this mercy not only as an attribute but as a commitment God has declared concerning His servants. In one sentence ownership, mercy and resurrection stand together: (6:12 — **“He has taken upon Himself to be Merciful.”**). The promise of mercy does not erase the gathering, and the gathering does not cancel the promise. Another passage turns the same declaration toward a person who has sinned: (6:54 — **“Your Lord has taken upon Himself to be Merciful.”**), and it completes the condition with repentance and reform. Mercy is therefore neither indifference to evil nor permission to persist in it. It opens return and receives the one who returns. The angels who carry the Throne are taught to plead by the same attribute, saying, (40:7 — **“Our Lord! You encompass everything in ˹Your˺ mercy and knowledge.”**). Knowledge appears beside mercy because perfect mercy is never blind sentiment; it reaches its object with full knowledge of need, guilt, repentance and consequence. The repeated names after the title of lordship thus explain the manner of divine nurture. Creation is not maintained by bare power. It is maintained under a mercy that gives, warns, receives repentance and still brings every person to judgement.

**A LINE DRAWN AROUND THE DIVINE NAME**

The two names differ in another way, and the difference shapes how a person may use them. The first may not be given to a created being at all, since it points to mercy that is the source of existence itself and belongs to God alone. The second may be used of a creature, and the Book in fact applies it to the Prophet in a verse praising his care for the believers: (9:128 — **“gracious and merciful to the believers”**). This is not a detail of vocabulary but a line drawn around worship. A servant may be merciful, and the Book praises the quality and commands it in its followers; a servant may not be the Most Compassionate, because the word carries the giving of being itself. The Book also shows that the two names are a door through which a person calls on God, and it places them together as the object of supplication: (17:110 — **“Call upon Allah or call upon the Most Compassionate—whichever you call, He has the Most Beautiful Names”**). The verse therefore teaches the servant what to call his Lord, and it settles the fear it first raised.

**MERCY THAT REACHES THE FAMILY BOND**

The revealed use of the first name also binds belief about mercy to the treatment of kin. The connection does not make human affection equal to divine mercy. It makes care for relatives one created effect and visible witness of that mercy. The Qur'an therefore joins reverence for God to the command to honour kin: (4:1 — **“family ties. Surely Allah is ever Watchful over you”**). A person cannot recite ***“the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”*** as an abstract description while treating dependence, age or family weakness as an inconvenience without moral claim. The names first teach what belongs perfectly to God, then disclose the standard under which limited human mercy must work. Family care does not exhaust that work, but it tests it where familiarity often erodes patience. The command gives the repeated names an ethical consequence without reducing them to an ethical slogan: mercy remains God's attribute, and maintaining the bonds He created becomes an act performed under it.

## Verse 1:4

> Master of the Day of Judgment

**TWO WAYS OF RECITING ONE WORD**

The fourth sentence names God as ***“Master of the Day of Judgment”***, and the first word of it is read in two ways that the community has received and recited. One reading gives the word as *mālik*, an owner; the other gives it as *malik*, a king. Both are transmitted readings of the same word, and both are true of God, so no reader is asked to choose between them. The difference between the two is not a difference about God but a difference of emphasis. An owner holds a thing and may dispose of it, and a king rules and commands; every king is an owner in this sense, while not every owner is a king. Those who preferred *malik* said that it is the more emphatic, because kingship is said of people and not only of property. Those who preferred *mālik* said that it reaches further, because ownership covers everything owned, while kingship belongs to the government of persons. The verse leaves both readings standing, and a person who recites it in either way has recited the same truth. The next word settles what kind of day is meant. *Al-Dīn* in this place is requital, the day on which every deed is repaid, and the earlier authorities explained it as the day of reckoning and the settling of disputes between created beings. Ibn ʿAbbās said of it that people will be judged according to their deeds, and that no judge will sit there except God.

**WHY THIS DAY IS SINGLED OUT**

A reader may ask why the verse ties mastery to one day, when God is master of every day. The answer the reports give is that on that day the claim of every other owner will be stripped away. Today a person speaks of what is his, defends it in courts, and writes it into documents; on that day the documents will mean nothing and the titles will belong to one. The Book states it as a question put to a silent assembly: (40:16 — **“Who does all authority belong to this Day? To Allah—the One, the Supreme”**). It states it again in a verse that gathers the whole earth into one hand: (39:67 — **“the ˹whole˺ earth will be in His Grip, and the heavens will be rolled up in His Right Hand”**). It states it once more in the plainest of its sentences: (82:19 — **“all authority on that Day belongs to Allah ˹entirely˺”**). The silence of that day is described in several places, and the description supports the same reading. No one will speak without permission: (78:38 — **“None will talk, except those granted permission by the Most Compassionate and whose words are true”**), and again: (11:105 — **“no one will dare speak except with His permission”**). A day on which no one may speak is a day on which no one may claim. A report carried by al-Bukhārī and Muslim, on the authority of Abū Hurayrah, has the Prophet describing that day in these words: *"Allah will grasp the earth and fold up the sky with His Right Hand and say, 'I am the King; where are the kings of the earth?'"*

**RECOMPENSE DOWN TO THE SMALLEST WEIGHT**

The word translated ***“Judgment”*** also carries the sense of recompense: a return fitted to what was sent ahead. This is why Ibn ʿAbbās explained the day as the time when good is repaid with good and evil with evil, except where God pardons. The verse does not describe a rough balancing in which small acts disappear beneath larger ones. It names a mastery under which the scale itself is just: (21:47 — **“We will set up the scales of justice on the Day of Judgment, so no soul will be wronged in the least.”**). The same passage says that even the weight of a mustard seed will be brought forward. Two of the shortest sentences in the Book make the reach of this accounting unmistakable: (99:7 — **“So whoever does an atom’s weight of good will see it”**) and (99:8 — **“And whoever does an atom’s weight of evil will see it”**). This precision explains why the last day is not merely an end to history. It is the disclosure of history's moral meaning. A kindness hidden from every witness and an injury protected by worldly power are both carried beyond death. The title ***“Master”*** guarantees not only that the hearing will occur but that its result can be enforced. The Book therefore joins payment to the removal of injustice: (40:17 — **“Today every soul will be rewarded for what it has done. No injustice Today! Surely Allah is swift in reckoning.””**). Mastery, knowledge and justice meet in the same day.

**THE DAY THAT DISCIPLINES THE PRESENT**

What a person believes about the last day changes how he spends the present one, and the verse puts that change in a single word. A merchant who knows that a full audit is coming keeps his ledger as he trades. He does not wait until the accounts are called for, because a ledger kept honestly in the course of the year is a different object from a ledger reconstructed under pressure. The knowledge that the Day of Judgment belongs to a master disciplines the ordinary transactions of a life. It tells a person that no wrong will go unrecorded simply because it went unnoticed, and that no good will go unpaid simply because it went unthanked. It also puts the ambitions of the powerful in their place. The verse names a day that no king can postpone and no court can overrule, and the naming is a comfort to everyone who has been treated unjustly by the strong. The Book turns this belief into a command for the present: (2:254 — **“a Day when there will be no bargaining, friendship, or intercession”**). The verse is recited in the middle of the sūrah's praise, so that the reader praises the Lord who is master of the day on which the accounts are settled. The order of the words teaches something in itself: the praise comes first, and the judgement is named as part of the reason for praise.

**A COURT THAT NEVER LOSES A FILE**

Think of a court whose files cannot be lost, whose witnesses cannot be bribed, and whose judge cannot be replaced. Such a court would make an honest man easy and a dishonest one uneasy, whether or not he ever appeared in it. The Day of Judgment works on human conduct in exactly that way, and the verse names its owner so that the reader knows whose court it is. A person who begins his prayer with this sentence begins it inside a courtroom that is not yet in session, and the knowledge steadies him or warns him according to what he carries. These days the world is loud about the things that can be measured and quiet about the things that are being recorded, and the verse speaks into that quiet. It does not ask a person to live in fear of a tyrant's whim. It asks him to live in the presence of a master who is perfectly just and who never sleeps through a hearing.

## Verse 1:5

> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help

**THE ADDRESS TURNS AROUND**

Up to this sentence the sūrah has spoken about God, and in this sentence it begins to speak to Him: ***“You ˹alone˺ we worship”***. The change is the hinge of the whole chapter, and the earlier authorities marked it. Until here the words were report and praise; from here they are address and request, and the sūrah never returns to the third person again. Nothing in the wording announces the turn, and that is part of its effect. Praise that never became address would leave the servant outside the sentence, looking at his Lord from a distance rather than standing in front of Him. A person reciting the sūrah finds himself carried from speaking about his Lord to standing in front of Him without a single word of transition. The Book uses the same movement elsewhere and names it plainly: (19:93 — **“There is none in the heavens or the earth who will not return to the Most Compassionate in full submission”**). The servant speaks as one of a company and not as a single man. He does not say *I worship*, and the reports explain the force of that choice. It joins the reciter to every other worshipper, so that he stands in a row rather than alone. It also keeps him from the self-importance of a private conversation, since a person who says *we* has already admitted that he is one of many. The plural also answers a difficulty that the solitude of private prayer creates, because the servant may imagine that he is the only one standing there. Some said that the choice gathers the whole creation into the sentence, since every created thing is a servant of God whether or not it knows it, and the human being is given the honour of saying on their behalf what the rest of them cannot say in words.

**THE OBJECT PLACED BEFORE THE VERB**

***“You ˹alone˺ we worship”*** carries its exclusiveness in the order of the Arabic words, and the order is the first thing the early teachers explained. The object is placed before the verb, and in Arabic that placement restricts the action to the object alone: it is You we worship, and no one else. The same construction then appears in the second half of the verse, so that worship and the request for help are both sealed against every rival. The word for worship itself deserves the explanation it received. It names the utmost humility before one who is loved and feared, and its root carries the sense of subduing. Ibn ʿAbbās explained the first half of the verse as turning to God as the only God and obeying Him, and he explained the second half as asking His help in that worship and drawing confidence from Him in obedience. The religion the Book teaches is gathered here, since the verse gives God what belongs to Him and then admits that the servant cannot deliver it without help. The order also teaches that a person's own strength is a gift rather than a possession, because the help he asks for is asked from the one he serves. The Book states the purpose of creation in the same terms: (51:56 — **“I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me”**), and it announces the same demand through every messenger: (21:25 — **“There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Me, so worship Me ˹alone˺”**).

**WORSHIP AND THE STRENGTH TO CARRY IT**

The verse joins ***“You ˹alone˺ we worship”*** to ***“and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help”***, and the order of the two halves teaches its own lesson. A person might think that a command to worship needs only to be heard and obeyed, and the verse corrects that thought by making the servant ask for assistance in the same breath. The help requested reaches further than the prayer mat. It is help in worship and help in everything else, and the reports describe the earliest readers asking for both. Ibn ʿAbbās read the request as help in the worship itself and as confidence drawn from God in obedience, and that reading keeps the two halves tied together. The Book commands the same pair: (2:45 — **“And seek help through patience and prayer”**), and it joins the two in a sentence addressed to the Prophet: (11:123 — **“So worship Him and put your trust in Him”**). Some of the earliest Muslims said that the Fātiḥah is the secret of the Qur'an and that these two words are the secret of the Fātiḥah, and the saying explains itself. A person who has said them has declared who he serves and who he relies on, and those two declarations are the whole of the religious life. The first half of the verse also clears the ground of every partner. Whatever a person leans on besides God, whether wealth, strength, or the opinion of others, is denied a share in the sentence before it is even mentioned.

The repeated ***“You ˹alone˺”*** before each verb also prevents one half from being swallowed by the other. Worship and the request for help are related, but each is independently restricted to God in the ultimate sense intended here. Asking another person for an ordinary means within that person's ability is not the rival the verse excludes. The Qur'an itself describes a man calling to Moses for help against an attacker: (28:15 — **“and the other of his enemies. The man from his people called to him for help against his foe.”**). That was a request for an available human act, not worship. The verse instead denies that any creature possesses power independently of God, hears the unseen appeal as God hears it, or deserves the inward dependence owed to Him. Its grammar therefore corrects both idolatry and self-reliance. Means may be used, but they are not made lords. The request is also left without a stated object: it does not say *help us to do this one act*. The first need is the ability to fulfil the declaration just made. A servant cannot worship sincerely, remain constant or escape pride by treating those achievements as self-produced. This is why another command joins exclusive trust to exclusive worship: (73:9 — **“so take Him ˹alone˺ as a Trustee of Affairs”**). The doubled construction gives action to the servant without giving him independence, and gives all effective power to God without turning obedience into passivity.

**THE HAND THAT HOLDS THE TOOL**

Picture an apprentice who works under a master craftsman. He brings the tool and the material to the bench, and he does the work that has been set for him. He cannot make the wood, and he cannot make his own hand steady, and both came from the one who taught him. The verse is the apprentice's sentence, and its two halves match the two things he must do. He must work at the task that was assigned, and he must ask the one who assigned it for the steadiness to do it. A person who tries to carry the first without the second burns out, and a person who asks for the second without doing the first is asking for nothing. The gathered voice of the sentence does the same work in a person's private life that the prayer hall does in a community. These days a person is trained to be self-sufficient and told that asking for help is a weakness, while the verse makes asking for help part of worship itself. The servant says both halves in every unit of prayer, and the two halves stay in their order: the worship first, and the request for help immediately behind it.

## Verse 1:6

> Guide us along the Straight Path

**A REQUEST FROM PEOPLE WHO ARE ALREADY GUIDED**

The petition begins here: ***“Guide us along the Straight Path”***. A reader may wonder why a person who has already been guided asks for guidance again, and the earlier teachers took up that question. The answer lies in what the word carries. Guidance in the Qur'an is not one thing. Some of it is the showing of the road, and that has been given to every human being through the messengers and through the reason God planted in him. Some of it is the enabling that keeps a person walking once he has seen the road, and some of it is the leading that brings him to the destination in safety. A guided person asks for the second and the third, and he asks for them because they are renewed at every step and not handed over once and left. Ibn ʿAbbās read the request as a plea to be kept firm in holding the religion, and another reading of his explains it as a plea for the religion God is pleased with. The Book puts the same request into the mouth of people who had already received guidance: (3:8 — **“Do not let our hearts deviate after you have guided us”**), and the wording shows that the danger the guided person fears is not blindness but slipping. The verse is also placed after the declaration of worship, which teaches that the road is walked by a servant who has already put himself in the right relation to his Lord.

**THE ROAD THAT HAS NO BRANCHES**

The Arabic word for the path in this verse describes a road that is wide and clear, and the scholars of the language agreed that it names a way with no turns and no forks. The term for the straight in it adds the same thing: the road runs to its destination without bending. Ibn ʿAbbās read the phrase as the established religion, and he added that it may mean the Book itself, so that asking for the path is asking to be held to what the Book commands and to what it forbids. Another reading of his says that the request is for firmness in holding fast, which makes the prayer a plea for grip rather than for information. The Book uses the same image in a verse that sets the straight way against the many ways that lead away from it: (6:153 — **“that is My Path—perfectly straight”**), and it gives worship as the road's content in another verse: (36:61 — **“This is the Straight Path”**). The straightness of the path carries a further sense, and it is the one the teachers pressed most often. The path is a middle. The reports describe it as the road that avoids two extremes: the one that falls short of what God asked and the one that goes past it. A person who adds to the religion out of zeal and a person who drops from it out of laziness have both left the same road, one on each side. That is why the request is phrased as a road and not as a rule-book, since a rule-book can be learned without being walked.

**BEING SHOWN THE WAY AND BEING CARRIED ALONG IT**

The wording asks for more than directions to the entrance. The construction can carry both being guided to the path and being guided along it, which joins knowledge of the truth to the strength to act upon it. The Qur'an distinguishes these senses without separating them. It says of the Prophet, (42:52 — **“And you are truly leading ˹all˺ to the Straight Path—”**), because a messenger shows and teaches the road; it also states, (2:272 — **“it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills.”**), because inward acceptance and success are His gift. A person may therefore know the rule while lacking resolve, begin an act without finishing it, or perform it without sincerity. ***“Guide us”*** asks for sound knowledge, a willing heart, correct action and continuance together. This also explains why guidance admits increase rather than existing only as a yes-or-no possession: (47:17 — **“As for those who are ˹rightly˺ guided, He increases them in guidance and blesses them with righteousness”**). Each act of obedience opens a need for clearer knowledge and firmer obedience after it. The prayer is renewed because none of these gifts becomes self-sustaining once received.

The Prophet gave the path a detailed form in a report from al-Nawwās ibn Samʿān. A straight road has walls on both sides, open doors in the walls and curtains over the doors. A caller at the road's entrance summons everyone to enter and not turn aside, while another caller above warns anyone who reaches for a curtain not to open it. The Prophet explained that the road is Islam, the walls are God's limits, the open doors are what He has forbidden, the caller at the entrance is the Book of God, and the warning from above is God's admonition in the heart of a Muslim (Musnad Aḥmad). The image adds something the bare idea of straightness does not. Departure often begins before a person walks another road; it begins when he lifts a curtain to inspect an opening beside the one he was told to follow. The limits are walls because they preserve movement toward the destination, not because they prevent it. Guidance thus includes a revealed map, an outward boundary and an inward warning. The call at the gate is addressed to a people entering together, while each person must still refuse the door nearest to him.

**GUIDANCE ASKED FOR AT EVERY STEP**

The request is short, and its shortness is part of its design. It asks for one thing, and the thing it asks for is the whole of a life, so the sūrah puts it in the mouth of the reciter at every standing in prayer. The Book describes the state of those who are guided in a sentence that makes the gift sound like a walk rather than a single event: (10:9 — **“their Lord will guide them ˹to Paradise˺ through their faith”**), where the guidance of the road and the arrival at the end are joined. A person who asks for guidance honestly will find the request changing his conduct before it changes his circumstances. He will start to check the road he is on, and the checking is itself part of the answer. The plural form of the request continues from the previous verse, so the person asking asks for a road for his community and not for himself alone. That choice also protects the request from selfishness, since a person who asks for guidance for a company of people has already admitted that he expects to walk with them. It also makes the request a public one, and a public request is harder to abandon quietly than a private wish.

**A LAMP ON A MOUNTAIN ROAD**

Picture a traveller on a mountain road at night. He carries a lamp that lights only the next stretch of ground, and he walks the whole pass by that small circle of light. He does not see the summit when he sets out, and he does not need to. The request in this verse is the traveller's prayer for the next stretch, and the answer is given in the same measure as the walking. A person who waits until he can see the whole road before he takes a step will stand at the bottom of the mountain for the rest of his life. These days a person is offered endless information about the way to live and very little guidance about which way to take, and the difference between the two is exactly what this verse asks God to supply. The servant says it at every prayer he makes, and by the end of a life it has been said more often than any other sentence he knows. The repetition is the point. A road is walked one stretch at a time, and the prayer is made for the stretch that is next.

## Verse 1:7

> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray

**A PATH MARKED BY THE PEOPLE ON IT**

The sūrah has asked for the straight path, and this verse tells the reader where that path runs by naming the people who are on it: ***“the Path of those You have blessed”***. The change of method is deliberate. A road can be described by its surface and its direction, and a person may still fail to find it; a road can also be described by the company travelling on it, and then the seeker knows both the way and the destination. The Book identifies the blessed company in a verse that gathers four ranks in one sentence: (4:69 — **“the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous”**), and it adds its own approval of the company: *"what honourable company"*. Ibn ʿAbbās read the blessed as the followers of the prophets who held to the religion while the favour lasted, and he also read them as the prophets themselves. Both readings point in the same direction, because the prophets are the leaders of the road and those who follow them are on it behind them. The request therefore becomes a request for company as much as for direction. A person who asks to be among the blessed has asked to be placed with people whose steps he can see, and the request is answered in the community before it is answered anywhere else.

**KNOWLEDGE THAT TURNED AWAY, STRIVING THAT MISSED**

The verse then states what the path is not, and it names two kinds of people rather than one: ***“not those You are displeased with”***, and ***“or those who are astray”***. The Prophet explained the two in a report carried by al-Tirmidhī. The Companion Adi ibn Hatim asked him about the two descriptions, and he answered that the first are those who knew the truth and turned away from it, and the second are those who sought a way and missed it. The two failures are therefore not the same failure, and the reports draw the line carefully. One is the failure of a person whose knowledge did not reach his conduct, and the other is the failure of a person whose effort did not reach the knowledge. The Book names both in other places, and it warns against each. It warns against the second wherever zeal follows inherited desire instead of revelation. It warns against the first by describing a people whose hearts were left unprotected: (2:90 — **“They have earned wrath upon wrath”**). The two failures also explain why the prayer asks for a road rather than for a prize, since both are failures of travelling and neither is a failure of receiving. The two are also joined in the reading of the verse as a single warning: the prayer asks for a road on which a person knows and acts, since the two failures are knowledge without action and action without knowledge, and the straight road is the one on which the two are held together.

The Prophetic explanation also names the communities in whose history these two patterns became especially clear. The same Companion reports that the Prophet identified the people marked by displeasure with the Jews and those marked by straying with the Christians; al-Tirmidhī transmitted the report and judged it fair but uncommon. The identification is evidence, but it does not turn the verse into a claim about blood or ancestry. The descriptions are moral and theological: the prayer is spoken by Muslims so that they do not reproduce either failure. The Qur'an attaches displeasure to acts of rebellion and false worship, not to an unexplained label: (5:60 — **“It is those who earned Allah’s condemnation and displeasure”**). It likewise explains straying through excess, imitation and the misleading of others: (5:77 — **“nor follow the vain desires of those who went astray before ˹you˺. They misled many and strayed from the Right Way.””**). Both cited clauses describe conduct and consequence. A person who possesses revelation but trades its demand for advantage enters the first danger, while a person whose devotion outruns revealed knowledge enters the second. Jews and Christians can bear features of both, as can anyone else; the report identifies the predominant historical examples, while the prayer makes their failures a daily warning for the reciter. Its force would be lost if a Muslim used it only to name others.

The grammar reinforces that warning with a precise distribution of agency. In ***“those You have blessed”***, the gift is attributed directly to God. The Arabic construction rendered ***“those You are displeased with”*** names people upon whom displeasure has fallen without making the divine name its grammatical subject, while ***“those who are astray”*** attaches the wandering to the travellers themselves. The translation makes the source of displeasure explicit, and the change of construction does not deny that every event falls under divine sovereignty. It teaches the courtesy of naming grace directly from its giver while describing failure through the conduct that earned it. The same arrangement appears throughout the prayer: the servant asks God for guidance, but he must walk, obey and refuse the side roads. Blessing is therefore not merely comfort or material abundance. It is the favour of knowledge joined to faithful action, identified by the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs and the righteous. Displeasure and straying are the breaking apart of those two gifts. One abandons action after knowledge; the other acts without sound knowledge. By ending on both, the sūrah refuses the idea that sincerity can replace truth or that information can replace obedience.

**WHY A PRAYER ABOUT GUIDANCE ENDS IN REFUSAL**

The last words of the sūrah are words of denial, and their place there teaches something about the nature of the request. A person who asks for a road asks by implication to be kept from every other road, and the sūrah states the implication rather than leaving it to be worked out. A prayer that named only the destination would be a wish; a prayer that also names the ways it refuses is a commitment. The recitation ends here, and the reports teach what follows the recitation. Abū Hurayrah preserves in al-Bukhārī and Muslim the Prophet's instruction: *"When the imam says Ameen, say Ameen"*. The word is a request that the prayer be answered, and it comes at the end of a sentence that has refused two failures by name. Nothing more is added after the refusal. The refusal does the work of a fence set beside a cliff, and the fence is part of the road and not an extra beside it. The absence of a closing clause is itself a lesson in the sūrah's method, because the reader is left holding a request rather than a conclusion. It also explains why the sūrah can be recited again a few minutes later without losing its point: a road prayer is spoken while the road is being walked. The sūrah does not close with a description of reward or a promise of rest. It closes with a person standing at the head of a road, having said whom he follows, whom he refuses to follow, and where he asks to be taken.

**THE RESPONSE SHARED WITH THE ANGELS**

The word said after this verse is not an eighth verse and does not add another idea to the sūrah. Ameen asks God to answer the petition already completed. The Prophetic instruction gives that response a communal and heavenly setting. Abū Hurayrah reports that the Prophet said that when the imam says Ameen, the congregation should say it, for the one whose response coincides with the response of the angels receives forgiveness for earlier sins (al-Bukhārī, Muslim). Abū Mūsā reports a shorter promise attached directly to the closing words: *"When the imam says, ‘or those who are astray,’ say Ameen; Allah will answer you"* (Muslim). These reports show that the congregation does not merely listen to the imam finish another person's request. Each worshipper adopts the request, confirms it and seeks its answer. The timing also joins earth to heaven: voices that may differ in language and strength are directed toward one petition and measured by sincerity rather than volume. The sūrah has moved from praise, to covenant, to need, and the response seals that movement without competing with it. Ameen belongs after the verse precisely because the last words remain a prayer awaiting an answer.
