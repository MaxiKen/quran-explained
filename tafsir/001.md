# Sūrah Al-Fatihah (Chapter 1) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir

## Introduction to the Sūrah

Al-Fātiḥah is the sūrah a Muslim says more often than any other words in the Qur'an, and the books that open with it begin by explaining why. Three names are recorded for it in the earliest material. Al-Baghawī gives the reason behind each one: it opens the written Book and the recitation, so it is the Opener; it is the root the rest of the Book grows from, so it is the Mother of the Qur'an; and its seven lines return in every unit of every prayer, so it is the Seven Repeated Verses. Most of the early scholars held it to be Makkan, revealed before the migration, and al-Alūsī records the same preference with the report that it counts among the first things sent down.

What the sūrah contains is described in the books as a summary of the whole religion. Al-Saʿdī reads it as a gathering of the three kinds of oneness the Qur'an teaches: God as creator and provider in the words about the Lord of all worlds, God in His names and qualities in the two names of mercy. God's right to be worshipped in the pledge that follows. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān makes the same point from the shape of the text, which begins by praising God and ends by asking Him for something, so that a reader learns the order of prayer before he learns its words.

The Prophet ﷺ described the sūrah as a conversation, and the books use that description as their key. Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim carries the report. Ibn Kathīr places at the head of his commentary and al-Baghawī quotes in full: God has divided the prayer between Himself and His servant, half for each, and the servant receives what he asks for. Al-Qurṭubī draws from it that the sūrah is not a text recited at God but a dialogue in which the worshipper speaks and is answered, verse by verse, from the first praise to the last request.

The books also show a reader how much is riding on a text he may have learned as a child. Ibn Kathīr records, from Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, that there is no prayer for the one who does not recite the Opener of the Book. The same chapter of the Ṣaḥīḥ reports that a companion used it as a healing recitation over a man who had been bitten, with the Prophet's approval. A community that repeats a text five times a day will either come to know it deeply or stop hearing it. The order of this sūrah — praise, mercy, ownership, a pledge, a request, a warning — is arranged to prevent the second outcome.

One question about the opening line runs through every classical book, and the disagreement is old and well documented. Al-Ṭabarī argues that the line beginning with the name of God is not counted as a verse of this sūrah, because the Book never places two identical lines side by side with nothing between them. Al-Qurṭubī sets out the positions with the scholars who held them: Mālik counted it as no verse of any sūrah, Ibn al-Mubārak counted it as opening every sūrah, and al-Shāfiʿī counted it as a verse of this one. Ibn ʿUthaymīn taught the same conclusion as al-Ṭabarī from the report of the division of the prayer, and Maʿārif al-Qurʾān records Abū Ḥanīfah's view that it is a verse revealed on its own to separate one sūrah from the next. The community recites it either way, and the sūrah is seven lines on the reading the Prophet ﷺ taught.

## Verse 1:1

> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**HOW THE BOOK TEACHES A PERSON TO BEGIN**

The sūrah opens with ***“In the Name of Allah”***, and the first thing the books notice is that the sentence is unfinished. The word for name is joined to God by a particle that needs a verb after it, and the verb is left out, because the speaker is not describing a rule but announcing an act. The grammar carries a lesson of its own. The particle needs a verb, the verb is left out, and al-Ṭabarī's reading of the omission is that the reader supplies it himself. That is why al-Saʿdī reads the opening as a promise to begin every act through the name of God rather than as a title. Why the same word for name is used in the singular: it stands for a noun joined to its owner, the way any single thing stands for its kind.

God did not leave the habit to be discovered. The habit was taught rather than discovered. Al-Ṭabarī reports that the Prophet ﷺ was trained to put the names of God in front of all his actions, and that the practice was then made a pattern for the whole creation. The history behind that training is supplied by Maʿārif al-Qurʾān. Before Islam, Arabs began their journeys and their dealings with the names of idols. The first words revealed to the Prophet ﷺ redirected the custom rather than banning the act of invocation: (96:1 — **“in the Name of your Lord Who created—”**). The community inherited the correction, and this sūrah keeps it at the front of the Book, where no reader can avoid it.

The merits the book reports for the line are not incidental, and they explain the care taken over its wording. A note from al-Suyūṭī carried in Maʿārif al-Qurʾān says that the divine books before the Qur'an also opened with the name of God, and the same book records the saying that the meanings of the Book are gathered in the Fātiḥah, and the meanings of the Fātiḥah in this line. The report that the words were dictated in stages — first the name alone, then with the first title, then the full line — is collected by al-Alūsī, who treats it as evidence that the opening was taught rather than improvised. Whether a reader accepts the story or not, he is looking at a sentence the community received before it composed anything.

Think of a house key kept on a hook by the door, where every member of the family has to take it before leaving. The key does not open the destination; it opens the house that makes the journey possible. A reading at that level of plainness is what al-Jalālayn gives of the line. The report in which the Prophet ﷺ named this sūrah the Mother of the Book and the Seven Repeated Verses, graded sound by al-Tirmidhī, stands in Ibn Kathīr's opening too. Everything else in the chapter arrives after a person has reached for the key.

**THE TWO TITLES AND WHAT THEY PROMISE**

The second half of the line names God twice from one root: ***“the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”***. The two titles are separated by reach rather than by strength in al-Baghawī's reading. The first describes mercy that pours out in this life on the whole creation, on the careful and the careless, in rain and in harvest and in breath. The second describes mercy that lands on those who turn to God and carries them into the next life. The same writer records a detail from the scholars of the language that makes the distinction concrete: people may be called by the second title, and no one but God is called by the first.

That difference becomes a rule about reading the names of God at all in al-Saʿdī's commentary. He writes that the scholars of the community agree on taking the names as they come, so that the mercy named here is a quality God possesses and a gift that reaches what He made, and that every blessing a person can name is a trace of it. The reason the two titles belong together at the head of the Book is given by al-Qurṭubī: a reader is about to be told that God owns the day of account. The kindness is named first so that the account is understood as the work of a merciful owner rather than the mood of a distant one.

The Book supports the reading with a sentence a reader can hold: (6:12 — **“He has taken upon Himself to be Merciful. He will certainly gather ˹all of˺ you together for the Day of Judgment”**). The strength of the phrase is the point: mercy is described as something taken on and written down, not as a feeling that may pass. The point is drawn out by al-Qurṭubī for a reader who needs to plan on it. The earliest gloss carried under Ibn ʿAbbās stays short and warm, calling the first title the Gentle and the second the Kind. The report that Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim carries from Abū Hurayrah reaches the reader through Ibn Kathīr's commentary with the same balance: if the believer knew the punishment that is with God, none of them would hope for His garden, and if the one who denies Him knew the mercy that is with Him, none of them would despair. The same report stands in al-Qurṭubī's reading of this sūrah, and two commentaries of very different habits agreeing is worth noticing.

Imagine a hospital where the sign at the gate carries the name of the director and, under it, the words that describe what the place does. A patient walking in reads both before he meets anyone. The two titles work the same way in this sūrah: they tell the reader what kind of house he is entering before he is asked for anything, and that is why the books describe them as the ground on which the rest of the sūrah stands.

**WHAT THE LINE ASKS OF THE PERSON SAYING IT**

The line is short enough to say without attention and large enough to reorganise a week, and the books argue that the difference lies in whether the speaker means it. Ibn ʿUthaymīn taught that beginning an act with the name of God is a claim on the act itself. A man who puts the name over his work and then cheats in the work has said a sentence he does not believe. The omission inside the sentence carries the same edge on al-Ṭabarī's reading: the verb a speaker supplies is his own, and no one supplies a verb he does not intend.

The line also settles a question about mercy that a reader will meet again in this sūrah. If the first title reaches everyone, then a person who asks for mercy and refuses it to those under him has contradicted his own opening. That conclusion is drawn by al-Saʿdī, and the distinction between the two kinds of mercy sharpens it: the wide mercy is meant to be imitated in reach, and the settled mercy is what a person hopes for himself. The same two writers treat the line as the reason a believer can hold fear and hope at once — the mercy is not so small that a person despairs. The owner is not so distant that a person forgets Him.

History gives the line a setting that is easy to picture. The words appear in the letter of Solomon that the Qur'an preserves, addressed to the queen of a wealthy kingdom. The paragraph before the demand is the name of God with the two titles of mercy: (27:31 — **“Do not be arrogant with me, but come to me, fully submitting”**). The same letter is used by al-Qurṭubī to show that the formula is not a private mark of one community but the way the Book has its messengers write. A household that adopts the order — kindness named before the request — finds its difficult conversations going differently, and the sources treat that as an application of the verse rather than a pleasant extra.

Imagine a foreman who has to tell a worker that yesterday's work was not good enough, and who begins by naming what the worker did well. The correction still comes, and it comes through a door the worker has left open. The reading closes on the same thought in plain form, in al-Saʿdī's words: the servant has named the two qualities he needs before he has asked for anything. The rest of the sūrah is the answer to that naming. The next line says who it is that the names belong to.

## Verse 1:2

> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds

**A PRAISE THAT CLAIMS THE WHOLE AND NOT A PART**

The verse begins with ***“All praise is for Allah”***, and the weight of the claim sits on the small word the. The definite article does work that no other word in the sentence can do, as al-Ṭabarī explains: a speaker who said praise belongs to God would only be reporting his own act, while "the praise belongs to God" gathers every act of praise there has ever been. The reason the claim can be that wide is given by al-Qurṭubī. Since the most beautiful names and the highest qualities are His, there is no honour that can be described anywhere without the source of it lying here.

What the word means, once the claim is understood, is wider than the gratitude a person feels for a good turn. Praise carries love and reverence as well as gratitude on the reading of Maʿārif al-Qurʾān, which is why a sentence built only on fear or only on fondness will not carry the meaning. Thanks is where the reading opens, in al-Ṭabarī's commentary, and the debt is owed for things no one could count: the body able to obey, the means to obey with, the food that keeps a person alive, and the guidance that leads past this life. The gloss carried under Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ṭabarī is close to that: praise is thanks, humility, and the admission that every blessing came from outside the one who received it.

Ibn ʿUthaymīn pressed his students toward the same conclusion by asking what kind of sentence this is. His answer was that it is a description, the fullest description with the fullest qualities ascribed to the One who deserves them, and that is a higher thing than a payment offered after a gift. A dispute recorded by al-Qurṭubī shows how the sentence was weighed: some held this line better than the confession that there is no god but God, because the confession of oneness sits inside it and praise is added, while others preferred the confession because it is the word that removes disbelief. The argument is worth following, because it shows what the early community thought they were saying when they opened their prayers.

Think of a chair standing in a shop, well made and strong. One person looks at it and names the maker; another buys it and thanks him later. The first is describing what the object is, the second is paying a debt for what he was given, and this verse does both in four words. That is why the report of the divided prayer matters here. The words in which God answers the one who recites the line stand at the head of Ibn Kathīr's commentary — the servant says the praise belongs to God, and God says that His servant has praised Him. A sentence with an answer is not a sentiment, and the answer is recorded in the collections this commentary rests on.

**A LORD WHO BRINGS WHAT HE MADE TO ITS END**

The second half of the verse is ***“Lord of all worlds”***, and the first of its two words is where the attention of the books goes. The lexical sense of the word is nurture: to bring a thing along by stages, each stage suited to what is good for it, until it reaches the purpose it was made for. Nothing in that description is about possession at all. It is about sustained work on a thing that cannot yet stand on its own, which is a claim about the present tense of every creature rather than a statement about who holds the deed.

Two kinds of care develop from that sense in al-Saʿdī's commentary, one wide and one particular. The wide care creates a creature and feeds it and guides it to what keeps it alive, and it falls on everything that breathes, careful or careless. The particular care takes hold of a person who turns to God and raises him by faith, steadies him in it, finishes what is lacking, and holds back what stands between him and his Lord. One observation of his rewards the reader who looks for it: the prayers of the prophets in the Qur'an mostly address God by this title, because everything they ask for belongs to that particular raising.

The ordinary sense of the word is not lost in the technical one. Ibn ʿUthaymīn, teaching a class, put the title in three plain words — creator, owner, disposer — and then followed them with the question of what a world is, until the class answered that it means everything besides God. The same three-part sense appears in al-Saʿdī's reading, joined to the verse around it: the One who made, owns and runs everything is the One whose praise is complete, so the two halves of the sentence are answering each other rather than sitting side by side.

Think of a father teaching a child to ride a bicycle. His hand is on the seat at the start, it stays longer than the child knows, and it comes away at the moment the balance can hold. The hand does not come away because the father has lost interest, and it does not stay because the child is hopeless. The next verse makes the same point in another key: al-Jalālayn reads the mercy that follows as existence and perfection flowing to everything in the measure that its own capacity allows, which is the same picture of care fitted to what is being cared for.

**THE WORLDS THE PHRASE GATHERS IN ONE LINE**

The second word of the pair is a plural, and the books take the trouble to say what it holds. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān lists what falls inside it: the sky and the earth, the sun and the moon, the stars, wind and rain, the angels, the jinn, animals, plants, minerals, and human beings. Ibn ʿUthaymīn's class arrived at the same scope by a shorter road, that the worlds are all created things and only God stands outside them. A phrase that gathers angels and rainfall and jinn and people into one word is not announcing a god of one tribe or one country, and the contrast with the names the Arabs of that time invoked over their own journeys and dealings is not accidental.

The placement of the phrase in the sūrah is deliberate, and al-Alūsī is the writer who explains why. He arranges the whole Fātiḥah as a summary of four kinds of knowledge: the roots of the religion, marked here by the two titles in this verse and the next; the reports of the prophets, marked by the mention of those God favoured; the return to God, marked by the line about the day of account; and the teaching of character, marked by the request for help and the straight path. Al-Alūsī then says that this is the reason the sūrah carries the names it does, and Ibn Kathīr records the same naming from the Prophet ﷺ, that this line is the Mother of the Book and the Seven Repeated Verses. A book that puts its whole argument in its first page is telling the reader how to read the rest of it.

Praise returned for praise is the movement of the whole chapter, and this verse is where the movement starts. The Book elsewhere names the Lord being thanked in the same breath as the greatness of His name: (55:78 — **“Blessed is the Name of your Lord, full of Majesty and Honour”**). Al-Ṭabarī notes in this connection that the praise of God is not something a creature invents for Him; the Book teaches the words first, and the servant repeats them. That order matters for how the verse is recited, because a person who believes he is composing a compliment has misunderstood both halves of the sentence at once.

Picture the index at the front of a long book, where every chapter is listed in a single page. Nobody thinks the index is the book, yet no reader would start the book without it, and the entries are in the author's own words. The sūrah works the same way for the whole Qur'an, and this verse stands at the top of the page with the owner's name on it. Al-Saʿdī draws the consequence for the reader's own life, which is that a man who says the worlds belong to God should stop behaving as though any part of them were his to use without leave, and should expect the raising described in this verse to continue in his own case, awkward stages and all.
