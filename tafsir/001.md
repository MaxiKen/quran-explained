# Sūrah Al-Fatihah (Chapter 1) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir

## Introduction to the Sūrah

Al-Fātiḥah is the sūrah a Muslim says more often than any other words in the Qur'an, and the books that open with it begin by explaining why. Three names are recorded for it in the earliest material. Al-Baghawī gives the reason behind each one: it opens the written Book and the recitation, so it is the Opener; it is the root the rest of the Book grows from, so it is the Mother of the Qur'an; and its seven lines return in every unit of every prayer, so it is the Seven Repeated Verses. Most of the early scholars held it to be Makkan, revealed before the migration, and al-Alūsī records the same preference with the report that it counts among the first things sent down.

What the sūrah contains is described in the books as a summary of the whole religion. Al-Saʿdī reads it as a gathering of the three kinds of oneness the Qur'an teaches: God as creator and provider in the words about the Lord of all worlds, God in His names and qualities in the two names of mercy. God's right to be worshipped in the pledge that follows. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān makes the same point from the shape of the text. Begins by praising God and ends by asking Him for something, so that a reader learns the order of prayer before he learns its words.

The Prophet ﷺ described the sūrah as a conversation, and the books use that description as their key. Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim carries the report. Ibn Kathīr places at the head of his commentary and al-Baghawī quotes in full: God has divided the prayer between Himself and His servant, half for each, and the servant receives what he asks for. Al-Qurṭubī draws from it that the sūrah is not a text recited at God but a dialogue in which the worshipper speaks and is answered, verse by verse, from the first praise to the last request.

The books also show a reader how much is riding on a text he may have learned as a child. Ibn Kathīr records, from Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, that there is no prayer for the one who does not recite the Opener of the Book. The same chapter of the Ṣaḥīḥ reports that a companion used it as a healing recitation over a man who had been bitten, with the Prophet's approval. A community that repeats a text five times a day will either come to know it deeply or stop hearing it. The order of this sūrah — praise, mercy, ownership, a pledge, a request, a warning — is arranged to prevent the second outcome.

One question about the opening line runs through every classical book, and the disagreement is old and well documented. Al-Ṭabarī argues that the line beginning with the name of God is not counted as a verse of this sūrah. The Book never places two identical lines side by side with nothing between them. Al-Qurṭubī sets out the positions with the scholars who held them: Mālik counted it as no verse of any sūrah, Ibn al-Mubārak counted it as opening every sūrah. Al-Shāfiʿī counted it as a verse of this one. Ibn ʿUthaymīn taught the same conclusion as al-Ṭabarī from the report of the division of the prayer. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān records Abū Ḥanīfah's view that it is a verse revealed on its own to separate one sūrah from the next. The community recites it either way, and the sūrah is seven lines on the reading the Prophet ﷺ taught.

## Verse 1:1

> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**HOW THE BOOK TEACHES A PERSON TO BEGIN**

The sūrah opens with ***“In the Name of Allah”***, and the first thing the books notice is that the sentence is unfinished. The word for name is joined to God by a particle that needs a verb after it. The verb is left out, because the speaker is not describing a rule but announcing an act. The grammar carries a lesson of its own. The particle needs a verb, the verb is left out, and al-Ṭabarī's reading of the omission is that the reader supplies it himself. That is why al-Saʿdī reads the opening as a promise to begin every act through the name of God rather than as a title. The reason the word for name appears in the singular is that it stands for a noun joined to its owner, the way any single thing stands for its kind.

God did not leave the habit to be discovered. The habit was taught rather than discovered. Al-Ṭabarī reports that the Prophet ﷺ was trained to put the names of God in front of all his actions, and that the practice was then made a pattern for the whole creation. The history behind that training is supplied by Maʿārif al-Qurʾān. Before Islam, Arabs began their journeys and their dealings with the names of idols. The first words revealed to the Prophet ﷺ redirected the custom rather than banning the act of invocation: (96:1 — **“in the Name of your Lord Who created—”**). The community inherited the correction, and this sūrah keeps it at the front of the Book, where no reader can avoid it.

The merits the book reports for the line are not incidental, and they explain the care taken over its wording. A note from al-Suyūṭī carried in Maʿārif al-Qurʾān says that the divine books before the Qur'an also opened with the name of God. The same book records the saying that the meanings of the Book are gathered in the Fātiḥah, and the meanings of the Fātiḥah in this line. The report that the words were dictated in stages, first the name alone, then the name with the first title, then the whole line, is collected by al-Alūsī. He treats it as evidence that the opening was taught rather than improvised. Whether a reader accepts the story or not, he is looking at a sentence the community received before it composed anything.

Think of a house key kept on a hook by the door, where every member of the family has to take it before leaving. The key does not open the destination; it opens the house that makes the journey possible. A reading at that level of plainness is what al-Jalālayn gives of the line. The report in which the Prophet ﷺ named this sūrah the Mother of the Book and the Seven Repeated Verses, graded sound by al-Tirmidhī, stands in Ibn Kathīr's opening too. Everything else in the chapter arrives after a person has reached for the key.

**THE TWO TITLES AND WHAT THEY PROMISE**

The second half of the line names God twice from one root: ***“the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”***. The two titles are separated by reach rather than by strength in al-Baghawī's reading. The first describes mercy that pours out in this life on the whole creation, on the careful and the careless, in rain and in harvest and in breath. The second describes mercy that lands on those who turn to God and carries them into the next life. The same writer records a detail from the scholars of the language that makes the distinction concrete: people may be called by the second title. No one but God is called by the first.

That difference becomes a rule about reading the names of God at all in al-Saʿdī's commentary. He writes that the scholars of the community agree on taking the names as they come, so that the mercy named here is a quality God possesses and a gift that reaches what He made, and that every blessing a person can name is a trace of it. The reason the two titles belong together at the head of the Book is given by al-Qurṭubī: a reader is about to be told that God owns the day of account. The kindness is named first so that the account is understood as the work of a merciful owner rather than the mood of a distant one.

The Book supports the reading with a sentence a reader can hold: (6:12 — **“He has taken upon Himself to be Merciful. He will certainly gather ˹all of˺ you together for the Day of Judgment”**). The strength of the phrase is the point: mercy is described as something taken on and written down, not as a feeling that may pass. The point is drawn out by al-Qurṭubī for a reader who needs to plan on it. The earliest gloss carried under Ibn ʿAbbās stays short and warm, calling the first title the Gentle and the second the Kind. The report that Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim carries from Abū Hurayrah reaches the reader through Ibn Kathīr's commentary with the same balance: if the believer knew the punishment that is with God, none of them would hope for His garden. If the one who denies Him knew the mercy that is with Him, none of them would despair. The same report stands in al-Qurṭubī's reading of this sūrah, and two commentaries of very different habits agreeing is worth noticing.

Imagine a hospital where the sign at the gate carries the name of the director and, under it, the words that describe what the place does. A patient walking in reads both before he meets anyone. The two titles work the same way in this sūrah: they tell the reader what kind of house he is entering before he is asked for anything, and that is why the books describe them as the ground on which the rest of the sūrah stands.

**WHAT THE LINE ASKS OF THE PERSON SAYING IT**

The line is short enough to say without attention and large enough to reorganise a week, and the books argue that the difference lies in whether the speaker means it. Ibn ʿUthaymīn taught that beginning an act with the name of God is a claim on the act itself. A man who puts the name over his work and then cheats in the work has said a sentence he does not believe. The omission inside the sentence carries the same edge on al-Ṭabarī's reading: the verb a speaker supplies is his own, and no one supplies a verb he does not intend.

The line also settles a question about mercy that a reader will meet again in this sūrah. If the first title reaches everyone, then a person who asks for mercy and refuses it to those under him has contradicted his own opening. That conclusion is drawn by al-Saʿdī, and the distinction between the two kinds of mercy sharpens it: the wide mercy is meant to be imitated in reach. The settled mercy is what a person hopes for himself. The same two writers treat the line as the reason a believer can hold fear and hope at once — the mercy is not so small that a person despairs. The owner is not so distant that a person forgets Him.

History gives the line a setting that is easy to picture. The words appear in the letter of Solomon that the Qur'an preserves, addressed to the queen of a wealthy kingdom. The paragraph before the demand is the name of God with the two titles of mercy: (27:31 — **“Do not be arrogant with me, but come to me, fully submitting”**). The same letter is used by al-Qurṭubī to show that the formula is not a private mark of one community but the way the Book has its messengers write. A household that adopts the order — kindness named before the request — finds its difficult conversations going differently. The sources treat that as an application of the verse rather than a pleasant extra.

Imagine a foreman who has to tell a worker that yesterday's work was not good enough, and who begins by naming what the worker did well. The correction still comes, and it comes through a door the worker has left open. The reading closes on the same thought in plain form, in al-Saʿdī's words: the servant has named the two qualities he needs before he has asked for anything. The rest of the sūrah is the answer to that naming. The next line says who it is that the names belong to.

## Verse 1:2

> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds

**A PRAISE THAT CLAIMS THE WHOLE AND NOT A PART**

The verse begins with ***“All praise is for Allah”***, and the weight of the claim sits on the small word the. The definite article does work that no other word in the sentence can do, as al-Ṭabarī explains: a speaker who said praise belongs to God would only be reporting his own act. "the praise belongs to God" gathers every act of praise there has ever been. The reason the claim can be that wide is given by al-Qurṭubī. Since the most beautiful names and the highest qualities are His, there is no honour that can be described anywhere without the source of it lying here.

What the word means, once the claim is understood, is wider than the gratitude a person feels for a good turn. Praise carries love and reverence as well as gratitude on the reading of Maʿārif al-Qurʾān, which is why a sentence built only on fear or only on fondness will not carry the meaning. Thanks is where the reading opens, in al-Ṭabarī's commentary. The debt is owed for things no one could count: the body able to obey, the means to obey with, the food that keeps a person alive, and the guidance that leads past this life. The gloss carried under Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ṭabarī is close to that: praise is thanks, humility, and the admission that every blessing came from outside the one who received it.

Ibn ʿUthaymīn pressed his students toward the same conclusion by asking what kind of sentence this is. His answer was that it is a description, the fullest description with the fullest qualities ascribed to the One who deserves them, and that is a higher thing than a payment offered after a gift. A dispute recorded by al-Qurṭubī shows how the sentence was weighed: some held this line better than the confession that there is no god but God. The confession of oneness sits inside it and praise is added, while others preferred the confession because it is the word that removes disbelief. The argument is worth following, because it shows what the early community thought they were saying when they opened their prayers.

Think of a chair standing in a shop, well made and strong. One person looks at it and names the maker; another buys it and thanks him later. The first is describing what the object is, the second is paying a debt for what he was given, and this verse does both in four words. That is why the report of the divided prayer matters here. The words in which God answers the one who recites the line stand at the head of Ibn Kathīr's commentary — the servant says the praise belongs to God, and God says that His servant has praised Him. A sentence with an answer is not a sentiment, and the answer is recorded in the two collections of sound reports.

**A LORD WHO BRINGS WHAT HE MADE TO ITS END**

The second half of the verse is ***“Lord of all worlds”***, and the first of its two words is where the attention of the books goes. The lexical sense of the word is nurture: to bring a thing along by stages, each stage suited to what is good for it, until it reaches the purpose it was made for. Nothing in that description is about possession at all. It is about sustained work on a thing that cannot yet stand on its own, which is a claim about the present tense of every creature rather than a statement about who holds the deed.

Two kinds of care develop from that sense in al-Saʿdī's commentary, one wide and one particular. The wide care creates a creature and feeds it and guides it to what keeps it alive, and it falls on everything that breathes, careful or careless. The particular care takes hold of a person who turns to God and raises him by faith, steadies him in it, finishes what is lacking, and holds back what stands between him and his Lord. One observation of his rewards the reader who looks for it: the prayers of the prophets in the Qur'an mostly address God by this title. Everything they ask for belongs to that particular raising.

The ordinary sense of the word is not lost in the technical one. Ibn ʿUthaymīn, teaching a class, put the title in three plain words — creator, owner, disposer — and then followed them with the question of what a world is, until the class answered that it means everything besides God. The same three-part sense appears in al-Saʿdī's reading, joined to the verse around it: the One who made, owns and runs everything is the One whose praise is complete. The two halves of the sentence are answering each other rather than sitting side by side.

Think of a father teaching a child to ride a bicycle. His hand is on the seat at the start, it stays longer than the child knows, and it comes away at the moment the balance can hold. The hand does not come away because the father has lost interest, and it does not stay because the child is hopeless. The next verse makes the same point in another key: al-Jalālayn reads the mercy that follows as existence and perfection flowing to everything in the measure that its own capacity allows, which is the same picture of care fitted to what is being cared for.

**THE WORLDS THE PHRASE GATHERS IN ONE LINE**

The second word of the pair is a plural, and the books take the trouble to say what it holds. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān lists what falls inside it: the sky and the earth, the sun and the moon, the stars, wind and rain, the angels, the jinn, animals, plants, minerals, and human beings. Ibn ʿUthaymīn's class arrived at the same scope by a shorter road, that the worlds are all created things and only God stands outside them. A phrase that gathers angels and rainfall and jinn and people into one word is not announcing a god of one tribe or one country. The contrast with the names the Arabs of that time invoked over their own journeys and dealings is not accidental.

The placement of the phrase in the sūrah is deliberate, and al-Alūsī is the writer who explains why. He arranges the whole Fātiḥah as a summary of four kinds of knowledge: the roots of the religion, marked here by the two titles in this verse and the next. The reports of the prophets, marked by the mention of those God favoured; the return to God, marked by the line about the day of account; and the teaching of character, marked by the request for help and the straight path. Al-Alūsī then says that this is the reason the sūrah carries the names it does. Ibn Kathīr records the same naming from the Prophet ﷺ, that this line is the Mother of the Book and the Seven Repeated Verses. A book that puts its whole argument in its first page is telling the reader how to read the rest of it.

Praise returned for praise is the movement of the whole chapter, and this verse is where the movement starts. The Book elsewhere names the Lord being thanked in the same breath as the greatness of His name: (55:78 — **“Blessed is the Name of your Lord, full of Majesty and Honour”**). Al-Ṭabarī notes in this connection that the praise of God is not something a creature invents for Him; the Book teaches the words first, and the servant repeats them. That order matters for how the verse is recited, because a person who believes he is composing a compliment has misunderstood both halves of the sentence at once.

Picture the index at the front of a long book, where every chapter is listed in a single page. Nobody thinks the index is the book, yet no reader would start the book without it, and the entries are in the author's own words. The sūrah works the same way for the whole Qur'an, and this verse stands at the top of the page with the owner's name on it. Al-Saʿdī draws the consequence for the reader's own life, which is that a man who says the worlds belong to God should stop behaving as though any part of them were his to use without leave. He should expect the raising described in this verse to continue in his own case, awkward stages and all.

## Verse 1:3

> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**MERCY NAMED A SECOND TIME, ON PURPOSE**

The line ***“the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”*** has already appeared in the opening of the sūrah. A book that repeats four words inside seven short lines is making a decision rather than filling a space. The repeat lands exactly where it is needed, because the title before it is the one that can raise a chill. The order is explained by al-Qurṭubī, and his reason is the warning that the title carries on its own. Describing God as Lord of all worlds can raise a chill, which is why the two names of mercy are joined to it, so that the reader holds hope and caution in the same hand. The same reasoning is recorded by Ibn Kathīr at this verse, and it is the kind of reading the early community taught, since it tells a reader how to hold two truths about one Lord at once.

The Book states that balance in more than one place, and the wording is short enough to carry. God tells His Prophet to announce both halves in a single instruction — (15:49 — **“Inform My servants ˹O Prophet˺ that I am truly the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”**) and, in the verse straight after, (15:50 — **“My torment is indeed the most painful”**). A second statement puts the two facts in one sentence about the same Lord: (6:165 — **“swift in punishment, but He is certainly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”**). Both verses are cited by al-Qurṭubī, who adds the report that Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim carries from Abū Hurayrah: if the believer knew what punishment is with God, none of them would hope for His garden. If the one who denies Him knew what mercy is with God, none of them would despair.

What the balance is for becomes clear when it is removed. A reader who is given only the title of ownership will treat God as a threat and will eventually stop hearing the verse at all. A reader given only the two names of mercy will treat the account at the end of the sūrah as scenery, because nothing in his picture of God can say no to him. The order of the lines prevents both failures at once, and that is why the scholars of the language treat the placement as part of the meaning of the text rather than a rhythm of words.

Think of a surgeon who tells a patient two things before an operation. The first is exactly what will be done and how serious it is; the second is that he will be present the whole time and will not leave the room. Neither sentence is the whole truth without the other, and a patient who hears only one of them will either run away or refuse to prepare. The two names of mercy are the second sentence of this sūrah, and the Master of the day of account is the first.

**WHAT THE TWO FORMS OF THE WORD CARRY**

The two names come from one root but not from one mould, and the difference between their shapes is where the dictionaries and the teachers go first. The pattern of the first word is where Ibn ʿUthaymīn pointed his class. Arabic builds words on the measure faʿlān to mean filled with a quality, in the way a furious man is called filled with rage. Means the name describes mercy at its fullness and breadth rather than mercy in small change. The second name on his account describes the act rather than the store, the One who delivers mercy to whom He wills. A quality that is full and a quality that is sent out are two halves of one kindness. The point is that mercy here is both a store and an act, which means a reader cannot separate what God is from what God does.

The number of people the kindness reaches is where the classical books divide it. The reading of the early scholars, which al-Baghawī records, gives the first name to this life and every creature in it, and the second to believers in the next. He adds a detail that keeps the pair from blurring: people may be described by the second name, and no one but God by the first. Al-Saʿdī puts the same division in his own terms, that the mercy of God is vast enough to encompass every living thing and is written down in full for those who fear Him and follow His messengers, while everyone else still holds a share of it.

The two names also describe two different works, which is the reading al-Jalālayn gives of them. The first is the mercy by which existence and perfection flow out to everything, measured to what each thing can hold. The second is the mercy that carries the human being to the sort of completeness that is settled in the life to come. On that reading the first name explains why anything exists and stays in being at all, and the second explains what the account ahead is for. The words in the sūrah are not two compliments to soften a description; they are a statement about how the world is run.

Picture a river that runs through dry country and the canals cut from it. The river itself reaches the whole valley and every field in it without exception, and the canals carry the same water to the plots that were prepared for it. The supply is one, the delivery is not, and nothing in the picture makes the river stingy. Al-Saʿdī ends this part of his reading with the widest statement in the Book on the subject, spoken by God about Himself in the middle of a prayer answered: (7:156 — **“My mercy encompasses everything. I will ordain mercy for those who shun evil, pay alms-tax, and believe in Our revelations”**).

**MERCY THAT DOES NOT CANCEL THE ACCOUNT**

A reader may wonder how the mercy just described leaves room for the reckoning promised in the next line, and the answer the books give is that both belong to the same will. Ibn ʿUthaymīn cited the verse that settles it, that God punishes whoever He wills and shows mercy to whoever He wills, and then names the return to Him in the same breath. The mercy is not a force that embarrassed its owner into cancelling the account, and the account is not a mood that interrupts the mercy. The point of holding both together is that neither can be used to escape the other, which is why the sūrah names the day of account in the line immediately after the two names of mercy.

The practical edge of the two names is that they can be imitated in reach even though they cannot be matched in power. Al-Saʿdī draws that lesson directly, that the God-fearing are the ones for whom the mercy of God is written in full, and following the messengers is what puts a person in that company. The Book says the same thing to the community in a verse that begins with an invitation no one deserves: (39:53 — **“Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins. He is indeed the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”**). A person who recites the two names five times a day and then despairs of God, or refuses mercy to a debtor, has recited them as sounds rather than as a description.

The repetition also does something for the reader's attention that a single mention could not. The words return between the praise and the account like a refrain, so that the two extremes of the sūrah are held by the same pair of names. A reader who looks at the shape of the seven lines sees mercy at both ends of the sentence about who is in charge. Al-Baghawī's note that no creature shares the first of the two names belongs here as well, since it tells the reader that the kind of care in question is not a quality he possesses in his own right, only a gift he is allowed to pass on.

Imagine a man who teaches his son to handle money. He hands over a small amount and says two things: that the money is his father's to give and could be taken back, and that he will always be ready to help if the son asks. The son who keeps both sentences in mind handles the money carefully and without fear. The next line of the sūrah names the day when the books are opened, and it is placed after the two names of mercy for exactly that reason.

## Verse 1:4

> Master of the Day of Judgment

**A CLAIM ABOUT OWNERSHIP THAT ONLY HOLDS ON ONE DAY**

The fourth line names the owner and the day together: ***“Master of the Day of Judgment”***. The pairing is the argument, because ownership is exactly what people dispute in this life, and the Book expects the dispute. Al-Ṭabarī draws the contrast in his reading of the line, that in the world kings and tyrants contended with God over rule and pushed to keep majesty to themselves, and that on this day the kingship is His alone with no partner left to argue. What a person owns here he owns by arrangement and under a power that can be taken from him, which is why the line does not say that God owns everything on that day; it says that on that day nothing else is even claimed.

The Book makes the same point in a single clause a reader can carry away: (82:19 — **“all authority on that Day belongs to Allah ˹entirely˺”**). Ibn ʿAbbās, in the report Ibn Kathīr carries, explained the line by saying that on that day no one owns anything he used to own in the world, which is not a denial of property but a statement about when property stops meaning anything. Even speech submitted to the same law in the report of the day, when no one will speak (11:105 — **“except with His permission. Some of them will be miserable, others joyful”**). A day on which nobody speaks without leave is a day on which nobody transacts without leave either, and the ledger that matters is not in the hand of any creature.

The word for the day is another of the book's short lessons. Al-Ṭabarī reads it as the day of recompense, and Ibn Kathīr records Ibn ʿAbbās giving it the plainest sense, that it is the day when creatures are repaid, evil for evil and good for good, except those whom God pardons. The name therefore describes what happens rather than when it happens. Means the verse is not telling a reader about a date; it is telling him what kind of day he will be standing in. Grace is inside the account as well, since the pardon is mentioned in the same sentence, and a reader who hears only the ledger has read half of what Ibn ʿAbbās said.

Picture the day a company is audited. The man who signed the cheques all year holds no more authority than the clerk who filed them, and the papers are laid on a table that belongs to neither of them. Everything the pair argued about in the corridor for twelve months is settled by one signature on the cover. The examination is not cruel; it is simply the moment when the truth about the year stops being negotiable. The sūrah places that moment at the end of the praise, before the servant has asked for anything.

**TWO READINGS OF ONE WORD, AND WHAT EACH ADDS**

The word for the owner of that day is recited in two ways, and the difference between them is one letter that changes the whole sentence. Al-Qurṭubī reports both readings as transmitted from the Prophet ﷺ himself, and from Abū Bakr and ʿUmar, with al-Tirmidhī recording them, and then sets out the argument about which carries more weight. The reading that gives God as king is wider, on the view he reports from the scholars of the language. Every king owns while not every owner reigns, and because a king's command runs over the owner's own property: a man may own a field and still be forbidden to build on it by the ruler above him.

The other reading, the one on the printed page, carries the weight of possession, and its argument is that owning covers more than ruling. A mālik owns people as well as things, on the formulation al-Qurṭubī records. The laws that decide what may be done with property are executed in his name, so the word adds a layer of possession to the layer of command. Both readings are therefore teaching something, which is why the books refuse to call one of them a mistake. What follows for a reader is that the verse names a Lord who commands and possesses at once, and that a person who only imagines authority without ownership, or ownership without authority, has a thinner picture of God than the two readings give him.

The same distinction appears in a report that keeps a human being out of the office entirely. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim record from Abū Hurayrah that the most despicable name with God belongs to a man who calls himself *"the king of kings"*. The report ends by saying that there is no owner but God. Ibn Kathīr brings the report into this verse, and the connection is not loose: the title a man reaches for when he wants to be called the king of kings is drawn from the one name whose owner is not in doubt. A ruler who understands this loses nothing except a claim he never had, and the line he recites in prayer becomes the honest description of his office.

Think of a tenant who sublets the rooms of a flat he does not own. His agreements with the subtenants work perfectly well until the owner returns, and then every contract signed in the landlord's absence is examined against the deed. Nothing is cruel about that examination, and the tenant who read the deed before subletting is the one who is relaxed on the day. The two readings of this word together tell a reader that the deed was never in his hand, and the Master of the day of account is the one who will hold it.

**WHY THIS LINE SITS AT THE MIDDLE OF THE SŪRAH**

The position of the line in the sūrah has been part of its reading for as long as the sūrah has been counted. Ibn ʿUthaymīn worked it out in front of a class by counting the verses: three lines before this one and three after it, which makes it the exact middle of the Fātiḥah, and that is the verse the report of the divided prayer treats as the turning point of the recitation. Al-Qurṭubī set out the same structure in the report that God has divided the prayer between Himself and His servant, with the first lines spoken for God and the last three returned to the servant. The transition happening where the servant's own condition enters the text.

What the middle position does for the reader is prepare the change of voice that comes next. The praise section is spoken about God in the third person, and from the next line the speaker turns and addresses Him directly with a pledge. A turn that sudden would feel unmotivated in prose, and the line about the account is what motivates it. The day named here is the day a person's own file is opened, so the language of report is no longer adequate. The only sensible thing left is to speak to the One holding the file. Al-Alūsī marks the same line as the verse in the sūrah that carries the return to God, and the return is the point at which worship becomes personal.

Ibn Kathīr draws one more consequence from the name of the day, that God is the true owner of everything and everyone. Supports it with the verse in which God describes Himself as (2:255 — **“To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth”**). The claim is present tense there, which means the ownership of the day of account is not a power God takes up when the world ends; it is a power He has never put aside and will simply stop being contested. That reading keeps a person from treating the day as an event that concerns God's future rather than his own, and it explains why the verse can be recited at the end of a set of praises without a break in the logic.

Think of a family that has always known who will read the will. Nothing about the reading is a surprise, and no one in the household plans as though the estate were unowned, because the name on the document has been known from the beginning. The sūrah tells its reader the same name four lines in, before it asks him for worship, before it asks him for help. Before it puts a request for guidance on his tongue. Everything asked afterwards is asked by someone who already knows who is holding the books.

## Verse 1:5

> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help

**A PRONOUN THAT SHUTS THE DOOR ON SHARING**

The sūrah turns in the middle of the word order of ***“You ˹alone˺ we worship”***. Arabic normally puts the verb first and the object after it. Moving the object forward is the plain way of shutting out rivals: the sentence does not say that we worship God among others, it says that the worship is His and stops there. Al-Ṭabarī reads the line as humility, lowliness and submission, a declaration that the Lordship belongs to God and to no one else, and he carries the gloss of Ibn ʿAbbās through his chain that the servant is singling out his Lord in fear and in hope.

The Arabic word for worship, on al-Ṭabarī's account, begins in the language of a road. The Arabs called a path muʿabbad when feet had trodden it smooth. The same root carries lowliness, which is why the scholars of the language insist that worship is not a mood but a submission with movement in it. That reading sits well with the place the line holds in this chapter. Nothing before it was addressed to God in the second person; the first four lines speak about Him, and this one speaks to Him. Ibn Kathīr treats as the sign of a servant who has finished praising and now stands in front of the One he praised.

One more detail in the line is easy to pass over and hard to justify once noticed. The reciter is alone, holding the Book or standing in prayer, and he says we and not I. The plural gathers him into everyone else who is saying the same sentence at that moment, in the same words, facing the same direction, whether he can see them or not. A community whose most repeated sentence is in the first person plural is being taught something about how to stand before God, which is not as an isolated client but as one member of a body with a single request.

Think of a crowd passing through a narrow gate at the same time. Nobody in it is anonymous to the one letting them through, and nobody claims a private door on the side. The pronoun makes the reciter one of that crowd. The rest of the sūrah is spoken in the same voice: the guidance asked for at the end is asked for everyone in the line, not for the speaker alone. A person who understands the line as a private transaction has lost the grammar and the company together.

**HELP ASKED FROM THE SAME SOURCE AS WORSHIP**

The second half of the line is ***“and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help”***, and the fronted pronoun appears a second time rather than being left implied. The repetition is the teaching, because it puts the search for help inside the same claim of exclusivity as the worship. A religion in which God is worshipped on one side of a person's life and the practical help for his problems is sought on the other is not the religion this line describes, however often it is recited.

The order of the two halves has been read as a rule by the books. Asking for help comes after the declaration of worship and not before it, so that the request is made by someone who has already put himself under the One he is asking, which is the difference between a servant making a petition and a customer placing an order. Al-Qurṭubī draws out the same point from the report of the divided prayer. God answers this line by saying that it stands between Him and His servant, because it contains the servant's humility along with his request for aid.

What the second half actually covers is wider than religion, and this is where the teachers of this sūrah are unusually concrete. Ibn ʿUthaymīn asked his class what part of the line belongs to God and what part to the servant. Led them to see that the second half belongs to the servant's whole life: he is asking for help with worship, with eating and drinking, with sleeping, with leaving the house, with everything. Al-Saʿdī states the same need from the other direction, that a servant depends on the help of God for every act of obedience he performs and every wrong he avoids, since nothing of it happens by his own strength.

Picture a carpenter with good tools and steady hands. He can work the wood, and he cannot supply the current that runs his machines. The power arrives from someone else's station, and the craftsman who forgets it is only reminded when the power fails. Effort and dependence are not rivals in that workshop, and the line holds the two together in a single breath: we do the worship. The help that makes it possible is asked from the same One. A person who thinks his obedience is self-generated has made the second half of the line unnecessary.

**THE LINE BETWEEN THE TWO HALVES**

The shape of the sūrah gives this sentence a position worth noticing. Al-Qurṭubī sets out the division recorded in the report of the divided prayer: the first three lines belong to God, this one is between God and His servant. The last three are the servant's, which means the chapter turns from report into conversation exactly where the reciter begins to speak about himself. What follows from that is a reader's rule rather than a curiosity of counting: everything a person asks for in the rest of the sūrah is asked of the One whom he has just declared to be alone in worship and in help.

The line also sets the terms for the request that comes next. Guidance is the one thing the servant asks for in this chapter. The two halves of this verse explain why nothing else needs to be named: the guidance is asked from the One who is worshipped. The strength to walk in it is asked from the same One who is relied on. Al-Saʿdī puts the whole sūrah on this footing when he notes that the chapter asks creation for nothing at all, from its first word to its last, which is why it can be recited by a person who owns nothing and needs everything.

A practical test of the line is available to anyone who recites it. The man who says that his help comes from God and then arranges his affairs by deceit has contradicted his own sentence. The contradiction is not a small lapse in manners; it is a denial of the words he recited. The same test runs the other way as well, since the person who trusts in God and then refuses the work he is able to do has misread reliance as idleness. The books are careful to keep the two halves from being used to cancel each other.

Imagine a man setting out on a road he has travelled before. He has made all his preparations and still stops to say that he has no strength of his own for the journey. The preparation and the dependence do not weaken each other; the second is what makes the first honest. The next line names the road, and the sūrah asks for it in the plural voice of someone who has just put down every other claim.

## Verse 1:6

> Guide us along the Straight Path

**A REQUEST FROM PEOPLE ALREADY ON THE ROAD**

The petition ***“Guide us along the Straight Path”*** is asked by someone who already knows the way he is asking for, and the books explain the apparent oddity rather than passing over it. Al-Ṭabarī reads the line in this place as a request for steadfastness upon the path, citing the gloss of Ibn ʿAbbās that the servant is asking God to keep him firm there, and he notes that the sense matches the line before it: a request for enabling in the part of a life that is still to come. The same reading appears in the supplication the Book teaches for people who are already guided: (3:8 — **“Do not let our hearts deviate after you have guided us”**).

What the word for guidance carries in the Book is wider than a map, and Ibn Kathīr gathers the evidence for that breadth. The verb is used for explaining the two roads to a human being, as the Book says of the one who was (90:10 — **“shown them the two ways ˹of right and wrong˺”**), and it is used for being led and made fit for an outcome, as when the people of the garden say that praise belongs to God (7:43 — **“Praise be to Allah for guiding us to this”**). Guidance in the Book therefore takes in the explanation, the direction and the enabling. A request for it is a request for all three, which is why it is not a strange prayer for someone already walking.

The request is repeated in every unit of the prayer, and al-Saʿdī draws the reason from the petition itself. What is asked is the most comprehensive thing a servant can ask for, because it covers both his knowledge and his action. A need that never stops has to be asked for without stopping. He notes as well that its place in every cycle of the prayer is not a formality but a consequence of the need. The danger of leaving the road belongs to the whole of a life and not to a single moment in it.

Think of a man walking a mountain path with a guide in front of him. He does not need to be told again which mountain he is climbing, and he does need to be told where the loose stones are, on this step, in this light. The request of this line is of that kind: not a fresh start each cycle of the prayer but the same steady keeping that carried the previous step, asked again because the path continues.

**TWO KINDS OF GUIDANCE IN ONE REQUEST**

Behind the single word for guidance sit two different needs, and separating them explains the reach of the request. Al-Saʿdī describes guidance to the path and guidance within the path: being brought to hold the religion and leave what contradicts it, and then being shown the detail of it, in knowledge and in practice, for as long as a person lives. The first kind is settled at the start and the second never is, which means a reader who thinks the request has been answered once has understood only the first half of it.

The short gloss in al-Jalālayn keeps the same sense at its plainest, that the line asks God to show the way to the path. The plain sense is enough to establish the point. What makes the petition so useful is that its two halves cover the two ways a person can fail. He can fail by missing the road entirely, which is why the request is made at all, and he can fail by losing hold of it while walking, which is why it is made again in every cycle of prayer. Between them, the two failures describe most of the danger a human being faces.

What guides the reader here is the shape of the sūrah rather than any single phrase in this line. The chapter has just refused the two roads that end in the next verse, one taken by people who knew and abandoned the truth and one taken by people who moved without knowledge, and this petition is the sūrah's own answer to both: knowledge that walks, and walking that knows. A reciter who asks for the path and wastes the means of learning is asking for something he is actively refusing, and the books treat that as a contradiction rather than a mystery.

Picture a student who has been admitted to a school and then asks his teacher to be kept in it. The admission is real and complete, and the keeping is what every term afterwards depends on, so the request is neither doubt nor flattery. That is the shape of this line in a prayer recited every day: it thanks God for the road already found and asks for the strength to stay on it, which is the one thing a person can lose while standing still.

**WHAT THE BOOK MEANS BY A STRAIGHT PATH**

The picture of a road is doing serious work in this verse, and the books are precise about what makes it straight. Al-Ṭabarī, in the reading Ibn Kathīr reports from him, holds that the community agreed the straight path is the clear road with no branches, and he adds a note from the usage of the Arabs that widens the word: they describe every deed and every statement, good or bad, as a path, so that an honest man is called straight and a wicked one crooked. If the word covers conduct and speech, then the road of this verse is not only a set of beliefs but the way a person's days actually run.

That reading explains why the rest of the sūrah is built as it is, with the people ahead and the people lost named in the next line instead of a list of instructions here. A path is known by the feet that have walked it, and the Book names its travellers in the verse after this one, which gives the reader a picture rather than a set of rules to hold in the head. The knowledge is in the description of the walkers, and the action is in the following of them, and the two cannot be separated without leaving the road.

One further consequence follows from the plain sense of the word for straightness here. A road can be straight in two ways: it can run without turning, and it can run without dividing the traveller between exits. Al-Baghawī's note on the next verse belongs to this one as well, in that he records the early reading of Sahl b. ʿAbd Allāh. Held that the people who incurred wrath are the people of innovation and the people astray are those who leave the practice of the Prophet ﷺ. The petition is narrow enough that a person who takes a shortcut has left the line rather than travelled it more efficiently.

Think of a railway line rather than a field. A train that stays on the rails reaches its destination and a train that uses the freedom of the field arrives nowhere, and nobody accuses the rails of being a restriction on movement. The path of this verse is of that kind. The guidance asked for in it is the grace that keeps a moving traveller on the line: not a single decision made long ago. A keeping that continues for as long as the journey does.

## Verse 1:7

> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray

**A ROAD DESCRIBED BY THE PEOPLE WALKING IT**

The last line answers the request of the previous one by naming a road, and it names the road by naming the people on it: ***“the Path of those You have blessed”***. The description is not a set of directions but a company. The Book gives the register of that company in a verse that reads like a graduation: (4:69 — **“the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous—what honourable company”**). Al-Saʿdī works from exactly that list, and Ibn Kathīr notes in the same place that the line defines the path by describing those who were favoured, so that the reader receives the road and the travellers on it at the same moment. The register grows wider in al-Ṭabarī's reading, where the favoured ones include the angels along with the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs and the righteous, and he treats the phrase as an explanation of which road is being asked for rather than a repetition of the request.

Naming the walkers rather than the rules is a teaching method, and its advantage is visible to anyone who has tried to describe conduct in a list. A rule can be memorised and kept in a book. A person can be watched, copied and asked, which is why the Book puts the people first and leaves the detail to be learned from them. This is also why the sūrah can end without a single instruction about what to do: after asking for the road, the reader is handed the company that walks it. The request for guidance is answered in the same chapter that asks for it.

The verse also settles what the person praying is asking for, which is not a private discovery. The path of those God favoured is a path already walked, already tested. Already documented in the lives of the prophets and the righteous, so the request is to be joined to something rather than to invent something. A community that reads this line five times a day is told repeatedly that its religion has a history and a company, and that being guided means walking where others have walked rather than breaking a new trail.

Think of a family teaching a child to swim. The instructions matter, and the child learns fastest by watching the ones who already swim and copying them in water. Nobody in that family thinks the child is being asked to invent swimming, and nobody thinks the watching replaces the practice. That is the shape of this verse, with the previous line asking for the ability and this one pointing at the ones who already have it.

**TWO WAYS OFF THE ROAD, NAMED IN ONE BREATH**

The verse closes by refusing two destinations instead of one: ***“not those You are displeased with”***, ***“or those who are astray”***. The two refusals are not decoration, and the books treat them as a map of the two ways a person can leave a road. Al-Saʿdī describes the first group as people who knew the truth and abandoned it, and the second as people who left the truth in ignorance and error. He identifies the two classes by the communities the Book criticises in those very terms. Making both refusals in a single line is what protects the request before it: the traveller asks for the road and immediately asks not to be put on either of the two roads that run beside it. Al-Jalālayn draws the subtlety out of the substitution, that the road of the blessed is defined by ruling out the other two, so the guided are neither of the communities named.

The identification is older than any commentary, and al-Qurṭubī records how it was established. The majority of the scholars held that the people who incurred wrath are the Jews and the people who went astray are the Christians, and he notes that the Prophet ﷺ explained it in the report of ʿAdī b. Ḥātim. Abū Dāwūd al-Ṭayālisī carries in his collection and al-Tirmidhī in his. The Book itself supplies the support, describing one community as those who earned (5:60 — **“those who earned Allah’s condemnation and displeasure”**). The Book describes the other group as people who (5:77 — **“went astray before ˹you˺. They misled many and strayed from the Right Way”**). Ibn Kathīr adds the nuance that both descriptions apply to both groups in part, since anger and straying are qualities rather than nationalities, and that one quality is merely more particular to each.

The language of the second refusal carries a picture that a reader can hold. Al-Baghawī records that the root of the word for going astray means to perish and to vanish, and that the Arabs said water had gone astray in milk when it disappeared into it. A person who is lost, on that usage, has not merely taken a wrong turn; he has become unfindable, swallowed by what he walked into. That is why the request needs both refusals: a traveller can leave the road by knowing better and refusing it, and he can leave it by walking confidently into the landscape until nobody can place him.

Picture two men who miss their train. The first read the timetable, knew the time and stayed in his seat because he preferred the argument he was having. The second never looked at the board and walked onto the platform in the wrong direction. The station master treats them differently and both of them have missed the train, which is the point of a line that mentions the two failures together rather than one of them alone.

**THE SEAL THE COMMUNITY PUTS ON THE PRAYER**

The sūrah ends with the reciter still asking, and the books record what the community does at that moment. Al-Baghawī, closing his commentary on the chapter, gives the practice of the Sunnah: the reciter says *"Āmīn"* once he has finished, with a pause between it and the last line of the sūrah, and he records the explanations of its meaning — a request for God to hear and answer, the word of one who says so let it be, and in one report a name of God. He also passes on the image the early readers used for it, that it is a seal placed on the supplication, like the seal on a document that protects what it contains.

What makes that practice worth noticing is where it sits in the sūrah's argument. The chapter has been a conversation from its second line, with the servant speaking and, in the report of the divided prayer, being answered line by line. The report records the answer to this final request in the words that it belongs to the servant and that he will receive what he asked for. The word said at the end is not an addition to the prayer; it is the reciter's acknowledgment that he has been answered, which is why it is said quietly and not written into the text.

Read as a whole, the chapter now has a shape that the earlier verses set up. It opens with a name spoken before anything else, moves through praise and mercy and ownership, turns where the servant speaks for himself. Then asks for one thing: the road, and the company on it. Nothing is asked for the body, nothing for wealth, and nothing for the length of life. Al-Saʿdī treats as the sign of a prayer that has its priorities straight, since a person who has the road has what everything else was for.

Think of a letter that ends with a single request and comes back sealed with the answer already written. The sender reads it again the next time he stands for prayer, and the seal tells him the matter is being dealt with. That is how the community has treated these seven lines from the beginning: not as a text recited at God. As the shortest and most complete conversation a human being can have with the One who owns the road he is asking for, and a conversation that begins again with the next prayer.
