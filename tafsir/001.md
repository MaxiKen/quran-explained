# Sūrah Al-Fatihah (Chapter 1) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir

## Introduction to the Sūrah

Everything in the Book that follows rests on these seven lines. They are recited in every unit of the daily prayer, and the sūrah is known by three names that each describe a role it plays. It is the Opening, because the Book begins with it. It is the Mother of the Book, because the whole of the revelation grows out of what it says. It is the seven often-repeated lines, because no other passage is repeated as often in worship, and that last name comes from the Qur'an's own wording about verses read again and again. The shortness of the sūrah is part of its purpose: twenty-five words set out the shape of the whole religion, and the hundred and thirteen sūrahs that come after fill that shape in. A reader who learns these lines has been given the plan of the building before he walks through it.

The sūrah moves in two halves, and the reader is the one who travels between them. The first half speaks about God: praise, lordship, mercy, and the Day when accounts are settled. The second half speaks to Him: worship, dependence, and a request for guidance that will not be answered inside these seven lines but across the rest of the Book. A single line carries the turn where the servant stops describing and begins addressing, and everything after it is spoken in the first person. The Prophet ﷺ reported that his Lord said, *"I have divided the prayer between Myself and My servant into two halves,"* as Muslim carries it. That report explains why the sūrah cannot be read as a statement about God alone. It is a conversation with two voices in it, and the second voice belongs to the person reading.

One question waits at the door of the sūrah, and it is honest to name it before the verses are read. The line that begins the chapter, the naming of God before anything else, is agreed by everyone to be part of the Qur'an, since it stands inside the letter Solomon sent to the queen of Sheba. What is debated is whether it is counted as the first verse of this sūrah or as a marker separating one sūrah from the next. The difference shows in prayer, where one school recites it quietly and another recites it aloud as part of the opening. Both positions come from the practice of the Companions, and each is carried by reports the reader can check. The disagreement is worth knowing about, and it is not worth fighting over, because no position on it changes what the words themselves ask of the person saying them.

Every day the prayer returns the reader to the same seven lines, and this is why their meaning is rehearsed rather than learned once. The words are said in the morning before the day has shown its face and at night after it has spent itself, so the same sentences have to hold both moods. They praise when a person has reason to be pleased and they ask when he has reason to be afraid. Between those two, they keep a course steady that no single day could be trusted to keep on its own. A sūrah repeated that often stops being a text and becomes a habit of speech, and a habit of speech shapes what a person notices and what he reaches for. Read it slowly, then, and it will not stay a formality for long.

## Verse 1:1

> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**A BOOK THAT OPENS WITH A NAME**

A person who starts a job without agreeing who it is for has already left part of it undone. This line settles that question before anything else is said: ***“In the Name of Allah”*** comes first, and the order is the point. The action behind those words is left unspoken, so the sentence bends itself to fit whatever the speaker is about to do. Since it works as well over a meal as over a journey, the reader supplies the deed and the line supplies the owner. In the age before Islam, people opened their journeys and their treaties with the names of their idols, and the habit was fixed enough that revelation corrected it by naming God instead. The first words given to the Prophet ﷺ came as a command to read in the name of his Lord, which placed learning itself under the same rule: (96:1 — **“Read, ˹O Prophet,˺ in the Name of your Lord Who created—”**). A classroom and a kitchen now stand on the same ground, because the same words open both of them.

The same words open a letter written by one prophet to a foreign queen, and the sūrah keeps that memory: (27:30 — **“It is from Solomon, and it reads: ‘In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”**). So the reader who says them is not inventing a private devotion. He is joining a practice older than his own language, used by a king writing to a ruler he did not serve, and used at the head of the Book he received. The Prophet ﷺ said, *"Any matter that is not begun with the Name of Allah is cut off,"* as Abū Dāwūd carries it. What is cut off is the connection rather than the result, since a task can reach its end and still stand alone, touching nothing beyond itself. Whoever names the owner first has tied his hour to Him, and that tie is what makes an ordinary hour worth more than its own product.

**MERCY NAMED TWICE BEFORE ANY REQUEST**

The second half of the line names mercy twice in a row, ***“the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”***, and the pairing does more work than one title could. A reader who has just been told who owns the day is now told what kind of owner he is dealing with, and the answer arrives before any request is made. Because the servant does not ask for anything until later in the sūrah, mercy is placed underneath the asking as the ground that makes it possible at all. The first title spreads over every creature that draws breath, the sparrow and the rain and the man who never thanks Him. The second belongs to those who answer and obey, and so it draws close. The Prophet ﷺ said, *"Allah has a hundred mercies, and He has sent down one of them by which creatures show mercy to one another,"* as al-Bukhārī carries it. One title is measured by how wide it reaches, and the other by how near it comes.

Mercy stated before any command changes the tone of everything that follows, and that is why the two titles stand where they stand. The order tells the reader that obedience is a response rather than a price. A servant who has been told the mercy first can hear a demand later without reading it as a threat. Think of a garden watered by rain that falls on the road and on the beds alike. The same rain becomes a harvest only in the ground that was prepared, and that is the difference the two names hold together. Nothing in the line asks the reader to feel anything in particular; it asks him to say something true about the One he is addressing, and to say it before he says anything about himself.

**WHAT THE LINE ASKS OF A READER**

The habit is small enough to be missed, which is why it needs naming. A person opens the day by reaching for a phone, then for work, then for food, and the name can be said before any of those without adding a minute to the schedule. Said over an exam paper, it settles the nerves, because the outcome has been handed to someone other than the one sitting the test. Said over a meal, it turns eating into something more than refuelling, and that is the point of the silence inside the sentence: one word about who is being served, and the work carries on. Today the practice costs a person nothing except attention, and attention is the thing most likely to be spent elsewhere. The line is short enough to be said between two tasks, and heavy enough to change what those tasks are for.

Nothing about the opening makes a person holy by itself, and that is worth saying plainly. A name spoken without thought adds nothing, because the words are not a charm but an address. What they do is place a life where it belongs in relation to its owner, and the placing is done again each time the mouth says it. This is why the sūrah begins with the name rather than with a request: the reader is being taught the order of speech before he is given anything to ask for. A child learns it before he learns the prayer, and an old man says it when his strength is gone and his schedule is empty. The line travels the whole length of a life without needing to be revised, which is a strange quality for five words to have. Nothing in it depends on the speaker's mood or on the size of the task in front of him. Because it belongs to no particular hour, it can be borrowed by every hour.

## Verse 1:2

> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds

**PRAISE THAT DOES NOT WAIT FOR A GIFT**

The sentence opens with the owner named and the praise handed straight to him: ***“All praise is for Allah”***, and the word chosen for praise does work that a plain thank-you cannot do. Thanks answers something already received, while praise can be spoken for what the one praised is in himself, before anything has changed hands. Because praise is not tied to a favour, it outlives the moment that prompted it, and this is why the sūrah begins with it. The noun is left definite rather than open, so the meaning is not one praise among many but the whole of it, gathered and placed where it belongs. A person can find the ground for it in things he never ordered: the air he is breathing while he reads, the body that has carried him this far, the fact that he woke up at all. Praising God for them is not flattery, since nothing is added to Him by the words. It is the honest naming of where they came from.

That praise is also the first half of a conversation, and the sūrah says so through a report the reader can check. The Prophet ﷺ reported that his Lord said, *"I have divided the prayer between Myself and My servant into two halves,"* as Muslim carries it. The report continues that when the servant says this sentence, the answer comes back that he has praised his Lord. So the reader is not talking to himself, and he is not filling a silence. He is speaking a line that has a reply attached to it, which makes the words heavier than their length suggests. A person who knows that will not rush the sentence. He will say it as something said to someone, because the report has told him who is listening.

**THE LORD WHO KEEPS WHAT HE MADE**

The next phrase explains why praise is owed rather than merely offered: ***“Lord of all worlds”***. The word used here for Lord is not only about owning; it describes the one who brings a thing into being, keeps it standing, feeds it, and moves it from one condition to another. Ibn ʿAbbās read it as the Lord of every living thing that walks the earth and of the dwellers of the heavens, and as the one who creates, provides, and changes his creation by turns. That is a wider claim than ownership, since a man can own what he is incapable of maintaining. Everything a creature needs to continue existing is supplied from outside itself, and the phrase says the supply has a single source. The praise then follows the gifts as a matter of plain honesty, because a life that is held together each day owes its thanks to the hand that holds it.

That care works in two ways, and the difference matters for how a person prays. There is the care that reaches every creature — being made, being fed, being guided to what it needs to survive. There is also a closer care reserved for those who turn to Him, which works by faith, removes what blocks the way, and steadies a person through trials he cannot see the end of. Because the prophets asked for the largest things with this name on their lips, the phrase teaches the reader where a request should be addressed. A man in debt, a woman afraid for her child, a student facing an examination: the same title covers them. This is why the line is not a cold statement about ownership but a promise about maintenance, and praise sits on top of it.

**WHERE EVERY WORLD IS COUNTED**

The worlds named in the phrase are not only this planet and its people. The word covers everything other than God: the heavens and what they carry, the jinn, the animals, the creatures nobody has described, the systems that run without a witness. The plural is deliberate, because each world has its own kind of existence and its own way of declaring what its Maker is. A person sees only a corner of that crowd, and the sentence quietly places him inside it. He is one voice among more than he can count, saying what the whole of creation says by its existence. Since praise is not his private property, he is spared the pride of thinking that he is doing God a service. He is joining a chorus that was already singing before he arrived.

All of this has to land somewhere in a working week, or it stays a theory. Think of a worker who signs for a delivery at the door, opens the package, and then tells the sender that he expects a better box. Nobody should treat a day that way. The reader opens his eyes to a body that works and a house that stands, and both were given before he asked. Today the practice is to spend the first waking minute naming where the day came from, before the inbox is opened and the plans begin. Thanking a giver does not make a person passive; it makes him accurate about his own position in the exchange. A person who has counted what he was given will not begrudge the small errands that come with it, and he will not treat his own strength as a title deed. The sentence keeps him standing where he actually stands, and that is a safer place to work from than the illusion of self-sufficiency.

## Verse 1:3

> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**MERCY SAID AGAIN, IN ITS OWN LINE**

These two titles have already been said once at the head of the sūrah, and they are said again here as a verse in their own right: ***“the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”***. A reader may ask why they return so soon, and the answer lies in what they follow. The line before this one called God the Lord of every world, and lordship on its own can sound like pure authority. Mercy is placed directly after it so that power and kindness are understood as one thing, not two moods taking turns. That order also answers two different fears at once: a person who takes his sins lightly is reminded who the Master is, and a person crushed by them is told how the Master deals. Because both are in every gathering, the two names have to stand together.

The Prophet ﷺ said, *"If the believer knew what punishment Allah has, none would hope for His Paradise; and if the disbeliever knew what mercy Allah has, none would despair of His mercy,"* as Muslim carries it. That report explains the pairing better than any argument can, because it puts a floor under fear and a ceiling on complacency in the same breath. Neither name is allowed to cancel the other. A reader who leans on mercy alone will drift, since he has decided in advance how the story ends. A reader who sees only the authority will break, since no one can carry that weight for long. The verse holds the two together and lets the reader find his own footing between them.

**WHY THE TWO NAMES FOLLOW LORDSHIP**

Nothing in existence is kept alive because God needs it. He loses nothing if a world disappears and gains nothing if it obeys, and this is why the care that runs the creation has to be explained by something other than need. It flows from mercy, the quality He has described Himself as having. The rain falls on land that will never be harvested and on land that will, and the heart keeps beating in a man who never thanks the one who made it. That is a description of generosity rather than of obligation. When the reader learns to see ordinary continuance as a gift rather than a standing arrangement, the whole day changes colour, because nothing in it is owed to him by right.

There is a second reason the titles are stated as a verse of their own, and it has to do with what comes two lines later. The sūrah will shortly hand the servant a request for guidance, and a request is only as large as the hand it is made to. By naming mercy immediately before the great ask, the sūrah tells the reader that the door he is about to knock on has been left open. Think of the sun, which gives its light to a wedding and a funeral in the same hour, asking no one for permission and feeling no strain. The reader stands under that light every morning, whether or not he looks up. Whatever else the sūrah does, it will not let a person say that he was never told the address.

**WHAT MERCY DOES WITH TIME**

Mercy in these two names is not only a feeling in the heart of God; it is the working force behind everything a person touches. The food he ate before he could earn, the teacher who happened to be kind, the accident that did not happen on the road he takes every day: these are not luck. The names say that the world is run by a will that prefers to give. Because the giving is steady rather than spectacular, it is easy to live inside it without noticing, the way a fish lives in water. The verse works against that blindness by asking the reader to say the two titles aloud, morning after morning, until they become the way he explains his own life.

The mercy described here does not remove the need to choose, and this is where the verse has to be handled carefully. A servant who says the names but ignores every instruction has not understood them; mercy is not permission. What the titles do is set the direction of the road before the reader takes a step on it. Today the reader can test his own reading of God by asking how he expects to be met when he fails: with a door slammed or with a hand held out. The sūrah's own answer is given before the question is asked, which is a mercy in itself, and it is repeated here so that no one can miss it. A person who expects to be treated gently will treat others gently in turn, and that habit travels further than any argument about the attributes of God. The verse ends up shaping conduct, which is what a confession of mercy is meant to do.

## Verse 1:4

> Master of the Day of Judgment

**A MASTER WHOSE CLAIM IS ONLY VISIBLE ONE DAY**

Three titles have passed, and each one described God by what He does for the reader: he is praised, he keeps every world, he is merciful. This one names a day rather than a gift: ***“Master of the Day of Judgment”***. The day named is the day of settling accounts, the day a person is paid for what he did and is met with what he chose. Ibn ʿAbbās described it as the day of reckoning and the settling of disputes between created beings, when people are judged according to their deeds and no judge remains but God. Naming the mastery there rather than only here says something about both days. Today a person can imagine that he owns his time, his house and his effort, because the paperwork is in his name. On that day every such claim lapses, and the ownership that was always real becomes the only one left standing.

That last point is where the verse stops being a statement about the future and starts working on the present. The world is a field of action, not a place of settlement, which is why a comfortable life proves nothing about a person's standing and a hard life proves nothing either, and why the Qur'an never treats worldly outcomes as announcements of who is loved. A house that stands on ground unfit for building looks exactly like one that stands on rock, until the weather changes. Wealth, health and power are not verdicts; they are conditions a person works inside. Because the reward is deferred, patience becomes possible and the success of the arrogant stops being confusing. A man who has watched an honest trader lose and a cheat prosper has already asked the question this line answers. Something is being kept for later, and the keeping is deliberate rather than delayed by accident, which means the question of who is winning cannot be answered from the middle of the race.

**WHY ONE DAY, WHEN EVERY DAY IS HIS**

A fair question stands next to this phrase: if God is Master of everything, why name one day as though the rest were otherwise? The answer is that on that day nobody else will be able to make even the appearance of a claim. The Qur'an puts the question to the whole of creation and takes the answer in advance: (40:16 — **“To Allah—the One, the Supreme”**). On that day no speaker opens his mouth without permission and no intercessor steps forward on his own account, so a truth that is hidden here by borrowed authority is exposed there to everyone at once. The verse names the day because it is the day the mastery becomes visible. What the reader says in this line, then, is not news to God; it is training for himself.

The Arabic word for this day carries the sense of repayment, which is the sense the whole line rests on. A life of receiving and spending is answered somewhere, and the answer is not a mood but an account. There is no room in that accounting for the kind of favour that cancels a debt without the debtor knowing it, only for a Master who can give or forgive as He wills. This is why the line is not frightening in the way it might first appear: mercy has just been named twice, and it is the same Lord on both sides of the ledger. The reader who says this sentence with the earlier ones still in his mouth is not describing a stranger who will one day hold power over him.

**THE ONE APPOINTMENT NO ONE RESCHEDULES**

Most of what people plan is planned around days they can move: a meeting is shifted, a deadline extended, a visit postponed. This is the one appointment that keeps itself, and the verse is a quiet reminder that no preparation for it can be outsourced. A person does not know the date of his own reckoning, since the day of his death begins it for him, and the same sentence that tells him who owns the day also tells him he cannot check the calendar. Because of that, the practical shape of belief is not dread but readiness. The reader who lives as though the account were real will spend his hours differently from one who treats them as his own possession.

Here the verse also settles a question people ask when they suffer injustice they cannot answer. The one who took what was not his, the one who lied and was believed, the one who broke a promise without cost: none of it is lost. It is stored, and it is answered on a day that has a Master who was never fooled. That is not a fantasy of revenge; it is the ground on which a person can let go of his own need to punish. Today a reader cheated out of money or reputation can hand the case over instead of carrying it in his chest. The line has already told him who holds the file. What remains for him is to keep his own hands clean in the matter, and that is easier once the case is out of them.

## Verse 1:5

> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help

**THE TURN FROM SPEAKING ABOUT HIM TO SPEAKING TO HIM**

Everything before this line has been spoken about God; here the reader turns and addresses Him: ***“You ˹alone˺ we worship”***. The change is small in print and large in effect, because it moves the sentence from description into presence. A person can discuss a ruler from a distance, but he can only answer him face to face. The reader now stands where the earlier lines have been leading him all along. What is more, he says it as one of a community: it is not my worship that is offered but ours, which takes the attention off his own small performance and joins him to everyone else who has ever bowed. Even a person praying alone is not alone, and the grammar keeps reminding him of it.

The word for worship here is not limited to ritual. It carries obedience, humility and the sort of submission that a road shows when it has been worn flat and made easy to walk. Everything that a person bends to is worship in this sense: the man who will not make a trade decision without consulting his appetite is serving his appetite. Because that is true, the sentence is not a decorative opening to a prayer but the correction of a life. The reader is saying that the One he addresses is the one he will actually obey, and he says it before a single want of his own has been voiced. Worship comes first in the sentence for the simple reason that it comes first in the ordering of a person's life.

**ASKING FOR HELP WITHOUT LOSING THE POINT**

The closing words, ***“and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help”***, add something the opening could not carry on its own. A person can acknowledge that God alone deserves worship and still try to carry the whole of his religion on his own shoulders. Asking for help admits that the strength to obey is itself a gift, and it joins the two halves into one movement: worship offered, and the ability to offer it requested. The Prophet ﷺ reported that his Lord said, *"I have divided the prayer between Myself and My servant into two halves,"* and that this verse is the half shared between them, as Muslim carries it. The report puts the sentence at the exact centre where the servant stops repeating God's praise and begins asking for his own need.

There is another reason the two halves belong in one verse, and it is the practical one. A person who only asks for help ends up treating God as a supplier, and a person who only offers worship ends up treating himself as sufficient, and both mistakes come from taking half a sentence for the whole of it. The verse closes both roads with a single sentence. The early authorities read that first phrase as a declaration that nothing is shared with God in worship and the second as an admission that no strength belongs to the self. Put together, the line teaches the shape of dependence: everything the reader gives is enabled by the one he gives it to, which is why asking does not weaken the offering, but completes it.

**THE WORD PLACED FIRST**

The order of the words carries an argument that a translation cannot show. The one addressed is named before the verb in each half, and Arabic uses that arrangement to shut the door on any partner: you, and no one else, we worship; you, and no one else, we ask. A reader can test himself with a simple question about the week he has just spent. Where did the hours go when no one was watching, and what was the last thing he leaned on when a matter got too heavy for him? Those two answers describe what he actually worships, whatever the sentence on his tongue may be. Think of a man who says the word for one master all week while carrying out the orders of another. The two answers expose the gap faster than any self-examination, because a habit leaves a trail and the trail is the evidence. The verse is designed to make that gap visible, because a person can only correct a habit he has noticed.

This is also the point where a reader's own voice becomes part of the Book's speech. From here to the end of the sūrah, the sūrah speaks with the servant's mouth, and everything it asks afterwards is asked in his name. The direction of the sūrah has reversed: the reader who has heard mercy called twice is now told to do the talking. Today a person can carry the line into the parts of his day that have nothing to do with prayer, since the shape of it applies to a morning's work as well as to a prostration. Worship and the request for help are one decision, made again each time the sentence is said, and the decision is small enough to repeat without strain. That is why the sūrah can put the greatest claim in the Book into seven short lines and expect a person to carry it into a Tuesday.

## Verse 1:6

> Guide us along the Straight Path

**A PRAYER TAUGHT BY THE ONE IT ASKS**

The sūrah has praised, described and declared; now it asks, and the thing it asks for is guidance: ***“Guide us along the Straight Path”***. Nothing is mentioned about food, health or victory, and this is deliberate, since the reader is being shown what a person should want first. A road is worthless to a traveller who cannot find it, whatever supplies he carries, and the request assumes that the destination matters more than the luggage. Notice also who is speaking: the worshipper who has just said that worship and help belong to God alone now asks for something only God can give. Every earlier line was building the posture that makes this request possible, and the request is the first thing done in that posture.

The word translated as guidance is not a single act but a course of leading. It begins with the general direction given to everything created, continues with the explanation of right and wrong that reaches a person through revelation and conscience, and reaches its highest point when God fixes a person on the road and keeps him there. The prophets themselves are described as guided and as guiding others, so the request cannot be about religious information alone. A person may know the way and still not walk it, which is why the prayer has to be made by everyone, including those who are already far along: what is asked is that the walking continue, not only that the map be handed over.

**THE REQUEST IS ABOUT TOMORROW AS MUCH AS TODAY**

Nothing here suggests that guidance is settled once and put away. The line is said in every prayer, several times a day, by people who have already accepted the religion. The reason is plain when the request is read closely: the reader is asking to be guided in the hours that have not happened yet. The knowledge he has is not the same as the strength he will need when the temptation arrives, and today's certainty does not pay for tomorrow's decision. So the prayer keeps re-opening a matter that pride would prefer to consider closed. The Prophet ﷺ told his companions that this request is met with the answer *"My servant will have what he asks for,"* as Muslim carries it. If the request had to be made only once, a lifetime of repetition would make no sense.

Something else is worth noticing in the words chosen. The path is described as straight, and the picture behind the word is a road with no forks in it, clear from end to end. Anyone who has driven in an unfamiliar city at night knows the difference between a road and a turnoff: the first lets a person relax, and the second forces him to choose with the little light he has. Because the sūrah asks for the road rather than for a sign, it teaches the reader to want the whole route correct and not only the next step. The Qur'an explains the same thing elsewhere in a single sentence: (6:153 — **“Indeed, that is My Path—perfectly straight. So follow it and do not follow other ways, for”**). The crooked alternatives are there in the world, and the prayer is the answer to them.

**ONE VOICE STANDING FOR MANY**

The reader asks in the plural: guide us, not guide me. A person who prays only for himself can improve in isolation and remain useless to the people around him, but the opposite is not true, since asking for a group includes the one asking. The plural also keeps a small grammar of humility alive. The Book itself has guided people through other people: a teacher, a parent, a friend who mentioned something at the right moment. Asking on behalf of all of them acknowledges the road the reader actually travelled, which was never a private route. It also asks for the outcome rather than the ownership of it, and that is the difference between wanting to be right and wanting to arrive.

The practical weight of the line shows up when a person is certain about a matter and the certainty is wrong. History is full of sincere people who were zealous on a road that turned, and this verse is the reader's protection against joining them. Today a person can say it in the morning and then watch the day present the options the prayer was about: the shortcut that skips an obligation, the lie that saves face, the account that can be paid late. Guidance is not an idea in those moments; it is the choice made when nobody is watching and the answer is not obvious. The sūrah asks for the whole of that, and it asks first. Because the request is made before the day has shown its hand, it reaches the hours that have not been planned for, and those are the hours that decide most lives. Nobody can pray for a particular temptation in advance, but anyone can ask for the road before he reaches the fork.

## Verse 1:7

> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray

**THE ROAD IS DESCRIBED BY THE PEOPLE WHO WALKED IT**

The request for guidance was made in the previous line, and here the road is shown: ***“the Path of those You have blessed”***. Rather than defining the way by rules, the sūrah points to the travellers, which is a kindness to a reader who has already heard that the path is straight but has not been told where it goes. The Book answers the question in one verse that names four ranks at once: (4:69 — **“And whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be in the company of those blessed by Allah: the prophets,”**) and it continues with the people of truth, the martyrs and the upright. Companionship is on offer there, not merely a route. A person walking a hard road with the right company survives what he could not survive alone, and that is part of what the line asks for.

The blessed ones, then, are not a crowd of the impressive. They are the truthful, the ones who died for what they believed, the ordinary upright, each at his own level, and the sūrah prays to be put on their road rather than to be made like them in every respect. Ibn ʿAbbās read the phrase as asking for the way of those God has favoured with the religion, and he named the prophets among them. The point is that guidance has visible results in ordinary lives: a person prays, pays what he owes, tells the truth when it costs him, and the shape of his days becomes evidence. Because the reader can see such lives around him, the prayer is not asking for something invisible. It is asking to be placed where they are.

**TWO WRONG ROADS, AND THEY GO WRONG DIFFERENTLY**

The next words rule out two alternatives, and the rule is specific: ***“not those You are displeased with”***, and then ***“or those who are astray”***. One group knew what was right and set it aside, and the other meant well and lost the knowledge. The first broke its covenant knowing what it was doing, so anger follows; the second wandered without a reliable map, so being lost describes it better. That difference matters, because it means failure is not one thing. A person can be ruined by pride in what he knows, and he can be ruined by effort spent in the wrong direction. The sūrah prays against both, which is why the verse carries two negations rather than one.

Both roads are worth understanding, because most people who take one believe they are on the other. A rule-keeper who has stopped asking why can end up in the first group without noticing, since habit and obedience look identical from outside. A searcher who reads widely and prays rarely can drift into the second, since interest in truth is not the same as surrender to it. The Qur'an describes the two conditions in its own language in both cases, and classical commentary applies them to the communities that received earlier scriptures and then parted from their purpose. Read that way, the ending of the sūrah is not an insult aimed at anyone's neighbours. It is a map of two failure modes every believer can recognise in himself once the day has gone wrong in a way he did not plan. The point of the map is to keep the traveller watching both hands: what he knows and what he does with it.

**THE ENDING THE SŪRAH CHOSE**

These two groups were named by the Prophet ﷺ himself, in an answer that removes the ambiguity. He said, *"The Jews earned the wrath, and the Christians went astray,"* as Ahmad carries it. The point of the report is not the names but the diagnosis: knowledge without practice and practice without knowledge, and every community since has produced both kinds. Notice also what this ending does to the reader's self-image. Seven lines that opened with mercy close with a warning about how a life can go wrong in either direction, and the warning is placed in the mouth of the person praying, not thrown at anyone else. The reader says it about himself, in company with everyone else praying, many times a day, which is why the warning does its work on the speaker before it ever reaches his neighbour.

The final word of the sūrah is the one sound everyone makes after it. The prayer concludes with the word for the plea to be answered, and the Companions used it behind the Prophet ﷺ openly in prayer. Its meaning is simply the request for what has just been asked, which is why it does not belong to the sūrah's letters but to the reader's own voice. A person does not merely finish a recitation at that point; he signs the request and asks for it to be granted, which is a different act from reading. A person who has said all seven lines has asked for guidance, named the company he wants and named the roads he refuses, and the word settles the matter as a request rather than a report. Today the whole sūrah can be said in the time it takes to walk to a bus stop. It leaves the reader with one line on his lips and the day's own choices waiting in front of him.

**CERTAINTY IS NOT IN THE PACKAGE**

A last observation belongs to the shape of the prayer rather than to its words. Guidance is asked for, not assumed, and the request is placed on the lips of people who have already been guided. That combination tells the reader that certainty about his own direction is not part of the package. A person who cannot imagine being wrong has already stopped asking, and the sūrah gives such a person nothing to lean on. The better posture is the one the words themselves describe: walking with the company of the blessed, using the knowledge that has reached him, and asking every day for the next stretch of the road. That is the whole religion in seven lines, and it is left open for every reader who takes them up.
