# Sūrah Al-Fatihah (Chapter 1) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir

## Introduction to the Sūrah

The Book opens with a prayer. The sūrah a Muslim meets first is the one he will recite most often in his life, and the names it carries describe that place. Three of them are set out by al-Baghawī: the opener of the Book, because God opened the Qur'an with it; the mother of the Qur'an, because everything after it is built on it, the way a town is built on the ground it was founded on; and the seven often-repeated verses, seven by the agreement of the scholars and repeated in every unit of prayer. Two further names are gathered by Ibn Kathīr from the reports — al-Hamd, and al-Salah, the prayer itself, since Muslim records on Abū Hurayrah's authority that God said of it, *"I have divided the prayer between Me and My servant, and My servant will have what he asks."*

Where it came down is settled enough for the reader's purpose. The reports are gathered by al-Alūsī, and the majority of them make the sūrah Meccan; Mujāhid alone called it Medinan, a lone view that some scholars counted a slip of his. A third report has it coming down twice, once in Makkah when the prayer was made obligatory and again in Madinah when the direction of prayer was changed, so that no one would think its place in the prayer had shifted with the qiblah. A report from Mujāhid, carried by al-Baghawī, adds that the sūrah was stored up for this community in particular.

Its shape inside is easy to see. The first three verses speak about God in the third person: praise, then the two names of mercy, then mastery of the Day of Judgement. The middle verse turns to face Him: worship, and the request for help. The last three are a prayer, asking for a path and then describing the company that walks it. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān puts it in one line: the opening three are praise, the closing three are request, and the middle verse carries both an aspect of praise and an aspect of prayer. Al-Ṭabarī, reading the reports that count the verses, held that the basmalah stands at the head of the sūrah but is not one of the seven; Ibn ʿUthaymīn draws the same conclusion from the hadith of the division, which begins with the praise and not with the Name. Agreement on one part of the question is recorded in Maʿārif al-Qurʾān: the words are a verse of the Qur'an, they stand in Sūrah al-Naml, and they are written at the head of every sūrah except the one of Repentance. What the schools disputed is whether they belong to al-Fātiḥah as one of its seven; Abū Ḥanīfah held that the words are a verse revealed to separate the sūrahs rather than a part of any one of them.

What the sūrah carries is larger than seven short lines suggest. Al-Qurṭubī notes that half of it gathers praise and half gathers needs, and that the prayer it teaches is greater than any request a servant could compose, since the words are God's own. Al-Saʿdī sees the whole creed folded into it: lordship in "Lord of all worlds", the oneness of worship in the Name and in "You alone we worship", and the names and attributes of God in "the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful". He adds that no other sūrah of the Qur'an, brief as this one is, gathers what this one gathers. Ibn ʿUthaymīn calls it the mother of the Book, the sūrah without which no unit of prayer stands, and a healing recitation besides, remembering the Companions who recited it over a man who had been stung and watched him rise as though released from a rope.

So the reader comes to these verses as a servant inside a conversation he did not begin. The commentary below follows each phrase as it comes: what the words carry, what the early authorities said of them, what the reports add, and what the verses ask of the person saying them tonight.

## Verse 1:1

> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**THE SŪRAH THE BOOK BEGINS WITH**

The first line of the Qur'an is not a report about God. It is an act done in His name, and everything in this sūrah rests on that beginning. Three names for the sūrah are recorded by al-Baghawī. It is the opener of the Book, because God opened the Qur'an with it. It is the mother of the Qur'an, because the rest of the Book is built on it. It is also the seven often-repeated verses, recited again in every unit of prayer, and the Qur'an's own phrase for it is the third of those names: (15:87 — **“the seven often-repeated verses and the great Quran”**).

Two further names are gathered by Ibn Kathīr from the reports: al-Hamd, and al-Salah, which is prayer itself. Muslim records, on Abū Hurayrah's authority, that God said of the prayer, *"I have divided the prayer between Me and My servant, and My servant will have what he asks."* A sūrah that can be called by the name of the act it belongs to is not a preface to be hurried past. A report from Mujāhid, carried by al-Baghawī, adds that the sūrah was stored up for this community in particular, kept back for it rather than given to the communities before it.

**THE COUNT OF ITS SEVEN VERSES**

Scholars differed over whether this first line belongs among the seven. One position holds that it stands at the head of the sūrah but is not counted with the verses, and al-Ṭabarī set it out in detail. His argument turns on repetition. No two verses of the Qur'an sit side by side repeating the same wording with nothing between them. The two names of mercy repeat here with nothing between them, so the line must stand outside the count. The count therefore begins with the praise, and the repetition is there because the line itself is not one of the verses. The same conclusion is reached by Ibn ʿUthaymīn, who argues from the hadith of the division: the hadith begins with the praise and not with the Name, so the sūrah begins where the hadith begins.

Agreement on one part of the question is recorded in Maʿārif al-Qurʾān. The words are a verse of the Qur'an, they stand inside Sūrah al-Naml, and they are written at the head of every sūrah except the one of Repentance. What the schools disputed is whether they are one of the seven. Abū Ḥanīfah held that they are a verse revealed to separate the sūrahs from one another, not a part of any single sūrah. On either count, the reader's first words in prayer are these, and the seven that follow are the prayer he has been taught to make.

**AN ACT DONE IN A NAME**

The verse does not open with a statement. It opens with an act: ***“In the Name of Allah”***. The preposition demands an action, and no action is spoken. The hearer supplies it from the speaker's purpose, so a servant who says these words places the whole act before him under the name he has spoken. Al-Ṭabarī works the point out further: God taught His Prophet ﷺ to put His name before all his affairs, and made the habit a path for the rest of creation, so that letters and requests and needs all begin the same way.

Two telling scenes in the Qur'an open with these very words. Noah is told to board with them, (11:41 — **“In the Name of Allah it will sail and cast anchor”**), and Solomon's letter to the queen of Sheba opens with them, (27:30 — **“In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”**). Since the line attaches the name to one Lord, and "name" is singular, the servant begins with every name of God and not with one of them; that is the reading al-Saʿdī draws from the wording. A report carried by al-Alūsī shows the practice taking shape in the community: the Prophet ﷺ first wrote *"In Your name, O Allah"*, then wrote the shorter form when the command of the ark came down, and finally the full words once the verse of Sūrah al-Naml had been revealed.

Think of a traveller who reaches a river and finds a boat tied at the bank. Nothing at the water's edge names the owner, and no sign tells him who will carry him across. The first words out of his mouth are the owner's name. The crossing begins with that name and not with the oars. The servant's day is that river, and naming the owner first is how each act is claimed by the One who owns it.

**THE TWO NAMES THAT CARRY MERCY**

The line closes with two names: ***“the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”***. Both point to a mercy that is vast, covers everything and reaches every living thing. It is written in full for those who are mindful of God and follow His messengers; the rest of creation still receives a share of it in this life. That is the reading al-Saʿdī gives, and it explains why the pair is not one name repeated. The first carries fullness and expanse, the way a man filled with rage is described by a form that means fullness. The second says that this mercy actually arrives at the one shown mercy. The difference between the two is drawn out by Ibn ʿUthaymīn, and the reading in al-Jalālayn matches it: the first name from the vastness of the giving, the second from mercy that reaches its object.

The report held for this sūrah renders the line plainly: *"name of Allah, the Beneficent, the Merciful."* Why the pair stands at the head of every sūrah is answered in al-Qurṭubī's gathering of the views. One view treats the line as a pledge set by the Lord at the head of each sūrah, which binds Him to what He has promised inside it. Another treats it as a summary of the whole law, since it points to the One named and to His attributes. Either way, the reader who begins his work with these words has named the mercy that stands over the act he is about to do. The reason mercy closes the line is that the servant names the Lord's dealing before he asks Him for anything at all.

## Verse 1:2

> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds

**PRAISE THAT ANSWERS THE NAME**

The second verse turns from an act into a declaration. Everything in it belongs to God: ***“All praise is for Allah”***. Thanks given purely to God is how al-Ṭabarī reads the line, apart from any object of worship set beside Him and apart from any created thing. The favors that call for thanks come from Him alone. They include the health that lets a man obey, the limbs that carry out what he is told, the provision that keeps him alive, and the teaching that points him toward a home that does not end.

The Qur'an puts the same sentence at the head of other sūrahs. Creation is brought into it at the start of Sūrah al-An'am, (6:1 — **“All praise is for Allah Who created the heavens and the earth”**), and Sūrah Sabaʾ attaches to it everything in the heavens and the earth, (34:1 — **“All praise is for Allah, to Whom belongs whatever is in the heavens”**). Praise in the Qur'an is never a mood. It is the response the gifts require, and a report carried from Ibn ʿAbbās has Jibrīl teaching the Prophet ﷺ this very sentence as the opening of the praise.

The shape of the sentence is noted by Ibn Kathīr. The praise is made definite and then claimed wholly for God, so that no share of it belongs to any creature. The favors behind it cannot be counted by anyone but God, as he reports from al-Ṭabarī. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān places the sentence inside the shape of the whole sūrah: three verses of praise, three of request, and a middle verse that belongs to both halves. A reader who says these words has already begun the prayer the sūrah is training him to make. Muslim records, from Abū Hurayrah, that God answers these very words with *"My servant has praised Me."*

**WHAT PRAISE GIVES BACK**

The reports gathered by al-Qurṭubī push the point further: praise is not only owed, it is profitable. Muslim carries a report from Anas that God is pleased with a servant who eats a mouthful and praises Him for it, or drinks a draught and praises Him for it. Another report, taken by al-Qurṭubī from the collection of Ibn Mājah, says that when God grants a servant a blessing and the servant answers with these two words, what he gives back outweighs what he received. Al-Hasan al-Basri said that no blessing exists for which praise is not better than the blessing itself.

That is a strange account to a man who thinks of gratitude as a debt paid off. The sources treat it as a trade in which the servant always gains, because the blessing passes away and the words remain. Which means the poor man praising God over a plain meal and the rich man praising Him over a harvest are doing the same thing, and the same two words weigh the same in both mouths. The difference the verse makes is not in the size of the gift but in whether the gift is answered at all.

**THE ONE WHO SUSTAINS EVERY WORLD**

A name follows the praise, and the verse hangs on it: ***“Lord of all worlds”***. The worlds are explained in the report held for this sūrah as everything that has a spirit and walks the earth, together with the dwellers of the heavens. The same report reads the words as Master of jinn and human beings, their Creator, their Provider, and the One who moves them from one state to another. The care inside the name is drawn out by al-Saʿdī: the Lord brings the worlds into being, prepares for each of them the means it needs, and keeps them. No creature stands outside that care.

The reading in al-Jalālayn reaches the same ground from the side of authority: possession and command spread over everything that exists besides God. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān arranges the point against a man's own life. Every person stands inside three stretches of time — a past he did not make, a present he does not control, and a future he cannot see — and all three are held by the One named here. That is why the verse does not say Lord of the world. A lord of one world could be one household's protector and another household's threat. A Lord of all worlds leaves no room for a private claim, since no family, no nation, no trade and no age has a lord of its own.

**WHEN THOUSANDS PRAISE AT ONCE**

A practical question is raised by Ibn ʿUthaymīn. A congregation can hold a thousand worshippers at once. One has just begun the praise, another is midway through it, a third is finishing, and the report says that each of them is answered. How can one Lord answer a thousand voices at the same moment? His answer is the breadth of the name in this verse. God's knowledge and mercy reach everything, so the Lord of all worlds hears every tongue at once, and the praise of each is received as though it were the only voice in the room. The same is true of the man saying these words alone at night, half asleep over a sink. The question of whether such a small act counts is already answered by the verse, because a Lord of all worlds is not too large to hear one of them.

Think of the rain that falls on a valley in the first week of the season. It comes down on the whole valley at once, on the orchard, the rock and the roof of a man still asleep. No farmer can say that it fell for him alone, and yet every field drinks what it needs. Mercy from the Lord of all worlds works the same way, and the praise of this verse is the sound of every field answering one rain.

## Verse 1:3

> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**THE TWO NAMES SAID A SECOND TIME**

The sūrah has just named the Lord of every world, and it now returns to the two names it opened with: ***“the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”***. Repetition inside a sūrah this short invites a question, and an answer is worked out by al-Ṭabarī in his treatment of the verse count. The basmalah, on his reading, is not one of the seven. The two names therefore do not repeat a verse at all. They open the basmalah at the head of the sūrah, and they open the third verse here, with the praise standing between them. Had the basmalah been counted, the sūrah would carry two neighbouring verses with the same words and nothing dividing them, and no such pair exists anywhere in the Book. The repetition, in other words, is itself evidence for the count.

Another answer treats the return of the names as something the verse needs. The point is taken by Ibn Kathīr from al-Qurṭubī: once the Lord of every world has been named, mercy said after it brings a warning and then an encouragement. The lordship carries the warning, since a Lord may punish, and the two names carry the encouragement, since this Lord is compassionate and merciful. The Qur'an pairs the two ideas elsewhere for the same reason, as in (15:49) and (6:165). Muslim records, in his collection, a saying of the Prophet ﷺ that sets the balance exactly: *"If the believer knew what punishment Allah has, none would hope in His Paradise; and if the disbeliever knew what mercy Allah has, none would despair of His mercy."* The two names in this verse are also the guard against two diseases, despair in the sinner and self-assurance in the worshipper.

**MERCY THAT IS WIDE, MERCY THAT ARRIVES**

The two names come from mercy, and they divide its work between them. The first describes mercy in its breadth. The second describes mercy in its arrival. Al-Saʿdī reads the pair as mercy that is vast and great, covering everything and reaching every living thing, written in full for those who are mindful of God and follow His messengers. Everyone else still has a share of it, which is why a disbeliever can be healthy, wealthy and loved in this life. The two names do not contradict each other, since one speaks of what God is and the other of what He gives.

The shape of the first name is pressed by Ibn ʿUthaymīn. Its form carries fullness and expanse, the way the form used of a man filled with rage says that rage has filled him. The form therefore says that mercy is an attribute that fills the Lord's dealing with all things. The second name then says that this mercy arrives at the one shown mercy. The reading in al-Jalālayn runs along the same line, with the first name bringing existence and completion to all things by the measure that wisdom requires, and the second giving what perfection it can to the ones who receive. The Qur'an states the pair as a description of God alone: (2:163 — **“the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”**). Al-Saʿdī adds the rule the early scholars agreed on concerning such names: they are affirmed as His own, and everything good that reaches a creature is an effect of them.

A picture may help here. Think of a farmer waiting at the edge of his field in the first rain of the season. The rain falls across the whole valley, on the red rock and the ploughed ground alike, and the rock has nothing to show for it. The same rain on the ploughed ground becomes a harvest. One rain, two names, and the difference between them is the difference between falling and arriving.

**WHAT THE PAIR ASKS OF THE RECITER**

A reader who says these two names has said something about himself as well. He has admitted that everything he has arrived by mercy, because a man brought into being, fed and taught did not earn any of it. He has also admitted that mercy is not permission. The names stand in the middle of a sūrah of praise and prayer, and a servant who leans on them as a licence to disobey has read the verse backwards. The teaching of al-Saʿdī is that this mercy is written in full for the mindful, which means the names push a person toward the obedience they praise God for, not away from it.

There is a practical edge to that. A man who has done something he is ashamed of can look at the second name and refuse to give up, because despair is a claim that mercy is too small. A man who has done something well can look at the first name and refuse to boast, because the deed itself came from mercy. The verse teaches the reciter to hold both, and the prayer he makes with these words on his tongue is a request as much as a praise.

## Verse 1:4

> Master of the Day of Judgment

**MASTER AND KING: THE READINGS HANDED DOWN**

Two readings of the first word of this verse were handed down and both were recited. The difference is worth knowing, because it changes what the sentence says about the day it names. The readings are set out by al-Ṭabarī: one reads ***“Master of the Day of Judgment”***, and the other reads the word as king. The two Arabic words come from two roots, one carrying ownership and the other carrying rule, and each reading brings its own weight. On the reading of king, rule on that day belongs to God alone, and the kings and tyrants who contested it in this life will stand subdued. On the reading of owner, possession itself is His when nothing else is possessed.

The reports on the two readings are gathered by al-Qurṭubī, who counts four forms the word can take and notes that both main readings are narrated from the Prophet ﷺ, and from Abū Bakr and ʿUmar, a point he takes from al-Tirmidhī. He also reports the disagreement over which reading carries more. One view holds that king is the broader, since every king owns what he rules and not every owner rules; on that view rule contains ownership. The other view holds that owner is the stronger, since a man can own what he cannot rule, and on the day in question the whole affair is God's to direct. The two readings disagree about the route and agree about the destination: on that day, no one stands above the One who owns and rules.

**THE DAY THAT SETTLES EVERY ACCOUNT**

The second half turns to the day itself, and the weight of the verse sits there: ***“the Day of Judgment”***. The report held for this sūrah explains it as the day of reckoning and the settling of disputes between created beings, when people are judged by their deeds and there is no judge but God. The reading in al-Jalālayn adds the resurrection to the day's work, and asks why this day is singled out when God owns every day. The answer it gives is that on that day the mastery of no one else will appear, as the Qur'an shows when it asks, (40:16 — **“Who does all authority belong to this Day?”**), and answers with God's own name.

Silence belongs to that day as well. The Qur'an describes the ranks standing before the Compassionate with none speaking unless He gives permission, (78:38 — **“None will talk, except those granted permission by the Most Compassionate”**), and says of the day's arrival that no one will dare to speak except by His leave, (11:105 — **“no one will dare speak except with His permission”**). Reckoning and permission belong together, because a court where everyone speaks freely cannot settle anything. What follows from the verse is a change in how a person measures his own affairs. He keeps his accounts now, on the days he can still pay, because a day is coming when the accounts are kept for him.

**WHAT THE KINGS OF THE EARTH WILL SAY**

The reports carried by Ibn Kathīr make the verse's claim concrete. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim both record a saying of the Prophet ﷺ, on Abū Hurayrah's authority, that the most despised name before God is that of a man who calls himself king of kings, while there is no owner except God. The same two collections record another saying: God will take the earth in His grasp and fold up the heavens in His right hand, and then say, *"I am the King! Where are the kings of the earth? Where are the tyrants? Where are the arrogant?"* The words are a question and not a search, since no one who hears it will answer.

A king in this life is a borrowed title, and Ibn Kathīr treats it that way. Saul is called a king in the Qur'an, and a king who seized ships by force is mentioned there as well; in both places the title belongs to a servant given a piece of authority for a while. The verse under discussion does not use the word that way. What the reader should carry from these reports is not a picture of God seizing power but the ending of every claim to it. A man who has never ruled anything still needs the warning, because every soul builds a small throne somewhere.

**MASTER OF ONE DAY, MASTER OF ALL DAYS**

A question sits under the verse: if God owns every day, why is this day singled out? Maʿārif al-Qurʾān answers that on the Day of Judgement the ownership is visible the way it is not visible now. Al-Saʿdī draws the same picture. On that day the ownership of created beings is cut off, and kings and subjects, slaves and free men stand in one line, each of them waiting on the same verdict from the same Lord. The day is named because it is the day the fact becomes plain, which means the verse is not restricting God's ownership but announcing the hour when no one disputes it.

Think of a market that trades every day of the year. Business is done across its alleys, prices are argued and goods change hands, and nobody stops to ask who owns the ground the market stands on. Then the season ends and the accounts are called in. One ledger is opened at one table, and every trader discovers how small his own figures look. The Day of Judgement is that morning, and this verse is the sign posted at the gate. A reader who says it in his prayer is doing something more than describing the future; he is choosing, now, whose ledger he wants his life written in.

## Verse 1:5

> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help

**THE VERSE THAT TURNS TO FACE HIM**

The sūrah has been speaking about God in the third person. It now turns and speaks to Him: ***“You ˹alone˺ we worship”***. The change of direction is explained by Ibn Kathīr. Once the servant has praised and thanked, he stands before his Lord and addresses Him directly, and the pronoun of the second person marks that standing. The wording also places the object before the action, and that order carries meaning. The order carries meaning: the worship is for God and for no one beside Him, and nothing competes with Him for it. The early scholars who called al-Fātiḥah the secret of the Qur'an called these two sentences the secret of al-Fātiḥah. Muslim records the answer to them: when the servant says these words, God says, *"This is between Me and My servant, and My servant shall have what he asks."*

**WHAT THE SERVICE IN THIS VERSE MEANS**

Worship is a small English word for a large idea, and the sources open it out. The idea behind it is traced by al-Ṭabarī to lowliness and submission among the Arabs, and he points to the road they call trodden flat because feet have passed over it until it lies down under them. He quotes a line of the poet Tarafah describing a camel's path worn smooth by travel, and notes that hope and fear travel with the lowliness. The same sense is given by Ibn Kathīr in one word: subdued. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān puts it in a sentence worth keeping: worship is the utmost humility offered out of intense love. Such an attitude cannot rightly be given to anyone but God, since no created thing deserves the love and the fear at the same time.

What worship includes is wider than most people assume. A comprehensive name for everything God loves and is pleased with, in speech and in deed, in what is seen and in what is hidden: that is how al-Saʿdī describes it. A list passed on by Maʿārif al-Qurʾān from al-Ghazālī's al-Arbaʿīn names ten forms: the prayers, alms, fasting, the pilgrimage, reciting the Qur'an, remembering God in every situation, and earning a living within the limits God set down, among others. A man who keeps his shop honestly is performing worship in the sense of this verse, and so is a woman who holds her tongue when she is angry. Plain terms for this service appear in the Qur'an, (2:21 — **“Worship your Lord, Who created you and those before you”**), and every rival is set aside in (16:36 — **“Worship Allah and shun false gods”**). The reading in al-Jalālayn keeps the point narrow and exact: the servant reserves the worship for God alone, as an acknowledgement of His oneness.

**THE HELP THAT FOLLOWS THE SERVICE**

The second half of the verse asks for something: ***“and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help”***. Fronting the pronoun does the same work here as it did in the first half, and al-Saʿdī draws the result. The sentence means that help is sought from God and from no one else, since a creature who cannot help himself cannot carry another man's need either. He also explains what the asking amounts to: it is reliance on God for bringing what benefits a person and pushing away what harms him, along with trust that God will in fact bring it about. The report held for this sūrah reads it more simply. A servant asks for help in worshipping his Lord, and from the same source he takes confidence in obeying.

The scope of the request is left open on purpose. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān notes that no purpose is named in the sentence, and most commentators read that silence as breadth: the servant asks for help in worship and in every worldly or later concern he has. A single sentence therefore covers a failing business, a sick child, a stubborn habit and a hard exam. The Qur'an gives the request a shape in other places, as when it joins help to patience and prayer, (2:45 — **“And seek help through patience and prayer”**), and when it tells the believer where to put his trust, (73:9 — **“so take Him ˹alone˺ as a Trustee of Affairs”**). Directing the request to God does not forbid a person from mentioning a prophet or a righteous man when he prays, as Maʿārif al-Qurʾān observes, since the asking itself stays with God and the mention is only a way of drawing near.

**WHY THE ORDER HOLDS**

Worship comes first and the request second, and the order is deliberate. Ibn Kathīr reads the two halves as one act of loyalty. The first declares the servant free of every rival to God, and the second admits that he has no power or strength of his own, since all affairs rest in God's hand. Between them, the whole religion is carried in six words. Al-Saʿdī adds a second reason from the wider duty to the narrower one: worship is what the servant owes his Lord in every case, and asking comes after, because the servant needs help to perform the worship he has just promised. The two together are the road to lasting happiness and the way out of every evil, since nothing else delivers a person from what threatens him.

The order has a practical consequence for prayer. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim both record, on the authority of Ubadah b. al-Samit, that the Prophet ﷺ said, *"There is no valid prayer for whoever does not recite al-Fātiḥah of the Book."* A worshipper therefore repeats this bargain in every unit of every prayer. Imagine a new worker who reports to a site on his first morning. He accepts the foreman's orders before he asks for a hammer, and the order of those two actions tells everyone on the site who is in charge. The servant at prayer does the same thing: he hands over the worship first, and only then asks for the tools.

## Verse 1:6

> Guide us along the Straight Path

**A REQUEST THE ASKED ONE TAUGHT**

The sūrah turns to asking, and its first request is ***“Guide us along the Straight Path”***. Guiding is not handing over information. It is taking a person by the hand and moving him toward where he is going, and al-Qurṭubī reads the sentence in that spirit: show us the path, direct us toward it, and show us the way of your guidance that leads to nearness to You. He adds that the sūrah gathers the whole of supplication between its halves, praise in one part and needs in the other, and that this request is better than anything a servant could compose, since the words came from God in the first place.

A question follows from the request, and it is raised by Ibn Kathīr. If a believer is already guided, why does he ask for guidance in every prayer? The answer is that guidance is not a single step taken once. A servant needs his Lord every hour to keep him firm on the road he has entered, and to strengthen him on it, so the request is repeated because the need is repeated. The Qur'an teaches the same prayer elsewhere, (3:8 — **“Do not let our hearts deviate after you have guided us”**), which is the request of people who have already been guided. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān makes the point through the degrees of guidance described by al-Iṣfahānī: the general guidance shared by everything that exists, (20:50 — **“has given everything its ˹distinctive˺ form, then guided ˹it˺”**), the guidance of instruction by which a person learns what is right, the guidance that moves him to act on it, and finally the guidance that brings him home. A prophet can stand on the first three and still ask for the fourth.

**THE PATH THAT HAS NO BRANCHES**

What the path is can be settled from the language before it is settled from doctrine. Ibn Kathīr passes on al-Ṭabarī's report that the community agreed on the meaning: the Straight Path is the clear path with no branches, in the speech of the Arabs. A poet they both quote describes a leader travelling on a path that stays straight while other paths bend away from it. The same word is used for a man's whole course of conduct, whether good or bad, and for that reason the Qur'an can use it of roads that lead somewhere other than where a person hoped. It is applied to the path of guidance, (16:121 — **“He chose him and guided him to the Straight Path”**), and it is also applied to the road down, (37:23 — **“then lead them ˹all˺ to the path of Hell”**). A traveller is always on one of the two, which is why the prayer asks for the path by name rather than asking for safety in general.

The Prophet's own description is recorded in the Musnad of Ahmad, on al-Nawwas b. Sam'an's authority. He described a straight path with two walls on either side, doors standing open in the walls with curtains over them, a caller at the gate of the path warning people not to stray, and another caller above it warning anyone who reaches for a door. Then he named what he had drawn: the path is Islam, the walls are the limits God set, the open doors are the things God prohibited, the caller at the gate is the Book of God, and the caller above is God's admonition in the heart of every Muslim. The report held for this sūrah gives a shorter description: the Straight Path is the religion God is pleased with, and some of the early readers said it is God's Book, while others said the request means: keep us firm in holding to it.

**THE ROAD AND THE WALKING OF IT**

There are two things a traveller needs on a journey, and al-Saʿdī shows that the verse asks for both. The first is guidance to the path: a person has to be brought to the religion of Islam and turned away from whatever else claims his loyalty. The second is guidance within the path: once he is on it, he needs to know its details and to act on what he knows, in belief, worship and daily conduct. The request therefore covers a person's whole life rather than a single decision. It is, on his reading, the most comprehensive supplication a servant can make, which is why it is required in every unit of prayer and not simply recommended once a day.

The reading in al-Jalālayn adds a small grammatical fact with a large consequence. The sentence means "show us the way to it", and the next verse picks up the path again and describes it by the people who walk it. So the path is not left vague. It is identified by its travellers, the way a road is identified by the sign that lists the towns along it. A man driving to a new town follows signs rather than a description of asphalt. In the same way, the safest knowledge of the road to God comes from those who have walked it, and the verse that follows this one names them.

The daily repetition of the request is itself a teaching. A person who has been on this road for years still says these words with his forehead on the ground, because staying on a road requires walking on it again today. A man who prays five times a day is therefore asking for the same thing five times, and the prayer keeps the request in his own ear. What changes over the years is not the words but the number of small decisions the words have covered: a wage that looked doubtful, a temper that rose, a habit that wanted a small allowance. The Straight Path is where those decisions happen, and the request is for every one of them.

## Verse 1:7

> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray

**THE PATH SHOWN BY ITS TRAVELLERS**

The request for the path is answered here by naming people who walk it: ***“the Path of those You have blessed”***. The reason the answer takes this shape is given by Maʿārif al-Qurʾān. A book on its own does not train a man; it tells him what to do and leaves the doing to him. A prophet does not remain in the world forever either. So the answer names, alongside the prophets, the kinds of people who will exist in every generation until the last day, and the Qur'an lists them in a single verse: (4:69 — **“the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous”**). Those four ranks are the company a worshipper asks to travel with, and the path is identified by them rather than described again from scratch.

The grammar of the sentence carries the point further, and it is noticed in al-Jalālayn. The words "those You have blessed" stand in the place of the path named in the verse before this one, so the path is set out twice: once as a road, and once as the people who walk it. Al-Ṭabarī works through the construction that follows at length, and reports two ways of reading it. That word can qualify the blessed ones, so that the path belongs to them and not to the others. It can also repeat the word "path", so that the sentence reads: the path of those You blessed, not the path of those who earned displeasure and not the path of those who are lost. Both readings settle on the same meaning, and the care the early scholars spent on the sentence shows how much weight they placed on it.

**WHAT BLESSING MEANS HERE**

The blessing in the verse is not a list of comforts. The report held for this sūrah explains it as the religion given to those whom God favoured, and then describes a people who received more than that: the followers of Moses, who were shaded by a cloud, and given honey and quails in the wilderness, before the blessings left them. The same report allows a second reading, that the blessed ones are the prophets. The four ranks named in Sūrah al-Nisāʾ gather the two readings together, and al-Saʿdī takes the verse that way: the blessed are the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs and the righteous, and the servant asks to be led on their road.

A subtle point about the shape of the verse is drawn out by Ibn ʿUthaymīn. The request is positive at its centre and fenced on both sides by negatives. The servant asks for the path of the blessed, and then clears away the two ways of missing it, so that nothing of anger and nothing of straying remains in what he has asked for. He supports this from a principle the scholars applied to God's descriptions: when God denies something of Himself, the denial affirms the perfection that stands opposite it, as in the verse where God says He wrongs no one, which affirms the fullness of His justice. The same pattern is at work here. A person asking for guidance is not simply asking to be pointed somewhere; he is asking to be kept from every road that ends in loss.

The temptation to read this worldly blessing is real, and the sources warn against it. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān points out that health, wealth and comfort are not by themselves signs of God's pleasure, because this world is not the place where deeds are paid for. A man may be rich and far, and a man may be sick and near. The blessings the verse is asking for are of a different kind, and the Qur'an states the ground of them plainly when the people of Paradise say, (7:43 — **“We would have never been guided if Allah had not guided us”**). Guidance is a gift before it is an achievement, which is why the servant asks for it instead of announcing that he has found it.

**TWO WAYS OF LEAVING THE ROAD**

The verse ends by naming the first company the traveller wants to avoid: ***“not those You are displeased with”***. Ibn Kathīr sets out the difference between that company and the one still to be named, and he reads the two from their treatment of the truth. One group knew the truth and left it; the other group wanted the truth and lost the way to it. The first earned displeasure because it had the means and refused them, and the second went astray because it looked for the truth in the wrong place and its search failed. The believer is taught to avoid both, since the road that keeps knowledge of the truth and acts on it is a single road.

The second company is named in the same breath: ***“or those who are astray”***. Both groups are attached to known peoples in the reports. Ibn Kathīr carries the saying of the Prophet ﷺ, recorded by Ahmad and graded by al-Tirmidhī as hasan gharib, *"Those who have earned the anger are the Jews and those who are led astray are the Christians."* The same identification is reported by al-Qurṭubī as the majority position among the commentators, explained from the Prophet ﷺ in the story of Adi b. Hatim's acceptance of Islam, a report carried by Abu Dawud al-Tayalisi in his Musnad and by al-Tirmidhī in his Jami. He supports it from the Qur'an's own words about each people, (5:60 — **“It is those who earned Allah's condemnation and displeasure”**), and (5:77 — **“do not go to extremes in your faith beyond the truth”**). He also records a second view, that the angry ones are the polytheists and the lost ones are the hypocrites. The reading in al-Jalālayn adds the quiet point that the substitution leaves no room for either group: the guided ones are neither the objects of anger nor the people who wander.

Maʿārif al-Qurʾān expresses the pair as one fault with two directions. The first group was short in obeying what it knew; the second went past the limit in what it believed, so that a prophet was raised to the rank of the divine. One failed by doing less than the command, the other by adding more than the truth allows. The Qur'an mentions the same two dangers side by side when it speaks of people who went astray and misled others, (32:21 — **“perhaps they will return ˹to the Right Path˺”**), since the correction it offers is a return rather than a new road.

Think of two men who miss the same train. The first has studied the timetable, knows the departure time to the minute, and stays in his chair because the journey does not suit him. The second arrives at the station without the timetable, reads none of the boards, and waits on the wrong platform until the train is gone. Neither is on board, and their reasons are opposite. The verse's two negations are those two men, and the servant asks to be kept from both kinds of missing the road.

**WHAT THE SŪRAH LEAVES IN THE HAND**

The sūrah ends, but the reader's work is not over, and the report held for this sūrah closes it with a word he has said all his life. The report adds that the final word of the sūrah is "Amen", and explains it as a request that God do what the sūrah has asked, so that the worshipper signs the prayer with a name for the answer he wants. It is also read as an appeal: O our Lord, do with us as we have prayed. Ibn ʿUthaymīn closes his treatment of the sūrah by pointing to how much meaning has been folded into seven short lines, meanings that are spread across the whole Qur'an in general terms. The sūrah is recited in every unit of prayer for that reason, and he adds that it is a healing recitation as well: the Companions once recited it over a man who had been stung, and he stood up as though he had never been ill. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim both record the story from Abu Saʿid al-Khudri, and it ends with the Prophet ﷺ telling the party to keep the payment they had taken, and asking for a share of it himself.

What the reader carries out of the sūrah is therefore a practice rather than a conclusion. He has praised God, named His mercy, acknowledged His ownership of the day of account, given his worship and asked for help, and then asked to be placed on the road of the people God has blessed. The next time he stands in prayer, the words will come past his tongue again in the same order. The one thing the sūrah cannot do for him is decide, at the small junctions of his day, which of the two companies he is imitating. That decision is the road itself, and the last word of the sūrah is a request that he be given the strength to stay on it.
