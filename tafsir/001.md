# Sūrah Al-Fatihah (Chapter 1) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir

## Introduction to the Sūrah

Seven verses, and the whole religion stands on them. The sūrah takes its name from its function: al-Fātiḥah, the Opening, because the Book opens with it and because every prayer opens with it. Al-Ālūsī counted its names at more than twenty. It is called the Mother of the Book and the Mother of the Qurʾān according to the majority of the scholars, because the meaning of the whole Book is gathered into it; in an authentic ḥadīth recorded by al-Tirmidhī, who graded it ṣaḥīḥ, Abū Hurayrah said that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: Al-ḥamdu lillāhi rabbi al-ʿālamīn is the Mother of the Qurʾān, the Mother of the Book, and the seven repeated verses of the Glorious Qurʾān. It is called al-Sabʿ al-Mathānī, the seven oft-repeated, because it returns in every unit of every prayer, and because the Qurʾān itself names the gift in a verse of the Makkan revelation: (15:87 — **“We have certainly granted you the seven often-repeated verses and the great Quran”**). It is called the Praise, because its own second sentence is praise; the Prayer, because the prayer is not complete without it — the Prophet ﷺ said that his Lord said, I have divided the prayer between Myself and My servant into two halves; the Cure, because it has been used as a healing; and the Recitation-for-healing, since Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī's companion recited it over a tribal chief who had been stung, and when the Prophet heard of it he said, What made you know that it is a recitation for healing?

There is no disagreement that the Fātiḥah contains seven verses — al-Baghawī records the agreement — and the greater number of the early scholars held it to be Makkan, among them Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatādah and Abū al-ʿĀliyah. Mujāhid alone among the early authorities held it to be Madinan, and it was also said that it came down twice, once in Makkah and once in Madinah, and that this is why it is called the repeated one; but al-Baghawī holds the first position to be the sound one, because God bestowed it upon His Messenger in the verse of al-Ḥijr — and al-Ḥijr is Makkan — so He could not have reminded him of a gift before it was given. Its rank was settled from the first days: no unit of prayer is complete without it, it stands at the head of the written Book, and its reports are carried in the two collections the community has received with the fullest acceptance.

The Prophet's own words give the sūrah its measure. To Ubayy ibn Kaʿb, while he was praying and the Prophet called him and he hurried to answer, the Prophet said, Shall I teach you a sūrah the like of which has not come down in the Torah, the Gospel, the Psalms or the Qurʾān? — and it was this one. The ḥadīth qudsī carried by Muslim states the sūrah's structure from God's side: I have divided the prayer between Myself and My servant into two halves, and My servant shall have what he asks for. When the servant says All praise is for God, God says, My servant has praised Me; when he says the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful, God says, My servant has glorified Me; when he says Master of the Day of Judgement, God says, My servant has entrusted his affair to Me; and what remains is for the servant, who asks and is given.

The arrangement follows that division. The first three verses speak of God in the third person — His praise, His mercy, His judgement. The middle verse turns the address around: the servant now speaks to God directly and gives his worship and asks his help. The last three verses are the petition the first two movements have prepared: the road, described by the people who walk it and by the two ways that have lost it. The sūrah teaches the reader what to say, in what order to say it, and what to ask when everything has been said.

## Verse 1:1

> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**THE UNSTATED VERB**

The Book opens with a sentence that leaves its own verb unspoken. Nothing in it states what is being begun — a recitation, a meal, a journey, a contract. That openness is deliberate: if the verb were written, the sentence would belong to one act and would need new wording for every other act, but because it carries no single act it can stand at the head of every act. Al-Ṭabarī draws out the teaching in this: God trained His Prophet to place the mention of His beautiful names before all his actions, and made what He taught him a sunnah for the whole creation, so that speech, letters and undertakings are opened with it, until the spoken in bismi Allāh stands sufficient for the unspoken intention beneath it. Ibn ʿAbbās read the unspoken verb from the servant's side: the reciter says, in effect, I begin my recitation with the naming of God — and al-Ṭabarī uses this reading to reject the claim that the sentence means, by Allah is the first of everything, because the servants were commanded to begin their affairs with the naming of God, not to report about His greatness: over slaughter and hunting, over food and drink, and at the opening of the recitation of God's revelation and the heads of their letters and books. The Book itself shows the sentence in both stations, at the opening of the revelation and inside it: (96:1 — **“Read, ˹O Prophet,˺ in the Name of your Lord Who created—”**), and in Noah's word over the ark: (11:41 — **“Board it! In the Name of Allah it will sail and cast anchor. Surely my Lord is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”**).

**THE WORDS OF THE SENTENCE**

God renders Allāh. Al-Khalīl and a group held it to be a proper name belonging to God alone, without derivation, like the proper names of the servants; and a group held it to be derived, and from alaha, meaning to worship — which is how Ibn ʿAbbās read the verse of the Heights, wa-yadharaka wa-ilāhataka, meaning and your worship of you. Al-Baghawī records the dispute over the word ism itself: al-Mubarrid derived it from al-sumū, height, as if the name rises over its meaning and the meaning stands beneath it, while Thaʿlab derived it from al-wasm, the sign — and al-Mubarrid's is the sounder, because the word takes its diminutive as al-sumayy. Al-Raḥmān, the Compassionate, is more emphatic and more encompassing, embracing the mercy by which existence is brought forth at all, while al-Raḥīm, the Merciful, indicates the particular mercy by which each creature is sustained, and it is for this reason that the Qurʾān describes the Prophet himself with it. The Book confirms the pairing elsewhere: (17:110 — **“Call upon Allah or call upon the Most Compassionate—whichever you call, He has the Most Beautiful Names.”**). ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib gave the sentence its medicinal rank: in bismi Allāh, he said, is a cure for every illness and an aid upon every remedy; and al-Raḥmān is an aid for everyone who believes in Him, and it is a Name by which none other than Him has been named.

**HOW THE SENTENCE CAME DOWN**

The reports carry the coming-down of the sentence as an event the Companions watched. Ibn Masʿūd said: we did not know the separation between two sūrahs until bismi Allāhi al-Raḥmāni al-Raḥīm came down; and Ibn ʿAbbās said the same in another report: the Messenger of Allah ﷺ did not know the end of a sūrah until it came down. Al-Shaʿbī carried the history further: the Messenger of Allah ﷺ wrote at the beginning, in the script of Quraysh, bismika Allāhumma — in Your name, O God — until the verse of Hūd came down, In the Name of Allah it will sail and cast anchor, and he wrote bismi Allāh; until Call upon Allah or call upon the Most Compassionate came down, and he wrote bismi Allāhi al-Raḥmān; until It is from Solomon, and it reads, In the Name of Allah, the Compassionate, the Merciful came down, and he wrote it in full. The sentence thus grew into its fullness verse by verse, and the same sentence appears in the middle of the Qurʾān, in the letter of Solomon to the Queen of Sheba: (27:30 — **“It is from Solomon, and it reads: ‘In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”**), which is how Ibn ʿAbbās knew the sūrahs apart: the Companions did not know where one sūrah ended and the next began until this sentence came down.

**ITS PLACE IN THE SŪRAH**

Whether the basmalah is a numbered verse of al-Fātiḥah is a live question of law. Al-Shāfiʿī held that it is a verse of the Fātiḥah, and his position wavered over the rest of the sūrahs: he said at one time that it is a verse of every sūrah, and at another that it is a verse only of the Fātiḥah alone — while there is no disagreement among anyone that it is a verse of the Qurʾān in the sūrah of the Ants. For the Fātiḥah he argued with the report carried by al-Dāraquṭnī from Abū Hurayrah: the Prophet ﷺ said, when you recite al-ḥamdu lillāhi rabbi al-ʿālamīn, recite bismi Allāhi al-Raḥmāni al-Raḥīm, for it is the Mother of the Qurʾān, the Mother of the Book, and the seven oft-repeated, and bismi Allāhi al-Raḥmāni al-Raḥīm is one of its verses. Muslim carries the ḥadīth of Anas in which the Prophet ﷺ dozed, then raised his head smiling and said, a sūrah has just come down upon me, and he recited, bismi Allāhi al-Raḥmāni al-Raḥīm, innā aʿṭaynāka al-kawthar. Al-Qurṭubī came down for Mālik's position, because the Qurʾān is established by mass transmission and not by single-chain reports, and Ibn al-ʿArabī added: it is enough for you that the people differ over it, while the Qurʾān is not differed over. Anas ibn Mālik reports that he prayed behind the Prophet, Abū Bakr, ʿUmar and ʿUthmān and they opened the recitation with All praise is for God, Lord of the worlds, without mentioning the basmalah aloud. The disagreement turns on counting, not on worth: no school omits it, and its presence at the head of every sūrah except the one that opens with a disavowal is part of what makes this sūrah the opening.

**THE WRITING OF THE SENTENCE**

The sentence has its own discipline in the pen. Al-Baghawī states it: the bāʾ is a particle that lowers what comes after it, and what it attaches to is suppressed, because the speech indicates it — the estimate is, I begin with the name of God, or say, with the name of God. The alif of ism was dropped for lightness and for the frequency of its use, and the bāʾ was lengthened. Al-Qutaybī said the lengthening was so that the opening of the speech of God's Book would open with a magnified letter; and ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz used to say to his scribes: lengthen the bāʾ, show the sīn, space the two letters apart, and round the mīm — out of reverence for the Book of God. It was also said that when the alif was dropped, its length was returned upon the bāʾ, so that the bāʾ would indicate the alif that had fallen; and when the alif was written back, in Read in the name of your Lord, the bāʾ was returned to its own shape, and the alif is never dropped when ism is added to other than Allah or comes with other than the bāʾ. Al-Ṭabarī preserves one early attempt to read the letters themselves — the report in which ʿĪsā, son of Mary, as a boy in the school asks his teacher what bism is, and answers, the bāʾ is God's splendour, the sīn His radiance, the mīm His kingdom — and al-Ṭabarī sets it aside: he fears it is a slip of the transmitter, who meant the letters separately, as beginners are taught in the school, and joined them; there is no meaning for this interpretation when the sentence is recited as the reciter recites it in God's Book.

## Verse 1:2

> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds

**A SENTENCE THAT WAS TAUGHT, THEN SAID**

The verse arrived with its instruction attached. Ibn ʿAbbās reports, in the chain of al-Ḍaḥḥāk from him, that Jibrīl said to Muḥammad: say, O Muḥammad, All praise is for God; and Ibn ʿAbbās said: All praise is for God is thanks to God, and submission to God, and the acknowledgement of His blessing and His guidance and His origination, and what is beyond that. In the second report of the same chain, Jibrīl said: say, O Muḥammad, All praise is for God, Lord of the worlds — and Ibn ʿAbbās read it: say, praise is for Allah, whose is all of creation — the heavens all of them and who is in them, the earths all of them and who is in them, and what is between them, of what is known and of what is not known; know, O Muḥammad, that this Lord of yours — nothing resembles Him. The praise is therefore not the servant's invention but God's own wording, placed in the servant's mouth so that the servant may praise God in the speech of God, since the servant's own words cannot reach the praise that is due. Ibn Jarīr gathered the meaning: all thanks are due purely to Allah alone, not to any of the objects worshipped instead of Him nor to any of His creation — for His innumerable favours that only He knows the amount of: the tools by which the creation worships Him, the bodies by which they implement His commands, the sustenance of this life, and the warning that points them to eternal dwelling in the residence of everlasting happiness; and it is a praise that Allah praised Himself with, indicating to His servants that they too should praise Him, as if He had said, say: All thanks and praise are due to Allah.

**PRAISE AND THANKS ARE NOT ONE THING**

The word is al-ḥamd, and the early scholars drew a line between it and thanks. A report from al-Ḥakam ibn ʿUmayr, who had companionship with the Prophet, carries the promise attached to it: when you say, All praise is for God, Lord of the worlds, you have thanked God, and He will increase you. It was also said that the saying of the one who says al-ḥamdu lillāh is praise of God by His beautiful names and attributes, while the saying of the one who says al-shukru lillāh is praise of Him by His blessings and His benefits — thanks is given for what has been received, praise is given for the qualities the praised one possesses, whether or not anything has been bestowed. Al-ḥamd is thus more general, and it is carried in the definite form to show that every form of praise, from every praiser, belongs to God alone. Al-Aswad ibn Sarīʿ carried from the Prophet ﷺ the reason the sentence opens the Book: there is nothing to whom praise is more beloved than Allah, and for that He praised Himself and said, All praise is for God. And when ʿUmar asked ʿAlī about this sentence, ʿAlī answered, it is a statement that God loved for Himself, was pleased with for Himself, and loves to have repeated.

**THE VIRTUE OF THE SENTENCE**

The ḥadīth gathers the virtue of it. In the report of Abū Hurayrah and Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī together: when the servant says al-ḥamdu lillāh, God says, My servant has spoken truly — the praise is Mine. Muslim records that the Prophet said God is pleased with the servant who eats a morsel and praises Him for it or drinks a drink and praises Him for it, and Ibn Mājah records, God does not bestow a blessing on a servant and the servant says, All praise is for God, except that what He gave is better than what He took. Al-Ḥasan said it plainly: there is no blessing but that All praise is for God is better than it. And in the Nawādir al-Uṣūl from Anas ibn Mālik: if the whole world, all of it, with its edges, were in the hand of a man of my community, and then he said al-ḥamdu lillāh — al-ḥamdu lillāh would be better than that; and al-Qurṭubī explains the measure: the man was given the world, and then given, upon its heels, this word until he spoke with it, so the word that was given after the world outweighs the world it came after. The Book makes the same sentence the closing word of the blessed: (10:10 — **“and their closing prayer will be, “All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds!””**), and the word of the righteous when the promise is fulfilled: (39:74 — **“Praise be to Allah Who has fulfilled His promise to us, and made us inherit the ˹everlasting˺ land to settle in Paradise wherever we please.” How excellent is the reward of those who work ˹righteousness˺”**).

**THE LANGUAGE OF THE PRAISE**

Al-Qurṭubī gathers the word's field: al-maḥmadah is the contrary of al-madhammah; aḥmada al-rajulu means his affair came to praise; aḥmadtuhu means I found him praiseworthy — you say, I came to such-and-such a place and aḥmadtuhu, meaning I found it praiseworthy and agreeable, when you were pleased with its dwelling or its pasture; a man who is ḥumadah praises things and says of them more than is in them; and ḥamadatu al-nār is the sound of the fire's flaming. The sentence's news-form carries its instruction: al-Baghawī reads it as a report whose meaning is a command — as if He informs that the one who deserves the praise is Allah, and in it is the teaching of the creation, its estimate being: say, al-ḥamdu lillāh.

**THE WORLDS HE OWNS**

Lord renders rabb, a word that gathers mastery, arrangement and ownership into one: the Master without a peer who arranges the affairs of His creatures and to whom all of creation belongs. Saʿdī observes that His nurturing of the worlds is of two kinds: a general nurturing by creation, provision and the guidance to what keeps creatures alive, and a particular nurturing of His friends by faith — its reality is the nurturing of success for every good and protection from every evil, and this is perhaps the secret of the fact that most of the prophets' supplications are carried by this very word, since their requests all fall under His particular nurture. The worlds themselves have been read in several registers: Ibn ʿAbbās said, in one report through ʿIkrimah, the jinn and mankind, and in another through Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, the Lord of the jinn and the men — and in the report of al-Ḍaḥḥāk, everything that God created, the heavens and all that is in them, the earths and all that is in them, and what is between them, known and unknown; and Zayd ibn Aslam read them as those who are given provision. The Qurʾān uses the same title to open its argument for worship elsewhere: (51:56 — **“I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me”**).

## Verse 1:3

> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**WHY THE NAMES RETURN**

The two Names have already appeared in the basmalah, and their return here is not repetition without a reason. Al-Qurṭubī read the placement for its effect on the heart: after describing Himself as Lord of the worlds, He described Himself as the Compassionate, the Merciful, because in lordship of the worlds there is a measure of intimidation, so He coupled it with what carries encouragement, that His attributes might gather the fear of Him and the longing to Him together, since that pairing is what assists obedience and preserves it — as He said, Inform My servants that I am the All-Forgiving, the Most Merciful, and that My punishment is the painful punishment, and Forgiver of sin and Accepter of repentance, severe in punishment, Lord of bounty. Muslim records the ḥadīth that sets the two registers in balance: the Prophet said that if the believer knew what God has of punishment, no one would hope for His Garden, and if the disbeliever knew what God has of mercy, no one would despair of His Garden. The believer is thus taught to recite mercy immediately after lordship and before judgement, so that the servant who stands before the Master of the Day of Judgement arrives already reassured of the disposition of his Master. The Book joins the two registers in the same movement elsewhere: (15:49 — **“Inform My servants ˹O Prophet˺ that I am truly the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”**), and: (6:165 — **“Surely your Lord is swift in punishment, but He is certainly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”**).

**THE ARGUMENT OF THE RETURN**

Al-Ṭabarī took the return as evidence that the basmalah is not itself a numbered verse of the Fātiḥah: if it were, the same verse would be repeated twice, in the same words and with the same meaning, with no intervening speech to separate them, and nothing like that exists in the Book — a verse is only repeated in full within a sūrah with separating passages and intervening speech that changes the meaning or the wording of the repeated verses, and there is no separator between the Compassionate, the Merciful of the basmalah and the Compassionate, the Merciful of the praise. A group of the commentators answered the difficulty another way: they held that this is of the delayed whose meaning is advanced, and the sense is, praise is for Allah, the Compassionate, the Merciful, Lord of the worlds, Master of the Day of Judgement — the description of kingship belonging beside lordship of the worlds, which reports His ownership of all the kinds of creation, and the description of mercy belonging beside the description of greatness and divinity. They pointed to Master of the Day of Judgement as their proof, since it teaches the servant to describe Him with kingship or with ownership — so the like of each description is joined to its like — and they cited the custom of the Arabs, in whose speech the advanced in the place of the delayed is too frequent to count, and the Book's own example, Praise be to Allah who sent down upon His servant the Book and did not make for it any crookedness, upright — where upright belongs to the Book and is delayed after the negation.

**TWO DEGREES OF ONE MERCY**

Both Names intensify the same word, raḥmah, but they are not synonyms. The Compassionate embraces the mercy by which existence is brought forth at all and by which every creature is sustained, so it applies to nothing but God, while the Merciful may be applied to creatures in a limited sense, and the Qurʾān uses it of the Prophet himself. Saʿdī states the compass of the two: a vast and immense mercy that has encompassed everything and reached every living thing, and it is written for those who fear Him. The Book states the same compass in Moses's closing word and God's answer to it: (7:156 — **“But My mercy encompasses everything. I will ordain mercy for those who shun evil, pay alms-tax, and believe in Our revelations”**). The servant who recites the verse stands therefore between two truths: the Lord he has just praised owns him by the right of the worlds, and the mercy he has just named has already encompassed him — and the praise in between is the servant's answer to both.

## Verse 1:4

> Master of the Day of Judgment

**TWO READINGS, ONE SOVEREIGNTY**

The word is recited in two ways, both handed down from the Prophet — al-Tirmidhī records both readings from him and from Abū Bakr and ʿUmar. ʿĀṣim, al-Kisāʾī and Yaʿqūb read mālik, meaning master or owner, and the rest read malik, meaning king; and a group said the two carry one meaning, like farihin and fārihīn, ḥadhirin and ḥādhirīn — the Lord is the master of the house and its mālik alike. Al-Qurṭubī counts four forms in the language — mālik, malik, malk (lightened from malik) and malīk — and records the rare readings beside the famous ones: Muḥammad ibn al-Samīfaʿ read mālika, accusative, and Nāfiʿ is reported to have lengthened the kasrah of malik into malikī, in the dialect of those who lengthen the vowels.

**WHICH READING CARRIES MORE**

The early scholars weighed which carries more. Abū ʿUbaydah and al-Mubarrid held malik the more general and more striking, because every king is a master but not every master is a king, and the king's command runs over the masters within his kingdom, so that the master disposes only by the king's arrangement. It was answered that mālik reaches further: Abū ʿUbaydah himself elsewhere notes that one says the mālik of the slave, the bird and the beast, while one does not say their malik, and that one may be malik of a thing without possessing it, while nothing is mālik of a thing except while possessing it; and it was added that the mālik is the one with the running of the laws of the religion, and with him is the increase of possession. Abū ʿAlī records from Abū Bakr ibn al-Sarrāj the argument of those who prefer the king's reading: God has already described Himself as the owner of everything in Lord of the worlds, so the reading mālik adds a repetition — an objection Abū ʿAlī answers. It was also said that malik is more in keeping with the rest of the Qurʾān, where He names Himself the King: So exalted is Allah, the King, the Truth, and the King, the Most Holy.

**THE DAY THAT HAS NO OTHER OWNER**

Judgement renders dīn, a word the early authorities read as requital: Mujāhid said the reckoning, reading with it the verse that is the upright dīn as the upright reckoning, and Ibn ʿAbbās, Muqātil and al-Suddī said the Judge of the Day of Reckoning, who reckons with the creation for their deeds, evil for evil and good for good, except those whom He pardons. Why this Day is singled out, when God is Lord of every day, Ibn Kathīr states plainly: on that Day no one will be able to claim ownership of anything at all, and no one will speak without His permission. The Qurʾān describes the silence of that assembly: (78:38 — **“None will talk, except those granted permission by the Most Compassionate”**), and (20:108 — **“All voices will be hushed before the Most Compassionate”**), and (11:105 — **“no one will dare speak except with His permission”**). The sovereignty belongs to Him alone, as the Day itself proclaims: (40:16 — **“Who does all authority belong to this Day? To Allah—the One, the Supreme”**), and: (82:19 — **“˹It is˺ the Day no soul will be of ˹any˺ benefit to another whatsoever, for all authority on that Day belongs to Allah ˹entirely˺”**). And He is the King in every register: (59:23 — **“the King, the Most Holy, the All-Perfect”**).

**AL-ṬABARĪ'S CASE FOR THE KING'S READING**

Al-Ṭabarī came down for malik, and his case is close: God has already informed them of His ownership of them in Lord of the worlds, so the description most fitting to follow — with the two verses so close and adjacent — is the one that the earlier descriptions have not already carried, and what those have not carried is kingship. To repeat ownership in other words would be to repeat different phrases with an identical meaning, giving the hearer no benefit he needs, and the wisdom of the Book does not run on repetition. He answers the one who confines Lord of the worlds to the worlds of this life alone: the same man must then say it means the worlds of Muḥammad's time alone, without the worlds of the ages before and after — and whatever he says in one place can be demanded of him in the other. And he preserves the report that carries the verse in the accusative — mālika yawmi al-dīn, addressed as O Master of the Day of Judgement — explaining it by the Arabs' habit in narrated speech, where the speaker turns to address and then returns to report, as a man says, I said to your brother: if you stood, I would stand; and Jibrīl's dictation gives the sūrah both movements, say, O Muḥammad: All praise is for God, Lord of the worlds, the Compassionate, the Merciful, Master of the Day of Judgement, and say also, O Muḥammad: You alone we worship. Al-Qurṭubī preserves both readings and the arguments of both sides, which is the honest position where the reports give two ways.

## Verse 1:5

> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help

**THE ADDRESS TURNS AROUND**

Up to this point the sūrah has spoken about God in the third person: His praise, His mercy, His Day. Now the servant turns and speaks to God directly, and the turn never turns back. Al-Qurṭubī names the movement: He returns from report to address, by way of the colouring of speech — from the beginning of the sūrah to this point has been report about God and praise of Him — and the reason is the servant's own progress: having praised and glorified, he now stands before the One he has described, and the description has become an audience. In the ḥadīth qudsī of the division carried by Muslim, this verse is the hinge of the sūrah: it is where God's half ends and the servant's half begins, and the servant's half is given to him in full. Ibn ʿAbbās gave the verse its meaning in one movement, in the report of al-Ḍaḥḥāk through Jibrīl's dictation: say, O Muḥammad, You alone we worship — You alone we single out, and fear, and hope in, our Lord, and none other than You.

**THE SECRET OF THE SŪRAH**

The object stands before the verb, and that placement carries the whole creed: You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help, meaning we worship You and no other, and we seek help from You and no other. Ibn Kathīr notes that some of the early scholars called this verse the secret of the Fātiḥah, and the Fātiḥah the secret of the Qurʾān, because the first half of it declares innocence of shirk and the second declares innocence of all power and strength of one's own, displaying the recognition that all affairs are controlled by Allah alone. Abū Ḥafṣ al-Farghānī, as al-Sulamī carries him in al-Qurṭubī, gave the verse its creedal standing: whoever acknowledges You alone we worship and You alone we ask for help has declared himself innocent of compulsion and of the denial of the decree. The utterance of the servant under obligation is itself an act: al-Qurṭubī notes that his pronouncing it is an acknowledgement of lordship and a realization of the worship of Allah, while the rest of mankind worship other than Him, of idols and the like.

**WORSHIP IN THE LANGUAGE AND THE RELIGION**

Worship renders ʿibādah, which in the language is the end point of humility — al-Ṭabarī states that worship among all the Arabs has humility as its root, and they call the road subdued and trodden by the feet that cross it muʿabbad, and of this is the verse of Ṭarafah — and in the religion it is the utmost love joined to the utmost humility and awe. Al-Qurṭubī reads naʿbudu as nuṭīʿ, we obey: the worship is obedience and humbling. Saʿdī defines it as the comprehensive name for everything God loves and is pleased with, of outward and inward words and deeds, and adds the two conditions that make worship worship: that it is taken from the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and that it intends the face of Allah — by these two it becomes worship.

**THE HELP ASKED UPON THE WORSHIP**

The help that follows is not a repetition but a condition: the servant declares his worship and immediately asks for the ability to continue it. Al-Ṭabarī answers the objection that a person who is already worshipping cannot need to ask for help to worship: the one who calls on his Lord to help him to obedience is calling for help for what remains of his life, not for what has already passed of his deeds, and God's giving of that help is pure grace and bounty, not a wage — and it is no injustice for Him to withhold His bounty from a servant busy with His disobedience, nor any corruption in His extending it to a servant who strains himself in His love. Al-Ṭabarī draws from the verse its doctrinal edge: God's command to His servants to say, You alone we worship and You alone we ask for help, meaning their asking Him for help upon the worship, is the clearest evidence against the people of delegation among the deniers of the decree, who hold that God does not command any servant with a command except after giving him the help to do it and to leave it — for if that were so, the asking for help would be void, since help would be due from God whether the servant asked or not, and withholding it would be injustice on their saying, and the one who says You alone we ask for help would only be asking his Lord not to act unjustly. Help renders istiʿānah, reliance upon God in the bringing of benefit and the warding of harm, with full trust that He will bring it; and Saʿdī explains why the asking is mentioned after the worship although it is contained in it: the servant, in all of his worship, needs the help of Allah — if Allah does not help him, he does not obtain what he wants of doing the commands and avoiding the prohibitions. The Book states the same pairing in the address to the Prophet: (11:123 — **“So worship Him and put your trust in Him. And your Lord is never unaware of what you do”**), and in the night prayer of the early Makkan period: (73:9 — **“There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him, so take Him ˹alone˺ as a Trustee of Affairs”**), and makes patience and prayer the instruments of the same asking: (2:45 — **“And seek help through patience and prayer. Indeed, it is a burden except for the humble—”**).

**THE READING OF THE ASKING**

Al-Qurṭubī preserves the verse's textual history: nastaʿīn is one sentence joined upon a sentence, and Yaḥyā ibn Waththāb and al-Aʿmash read it with the kasrah on the nūn — nastaʿīn — in the language of Tamīm and Asad and Qays and Rabīʿah; and the word's own build carries its history, for its origin is nastaʿwin, the movement of the wāw transferred to the ʿayn so that it became a yāʾ, as istiʿānah comes from istiʿwān.

## Verse 1:6

> Guide us along the Straight Path

**THE GREATEST SUPPLICATION**

The request comes after the praise, because the servant has been taught to open with praise before he asks. Al-Qurṭubī states its standing: Guide us is a supplication and a desire from the nurtured to the Nurturer, and its meaning is: show us the Straight Path, direct us to it, and make us see the path of Your guidance that arrives at Your intimacy and Your nearness — God placed the greatness of the supplication and its sum in this sūrah: half of it is the gathering of praise, and half the gathering of needs, and He made this supplication more excellent than the one a supplicant supplicates with, because this is speech the Lord of the worlds spoke, so you are supplicating with a supplication that is His own speech. It was also said that its meaning is: direct us by employing the sunnahs in the performance of Your obligations. Saʿdī states that this supplication is the most comprehensive and the most beneficial a servant can make, which is why it is obligatory upon a person to say it in every rakʿah.

**GUIDANCE TO THE PATH AND ALONG IT**

The word guidance, hidāyah, carries several degrees, and the verse asks for them together. Ibn ʿAbbās read the request as a request for steadfastness — grant us success in remaining firm upon it — and this is al-Ṭabarī's own reading, that the verse means: grant us success in remaining firm upon what You have approved, and granted success to those whom You have blessed among Your servants, of saying and doing — and that is the Straight Path; for whoever is granted success in what those whom God blessed, of the prophets, the truthful and the martyrs, are granted success in, is granted success in Islam, and the confirmation of the messengers, and holding to the Book, and acting on what God commanded, and refraining from what He forbade, and following the way of the Prophet ﷺ and the way of Abū Bakr and ʿUmar and ʿUthmān and ʿAlī, and every righteous servant of Allah — and all of that is of the Straight Path. The person who has already been guided still asks to be guided, because guidance to the path is one thing and guidance along the path, in all its details of knowledge and action, is another, and the servant needs both in every unit of prayer. Saʿdī puts the two together: guide us to the Straight Path and guide us in the Straight Path — the first is the holding to Islam and leaving what is besides it of religions, and the second embraces guidance to all the details of the religion, in knowledge and in action.

**THE PATH NAMED BY ITS NAMES**

The words of the verse have been read as names for one reality. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib said of the Qurʾān, from the Prophet ﷺ, that it is the Straight Path, and in his own word, the Straight Path is the Book of God. Ibn ʿAbbās said through Maymūn ibn Mihrān, that is Islam; and al-Suddī carried the same from many mouths — from Abū Mālik and from Abū Ṣāliḥ, from Ibn ʿAbbās, and from Murrah al-Hamdānī, from Ibn Masʿūd, and from a number of the Companions of the Prophet ﷺ: Guide us to the Straight Path is Islam. Ibn Jurayj carries from Ibn ʿAbbās the simplest of the readings: the Path. Abū al-ʿĀliyah said it is the Messenger of God and his two companions after him, and when this was mentioned to al-Ḥasan he said, Abū al-ʿĀliyah has spoken truly and given sincere counsel. The readings gather rather than divide: the path is the Book, and the Book is carried by the Prophet and his Companions, and all of it is Islam. The Book uses the same word in its degrees: for the showing of the choice: (90:10 — **“and shown them the two ways ˹of right and wrong˺”**), for the guidance to the path: (16:121 — **“˹So˺ He chose him and guided him to the Straight Path”**), for the Prophet's office: (42:52 — **“And you are truly leading ˹all˺ to the Straight Path—”**), for the end of the guided: (7:43 — **“Praise be to Allah for guiding us to this”**), and even for the road of the lost, that the word may be seen as a road whatever walks it: lead them to the path of the blazing fire. And the Book describes the guidance's fruit: (5:16 — **“through which Allah guides those who seek His pleasure to the ways of peace, brings them out of darkness and into light by His Will, and guides them to the Straight Path”**).

**THE LANGUAGE OF THE PATH**

Ṣirāṭ in the language of the Arabs is simply the road, and the straight one is the clear road without branches, which is why the Arabs called the honest man straight and the crooked man deviant. Al-Qurṭubī carries the word's deeper history: the root is the inclining — from it is indeed we have turned to You, and the Prophet came out in his illness leaning between two men, and from it is the gift, because it is inclined from one king to another. Al-Naqqāsh claimed that al-ṣirāṭ is the road in the language of the Romans, and Ibn ʿAṭiyyah set the claim aside as very weak. The readings carry the word's sounds: it is read with the sīn — al-sirāṭ — from the swallowing, as if the road swallows those who walk it; and it is read between the zāy and the ṣād; and with the pure zāy, which al-Farrāʾ carries as the language of ʿUdhrah and Kalb and Banū al-Qayn, the same tribes who say azdaq for aṣdaq.

**THE PARABLE OF THE PATH**

The Prophet himself gave the path its shape in the report of al-Nawwās ibn Samʿān al-Anṣārī: God struck the parable of a straight path — and the path is Islam. The ḥadīth qudsī of the division answers this very verse: when the servant says Guide us along the Straight Path, God says, This is for My servant, and My servant shall have what he asks for.

## Verse 1:7

> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray

**THE PATH DESCRIBED BY ITS WALKERS**

The request now defines its road, and it does so by the people who travel it rather than by its features. The second mention of the path stands in apposition to the first — al-Qurṭubī calls it the substitution of the thing for the thing, as a man says, Zayd came to me, your father — and it carries a doctrine: its meaning is, make our guidance lasting, for a person may be guided to the road and then be cut off upon it; and Jaʿfar ibn Muḥammad read the second path as another path, whose meaning is the knowledge of God and the understanding from Him. The people of the blessing are named elsewhere in the Book: (4:69 — **“the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous—what honourable company”**), and the majority of the commentators took their definition from that verse. The reports name them in several registers: ʿIkrimah said, You blessed them with firmness upon faith and uprightness, and they are the prophets; Ibn ʿAbbās, in one report, named them as the people of Mūsā and ʿĪsā before they altered their religions, which preserves the same idea — the blessed ones are the ones who kept the revelation whole; ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Zayd said they are the Prophet ﷺ and those with him; Abū al-ʿĀliyah said they are the family of the Messenger, and Abū Bakr and ʿUmar, and the people of his house; and Shahr ibn Ḥawshab said they are the Companions of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and the people of his house. Al-Ṭabarī gathers the promise under the description: the path is the path of those whom God described in His revelation, and He has promised that whoever walks it and stands upon it obedient to Allah and His Messenger, He will bring him to their watering-places — and Allah does not break the promise. The verse teaches the servant to ask for companionship rather than directions: the road is known by the company it carries.

**THE TWO WAYS THAT HAVE LOST IT**

The road is then cleared of its two deviations. Those who earned displeasure are the ones who knew the truth and turned from it, and those who went astray are the ones who lost the knowledge itself and wandered without it — al-Ḍaḥḥāk carries from Ibn ʿAbbās: and not the strayed are, other than the path of the Christians, whom Allah led astray by their slander against Him. Ibn Kathīr draws the lesson the early scholars took from the pairing: the path of the believers is knowledge of the truth combined with acting upon it, and whoever abandons one of the two falls into one of the two deviations. The Qurʾān names the displeasure in its place: (5:60 — **“those who earned Allah's condemnation and displeasure”**), and the straying in its place: (5:77 — **“They misled many and strayed from the Right Way”**). The negation is repeated so that both paths are refused, not one, and the servant who says the verse refuses them in himself before he refuses them in anyone else.

**THE READINGS AT THE SEAL**

The verse has its own textual history. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb and Ubayy ibn Kaʿb read both exclusions with the word for other repeated — ghayri al-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim wa-ghayri al-ḍāllīn — keeping the two refusals parallel in form. The position of ghayr was argued over by the grammarians, and al-Ṭabarī preserves the reading of the accusative — ghayra — while refusing to recite with it: what is singular and anomalous from the readings, against what the community has carried in open and widespread transmission, is opinion contrary to the truth, leaning away from the path of Allah and the path of His Messenger and the path of the Muslims. And the pronoun of upon them was read in ten ways by the imams of the reciters, six of them carried from the imams — with the ḍamm of the hāʾ and the stilling of the mīm, with the kasr of the hāʾ and the stilling of the mīm, and the rest with the joining yāʾs and wāws — and Ḥamzah read the hāʾs of upon them, with them and to them with the ḍamm throughout.

**THE SEAL UPON THE BOOK**

The sūrah closes on the servant's reply to what he has just been taught to say. In the ḥadīth qudsī carried by Muslim, this verse is where the promise lands: after it God says, This is for My servant, and My servant shall have what he asks for. When the recitation ends, the servant says Āmīn, and al-Qurṭubī records the report of the man who said it as he finished the Fātiḥah and was told that it is like a seal upon the book, with the instruction reported in al-Bukhārī and Muslim that the servant should say it when the imam says it. The whole sūrah is a supplication, and Āmīn is the servant's signature at its foot: so be it, Lord of the worlds.
