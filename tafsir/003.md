# Sūrah Ali 'Imran (Chapter 3) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir

## Introduction to the Sūrah

Āl ʿImrān is the third sūrah of the Qur'an and one of the two long Madinan sūrahs that stand at the head of the
Book. Al-Qurṭubī states that the sūrah is Madinan by the agreement of the scholars, and Ibn Kathīr gives the reason
the order of its verses makes that plain, since the first eighty-three verses of it deal with the delegation of
Najrān, a party of Christians from the Yemen who came to Madinah in the ninth year after the migration and argued
with the Prophet ﷺ about Jesus. The sūrah was therefore revealed in a city where the community was living beside
Jews and Christians, with a treaty to keep and a constitution to hold, and its opening questions are the questions
that such a neighbourhood asks.

The sūrah carries the name of a family. ʿImrān is the father of Maryam, and the sūrah tells the story of his
household in its early verses, the prayer of his wife, the birth of Maryam, her upbringing in the sanctuary, the
service of Zakariyyā and the arrival of ʿĪsā. Al-Qurṭubī reports from al-Naqqāsh that the sūrah's name in the
Torah is Ṭaybah, the good city, and the report is worth knowing because it places the sūrah in the line of the
scriptures it discusses. A chapter named after an ordinary family is a chapter willing to argue about a family,
and the argument of the sūrah with the Christians of Najrān is an argument about exactly that.

The sūrah rests on the sūrah before it. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān reads the opening of al-Baqarah as the answer to the
prayer that closed al-Fātiḥah — a community asking to be shown the road, and a Book arriving that shows it — and it
reads this sūrah as the next turn of the same conversation, since the Book that answered the prayer now turns to
the communities that had received books before and asks them to recognise their own scripture in the new one. The
sūrah speaks to the People of the Book by name, and it speaks to the believers about the two battles that defined
their first years.

The opening of the sūrah is the same three letters that open al-Baqarah. Al-Wasīt notes that twenty-nine sūrahs of
the Qur'an begin with letters of this kind and records the two positions the scholars took about them, one that
their meaning belongs to God alone and one that they carry a purpose connected with the inimitability of the Book.
Al-Mukhtaṣar gives the second reading in practical terms, that a Book composed of the same letters the Arabs used,
and which no Arab could match, is evidence against a nation that prided itself on its language. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān
observes that the opening of the sūrah proceeds in a particular order, beginning with a rational proof of the
oneness of God and continuing with the proof that arrives from revelation itself.

The middle of the sūrah holds the two battles that tested the community. It mentions the day of Badr, when a small
band faced a larger one, at (3:13 — **“There has already been a sign for you in the two armies that met.”**), and
it devotes a long passage to the day of Uḥud, when the believers were tested by a setback, and to the reasons the
sources give for it. The sūrah also records the invitation of the Prophet to a solemn mutual invocation against
the delegation of Najrān at (3:61 — **“Come, let us call our sons and your sons, our women and your women, ourselves
and yourselves.”**), a verse that Ibn Kathīr explains at length when he tells the story of the delegation. The
sūrah is therefore a book of doctrine, a book of history and a book of instruction for a community that had just
begun to govern itself.

The end of the sūrah returns to the argument from the created world and then hands the community a command. The
verse (3:190 — **“Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of the day and night
there are signs for people of reason.”**) presents the proof of God's oneness in the sky and the calendar, and the
sūrah closes with an instruction that a community under pressure needs: (3:200 — **“O believers! Patiently endure,
persevere, stand on guard, and be mindful of Allah, so you may be successful.”**). The last word of the sūrah is
therefore the same as its first subject, a community holding its ground.

## Verse 3:1

> Alif-Lãm-Mĩm

**THE LETTERS THAT OPEN THE SŪRAH**

The sūrah opens with three letters and no sentence: ***“Alif-Lãm-Mĩm”***. Al-Jalalayn records the position of the early scholars on the letters in the shortest possible way, that God knows best what He means by them. Al-Mukhtaṣar refers the reader to the discussion at the beginning of the second sūrah, where the same letters appear, and explains the purpose the scholars have drawn from them, that a Book made of the same letters the Arabs used, and which no one among them could match, is evidence of the inability of a whole nation to produce its like. Ibn ʿAbbās records the reports that have come down about the letters and about the occasion of the sūrah's opening, and Maʿārif al-Qurʾān places the letters at the head of the first section of the sūrah and treats the whole opening as the beginning of the answer to the prayer that closed the first sūrah.

The reader who has just finished al-Baqarah arrives at the same three letters again, and the repetition is worth noticing. The Book opens several of its sūrahs with letters of this kind, and the sūrahs that follow some of them begin with a statement about the Book itself: (15:1 — **“These are the verses of the Book; the clear Quran”**), and (10:1 — **“These are the verses of the Book, rich in wisdom”**). The letters themselves are left unexplained, and the silence is part of the teaching, since a Book that unfolds every other matter begins by handing the reader three sounds and asking him to read on. The sūrah that follows will be a long one, and the opening of three letters is a small door into a large room. Imagine a great library whose entrance is a plain wooden gate with no name on it; the gate does not tell the visitor what he will find, and it does tell him that he is expected to walk in.

**WHAT THE LETTERS SAY ABOUT THE BOOK**

Al-Wasīt records that twenty-nine sūrahs of the Qur'an open with letters of this kind, and it sets out the two readings the scholars have taken. The first reading holds that the meaning belongs to God alone and that the letters are among the matters whose knowledge is reserved for Him, and the second holds that they carry a purpose connected with the Book that follows them. Al-Mukhtaṣar takes the second reading and applies it to the community that first heard the words, since those listeners were the most eloquent people of their age and their pride was their language, and the Book they were challenged to match begins with the same letters they used every day. A challenge issued in a person's own alphabet is a challenge he cannot dismiss as foreign.

Tafsir al-Saʿdī states the first reading in a single sentence, that the letters are among those whose meaning is known only to God, and the two positions can be held together without difficulty, since the letters may have a purpose in the Book's arrangement while their precise sense rests with God. The sūrah of the bee describes the revelation as a book that makes everything clear, and the same Book leaves a few of its own letters unopened. The contrast is a lesson about a knowledge that is larger than a reader's grasp. Imagine a master craftsman who hands an apprentice a set of tools and says nothing about two of them; the apprentice can build everything he has been hired to build with the tools he understands, and the drawer with the two closed instruments teaches him something about the size of the trade he is entering.

## Verse 3:2

> Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining

**THE NAME THAT STANDS ALONE**

The second verse states who is speaking: ***“Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him”*** ***“the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining”***. Al-Jalalayn reads the sentence as the declaration that there is no god except the One who lives and sustains, and he explains the two names, the Living who does not die and the Eternal who has no beginning. Al-Mukhtaṣar gives the same reading in fuller language, that God alone deserves to be worshipped, that He lives perfectly without any deficiency, that He exists by Himself and needs nothing from His creation, and that the creation exists through Him and stands in need of Him at every moment. Ibn ʿAbbās reads the two names the same way. The Book has opened a sūrah about the family of ʿImrān and about the people of the Book with a sentence about the oneness of God, and the order tells the reader what the sūrah considers the foundation of everything that will follow.

The statement has the same shape as the greatest verse of the previous sūrah, and the comparison is deliberate in the book's arrangement: (2:255 — **“There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him, the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining”**). The two sūrahs open their account of God with the same two names, and the sūrah of Ṭā Hā states the same claim and adds a different conclusion: (20:8 — **“there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. He has the Most Beautiful Names”**). A reader who has spent the previous sūrah watching the Book argue with a community that had divided God into parties arrives at this sentence with the argument already made. The sūrah of ʿImrān will deal with Christians and Jews and with the claims each community made about its own line, and it puts the sentence that both claims have to answer at the front of the page.

**WHY THE PROOF COMES BEFORE THE STORY**

Maʿārif al-Qurʾān observes that this section of the sūrah begins with a rational proof of the oneness of God and then continues with a reported proof, that is, with the revelation itself and the scriptures that confirm one another. The order matters because the sūrah is about to speak to communities that had grown used to arguing from their own books. Before the Book tells them what it says about Abraham and about the family of ʿImrān, it tells them what it says about God, and it makes the oneness of God the standard by which the rest of the sūrah will be measured.

The Book states the same claim with the witnesses named in another verse of this sūrah: (3:18 — **“Allah ˹Himself˺ is a Witness that there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—and so are the angels and people of knowledge”**). The sentence before the reader is short because it is meant to be carried, and a community that has learned it by heart has a sentence to answer the claims it will meet in the street. The sūrah will return to this claim in a few verses with a sharper form of the same argument, and the reader who begins at this verse will recognise the destination when he arrives. Imagine a household that begins every dispute by reading out the name of the person who owns the house; the dispute is not settled by the reading, and everyone in the room knows whose property they are standing on.

## Verse 3:3

> He has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book in truth, confirming what came before it, as He revealed the Torah and the Gospel

**A BOOK THAT CONFIRMS WHAT CAME BEFORE**

The verse names the revelation and its relationship to the books that preceded it: ***“He has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book in truth, confirming what came before it”*** ***“as He revealed the Torah”*** ***“and the Gospel”***. Al-Jalalayn reads the sentence as the statement that the Book was revealed with the truth and confirms the books that came before it, and he identifies the Torah as the book given to Moses and the Gospel as the book given to Jesus. Al-Mukhtaṣar adds the explanation that the Qurʾān's stories are true and its laws just, and that there is no contradiction between the divine books, since they come from the same source. Ibn ʿAbbās reads the verse with the same confirmation, that the Book corroborates what was revealed before it and declares the oneness of God. The Book is presenting itself not as a replacement for a dead religion but as the confirmation of a family of revelations, and the claim is made in the same breath as the claim about God.

The Book states the same relationship in the second sūrah, where the community is told what to do with the revelations that had reached them: (2:41 — **“Believe in My revelations which confirm your Scriptures”**). It states it again in the sūrah of the cattle, where the same confirmation is given a purpose, that the messenger may warn the mother of cities and everyone around it: (6:92 — **“This is a blessed Book which We have revealed—confirming what came before it—so you may warn the Mother of Cities and everyone around it”**). The Book has placed a long account of a nation's history in the previous sūrah and is now beginning a different kind of sūrah, one that speaks to the people of the Book directly. The Book has arranged its books so that each new one arrives with the older ones named in its company, and a community that reads the family tree is less likely to treat the newest arrival as an orphan. The verse gives the reader the framework before the conversation begins, and the framework is that the Qurʾān is the newest member of an old family rather than a stranger arriving at the door. Imagine a household whose eldest son returns from a long journey carrying letters from relatives the family has not seen for years; the letters do not replace the family, they tell the family who it is.

The verse names the two earlier books by the language of their peoples, and the naming is itself a claim about history. The Book does not treat the Torah and the Gospel as inventions or as the private property of the communities that carried them, and it does not treat its own arrival as the cancellation of what God had said before. A reader who has followed the Book's argument in the previous sūrah about the covenant with the Children of Israel will understand the shape of this opening, since the same God who made a covenant with one family and sent messengers after them has now sent a Book that confirms what was given to the family. The next verse will name what those books were for and what the new Book contains in addition, and the reader is being prepared for a claim about authority as well as a claim about continuity.

## Verse 3:4

> previously, as a guide for people, and ˹also˺ revealed the Standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺. Surely those who reject Allah’s revelations will suffer a severe torment. For Allah is Almighty, capable of punishment

**GUIDANCE AND THE STANDARD**

The verse completes the account of the earlier books and introduces the one that decides between them: ***“previously, as a guide”*** ***“for people, and ˹also˺ revealed the Standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺. Surely those”***. Al-Jalalayn reads the first part as the statement that the Torah and the Gospel were revealed beforehand as a guidance for people, and he explains the Standard as the Book that distinguishes between truth and falsehood. Al-Mukhtaṣar reads the same phrase as the criterion that separates what is true from what is not. Ibn ʿAbbās reads the verse with the same two functions, the earlier books as guidance and the Standard as the divider between the true and the false. The Book is making a claim about authority here, since a book that distinguishes between truth and falsehood has been given the office of the judge.

The Book states the same office in the sūrah of the table in language that is even more direct: (5:48 — **“We have revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ this Book with the truth, as a confirmation of previous Scriptures and a supreme authority on them”**). The two verses together give the reader the Book's own account of where it stands in relation to the scriptures that came before, and the account is not that the earlier books were false but that the newest Book guards them. A community that received the Standard had the responsibility of living by what it decided, and the sūrah will shortly describe communities that did the opposite. A reader who has been following the Book's argument about the covenant will remember that the earlier scriptures were never described as the private possession of a single family, and the sūrah of ʿImrān will press that point against the people who had made it so. The verse also names the purpose of the earlier books as guidance for people rather than as the property of one nation, and the phrase is a quiet answer to a claim the reader will meet later in the sūrah.

**THE TORMENT AND THE POWER**

The verse then states what happens to the people who reject what they have been given: ***“who reject Allah’s revelations will suffer a severe torment.”*** ***“For Allah is Almighty, capable of punishment”***. Al-Jalalayn reads the sentence as the statement that the people who reject the revelations of God will meet a severe punishment, and he explains the two names that close the verse, the Almighty and the possessor of vengeance, as the answer to the people who thought that no one would call them to account. Al-Mukhtaṣar reads the same warning in the same terms, and Ibn ʿAbbās reads the verse with the same conclusion. The Book has just described a line of revelations and a Standard that decides between them, and it now tells the reader what is at stake in the decision.

The Book names the same two qualities in the verse about the covenant of the Children of Israel: (3:19 — **“Whoever denies Allah’s signs, then surely Allah is swift in reckoning”**). A reader who has followed the previous sūrah through its account of a people who broke their covenant and then asked for more signs will recognise the pattern that the sūrah of ʿImrān is about to examine in a different community. The warning in this verse is short and it closes a paragraph that began with the name of God, and the arrangement is the sūrah's own outline of its argument. Imagine a court that opens by naming the law it administers and the books it keeps on its shelf, and then states the penalty for the litigant who tears up the paper the court has handed him. The verse has done all three things in four short clauses, and the reader knows what kind of book he is holding.

**THE SENTENCE THE PEOPLE OF THE BOOK HAD TO ANSWER**

The verse names the people who reject the revelations, and the phrase is worth reading beside the story the sūrah is about to tell, since the delegation that came from Najrān was not a group of ignorant idolaters. They were Christians who had received a scripture and who argued from it. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān describes the argument of the opening section as a set of proofs addressed to people who already accepted the existence of God, and it notes that the sūrah moves from the rational argument to the reported one because its audience accepted the authority of a revelation. The warning at the end of this verse is therefore addressed to readers who had

something to lose, which is the category the sūrah deals with for its first eighty-three verses.

The Book describes the same failure in another community in the previous sūrah, where a nation that had been given the Book is told that it traded its revelation for something small: (2:41 — **“Do not be the first to deny them or trade them for a fleeting gain”**). The verse before the reader names the punishment in the same terms that the earlier sūrah used for the same offence, and the two passages belong to one argument that runs across both sūrahs. The community in Madinah was living beside the people the warning describes, and it was in the middle of learning how to speak to neighbours who held an older scripture. The verse gives it the two facts it needs: the books came from God, and the people who reject what God sends answer to God alone. Imagine two families in one street whose grandfathers signed the same deed; the deed is still in the drawer, and the verse says that the one who tears it up has not escaped the signature on it.

## Verse 3:5

> Surely nothing on earth or in the heavens is hidden from Allah

**NOTHING IS HIDDEN FROM HIM**

The verse closes the opening section with a statement about the reach of God's knowledge: ***“Surely nothing on earth”*** ***“or in the heavens is hidden from Allah”***. Al-Jalalayn reads the sentence as the statement that nothing in the earth and nothing in the heavens is hidden from God, and he places it as the answer to the people who imagined that their secrets were theirs alone. Al-Mukhtaṣar reads the same sentence in the same way, and Ibn ʿAbbās reads the verse with the same emphasis on the knowledge that reaches everything. Maʿārif al-Qurʾān notes that the verse is placed directly after the mention of the people who reject the revelations, and he reads it as the reason the warning is not an empty threat.

The Book states the same truth with a detail that a human mind can hold: (40:19 — **“Allah ˹even˺ knows the sly glances of the eyes and whatever the hearts conceal”**). It states it again in the sūrah of the mutual disregard, where the knowledge is described as covering what people conceal and what they reveal: (64:4 — **“He knows whatever is in the heavens and the earth. And He knows whatever you conceal and whatever you reveal”**). The Book returns to this statement at the beginning and at the end of a sūrah because it is the ground of every other statement in it. A community that believes that its thoughts are private can keep a public obedience and a private refusal, and the verse tells such a community that the room it thinks is empty has been occupied all along.

Al-Mukhtaṣar reads the sentence as the statement that God's knowledge covers the hidden and the open alike, and Ibn ʿAbbās reads it with the same reach, so that no intention is lost to the One who will settle the account. The two readings agree that the verse is not a threat about eavesdropping but a statement about the ground of the accounting itself. The sūrah of ʿImrān will deal with two kinds of reader, the one who believes what he is told and the one who argues for the sake of arguing, and the verse before the reader tells both of them where the argument is being heard. The same sentence has closed the greatest verse of the previous sūrah, where the knowledge of God was described as encompassing the past and the future, and the two verses belong to the same account of who God is. The Book says that the knowledge of God reaches what a person has not yet done as well as what he has done, and the verse before the reader is placed at the head of a sūrah that will describe the inner arguments of the people who had received the Book. A reader who understands the verse knows that the arguments in the street were heard in a room that has never been empty. The section that follows will name the people who have received the Book and the people who have turned the signs of God into an argument, and the reader who begins there has this verse in his hand. Imagine a man who tells a lie in an empty room and then discovers that the owner of the house was sitting in the next room with the door open. The verse does not raise its voice; it simply tells the reader that the door has been open all along.

## Verse 3:6

> He is the One Who shapes you in the wombs of your mothers as He wills. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Almighty, All-Wise

**THE HAND THAT SHAPES A CHILD**

The verse carries the argument from the Book to the womb: ***“He is the One”*** ***“Who shapes you in the wombs of your mothers”*** ***“as He wills. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him”*** ***“the Almighty, All-Wise”***. Al-Jalalayn reads the sentence as the statement that God forms every person in the womb as He wills, whether male or female, and with whatever colour and shape He chooses, and he reads the two names at the end as the power that cannot be resisted and the wisdom that is never confused. Al-Mukhtaṣar lists the same variations, male and female, handsome and ugly, black and white, and draws the conclusion the verse is making, that the One who arranges the formation of a human being in the dark is the One who deserves to be worshipped alone. Ibn ʿAbbās gives the same list and adds the pair the sūrah will need later, the fortunate and the unfortunate.

Maʿārif al-Qurʾān notes the place of the verse in the section and observes that the sūrah has moved from the statement of God's oneness to the evidence for it, since the shaping of a person in the womb is put forward as a proof that a reader can verify in his own body. The Book uses the same argument in the sūrah of the sovereignty, where lifeless earth is turned into a sign of the resurrection: (57:3 — **“He is the First and the Last, the Most High and Most Near, and He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things”**). A person who has watched a family grow knows that no human hand arranged the features of the children in it, and the verse asks him to look at what he already knows. The same sūrah will return to this argument at its end, when the creation of the heavens and the earth is placed before the people of reason, and the two verses are the two ends of one rope.

**WHAT A READER CAN SEE IN HIS OWN FAMILY**

The Book's choice of the womb as a proof is a choice that suits a community of ordinary people, since every household in Madinah had seen a baby arrive and had wondered at the face on the cushion. The Book does not ask the reader to study the stars before he believes; it asks him to look at his own children, and it names the womb as the workshop where the shaping happens without a witness. Al-Mukhtaṣar draws the argument out in a single line, that the One who arranges such variety from one place is the One who is capable of everything the sūrah will promise later. A reader who has accepted this verse has accepted the foundation of the resurrection, long before the sūrah reaches the argument.

The Book states the same power with the same simplicity in a verse that a dying person can hold: (6:104 — **“Indeed, there have come to you insights from your Lord. So whoever chooses to see, it is for their own good. But whoever chooses to be blind, it is to their own loss. And I am not a keeper over you”**). The reader is given the evidence and left with the decision, and the verse before him closes with the two names that answer the two halves of the argument. The Almighty answers the question of power, and the All-Wise answers the question of purpose, since a reader may wonder why one child is born healthy and another is not. Imagine a potter who makes bowls of different sizes and colours from the same clay and who sells them to the customers who need them; the difference in the bowls is not a mistake in the workshop, and the verse tells the reader that the shaping of a human being is the work of a hand that knows what it is doing.

## Verse 3:7

> He is the One Who has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book, of which some verses are precise—they are the foundation of the Book—while others are elusive. Those with deviant hearts follow the elusive verses seeking ˹to spread˺ doubt through their ˹false˺ interpretations—but none grasps their ˹full˺ meaning except Allah. As for those well-grounded in knowledge, they say, “We believe in this ˹Quran˺—it is all from our Lord.” But none will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason

**TWO KINDS OF VERSE IN ONE BOOK**

The verse is the longest and most debated in the opening section, and it begins with the Book: ***“He is the One”*** ***“Who has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book, of”*** ***“which some verses are precise”*** ***“they are the foundation of the Book”*** ***“while others are elusive. Those with deviant hearts follow the elusive verses seeking ˹to spread˺ doubt through their ˹false˺ interpretations”***. Al-Jalalayn explains the precise verses as those that are clear in their proof and that form the mother of the Book and the basis of its rulings, and the elusive verses as those whose meanings are known to God alone, such as the opening letters of some sūrahs. Al-Mukhtaṣar states the same division but adds the practical weight the scholars have given it, that the clear verses are the majority and that they are the reference point when there is disagreement, while the elusive verses are those that carry more than one possible meaning. Ibn ʿAbbās reads the precise verses as those that settle the lawful and the unlawful and that are acted upon, and the foundation of the Book as the base on which the rest stands.

Maʿārif al-Qurʾān supplies the occasion which makes the verse's argument concrete, since a group of Christians came to the Prophet ﷺ and began to argue about religion, and the answer they received distinguished between the part of the Book that settles a matter and the part that opens a door. The same kind of argument is described in the sūrah of the cow, where the Book's use of a small creature as a comparison is turned into a dispute by the people who had already decided not to believe: (2:26 — **“Surely Allah does not shy away from using the parable of a mosquito or what is even smaller. As for the believers, they know that it is the truth from their Lord. And as for the disbelievers, they argue, “What does Allah mean by such a parable”**). The Book is telling the reader that a quarrelsome person can find a verse for his quarrel in any text, and it has given him the instrument for telling the two kinds of verse apart.

**THE HEARTS THAT GO LOOKING FOR TROUBLE**

The verse then describes the people who misuse the second kind of verse, and the description is a portrait of a habit rather than of an accident. Al-Jalalayn reads the phrase as the description of people whose hearts have leaned away from the truth and who follow the verses that carry more than one meaning, looking for a reading that will support the doubt they are carrying and a way to divide the community. Al-Mukhtaṣar states the same description and adds the purpose the Book names, that they are trying to spread confusion and to bend the text towards what they have already decided. Ibn ʿAbbās reads the sentence with the same portrait, of a person who picks the verse that suits his argument and leaves the rest of the Book on the shelf.

The Book calls such people people of deviation, and the word is worth noticing because it describes a movement rather than a position. A person who has already turned away from the truth will always find a sentence somewhere that he can use, and the Book tells the community how to recognise the habit. The Book states the consequence of the same behaviour in the sūrah of the cow, where the people who argue over the parables are described as deaf, dumb and blind, so that they will never return: (2:18 — **“They are ˹wilfully˺ deaf, dumb, and blind, so they will never return”**). The reader is shown a disease that begins with a taste for argument and ends with an inability to hear, and the sūrah takes the disease seriously enough to name it in its opening section.

**THE WELL-GROUNDED AND THEIR SENTENCE**

The verse then sets the two groups side by side: ***“but none grasps their ˹full˺ meaning except Allah.”*** ***“As for those well-grounded in knowledge, they say, “We believe in this ˹Quran˺”*** ***“it is all from our Lord.””***. Al-Jalalayn records the two readings the scholars took of the pause in this sentence, whether the full meaning of the elusive verses is known to God alone, in which case the people of knowledge begin their sentence at the next word, or whether the people of knowledge are joined to God in the same clause, so that they know the meaning and say that it all comes from their Lord. Al-Mukhtaṣar takes the second road and explains the qualities that make a person well-grounded, which are knowledge and humility together. Ibn ʿAbbās reads the verse in the same way, and Maʿārif al-Qurʾān draws the lesson of the sentence, that the people of knowledge answer a hard verse with belief rather than with a quarrel, and that they do not use what they do not understand as a weapon against what they do understand.

The sentence of the people of knowledge is short because it is meant to be said, and the Book gives it to the community as a formula for the moment when a verse does not open. The Book says the same thing about the whole religion in another verse where the answer of the believers is described: (4:65 — **“they will never be ˹true˺ believers until they accept you ˹O Prophet˺ as the judge in their disputes, and find no resistance within themselves against your decision and submit wholeheartedly”**). The sūrah of ʿImrān will later tell the community that the messenger and the believers all accept what has been revealed to them, and the verse before the reader is the rehearsal of that acceptance. Imagine a group of travellers standing at a fork in a road where the older map has a small blurred mark in the corner; one of them uses the blur to argue that the map is worthless, and another reads the rest of the map, which is perfectly clear, and says that the road they came by was drawn accurately, so the mark in the corner is not a reason to throw the paper away. The Book gives that sentence to the people of knowledge and names them as the ones who keep the map.

**THE REMINDER AT THE END OF THE VERSE**

The verse closes with the sentence that has already appeared twice in the Book's opening pages: ***“But none will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason”***. Al-Jalalayn reads the ending as the statement that only the people of understanding take the lesson, and he places it after the description of the two groups so that the reader is asked which of them he belongs to. Al-Mukhtaṣar reads the same ending in the same way. Ibn ʿAbbās reads the verse with the same conclusion, and the Book has used the identical sentence at the end of its verse about wisdom in the sūrah of the cow: (2:269 — **“But none will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason”**). The repetition is a signature, and a reader who has followed both sūrahs will recognise it as the Book's own way of saying that understanding is not the same as information.

The sūrah of the cow opened by describing the Book as a guide with no doubt in it, and this verse shows the community how the guide is to be read when a page of it is difficult. A reader who quarrels with the difficult page has abandoned the clear ones, and a reader who holds the clear ones has a place to stand while he waits for the difficult page to open, or accepts that it belongs to God. The Book's own words about the elusive verses do not invite the reader to stop thinking; they tell him where thinking should begin. The words of the verse are followed immediately by a prayer that the community should use about its own heart, and the next verse is that prayer.

## Verse 3:8

> ˹They say,˺ “Our Lord! Do not let our hearts deviate after you have guided us. Grant us Your mercy. You are indeed the Giver ˹of all bounties˺

**THE PRAYER OF THE PEOPLE WHO KNOW**

The verse continues the sentence of the people of knowledge and turns it into a request: ***“˹They say,˺ “Our Lord!”*** ***“Do not let our hearts deviate after you have guided us. Grant us Your mercy. You are”*** ***“indeed the Giver ˹of all bounties˺”***. Al-Jalalayn reads the prayer as the plea that God not cause their hearts to lean away from the truth after He has guided them, and he explains the phrase in the light of the previous verse, since the people of knowledge had watched the people of deviation and had understood where deviation begins. Al-Mukhtaṣar reads the same prayer in the same way, that those who are firm in knowledge ask God to keep them from the fate of the people who moved away from the truth. Ibn ʿAbbās reads the request as the plea for steadfastness in the religion, and the reader is shown that the people who fear deviation most are the people who know the most.

Maʿārif al-Qurʾān notes the sequence of the passage and observes that the previous verse had praised the people of knowledge for their humility before what they did not understand, and that this verse shows a further quality of theirs, which is that they do not trust their own knowledge to keep them upright. The Book teaches the community to make the same request in another sūrah, where the believers are told to pray against the leaning of their own hearts: (2:250 — **“Shower us with perseverance, make our steps firm, and give us victory over the disbelieving people”**). A person who has understood a page of a difficult book may still lose his footing on the next one, and the prayer of these people is the prayer of travellers who have seen a companion fall.

**THE GIVER OF EVERY BOUNTY**

The prayer then names the source of everything it asks for: ***“indeed the Giver ˹of all bounties˺”***. Al-Jalalayn reads the name as the statement that God is the giver of every bounty and that a believer's steadfastness is itself a gift rather than a personal achievement. Al-Mukhtaṣar reads the same name in the same way, and Ibn ʿAbbās reads the verse with the same request for mercy. The Book has been speaking about verses that are clear and verses that are elusive, and the prayer it places in the mouths of the people of knowledge asks for the mercy that keeps a person's heart on the road he has already been shown.

The name at the end of the verse is the answer to the fear in the middle of it, since a person who is afraid of losing his guidance is looking for a hand that can keep it for him. The Book says that the guidance itself is a gift in the verse that gives the community its first request: (2:2 — **“There is no doubt about it—a guide for those mindful ˹of Allah˺”**). A community that has received the Book has received a lamp and a road, and the prayer of this verse is the request for the strength to keep walking with them. Imagine a man who has been given a lantern on a dark road and who stops to ask the person who gave it for enough oil to reach the end; the request does not insult the gift, it shows that the traveller understands how long the road is.

## Verse 3:9

> Our Lord! You will certainly gather all humanity for the ˹promised˺ Day—about which there is no doubt. Surely Allah does not break His promise.”

**A PRAYER THAT LOOKS AHEAD**

The next verse continues the prayer of the people of knowledge and turns it towards the end of the road: ***“Our Lord! You will certainly gather all humanity”*** ***“for the ˹promised˺ Day”*** ***“about which there is no doubt. Surely Allah does not break His promise.””***. Al-Jalalayn reads the sentence as the statement that God will gather humanity on a day of which there is no doubt and will repay every person for what he has done, and he notes the change in the verse's address, since a request that began as a plea ends as a declaration of faith. Al-Mukhtaṣar reads the same declaration, that God will gather all people for the accounting and that He does not break His promise. Ibn ʿAbbās reads the verse with the same confidence, and the reader is shown a community whose prayer rests on a certainty about the last day rather than on a hope.

The Book places the same certainty in the mouth of the community in the sūrah of the family of ʿImrān at its close, where the believers ask their Lord for forgiveness and describe the return as their destination. The prayer of the people of knowledge in this verse is one of the reasons their knowledge is described as a foundation, since a person who is certain about the gathering will live in this world with a different set of priorities. The verse names no punishment and no reward; it names the day and the promise, and it leaves the reader to draw the conclusion that the details of his life are being recorded for it. The sūrah will return to the same subject in the account of ʿImrān's family, where a prayer is answered across generations.

**THE DAY NO ONE WILL MISS**

Al-Qurṭubī notes that the verse is placed among the prayers of the people of knowledge, and he reads the word gather as a description of a collection that leaves no one out, since the Book uses the same language for the gathering of the dead from the graves and the gathering of a scattered people. Ibn Kathīr explains that the day named by the verse is the day of the resurrection, and that the certainty of it is the reason the people of knowledge can pray about it without hesitation. The verse says that humanity will be gathered, not that a nation or a generation will be gathered, and the wideness of the word is the answer to anyone who assumes that the accounting concerns other people. The Book repeats the same word in the sūrah of the cow when it describes the day on which no soul will be able to help another, and the two verses give the reader the same picture from two angles.

The prayer rests on the promise that God has made about the day and on the character of the One who made it, and the second half of the verse is a statement about that character rather than a request. The Book tells the reader in the sūrah of the bee that God's promise arrives on a day that cannot be delayed, and the sūrah of ʿImrān uses the same certainty to steady a community that had seen its enemies prosper: (16:61 — **“But He delays them for an appointed term. And when their time arrives, they cannot delay it for a moment”**). The reader is being taught that the delay of the day is not a sign that the day is uncertain, and that a promise kept late is still a promise kept.

**WHAT A BELIEVER ASKS FOR LAST**

The two verses of prayer in this passage show the reader what a believer asks for after he has learned the religion. The people of knowledge asked for a heart that stays on the road, for mercy, and for the gathering that they know is coming, and they asked for nothing from the world in the same breath. Al-Mukhtaṣar reads the prayer as a model that the community should copy, and the requests rise from the smallest to the largest, from the safety of the heart to the success of the final accounting. The order is worth noticing, because a reader who asks for the last thing first has understood where the road is going.

The Book closes the same sūrah with a prayer that names the destination of the believer, and the words of the closing verses are the summary of everything these verses ask for: (3:200 — **“Patiently endure, persevere, stand on guard, and be mindful of Allah, so you may be successful”**). Imagine a family that has decided to travel to a distant city and that packs only what the road will need, leaving the heavy furniture with a neighbour who is not travelling; the prayers of these verses are the packing list of a people who expect to move.

## Verse 3:10

> Indeed, neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah—and they will be the fuel for the Fire

**WHAT WEALTH AND CHILDREN CANNOT DO**

The verse turns from the prayer of the believers to the situation of the people who reject: ***“Indeed, neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah”*** ***“and they will be the fuel”***. Al-Jalalayn reads the sentence as the statement that neither the wealth nor the children of the disbelievers will be of any use to them with God, and he explains the second half as the fire that they will feed, since the fuel of a fire is what keeps it burning. Al-Mukhtaṣar reads the same sentence in the same way, and Ibn ʿAbbās gives the same sense of a people whose possessions will not stand between them and the consequence. The Book is answering the argument that the sūrah has been developing, since a community that measures a man by his property and his sons has been told what those two things are worth on the day that matters.

The Book uses the same comparison for the world's ornaments in the sūrah of the cave, where wealth and children are described as the decoration of the present life and good deeds as the lasting weight: (18:46 — **“Wealth and children are the adornment of this worldly life, but the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord”**). The verse before the reader states the same truth from the other side, and it names the two things a community in Madinah would have counted first. The Book's argument is not that wealth and children are evil; it is that they cannot carry a person across the boundary where the accounting happens. A reader who has understood the verse stops asking what a man owns and starts asking what a man has sent ahead. Imagine a traveller who arrives at a border with a caravan of goods and no papers; the goods are real and they are worth a great deal at the market behind him, and the officer at the gate has no use for any of them.

**FUEL FOR A FIRE**

The image at the end of the verse is one of the harshest in the sūrah: ***“and they will be the fuel”*** ***“for the Fire”***. Al-Jalalayn explains the words as the statement that the disbelievers are the fuel that feeds the fire, and he places the picture beside the promise the Book makes about wealth, so that the reader understands what the possessions of a life will have become. Al-Mukhtaṣar reads the same ending in the same way, and Ibn ʿAbbās reads the verse with the same image. The Book has used the picture of fuel before, in the sūrah of the cow, where it describes the fire prepared for the people who reject and names its fuel as people and stones: (2:24 — **“fear the Fire fuelled with people and stones, which is prepared for the disbelievers”**). The image is consistent, and the reader is meant to carry it into every verse of the sūrah that follows.

The Book describes the same end with the same words in the sūrah of the pilgrimage, and it uses the picture of fuel in the verse that tells the believers to protect their families: (66:6 — **“Protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones”**). A community that has read the picture twice has been given a reason to take the next several verses of the sūrah seriously, since the sūrah is about to describe the arguments of the people who had the evidence in front of them and went on arguing. The verse before the reader is short and it ends the opening paragraph of the sūrah with an image rather than a proposition, and the choice of an image is deliberate, because a picture stays with a person longer than a sentence. The next verses will describe what those people did with the signs they were given.
