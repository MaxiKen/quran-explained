# Surah Ali 'Imran (The Family of 'Imran, 3) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 71–80

The subject changes at verse 71. Up to now the surah has been arguing about **doctrine** — who Jesus is, who Abraham is, what the true account is. From here it turns to **method**: what people do with a religion once they have one.

And what it describes is unflattering in a way that has not dated. Verses 71 to 78 set out five tactics, and they are recognisably the same five that corrupt religious communities in every century:

1. **Mixing** truth with falsehood so the two cannot be told apart, and concealing the truth while knowing it (71).
2. **Staging** a conversion and an apostasy in the same day to shake other people's confidence (72).
3. **Gatekeeping** — restricting trust and revelation to insiders, because if outsiders knew what you know they could argue you down (73).
4. **Legalising** dishonesty against outsiders by attributing the permission to God (75).
5. **Distorting** scripture in the act of reciting it, so the hearer takes your words for the text (78).

Then verses 79 and 80 state the principle that answers all five, and it is one of the great verses of the Qur'an: no human being to whom God has given the Book, judgment and prophethood would ever say to people, "Be worshippers of me instead of God." What he would say is: *be people of the Lord — because you teach the Book and you study it.*

Three threads to watch. The first is the formula **"while they know"** (*wa-hum ya'lamūn*), which appears three times in these ten verses (71, 75, 78). The surah's charge is never ignorance. The second is the root *w-f-y*, which we met at 3:55 and 3:57 and which returns at 3:76 — God takes in full, pays in full, and requires His servants to fulfil in full. The third is the word *rabb*, lord: verse 64 of the previous batch forbade taking one another as lords, and verses 79–80 close the same door from the other side by forbidding angels and prophets as lords. Between them, the two ends of this passage close every avenue.

One caution that should be stated before we begin, because it governs how all of this should be read. Every charge in this passage is levelled at a **party** — *ṭā'ifah*, *farīq*, *minhum* — never at a people. The Qur'an does not believe in inherited guilt, and a reading of these verses that turns them into an indictment of Jews or Christians as such is not reading them.

## Verse 71

**"O People of the Book! Why do you mix the truth with falsehood and hide the truth knowingly?"**

In simple terms: O People of the Book, why do you clothe the truth with falsehood, and conceal the truth when you know it?

**Two different acts, and the verse names both.** This matters, because they are not the same sin and the distinction is precise.

The first is *talbīs* — *lima talbisūna al-ḥaqqa bi-al-bāṭil*. The root *l-b-s* means to clothe, to cover over, to make one thing look like another. *Talbīs* is not simply mixing, as though you poured two liquids together and could still see the swirl. It is **dressing falsehood in the garment of truth** so that the two become indistinguishable.

That is a much more serious charge, and a much more accurate description of how traditions actually get corrupted. The false teaching rarely arrives saying "I am false." It arrives wearing the vocabulary, the tone and sometimes the actual text of the true one.

The second act is *kitmān* — *wa-taktumūna al-ḥaqqa*. Concealing. **Removing** something true. Where the first adds, the second subtracts.

So the verse identifies both directions of corruption: addition and suppression. And you will notice that most corruption in religious communities is one or the other.

**"While you know."** *Wa-antum ta'lamūn.* The circumstantial clause again — the state you are in while doing the thing. And the state named is knowledge.

This is the fourth time this formula has appeared in recent verses (3:70, and here, and again at 75 and 78) and it is the surah's consistent position: **the problem is never that these people do not know.** The problem is that they know and act otherwise. The Qur'an's harshest language throughout is reserved for that gap, not for ignorance. Ignorance can be taught. A person who knows and conceals has removed the very instrument that would correct him.

**The parallel verse.** Compare 2:42: "Do not mix the truth with falsehood and conceal the truth while you know." The wording is nearly identical, and the repetition tells you this is a standing charge rather than a local one. Also 2:159 and 2:174, on those who conceal what God sent down of the Book.

**And the connection to what follows.** Verse 71 states the general charge. Verse 72 gives you a worked example of it: a scheme in which a genuine declaration of faith is performed and then withdrawn, so that something true is used to produce a false impression. That is *talbīs* in action.

**Now bring this into ordinary life, because this verse describes the most common form of dishonesty there is.** *Talbīs* is very often not a lie. It is a true statement, arranged so as to produce a false conclusion.

You quote the source — but stop short of the sentence that qualifies it. You describe the event accurately — but leave out the part that changes what it means. You report what someone said — but not that he retracted it. Nothing you said was false. What you produced was false. And the word the Qur'an uses is *clothing*: you have dressed it in something it isn't.

The second half is the one that is harder to notice in ourselves because it is a sin of omission, and omissions leave no trace. The verse you don't mention when making your case. The qualification your opponent would need in order for your ruling to look different. The scholar you don't cite because he's on the wrong side. Concealment is easier to commit and much harder to catch, which is presumably why the verse names it separately.

**And the practical test the verse implies.** Before you present something as the teaching of a tradition, ask two questions. Have I added anything to it that isn't there? Have I left anything out that is? The verse prosecutes both.

## Verse 72

**"A group among the People of the Book said ˹to one another˺, “Believe in what has been revealed to the believers in the morning and reject it in the evening, so they may abandon their faith."**

In simple terms: a party among the People of the Book said to each other — enter the faith in the morning and leave it in the evening, so that they may turn back from their religion.

**"A group."** *Ṭā'ifatun.* Again, a party, not the whole. The surah has been scrupulous about this throughout, and it will be again at verse 78 (*farīq*) and verse 75 (*minhum*).

**The scheme.** Publicly accept Islam at the beginning of the day. Publicly renounce it at the end. Do it visibly, in front of the community.

**Why would that work?** This is the interesting question, and the answer is what makes the verse worth reading carefully.

The scheme does not attack Islam's arguments. It does not produce a counter-argument, or point out a difficulty, or offer an alternative. What it attacks is **confidence**. And it attacks it by a route that argument cannot reach: the sight of people trying something and walking away.

Consider the arithmetic. If ten people join a religion in the morning and all ten leave by nightfall, the conclusion a bystander draws is not "those ten were insincere." It is "there must be something wrong with it." The bystander has no way to distinguish a genuine change of mind from a staged one, and the whole point is that he doesn't need to. The performance does the work.

That is why this is more dangerous than any critique. A critique can be answered. A defection cannot — and the people orchestrating it know that.

**"The face of the day."** *Wajha al-nahāri* — literally "the face of the day," meaning its first part, its beginning. It is a lovely Arabic idiom, and the supplied translation's "in the morning" is exactly right. The pairing is with *ākhirahu*, "its end." So the whole day is used: in at the front, out at the back.

**"So they may abandon their faith."** *La'allahum yarji'ūn* — so that they might return, turn back. Note that the verb is about the *Muslims* turning back, not the plotters. The objective is apostasy in the other camp.

**What the verse reveals about the plotters.** This is the point I would most want a reader to take. If the arguments could be answered, they would be answered. If the evidence could be met, it would be met. Instead of a rebuttal they devise a **performance** — and the existence of the performance is itself evidence that the case cannot be met on its merits.

Maududi makes the same observation about the mubāhalah at verse 61: the purpose of proposing it was to expose that their position was not held with conviction. The pattern repeats here. Every time the surah catches the opponents in a manoeuvre, the manoeuvre is a substitute for an argument.

**The hadith and history here are thin.** Reports connect the verse to a group in Madinah who proposed and attempted this, and some reports name individuals. These are transmitted in the tafsir and sīrah literature without strong chains, and I would read them as reported rather than established. The verse's point does not depend on who did it.

**Now into the present, where this verse is more immediately useful than almost anything else in the batch.**

What is being described is, in modern terms, a confidence operation — the manufacture of a social fact in order to produce a belief. And it is everywhere now, because the technology for it has become cheap.

The "ex-Muslim" account that is amplified far beyond its reach, because it does more damage than any argument could. The testimonial that is actually an advertisement. The review written by nobody. The trend manufactured by accounts that do not exist. The crisis that dominates your feed and turns out to have been generated.

The verse tells you three things about this, and all three are useful.

First: **it is old.** The tactic is not a product of the internet; the internet just made it cheap. That is oddly steadying.

Second: **it works on the emotions, not the mind.** Doubt spreads socially. Seeing someone leave does something that hearing someone argue does not. So the appropriate defence is not to panic and try to answer every defection, because that is answering on the plotter's chosen ground.

Third — and this is the Qur'an's own answer, given in the next verse: **guidance is God's.** Not yours to protect by managing impressions, and not in the plotters' gift to destroy. The counter the surah supplies to a manufactured crisis of confidence is not better publicity. It is the recognition of whose hands the matter is actually in.

**And there is a caution for the reader on the other side of it.** Communities that have been targeted this way develop a habit of assuming that every defection is a plot and every questioner is an agent. That is its own corruption, and it is a form of the same gatekeeping that verse 73 is about to condemn.

## Verse 73

**"And only believe those who follow your religion.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Surely, ˹the only˺ true guidance is Allah’s guidance.” ˹They also said,˺ “Do not believe that someone will receive ˹revealed˺ knowledge similar to yours or argue against you before your Lord.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Indeed, all bounty is in the Hands of Allah— He grants it to whoever He wills. And Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing.”**

In simple terms: [they said] "Do not trust anyone except one who follows your religion." Say: guidance is God's guidance. [And they said:] "Do not accept that anyone could be given the like of what you have been given, or that they could argue you down before your Lord." Say: all bounty is in God's hand — He gives it to whom He wills, and God is All-Encompassing, All-Knowing.

**This is the grammatically hardest verse in the passage, and it is worth going slowly, because the sense only appears once you see how it is put together.**

The verse interleaves **two voices**: the voice of the plotters, and God's interruption of them. The supplied translation helps you by putting the interruptions in half-brackets, and that is the right way to read it.

**Their instruction, part one:** *wa-lā tu'minū illā li-man tabi'a dīnakum* — "do not trust anyone except one who follows your religion." Trust is to be confined to insiders. Knowledge is not to be shared across the boundary.

**God's first interruption:** *qul inna al-hudā hudā Allāh* — "Say: the guidance is God's guidance."

**Their instruction, part two** — and this is where the grammar matters: *an yu'tā aḥadun mithla mā ūtītum aw yuḥājjūkum 'inda rabbikum* — "[and do not believe] that anyone will be given the like of what you have been given, or that they will argue you down before your Lord."

**The crux, stated plainly.** The phrase *an yu'tā aḥadun* ("that anyone be given") is the **object of the earlier verb** *lā tu'minū* ("do not believe"). Al-Jalālayn says this explicitly: the clause *qul inna al-hudā hudā Allāh* is a parenthetical insertion (*jumlah mu'tariḍah*) into their speech, and *an yu'tā* resumes what they were saying.

So their sentence, read continuously, is: "Do not believe except in the one who follows your religion... do not believe that anyone will be given the like of what you were given, or that they will argue against you before your Lord."

**There is also a transmitted variant reading.** Some read *a-an yu'tā*, with an interrogative hamza — "**Do** you [now] accept that someone will be given...?" — which turns the clause into a rebuke rather than a continuation. Al-Jalālayn records it, describing the hamza as one of censure. Both readings are transmitted; the first gives the smoother and, I think, stronger sense, but the difference is worth knowing about.

**"Say: the guidance is God's guidance."** *Qul inna al-hudā hudā Allāh.*

Note the construction: *al-hudā* — **the** guidance, definite — *hudā Allāh* — **God's** guidance. It is an identification, not a description. Guidance is not a genus with several species of which God's is one. Whatever is guidance is God's, and whatever isn't His isn't guidance.

That is the direct answer to the first gatekeeping move. Guidance is not a tribal property. It cannot be held in-house, because it was never anyone's to hold.

**"That anyone will be given the like of what you were given."** What were they given? Scripture, wisdom, prophethood — the endowments named explicitly at verse 79. The claim being refused is that these belong to one lineage by right.

**"Or that they will argue you down before your Lord."** *Aw yuḥājjūkum 'inda rabbikum.*

**This is the motive, and it is the most striking clause in the verse.** They are not trying to prevent a political defeat or a loss of standing. They are trying to prevent being **out-argued on the Day of Resurrection**.

The fear is this: if the gentiles come to know what Israel knows, then on the Day when everyone stands before God, those gentiles will be able to say — you knew, and you did not follow. And there will be no answer.

That is a remarkable thing to find in a seventh-century text, and it tells you something about how seriously the Qur'an takes the life to come as a motive for action in this one. These people are behaving as they do because of what they think will happen to them in the next world.

**God's second answer:** *qul inna al-faḍla bi-yadi Allāhi yu'tīhi man yashā'u wa-Allāhu wāsi'un 'alīm.*

**"All bounty is in the hand of God."** The word is *faḍl* — grace, bounty, supererogatory gift. And it is the operative word of the verse, because **grace is by definition not a possession.** If prophethood were a right, an inheritance, or a property, it could be guarded and restricted. Being grace, it goes where its Giver wills it, and no gatekeeping can stop it.

*Bi-yadi Allāh* — in God's hand. We apply here the same rule we applied to God's *yad* at 3:26 and to His *makr* at 3:54: affirm it as He stated it, without asking how and without likening it to a creature's limb. In this context it is an Arabic idiom of exclusive disposal — the thing is entirely His to give.

**"And God is All-Encompassing, All-Knowing."** *Wāsi'un 'alīm.* *Wāsi'* means capacious, vast, not narrow — and the contrast being drawn is with the **narrowness of the gatekeepers**. They have made God's grace small enough to fit inside one community. Maududi notes that the Qur'an uses *wāsi'* against three kinds of narrowness: mean-mindedness, miserliness, and the habit of conceiving God in finite terms because our imaginations are finite.

And *'alīm*, All-Knowing, closes the thought: the giving is not arbitrary. God knows who receives and why. The pair — *wāsi'un 'alīm* — holds together the two things that are always being pulled apart: that grace is utterly free, and that it is not random.

**The theological core of the verse, stated plainly.** This is the Qur'an's rejection of the idea that revelation is an ethnic or institutional monopoly. And it is worth being clear that this is not a small point: **it is the ground of Islam's own possibility.** If prophethood belonged to Israel by descent, there could have been no Arabian prophet, and this whole surah could not exist. The verse is not a side-skirmish. It is load-bearing.

**Two applications, then.**

The first is about **gatekeeping**, which is one of the most durable diseases of religious communities. "This knowledge isn't for people like you." "You need the right background to understand." "Stay within your level." The verse's answer is not that everyone should be handed everything at once — there is such a thing as sequencing, and the Qur'an itself teaches in stages. The answer is that grace is in God's hand, which means no human being gets to decide in advance who it can reach.

The second is about the **fear in the verse**, and it is the searching one. *Yuḥājjūkum 'inda rabbikum* — they will argue against you before your Lord. These people were hoarding knowledge because they were afraid of being answerable to the people they kept it from.

Sit with that. Every teacher, every parent, every person in a position of religious authority carries a version of it: the thing you did not tell someone because telling them would have made you accountable. The qualification you left out because including it would have limited you. The verse says that this is not caution. It is a fear of a specific future conversation, and it is being named as a motive for corruption.

**One necessary caution.** This verse is sometimes quoted to support hostility towards Jews, as though it were saying that a particular people withheld revelation out of malice. That is a misuse. The verse is about a **party's tactic** and about the **principle** that grace is not a tribal possession. It indicts a manoeuvre, not a lineage, and the Qur'an has no concept of inherited guilt. A Muslim who reads it as an ethnic charge has misread the surah he is reading.

## Verse 74

**"He chooses whoever He wills to receive His mercy. And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty."**

In simple terms: He singles out for His mercy whom He wills. And God possesses immense bounty.

**This short verse seals the argument of verse 73.** Having established that grace is in God's hand, it states what follows from it.

**"He chooses."** *Yakhtaṣṣu*, from the root *kh-ṣ-ṣ*, which carries particularity — to single out, to make something specific to someone. The form here is intensive: He particularly, specifically selects.

**"For His mercy."** *Bi-raḥmatihi.* And this is the word that does the work.

The thing given is named **mercy**, not reward, not wage, not right. That single word settles two objections at once. It settles the objection of the person passed over, who might say the selection was unjust — mercy cannot be unjustly withheld, because it was never owed. And it settles the objection of the person selected, who might be inclined to boast — mercy received is not an achievement.

**"Whom He wills."** *Man yashā'u.* The plain Qur'anic affirmation of divine will.

**Now, how to hold this.** This is a verse that sits at the centre of the *qadar* discussion, and the right handling of it is the right handling of all such verses: to affirm what the text affirms, and to hold it alongside what the rest of the text affirms, without constructing a system out of either half.

The Qur'an affirms divine election constantly — 6:125 ("whomever God wills to guide, He expands his breast to submission"), 76:29–30, 81:28–29. And it affirms human responsibility with equal constancy — the same 81:28 says "for whoever among you wills to take a straight path," and then immediately, "and you do not will except that God wills."

Both are in the text. Both are true. The reconciliation is not given to us, and the tradition's consistent advice is that attempting it is not what these verses are for. They are not a puzzle set for the intellect. They are intended to produce two states: humility in the person who has been given something, and hope in the person who has not.

**And note what the verse is NOT saying.** It is not saying that the excluded are excluded arbitrarily, because the previous verse closed with *'alīm*, All-Knowing. It is not saying the selected may relax, because the next verse will require *awfā bi-'ahdihi wa-ttaqā*. Election is described, and it is described as an act of mercy, and then the passage moves straight to obligation.

**The practical use of this verse is the cure for two things.**

The first is **envy of other people's gifts.** If what someone else has is *raḥmah* — mercy — then it is not a zero-sum competition. There is no fixed quantity of grace being distributed, and her having it did not take it from you. *Wāsi'*, All-Encompassing, from the verse before: there is no scarcity here.

The second is **religious pride**, which is the more dangerous of the two because it wears the costume of gratitude. If your community was selected by mercy rather than merit, the only coherent response is thanks. The moment "we were chosen" starts to mean "we are better," the word *raḥmah* has been quietly replaced with something else, and the verse no longer describes what you believe.

## Verse 75

**"There are some among the People of the Book who, if entrusted with a stack of gold, will readily return it. Yet there are others who, if entrusted with a single coin, will not repay it unless you constantly demand it. This is because they say, “We are not accountable for ˹exploiting˺ the Gentiles.” And ˹so˺ they attribute lies to Allah knowingly."**

In simple terms: among the People of the Book is one who, if you entrust him with a treasure, returns it to you — and among them is one who, if you entrust him with a single coin, will not return it unless you stand over him demanding it. That is because they say: "We are not answerable with respect to the gentiles." And they speak lies against God while they know.

**Start with what the verse does first, because it is easy to miss: it praises.**

*Wa-min ahli al-kitābi man in ta'manhu bi-qinṭārin yu'addihi ilayk.* Among the People of the Book is one who, if you entrust him with a *qinṭār* — a great hoard of gold — returns it to you in full.

The surah will not flatten a community. It opens with the best case. This is the same instinct we saw at 3:113 ("they are not all alike — among the People of the Book is an upright community") and it is one of the most consistently ignored features of this surah, by readers on every side.

**Then the contrast.** *Wa-minhum man in ta'manhu bi-dīnārin lā yu'addihi ilayka illā mā dumta 'alayhi qā'imā.*

And among them is one who, if you entrust him with a single *dīnār*, will not return it — *illā mā dumta 'alayhi qā'imā*, "except as long as you keep standing over him."

The Arabic is vivid: you have to remain **standing** over this man. The image is of a creditor who has to plant himself in front of the debtor permanently to recover what is his own. The amounts are set at opposite extremes — a treasure versus a coin — and the contrast is the argument: this is not about the size of the sum. The one who returns a hoard is trustworthy with anything; the one who withholds a coin is trustworthy with nothing.

**"That is because they say..."** *Dhālika bi-annahum qālū.* Now the verse gives the cause, and the cause is a **statement**, not a disposition.

**"We are not accountable with respect to the gentiles."** *Laysa 'alaynā fī al-ummiyyīna sabīl.*

*Al-ummiyyīn* — the gentile peoples, those without a scripture; in the Madinan context, the non-Israelite Arabs. *Laysa 'alaynā sabīl* — there is no road against us, no recourse, no liability.

**And here is what the verse is actually charging.** The claim is not "we dislike these people." It is a **legal** claim: our law provides the outsider no remedy against us, and we have converted that legal immunity into a religious permission. We may take their property, because there is no path of blame to us in it.

**"And they attribute lies to God while they know."** *Wa-yaqūlūna 'alā Allāhi al-kadhiba wa-hum ya'lamūn.*

Note precisely where the lie is located. Not in cheating. The lie is in **attributing the permission to God**. The sin being prosecuted is the move from "no one can stop me" to "God has permitted this."

That is the verse's real target, and it is worth stating as plainly as possible, because it is the most transferable thing in the whole passage: **converting a legal loophole into a divine licence is a lie told about God.**

**Now four cautions, all of them necessary, because this is the most misused verse in the batch.**

**First:** it is about **some** of them (*minhum*), not all of them. The verse says so in its own first clause, which praises the scrupulous among them.

**Second:** what is condemned is a **claim attributed to God**, not an ethnic trait. The grammar is explicit: *they said*, and in saying it they lied. It is a false religious ruling, not a racial characteristic.

**Third, and this one I want to be especially careful with.** Maududi's commentary on this verse adduces Deuteronomy 15:1–3 and 23:20, which do distinguish between an Israelite and a foreigner in matters of debt-release and interest. Those texts are real and say what he says they say. But Maududi also quotes later rabbinic statements attributed to "Rabbi Samuel" and "Rabbi Ishmael" to the effect that a Jew may profit from a gentile's error and that a Jewish judge should find in favour of a Jew against a gentile.

Those attributions are **contested**, and I would not repeat them as though they were established. More importantly: mainstream Jewish teaching does **not** permit deceiving or defrauding gentiles. Rabbinic law prohibits *geneivat da'at* (deception) broadly, holds gentiles under moral obligation through the Noahide framework, and treats the defrauding of a non-Jew as a sin — in many formulations a more serious one than defrauding a Jew, because of the desecration of God's name involved.

So the claim this verse attributes to a party in Madinah is not a description of Jewish law, and it should not be presented as one. What the verse reports is a **legal fiction some people invented**, and the Qur'an's response is to call it a lie — which is precisely the right response to a legal fiction.

**Fourth:** the Qur'an's indictments in this passage are always of conduct, never of descent. Islam has no doctrine of inherited guilt, and no verse in this surah may be used to attribute a moral failing to a people.

**Now the positive principle, which is enormous and is the reason this verse matters.**

**Trustworthiness is not a communal property. It is individual.** The verse's whole structure is built on that: some of them, and some of them. Which means you do not judge a person by his community, and — by symmetry, and this is the part that bites — your community does not cover you.

**And the law that rests on this is the law of *amānah*** — trust, deposit, safekeeping. The principle derived by the jurists from this verse and others like it: what has been entrusted to you must be returned, regardless of who entrusted it and regardless of what you think of them. The obligation does not vary with the vulnerability of the owner.

**Bring it into the present.** Once you see the structure, you see it everywhere, and the structure is always the same: **I can get away with it, therefore it is permitted.**

The employer who withholds the migrant worker's wages because the worker has no recourse. The contractor who inflates the bill for the client who doesn't know the market. The business that has one price for the customer who checks and another for the customer who won't. The refund not processed because the customer won't chase it. The deposit from the tenant who can't afford a lawyer. The expense claim padded because the company is large.

In each case the reasoning is *laysa 'alaynā sabīl* — there is no road against us. No legal consequence. And in each case the verse's charge is the same: the step from "no one can make me" to "God permits this" is the lie.

**And there is a specifically religious form of it that deserves naming.** The transaction structured to look compliant while achieving what the law forbids. The loophole found, then presented as *halāl*. The scholar whose opinion is sought until one is found that permits what the client wanted to do.

That is *laysa 'alaynā fī al-ummiyyīna sabīl* translated into fiqh, and the verse calls it what it is: speaking lies about God while knowing.

**One more thread.** *Wa-hum ya'lamūn* — while they know. The second occurrence of the formula in this passage (after verse 71; verse 78 will be the third). The consistency is the point: the surah is not describing people who have made an honest mistake about a difficult question. It is describing people who know.

## Verse 76

**"Absolutely! Those who honour their trusts and shun evil—surely Allah loves those who are mindful ˹of Him˺."**

In simple terms: Yes indeed — whoever fulfils his covenant and is mindful of God, then God loves those who are mindful of Him.

**"Absolutely!"** *Balā.* This little word is doing a great deal of work, and the supplied translation renders it well.

*Balā* in Arabic is the particle that **overturns a negation**. It answers "no" with "yes, on the contrary." What it is overturning here is the claim quoted in the previous verse — that there is no accountability with respect to outsiders. The verse answers: **there is.** Completely, and in full.

So verse 75 and verse 76 are a matched pair of claim and rebuttal, and *balā* is the hinge between them.

**"Whoever fulfils his covenant."** *Man awfā bi-'ahdihi.*

**And here is the thread worth catching.** *Awfā* is from the root *w-f-y* — the **same root** as *mutawaffīka* at 3:55 (I will take you in full) and *yuwaffīhim* at 3:57 (He will pay them in full).

This is the third occurrence in twenty verses, and the pattern is deliberate: God takes in full, God pays in full, and here the believer is required to **fulfil in full**. The same standard of completeness runs through all three. Nothing half-taken, nothing half-paid, nothing half-returned.

**"And shuns evil."** *Wa-ttaqā.* *Taqwā* — the guarding awareness of God that runs through this whole surah. We met it at 3:28 (*tuqāh*, from the same root), at 3:50 ("be mindful of God and obey me"), and it will recur throughout.

**The pairing is the point.** Fulfilling the covenant is the **outward** act — reliability in what has been entrusted to you. *Taqwā* is the **inward** state — awareness of God when no one can enforce anything.

The verse joins them, and in doing so it forecloses two half-versions of the religious life. There is the scrupulously honest person with no God-consciousness: reliable in business, utterly reliable, and the reliability is temperament or reputation management. And there is the devout person who would not dream of missing a prayer and is casually unreliable with other people's money or time.

Both are common. The verse requires both halves.

**"Surely Allah loves those who are mindful of Him."** *Fa-inna Allāha yuḥibbu al-muttaqīn.*

And the surah's love-vocabulary returns. We saw it at 3:31 — "if you love God, follow me, and God will love you" — and at 3:57, where the verse closed with "God does not love the wrongdoers." Being loved by God is the goal this surah sets at its opening, and this verse tells you it attaches to *amānah* and *taqwā*.

**Now notice the word the verse opens with: *man*.** "Who**ever**." Not "whoever among you." Not "whoever of this community."

The category is deliberately open — and it is open because of what verses 73 and 74 just established. If grace is not a tribal possession, then the love of God is not one either. The trustworthy person is the one God loves, wherever he is found, whichever community he belongs to.

That is a genuinely radical thing to say in the middle of a passage prosecuting a specific community's failures. The surah refuses to let the argument become tribal even while it is arguing with a tribe.

**The practical content.** Trustworthiness is not, on this verse, a social virtue that religion happens to approve of. It is the thing that attracts the love of God. That reorders the stakes considerably.

Most of us think of returning what we owe as basic decency, somewhere near the bottom of the scale of religious achievement — below prayer, below charity, below the things that feel spiritual. The verse places it next to *taqwā* and attaches to both of them the single thing this surah says we should be aiming at.

And there is a hard edge for anyone in a position of trust: the person who has been given an *amānah* — a role, a fund, a confidence, a responsibility — and treats it as his own. The verse says the measure of that person before God is what he did with it.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 77

**"Indeed, those who trade Allah’s covenant and their oaths for a fleeting gain will have no share in the Hereafter. Allah will neither speak to them, nor look at them, nor purify them on the Day of Judgment. And they will suffer a painful punishment."**

In simple terms: those who sell God's covenant and their own oaths for a small price — they have no share in the Hereafter. God will not speak to them, will not look at them, will not purify them on the Day of Resurrection, and theirs is a painful punishment.

**The grammar of the transaction first, because it is striking.** *Yashtarūna bi-'ahdi Allāhi wa-aymānihim thamanan qalīlan* — literally: they **buy with** the covenant of God and their oaths **a small price**.

The preposition is worth noticing. The covenant and the oaths are the **currency being spent**. The small price is what is acquired. So the picture is of a person who takes the most precious thing he possesses — his bond with God, and his own sworn word — and sells it for something trivial.

**"A small price."** *Thamanan qalīlan.* Not "a price" — a **small** one. The Qur'an almost always adds this note when describing what people gain by corruption, and the disproportion is the point of the indictment. What they got was not worth it, and they knew it was not worth it, and they did it anyway.

**And note what is being sold.** Two things: the **covenant of God** — the bond of acknowledgment and obedience that every human being has with his Creator — and **their oaths**, meaning their sworn word. The specific sin here is invoking God's name to guarantee a lie.

That is the worst form of dishonesty, and it is worth saying why: because it **uses God as the instrument of the fraud**. A person who lies has done something bad. A person who swears by God in order to make his lie credible has recruited God into it. That is what the verse is prosecuting.

**Four consequences, and they escalate in intimacy:**

1. *Lā khalāqa lahum fī al-ākhirah* — no share in the Hereafter.
2. *Wa-lā yukallimuhumu Allāhu* — God will not speak to them.
3. *Wa-lā yanẓuru ilayhim yawma al-qiyāmah* — nor look at them on the Day of Resurrection.
4. *Wa-lā yuzakkīhim* — nor purify them.

**The second and third are the ones to stop at**, because they are not what you would expect. The verse does not lead with punishment. It leads with the **withdrawal of attention**.

For a person whose entire hope is to be addressed by God and seen by Him — and that is the whole religious life, on this surah's account — to be neither spoken to nor looked at is the punishment. Everything else is downstream of it.

**A necessary qualification, and it comes from the commentators themselves.** These are negations of **gracious** speech and **merciful** regard. Al-Jalālayn and Ibn Kathīr both make this point: God will not speak to them with kindness, and will not look at them with mercy. That qualification matters, because the Qur'an elsewhere has God addressing the inhabitants of the Fire — see 23:105–108 and 40:50, where He speaks to them and they answer. So the negation here is of the specific thing the believer wants: a word and a look of acceptance.

**"Nor purify them."** *Wa-lā yuzakkīhim.* *Tazkiyah* is purification and growth — the process by which a person is cleansed and made to grow. Al-Qushayrī's reading is worth having: the development of the self can only happen in this life, and the person who has spent his life in this trade arrives undeveloped and unpurified, with nothing left to grow.

**The hadith.** Ibn Kathīr and others bring several narrations under this verse. The most cited is from Abū Dharr, in which the Prophet ﷺ said — and repeated it three times — that there are three to whom God will not speak on the Day of Resurrection, nor look at, nor purify, and theirs is a painful punishment: *al-musbil* (one who lets his garment hang below the ankles out of pride), *al-munaffiq sil'atahu bi-al-ḥalifi al-kādhib* (one who sells his goods by means of a false oath), and *al-mannān* (one who reminds people of his favours).

And in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, from 'Abdullāh ibn Mas'ūd: "Whoever swears an oath, knowing it to be false, in order to seize the property of a Muslim, will meet God while He is angry with him." In some versions the Prophet ﷺ recited this verse in connection with it.

**Note what the hadith does.** It takes a verse whose immediate context is a specific group's covenant-breaking and generalises it to anyone who swears falsely for gain. That is the established method: the Qur'an's statement is general in form (*alladhīna yashtarūna* — "those who"), and the Sunnah shows how it applies.

**Now the contemporary application, and it is uncomfortably direct.**

The obvious form is **perjury**: the false oath in court, the sworn statement that isn't true, the signature on a document you haven't read and don't intend to stand behind.

But the form that is much more widespread, and more relevant to most readers, is what you might call **religion as a sales instrument**. The product branded "Islamic" in order to sell it. The charity appeal with a verse across the top that doesn't use the money as it says it does. The course, the book, the scheme warranted by a religious claim that won't bear examination. The investment product with a scholar's name on it whose opinion was bought.

That is *yashtarūna bi-'ahdi Allāhi wa-aymānihim thamanan qalīlan* almost exactly: God's covenant and one's own sworn word, converted into a small commercial advantage.

**And note the smallness again.** *Qalīlan.* What people actually get for these things is nearly always small. That is the tragedy the verse is pointing at. The price is always small. It is small by definition, because anything large enough to be worth your covenant with God would not be obtainable this way.

**One last thing, and it is for the reader rather than about anyone else.** There is a form of this available to religious people that doesn't involve money at all: using God to win an argument. Swearing to something you are not sure of because it makes your case. Attributing to God a ruling He has not given because it settles the dispute in your favour. The verse's mechanism — spending the covenant as currency — covers that too.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 78

**"There are some among them who distort the Book with their tongues to make you think this ˹distortion˺ is from the Book—but it is not what the Book says. They say, “It is from Allah”—but it is not from Allah. And ˹so˺ they attribute lies to Allah knowingly."**

In simple terms: and among them is a party who twist their tongues with the Book so that you would reckon it to be from the Book — and it is not from the Book. They say "it is from God," and it is not from God. And they speak lies against God while they know.

**"They twist their tongues."** *Yalwūna alsinatahum.* The root *l-w-y* means to bend, to turn, to twist aside. The picture is physical: the tongue being turned around something.

**And note what kind of distortion this is.** This is not textual alteration. The verse says they distort **with their tongues** — in the act of reciting. The change happens in the mouth, in the delivery: a shift in pronunciation, a slight change of wording, an emphasis placed where it does not belong, a phrase run into the next one so that the sense slides.

The hearer has no way to check, because he is hearing it performed. He cannot stop the reciter and compare. And that is precisely the advantage being taken.

**"So that you would reckon it to be from the Book."** *Li-taḥsabūhu mina al-kitābi.* The purpose clause states the intent: the aim is that the hearer **counts** it as scripture. Not that he is entertained, or persuaded — that he takes it as the text.

**Then the triple exposure.** Read the verse's second half and notice that the denial follows the claim each time, immediately, with no interval:

- They twist it → *wa-mā huwa mina al-kitāb*, **and it is not from the Book**.
- They say "it is from God" → *wa-mā huwa min 'indi Allāh*, **and it is not from God**.
- They lie about God → *wa-hum ya'lamūn*, **while they know**.

Three claims, three refusals, and the third refusal is about their own state of mind. The verse is not only saying they are wrong. It is saying they are wrong and cannot not know it.

**"While they know."** The third occurrence in ten verses (71, 75, 78). The surah's charge, repeated until it cannot be missed: this is not error. It is knowing falsification.

**A careful word on what this verse implies about the previous scriptures**, because the question is a large one and this verse is often used crudely.

The verse describes **oral** distortion — twisting with the tongue. It does not say the written text was rewritten. In the classical discussion, a distinction is drawn between *taḥrīf al-lafẓ* (alteration of the wording) and *taḥrīf al-ma'nā* (distortion of the meaning while the wording stands). This verse is usually placed in the second category: the text is there, and the meaning is bent in the delivery.

That is the mainstream reading, and it matters, because it keeps this verse from being used to make a claim about manuscripts that the verse itself does not make. The Qur'an's broader position on the previous scriptures is more nuanced than a single slogan: it affirms that they contained guidance and light (5:44), that they contained the description of the Prophet ﷺ (7:157), that some among their people are upright and recite it rightly (3:113, 3:199), **and** that some displace words from their places (4:46, 5:13). All of that has to be held together.

What this verse establishes beyond dispute is narrower and sufficient: **a party among them distorted the meaning in oral delivery and claimed the result was from God.**

**Now the application, and this is the sharpest in the batch, because the tactic has not changed at all.**

The verse describes **quoting a text in a way that changes its meaning while the hearer believes he is hearing the text.** That is the single most common form of religious dishonesty in circulation today, and it takes several standard shapes:

- Quoting half a verse, where the second half limits or reverses the first.
- Quoting a hadith without the qualification that governs it.
- Translating a line in a way the original will not bear, and then arguing from the translation.
- Reading a passage aloud with a tone or emphasis that makes it say what it does not say.
- Citing a scholar's words shorn of the passage that reverses them.
- Presenting a minority opinion as though it were the position.

In every case nothing false has been said, exactly. What is false is the hearer's reasonable belief that he has just heard the source. And that is the sin the verse names: *li-taḥsabūhu mina al-kitāb*.

**And the practical discipline it suggests is simple.** When someone quotes scripture at you and it sounds decisive — when the quotation seems to settle the matter a little too cleanly — ask to see it in context. Not because the person is necessarily dishonest; often he is repeating what he heard. But because the verse tells you this specific move is the standard instrument of corruption, and the only defence against it is the one thing the verse's victims could not do: stop and look at the page.

That is worth doing for your own quoting too.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 79

**"It is not appropriate for someone who Allah has blessed with the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood to say to people, “Worship me instead of Allah.” Rather, he would say, “Be devoted to the worship of your Lord ˹alone˺”—in accordance with what these prophets read in the Scripture and what they taught."**

In simple terms: it does not belong to any human being — that God should give him the Book, judgment and prophethood, and then he should say to people, "Be servants of me instead of God." Rather: **be people of the Lord**, because of what you have been teaching of the Book and because of what you have been studying.

**This is one of the great verses of the Qur'an, and it is the climax of everything from verse 71 onward.**

**"It is not for any human being."** *Mā kāna li-basharin.* Note the word: **bashar**, a mortal, a human. The argument is not "it is not for a prophet to say this." It is "it is not for a **human being**."

That is the strongest possible form of the statement. The impossibility is located in what a human being is, not in the rank he holds. And the *mā kāna* construction carries the sense of **it does not befit, it is not possible, it cannot happen** — a logical and moral impossibility, not merely a fact that no one has happened to do.

**Three endowments.** *Al-kitāba wa-al-ḥukma wa-al-nubuwwata* — the Book, judgment (or wisdom: the understanding by which one judges), and prophethood.

The three are cumulative, and that is the force of the argument: take the most complete religious endowment a human being can have — revelation, the capacity to judge by it, and the office of a prophet — and it still does not get him worship.

**"And then he should say."** *Thumma yaqūla.* The *thumma* is rhetorical: *and after all that*, would he then turn around and say...? The sequence is what makes the thing absurd.

**"Be worshippers of me instead of God."** *Kūnū 'ibādan lī min dūni Allāh.* *Min dūni* — in place of, to the exclusion of.

**And now the positive statement, which is the heart of the verse: *wa-lākin kūnū rabbāniyyīna bi-mā kuntum tu'allimūna al-kitāba wa-bi-mā kuntum tadrusūn.***

***Rabbāniyyīn*** — this is the crux word, and it is worth unpacking properly.

Al-Jalālayn derives it from *rabb*, "Lord," with the augmented *alif* and *nūn* producing an intensive form: **those who belong to the Lord**, those wholly His. Ibn 'Abbās, Abū Razin and others are reported to have glossed it as "wise, learned and forbearing" (*ḥukamā', 'ulamā', ḥulamā'*), which is the same idea from the other side — the person who knows, acts, and teaches. Maududi notes that the term was used for the Jewish religious scholars who were charged with giving true guidance and implementing the law, and that the same word appears at 5:44 and 5:63.

So a *rabbānī* is: someone who is the Lord's, learned in the Book, acting on it, and teaching it. **Not a devotee of a human being — a devotee of the Lord who therefore teaches.**

**And the clause that grounds it: "because of what you have been teaching of the Book and what you have been studying."** *Bi-mā kuntum tu'allimūna al-kitāba wa-bi-mā kuntum tadrusūn.*

**This subordinate clause is the entire basis of religious authority in Islam, and it is tucked away in a prepositional phrase.** The authority to be a *rabbānī* comes from the **text** — from teaching it and studying it — not from a person, an office, a lineage, or an institution. You are a teacher because you teach the Book. Nothing else.

**A note on the readings.** The majority read *tu'allimūna* — "you teach." Al-Jalālayn records also *ta'lamūna* — "you know." Both are transmitted; the sense is complementary (you teach it and you know it), and the difference is not consequential.

**The occasion of revelation, and both halves of it matter.** Al-Jalālayn records that this verse was revealed **both** when the Christians of Najrān claimed that Jesus had commanded them to take him as a divinity, **and** when some Muslims asked the Prophet ﷺ for permission to prostrate to him.

That second half is the one to hold on to. The verse is not only aimed outward at a Christian doctrine. It was also revealed because **Muslims wanted to venerate the Prophet ﷺ beyond what is permitted**, and asked him for leave to do it.

**And the hadith that enacts it.** In Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 'Umar reported that the Messenger of God ﷺ said: "Do not exaggerate in praising me as the Christians praised the son of Mary. I am only a servant, so say: the servant of God and His messenger."

There it is. The man with the Book, the judgment and the prophethood, and the request in front of him, saying: I am a servant. Say "the servant of God and His messenger."

**The logic, stated in the form the commentators give it.** Ibn Kathīr puts it exactly: if this is not the right of a prophet or a messenger, then it is certainly not the right of anyone else. The verse is, in effect, the QED of *tawḥīd*: take the highest possible human being, give him the highest possible endowment, and he still does not receive worship. There is no case left in which anyone does.

**And note that the impossibility is internal, not merely factual.** It is not that no prophet happened to do this. It is that the two things cannot coexist. Receiving the Book **is** being told to point away from yourself. The man who says "worship me" has by that statement demonstrated that he does not have what he claims — or is not what he claims. The verse is a test, not just a prediction.

**Now the application, and I want to be honest about where it lands.**

The verse's occasion includes Muslims who asked to prostrate to the Prophet ﷺ, which tells you that the tendency it forbids is not a Christian failing. It is a **human** failing, and it recurs wherever a religious figure is intensely loved.

In its gross form it is the ascription of divinity or of divine attributes to a created being — which is what the verse prohibits outright, and which mainstream Christianity would also reject as a description of its own doctrine.

In its subtler forms, which are much more common: seeking from the dead or the absent what only God can give; asking a saint rather than God; treating a living teacher's word asfinal in a way that the text itself is not; the mawlid poetry that attributes to the Prophet ﷺ knowledge of the unseen; the shrine practice of petitioning the occupant of the grave.

I should be clear about the rule without adjudicating every intra-Muslim dispute in detail: **directing an act of worship — or a request that only God can answer — to anyone other than God, living or dead, is a violation of this verse.** That is the mainstream position, and the verse is its clearest textual basis.

And then there is the form that is available to every teacher and every student: the teacher whose students quote him before they quote the text. That is the beginning of the same movement, and the verse's cure for it is in its own final clause — *bi-mā kuntum tu'allimūna al-kitāb*. Teach the Book. Then people will quote the Book.

**And the positive command, which is beautiful.** *Kūnū rabbāniyyīn* — be people of the Lord.

Note what the verse substitutes. In place of a vertical object of worship — me — it puts a horizontal task: **learn the Book and teach it.** A prophet does not say "worship me." He says: be the Lord's, and here is the Book, and you've been studying it, so go and teach it.

That is what a religious community is for, on this verse's account. Not veneration of a founder. Transmission of a text.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 80

**"And he would never ask you to take angels and prophets as lords. Would he ask you to disbelieve after you have submitted?"**

In simple terms: nor would he command you to take the angels and the prophets as lords. Would he command you to disbelieve after you have submitted?

**The verse extends verse 79's principle outward.** Not only would a prophet not say "worship me" — he would not command taking **angels** or **prophets** as lords. Which covers everyone else who could plausibly be venerated.

**Why angels?** Because angel-veneration was a live practice in Arabia, not a hypothetical. The pre-Islamic Arabs are reported to have worshipped angels and called them the daughters of God — see 53:19–27 on al-Lāt, al-'Uzzā and Manāt, and 43:19, 16:57, 17:40, 21:26, 25:3, 34:40–41. So the verse is refusing a local practice as well as a Christian one, and it is doing both with the same sentence.

**"As lords."** *Arbāban*, plural of *rabb* — the same word used in the third clause of 3:64, "take not one another as lords besides God."

**And that is the thread to pull.** Verse 64 forbade taking **one another** as lords — human beings over human beings, religious authorities over their followers. Verses 79–80 forbid taking **angels and prophets** as lords. Between them, the two ends of this passage close every avenue: not a religious authority, not an angel, not a prophet, not a human being at all.

**"Would he command you to disbelieve after you have submitted?"** *A-ya'murukum bi-al-kufri ba'da idh antum muslimūn.*

**And note the word.** The verse does not say "would he command you to sin," or "would he mislead you." It says *kufr* — the very word for rejecting the truth. So the ascription of lordship to a created being is not being treated here as a refinement added to monotheism. It is being treated as its **reversal**.

*Ba'da idh antum muslimūn* — after you have submitted. And *muslimūn* is again in its descriptive sense: you have already surrendered. Would he now command you to unsurrender? The question answers itself.

**The rhetorical method is worth naming.** The verse does not argue. It asks a question that cannot be answered in the affirmative, and the asking is the proof.

The whole passage has been doing this. Verse 65: "why do you argue about Abraham, when the Torah and the Gospel came after him?" Verse 70: "why do you reject the signs of God while you witness?" Verse 71: "why do you mix truth with falsehood and conceal it while you know?" And here: "would he command you to disbelieve after you have submitted?"

The Qur'an is not only making arguments. It is teaching its reader **how to interrogate a claim** — by asking a question whose answer is already contained in what everyone involved grants.

**And the test this verse gives you is remarkably clean.** Any teaching that requires you to give to a created being what belongs only to the Creator is, by this verse's own definition, a command to disbelieve — regardless of how religious the language is, and regardless of who is saying it.

That is usable. It does not require you to master the history of the early councils, or to adjudicate fine questions of Christology, or to know anything about anyone. It requires one question: **is this asking me to give something to a creature that belongs to God?** If yes, the verse has already answered.

**And there is an inward form of it.** The person who has made a religious figure, a movement, or an institution into the thing he actually serves — praying correctly, giving correctly, behaving correctly, and all of it organised around loyalty to something other than God. The verse's question reaches that too: would he command you to disbelieve after you have submitted?

**The passage closes here**, and it closes on the clearest statement in the surah of what prophethood is **for**. A prophet is not an object of veneration. He is a man with a Book who tells you to be the Lord's, and teaches you the Book so that you can.

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

These ten verses fall into two halves that answer each other.

**Verses 71–78 are an anatomy of religious corruption.** Five tactics are named, and they share a single structure: **each one uses the appearance of religion to serve an interest.** Mixing truth with falsehood so the two can't be separated (71). Staging a conversion to manufacture doubt (72). Restricting revelation to insiders to avoid being argued down (73). Converting a legal immunity into a divine permission (75). Distorting the text in the mouth so the hearer takes your words for scripture (78).

And through all five runs the same phrase: *wa-hum ya'lamūn*, **while they know**. Three times in ten verses. The surah's charge is never that these people are ignorant. It is that they know and proceed anyway. Which is the most serious thing that can be said about anyone, and also the thing that leaves the door open — because a person who knows can stop.

**Verses 79–80 are the principle that answers all five.** The man with the Book, the judgment and the prophethood does not say "worship me." He says: be the Lord's, because you teach the Book and you study it. And he certainly does not tell you to take angels and prophets as lords, because that would be commanding disbelief to people who have already surrendered.

**Three threads tie the batch together.**

The first is the **root *w-f-y***, now appearing for the third time in this surah. God takes Jesus in full (3:55). God pays the believers in full (3:57). And the believer is required to fulfil his covenant in full (3:76). The same standard of completeness is being applied to God and demanded of us.

The second is the **word *rabb*, lord**. The previous batch's verse 64 forbade taking one another as lords. This batch's verses 79–80 forbid taking angels and prophets as lords. Between them they close the door from both sides: no human authority, no angel, no prophet, no human at all.

The third is the **surah's refusal to flatten a community, even while prosecuting it.** Verse 75 opens by praising the trustworthy among the People of the Book before condemning the cheats. Verse 72 says *ṭā'ifah*, a party. Verse 78 says *farīq*, a faction. Verse 79's own occasion includes Muslims who wanted to prostrate to the Prophet ﷺ. The surah is not writing an indictment of a people. It is describing what human beings do with a religion, and it keeps showing us that we do it too.

**And the verse I would leave you with is 79**, for two reasons. Its logic is final — if the highest human being with the highest endowment does not receive worship, no one does. And its occasion is a warning aimed inward: it was revealed partly because the Prophet's own companions asked to bow to him. The failure it prohibits is not someone else's failure. It is ours, and it is always available.

Which is why the verse's command is what it is. Not "do not worship me." But: **be people of the Lord — because you teach the Book, and because you study it.**

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given, because this is a synthesized commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and inventing precise references would be worse than omitting them.

Three places in this batch required particular care, and the reader should know where the limits are.

**The first is 3:73.** This is the grammatically most difficult verse in the passage, and the sense depends on seeing that *qul inna al-hudā hudā Allāh* is a parenthetical interruption and that *an yu'tā aḥadun* resumes the plotters' speech as the object of *lā tu'minū*. I have set that out, recorded the transmitted variant *a-an yu'tā*, and noted that both are transmitted readings. I have also tried to draw attention to the verse's most striking clause — *aw yuḥājjūkum 'inda rabbikum*, the fear of being out-argued on the Day of Resurrection — which is what makes the verse more than a procedural complaint.

**The second is 3:75.** This verse is the most misused in the passage and I have handled it with four explicit cautions: it concerns some of them, not all; it indicts a claim attributed to God rather than an ethnic trait; the rabbinic attributions adduced in some commentaries are contested and must not be repeated as established; and mainstream Jewish teaching prohibits defrauding anyone, gentile included. I have stated plainly that the claim the verse reports is a legal fiction some people invented, not a description of Jewish law, and that no verse here may be used to attribute a moral failing to a people.

**The third is 3:78.** I have distinguished the oral distortion this verse describes — *layy al-lisān*, twisting with the tongue — from textual alteration, and placed it in the category classical scholarship calls *taḥrīf al-ma'nā*, while noting that the broader question of the previous scriptures requires holding several Qur'anic statements together and cannot be settled by this verse alone.

**On the translation.** Where the supplied English has chosen or added, I have flagged it. In several places the additions are genuinely helpful and I have said so: the half-bracketed speech-attributions in 3:73, which are essential for following whose voice is speaking; "Absolutely!" for *balā* in 3:76, which captures the rebuttal force exactly; and "in the morning" for *wajha al-nahāri* in 3:72. In one place the translation loses something the Arabic has: at 3:79, "be devoted to the worship of your Lord" renders *kūnū rabbāniyyīna*, and the English phrase is a description of an activity where the Arabic is a **name for a kind of person** — *rabbāniyyīn*, those who belong to the Lord, the learned who teach and study. That is the word the verse turns on, and I have tried to restore it in the commentary.
