# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 171–180

This batch moves from the failure of hearing to the shape of a whole life. It opens with the parable of the flock that hears the shout and comprehends nothing (171), then turns to the ordinary: eat what We have provided, and be thankful (172). Then the dietary prohibitions are given — four only, with the exception of necessity built into the same verse (173). Then the severest passage of the batch: those who sell the Book for a price will consume fire (174), the trade named and the irony of their patience (175), and the reason given — the Book came with the truth, and those who dispute it are in a distant schism (176). Then the great definition of *birr* — righteousness is not a direction; it is a person who believes, gives what he loves, prays, keeps his word and endures (177). Then the law of retaliation, given with the killer called the victim's brother and with life named as its purpose (178–179). And finally the deathbed bequest, the ruling on which is one of the classic disputed cases in the whole of the law (180).

## Verse 171

**"The example of the disbelievers ˹not responding to the Messenger’s warning˺ is like a flock not comprehending the calls and cries of the shepherd. ˹They are wilfully˺ deaf, dumb and blind so they have no understanding."**

In simple terms: the likeness of those who disbelieve is as the likeness of one who shouts at that which hears nothing except a call and a cry. Deaf, dumb, blind — so they do not reason.

*Wa mathalu alladhina kafaru ka-mathali alladhi yan'iqu bima la yasma'u illa du'a'an wa nida'an* — "and the likeness of those who disbelieve is as the likeness of him who shouts at that which hears nothing but a call and a cry."

*Yan'iqu* is from a root meaning to drive animals by shouting — the *na'iq* is the one who calls out to a flock. And the simile has been read two ways, both transmitted.

On the reading that follows the grammar most directly, the disbeliever is compared to the **shouter**: someone crying out at animals that register the noise and nothing of the meaning. On that reading the object is the idols invoked, or the call itself — a great deal of sound going out and nothing being received. On the second reading, the disbeliever is compared to the **animals**: the caller is the Prophet ﷺ, and the call is heard as noise without comprehension.

The two converge on the same verdict, which is what matters: **a call is going out and nothing is being understood.** Whether the failure is located in the invocation or in the hearing, the result is identical — sound without meaning.

*Summun bukmun 'umyun* — "deaf, dumb, blind."

**And note that this is the second occurrence of this exact phrase in the surah.** It appeared at 2:18, describing the hypocrites: "Deaf, dumb, blind — so they will not return." The surah used it at its outset and returns to it here, which means the condition being described is not a starting point but a settled one: the faculty that was available has been made unavailable by use.

The order is worth noting too: **deaf** (nothing received), **dumb** (nothing given back), **blind** (nothing seen). Reception, response, and perception — all three closed.

*Fa-hum la ya'qilun* — "so they do not reason." And note the *fa*: the non-reasoning is a consequence of the three closures, not a separate fact about them.

**The thread with the preceding verses is tight.** Verse 170 described the ancestors as *la ya'qiluna shay'an* — understanding nothing at all. Verse 171 closes with *la ya'qilun*. And verse 164 opened the same stretch by praising *qawmin ya'qilun* — a people who reason. So the surah has, in six verses, established the faculty, shown it absent in the ancestors being followed, and now shown it absent in the followers.

For practice, this verse is a diagnostic, and it is one of the most uncomfortable in the Qur'an because it describes a condition that is invisible from inside.

**The distinction the verse turns on is between hearing and understanding**, and the gap between them is where most religious failure actually lives. Very few people reject the religion having never heard it. The common condition is having heard it constantly — a lifetime of it, in some cases — and comprehending nothing of it, because comprehension requires a faculty that the verse describes as closed. A person can sit in a mosque for forty years, or read the Qur'an daily in a language he does not process, or hold a complete set of correct positions, and be in the condition this verse names.

The test is small and it is the only reliable one: **does what I hear change anything?** Comprehension is an event, not an intention. A person who hears the Qur'an and is not altered by it — not troubled, not instructed, not moved, not stopped — should consider the possibility that he is hearing a call and a cry.

There is a second application for the one doing the calling. The simile's first reading places the failure on the shouter's side: he is making noise at something that cannot receive it. That is a genuine hazard for anyone who teaches or invites. There is a form of religious speech that is loud and constant and conveys nothing, and the remedy for it is not volume. It is knowing what the hearer can actually take.

## Verse 172

**"O believers! Eat from the good things We have provided for you. And give thanks to Allah if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺."**

In simple terms: O you who believe, eat of the good things We have provided you, and give thanks to Allah, if it is Him alone you worship.

*Ya ayyuha alladhina amanu kulu min tayyibati ma razaqnakum* — "O you who believe, eat of the good things of what We have provided you."

And note the address. Verse 168 said *ya ayyuha al-nas* — O mankind — and required *halalan tayyiban*, lawful and good. This verse says *ya ayyuha alladhina amanu* — O you who believe — and requires *tayyibat*, the good things. The shift from a universal address to the believers, and from two qualifications to one, is the ordinary movement of this surah: what is stated for humanity generally is then stated for the community specifically, in the register of relationship rather than of law.

*Tayyibat* is the good, pure, wholesome things. *Razaqnakum* — **We** have provided you. And note that the provision is attributed to Allah in the same clause as the instruction to eat it, which is the ground of what follows.

*Wa-shkuru lillahi in kuntum iyyahu ta'budun* — "and give thanks to Allah, if it is Him you worship."

The crux is the grammar of the condition. *Iyyahu* is the separated pronoun placed **before** the verb, which in Arabic produces restriction: "Him **alone** you worship." So the verse does not say "if you worship Him." It says: **if it is Him alone that you worship.**

**And that makes gratitude the proof of worship rather than an addition to it.** The logic runs: if your worship is directed to Him alone, then you give thanks to Him alone — because you have recognised the provision as His. A person who receives and does not thank is, by the verse's own test, a person whose worship is not exclusively His. The gratitude is not a courtesy added to the worship; it is the evidence that the worship is real.

The parallel is 16:114: "So eat of what Allah has provided for you [which is] lawful and good, and be grateful for the favour of Allah, **if it is Him alone that you worship**" — the identical conditional closing, with the *halalan tayyiban* of 2:168 restored.

And the thread back to 2:152 is direct: *wa-shkuru li wa-la takfurun*. Gratitude as the response to the gift, and ingratitude as the same word as rejection.

For practice, this verse quietly overturns a division almost everyone carries.

The ordinary assumption is that some parts of life are religious and the rest is just living — eating, working, sleeping, earning. The verse places eating, the most repeated and bodily act there is, inside the address to the believers and attaches gratitude to it. Which means the act itself is inside the relationship.

The tradition's formulation is that the permissible act becomes worship by intention and by gratitude, and that a believer eating his meal with thanks is doing something religious. That is not a pious exaggeration; it is what this verse says. A person who eats what God provided and thanks Him for it has performed an act of worship at dinner, and the reverse is also true: the same meal eaten without any reference to the Provider is merely fuel.

And the second application is the *iyyahu*. The condition is exclusivity, and the test of exclusivity here is gratitude — which is a more searching test than it sounds. A person can say he worships God alone and then spend his life feeling that what he has is owed to him, or achieved, or luck. Gratitude is the correction of that, and its absence is the symptom.

## Verse 173

**"He has only forbidden you ˹to eat˺ carrion, blood, swine, and what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah. But if someone is compelled by necessity—neither driven by desire nor exceeding immediate need—they will not be sinful. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful."**

In simple terms: He has forbidden you only carrion, blood, the flesh of swine, and what has been dedicated to other than Allah. But whoever is compelled, neither desiring nor exceeding, no sin is upon him. Allah is Forgiving, Merciful.

*Innama harrama 'alaykumu al-maytata wa al-damma wa lahma al-khinziri wa ma uhilla bihi li-ghayri Allahi* — "He has only forbidden you the carrion, and blood, and the flesh of swine, and what has been dedicated to other than Allah."

***INNAMA*** — "only" — and this single word carries the entire jurisprudence of food.

The particle restricts. He has forbidden **only** these. Which means the default position in created things is permissibility, and prohibition is established solely by text. This is one of the load-bearing principles of *usul al-fiqh*: *al-asl fi al-ashya' al-ibaha* — the presumption regarding things is permissibility — and this verse with its *innama* is among its primary proofs.

The parallel verses say the same thing with the same particle. 16:115: "He has only forbidden to you carrion, blood, the flesh of swine, and that which has been dedicated to other than Allah." 6:145: "Say: I do not find within that which was revealed to me [anything] forbidden to one who would eat it unless it be a dead animal or blood poured forth or the flesh of swine — for indeed, it is impure — or it be [that slaughtered in] disobedience, dedicated to other than Allah." And 5:3 gives the fuller list, adding the strangled, the beaten, the fallen, the gored, what a predator has partially eaten, what is sacrificed on stone altars, and the divining arrows.

Now the four, one at a time.

**1. *Al-maytah*** — carrion: any animal that died without valid slaughter. The Sunnah specifies exceptions. The Prophet ﷺ said, of the sea, "Its water is pure and its dead are lawful" (recorded by Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi, al-Nasa'i and Ibn Majah from Abu Hurayrah). And a narration transmitted by Ibn Majah and Ahmad states that two kinds of dead animal and two kinds of blood have been made lawful: **fish and locusts**, and **the liver and the spleen**.

**2. *Al-dam*** — blood. And note that 6:145 specifies *daman masfuhan* — **blood poured forth**. That specification is what the jurists worked with: the flowing blood is prohibited; what remains in the meat and in the organs is not, which is why the liver and the spleen are lawful.

**3. *Lahm al-khinzir*** — the flesh of swine. And here is a detail that is easy to miss and that matters: **of the four, only pork is given a stated reason** — 6:145 adds *fa-innahu rijsun*, "for indeed, it is impure." The other three are prohibited by command without a stated reason.

That is a significant fact for how the law is reasoned about, and the tradition drew the distinction carefully. Where a reason is stated in the text, jurists may reason with it. Where no reason is stated, the ruling stands on the command, and the absence of a reason is not a defect in the ruling. A person who declines pork because it is commanded and not because he has found the reason has the sounder position — and a person who obeys only what he can explain has made his understanding the criterion rather than the command.

**4. *Ma uhilla bihi li-ghayri Allahi*** — what has been dedicated to other than Allah. *Uhilla* is the invocation made at slaughter: the animal over which a name other than God's was invoked.

This is the item that generates the furthest-reaching juristic discussion, and it should be reported as a discussion rather than settled. On the classical rulings: the food of the People of the Book is lawful (5:5), and the majority held their slaughter valid where they do not invoke other than God. The scholars differed on whether the invocation of God's name by the slaughterer is a condition of validity or a Sunnah whose omission is excused. And the question of slaughter by mechanical means, and whether the conditions are met, has generated a large and genuinely divided contemporary juristic literature, with responsible scholars and councils arriving at different conclusions. What should be said plainly is that this is a live disagreement among qualified people and not a matter on which a general reader should be given false confidence in either direction.

*Fa-mani idturra ghayra baghin wa la 'adin fa-la ithma 'alayhi* — "but whoever is compelled, neither desiring nor exceeding, no sin upon him."

*Idturra* is form VIII of the root meaning necessity and compulsion — the same root as the *adtarruhu* of verse 126, "I will drive him to the punishment of the Fire," though in a different form and a wholly different sense.

***GHAYRA BAGHIN WA LA 'ADIN*** — and these two conditions are the crux of the whole exception.

*Ghayra baghin* — not desiring, not seeking it out, not transgressing by choice. *Wa la 'adin* — not exceeding the limit, not taking more than the necessity requires.

**And from these two conditions the jurists derived one of the great maxims of the law: *al-darurah tuqaddar bi-qadariha* — necessity is measured by its extent.** The prohibition is lifted to the degree the necessity requires and no further. The verse is the primary textual proof for that maxim.

*Fa-la ithma 'alayhi* — no sin upon him. *Inna Allaha ghafurun rahim* — Allah is Forgiving, Merciful.

For practice, three things.

The first is *innama*, and it is the answer to two opposite errors. The error of laxity — treating the prohibitions as negotiable — is answered by the four being stated as prohibitions. And the error of scrupulosity — inventing prohibitions, treating doubtful matters as forbidden, narrowing the religion until it is unliveable — is answered by the *only*. The religion's food law is four items long. A person who is anxious about more than that has added to the religion, and the verse is the correction.

The second is the necessity clause, and its two conditions translated into practice: the necessity must be genuine rather than manufactured by appetite, and the licence is bounded by the need. A starving person may eat what is forbidden. He may not eat until he is full if a mouthful suffices, and he may not use a mild inconvenience as a licence.

And the third is the structure of the verse as a model. **The prohibition and its exception are in the same sentence.** The law is not given and then softened by later revision; the mercy is inside the ruling from the first statement of it. That is worth noting in a surah where the reader has already met the subject of abrogation at verse 106: this is what a ruling looks like when the exception is part of the original text rather than a later adjustment.

## Verse 174

**"Indeed, those who hide Allah’s revelations, trading them for a fleeting gain consume nothing but fire into their bellies. Allah will neither speak to them on the Day of Judgment, nor will He purify them. And they will suffer a painful punishment."**

In simple terms: those who conceal what Allah sent down of the Book and purchase with it a small price — those consume nothing into their bellies but fire. Allah will not speak to them on the Day of Resurrection, nor will He purify them, and theirs is a painful punishment.

*Inna alladhina yaktumuna ma anzala Allahu mina al-kitabi wa yashtaruna bihi thamanan qalilan* — "indeed those who conceal what Allah sent down of the Book and purchase with it a small price."

*Yaktumuna* is the same verb as at 2:42, 2:146 and 2:159 — the surah's running charge against suppression.

**And note *thamanan qalilan* — "a small price" — which is the exact phrase used at 2:79.** There it described those who wrote the book with their own hands and said "this is from Allah" in order to sell it for a small price. The same figure, the same phrase: the forger of 79 and the concealer of 174 are engaged in the same transaction. One adds to the Book for a price; the other withholds it for a price.

What price? The commentators named what it was in their case: the payments, the status, the offerings and the position they held among their followers, all of which depended on the truth about the coming prophet not being known.

*Ula'ika ma ya'kuluna fi butunihim illa al-nara* — "those consume nothing into their bellies but the Fire."

**And this image is precise rather than merely violent.** What did they consume? The price they took for the Book. And what does the verse say that price actually is? Fire. The transaction is literal: they traded the Book for a price, and the price they received is, in reality, fire going into them.

The construction is used elsewhere for a different crime, which sharpens the point: 4:10 — "Indeed, those who devour the property of orphans unjustly are only consuming into their bellies fire." The same image, the same verb, the same organ. The thing wrongly consumed is itself the fire.

*Wa la yukallimuhumu Allahu yawma al-qiyamati* — "and Allah will not speak to them on the Day of Resurrection."

The crux is what this negation means, and it needs handling. The majority reading — and the one that avoids contradiction with the many verses in which Allah does address the inhabitants of the Fire — is **the withholding of the speech of honour, pleasure and approval**. He will not speak to them with kindness, or with acceptance, or in any way that is good for them. The negation is of the speech of grace, not of every address. Some commentators took it more broadly. The majority reading is the safer and the more consistent with the rest of the Qur'an.

*Wa la yuzakkihim* — "nor will He purify them."

**And note the inversion, which is the sharpest thing in the verse.** *Zakka* is the same root as the *yuzakkikum* of 2:129 and 2:151 — the messenger's third function is to **purify** the people. These are the people who withheld the Book, and the thing withheld from them is precisely *tazkiyah*. They refused to be the means of purification and are denied purification. The punishment fits the crime exactly, by the same word.

*Wa lahum 'adhabun alim* — "and theirs is a painful punishment."

For practice, two things, and the first is the one that lands on nearly everyone who reads it.

The verse is about **taking a price for the religion**, and its contemporary forms are not exotic. They are: the teacher who softens a ruling because the hard one would cost him his following. The institution that stays silent about something it knows because saying it would cost funding. The writer who says what sells rather than what is true. The preacher who tells people what they want to hear because the alternative empties the room. The person who knows something of the religion and does not say it because saying it would be socially expensive.

The price in each case is small — *thamanan qalilan* — which is the verse's own assessment and the most damning thing about it. Nobody sells the truth for something great. They sell it for a following, or a salary, or a reputation, or a quiet life.

The second is the *tazkiyah* inversion, turned positively. The messenger's function is to purify, and purification is the thing the religion exists to bring. A person who has any role in transmitting it — parent, teacher, imam, writer, or simply the Muslim who speaks to his neighbour — is in the position of either being a means of purification or withholding it. There is no neutral third condition.

## Verse 175

**"They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance and forgiveness for punishment. How persistent are they in pursuit of the Fire!"**

In simple terms: those are they who purchased misguidance with guidance, and punishment with forgiveness. And how patiently they will endure the Fire.

*Ula'ika alladhina ishtarawu al-dalalata bi-al-huda wa al-'adhaba bi-al-maghfirati* — "those are they who purchased misguidance with guidance, and punishment with forgiveness."

*Ishtaraw* is to buy, to purchase — the language of commerce. And the image is not new in this surah.

**It is the same image as 2:16**, the opening description of the hypocrites: "Those are the ones who have purchased error [*al-dalalah*] in exchange for guidance [*bi-al-huda*], so their transaction has brought no profit, nor were they guided."

So the surah opened with this image applied to the hypocrites and now applies it to the concealers of the Book. And note that here the pair is **doubled**: not only misguidance for guidance, but punishment for forgiveness. 2:16 had one exchange; 2:175 has two.

Related images run through the surah: 2:86 — "Those are the ones who have bought the life of this world [at the price] of the Hereafter"; 2:41 — "And do not exchange My signs for a small price."

*Fa-ma asbarahum 'ala al-nari* — "and how patiently they will endure the Fire."

**And here is the crux, and it is the single most devastating thing in the batch.**

The word is ***SABR*** — patience. The same word that was commanded at 2:153 (*ista'inu bi-al-sabr*), that was promised with-ness at 2:153, that was promised blessings and mercy and guidance at 2:155–157 (*bashshir al-sabirin*), and that appears again in 2:177's closing list.

The commentators read the phrase as an exclamation of wonder and irony, and some read it as a rhetorical question of rebuke — "what has brought them to endure the Fire?" Either way the word does its work:

**They are described as patient — patient upon the Fire. The patience they would not exercise for a short obedience they will exercise without end in the punishment.**

That is why the surah's patience-passages and this verse must be read together. The *sabirun* of 155–157 are told that upon them are blessings, mercy and guidance. The concealers of 175 are told that their patience is for the Fire. Same word. Same faculty. Object entirely different.

For practice, two things.

The first is the commerce image, and it supplies a question that is unusually clarifying: **what am I buying, and what am I paying with?** Every choice a person makes is a purchase. The verse's point is not that the concealers chose something worthless — it is that they chose something worthless **and paid for it with something of infinite value**. Guidance was theirs and they spent it. Forgiveness was available and they traded it away. The transaction was not forced on them; they made it.

The second is the *sabr*, and it is the application that most readers need.

**Everyone has patience for something.** There is no such person as an impatient person — only a person whose patience is allocated elsewhere. The colleague who cannot bear ten minutes of the prayer will sit for six hours at a screen. The person who cannot bear a month of restraint will bear ten years of a grudge. The question the verse poses is not whether you have patience but where yours is going — because the same faculty that would have carried you through a short obedience will carry you through something else if you do not direct it.

## Verse 176

**"That is because Allah has revealed the Book in truth. And surely those who differ regarding it are totally engrossed in opposition."**

In simple terms: that is because Allah sent down the Book with the truth; and those who differ concerning the Book are in a far-removed schism.

*Dhalika bi-anna Allaha nazzala al-kitaba bi-al-haqqi* — "that is because Allah sent down the Book with the truth."

*Dhalika* refers back to the punishment just described, and the *bi-* is causal: the reason for it is that Allah sent down the Book **with the truth** — *bi-al-haqq*, containing the truth, in truth, with the claim of truth upon those who receive it.

And that is the whole justification. The Book did not arrive as one option among others. It arrived as the truth, which means that disputing it is not a difference of taste.

*Wa inna alladhina ikhtalafu fi al-kitabi la-fi shiqaqin ba'id* — "and indeed those who differ concerning the Book are in a distant schism."

***Shiqaq*** is the word from 2:137 — "and if they turn away, then they are only in *shiqaq*" — from the root meaning to split into two. And here it is qualified: *ba'id*, **far**. A schism that is far from the truth and deeply separated from it.

The commentators read *ikhtalafu fi al-kitab* three ways: they disputed **about** it, some accepting and some rejecting; they made it a subject of contention rather than of submission; or they differed by altering it. All three are transmitted and all three describe the same posture: the Book treated as a matter for argument rather than a matter for acceptance.

**And now the distinction that must be drawn, because without it this verse is used to destroy something the religion needs.**

The Qur'an condemns *ikhtilaf* in one sense and accommodates it in another, and confusing the two has done real damage.

What this verse condemns is **dispute against the Book**: treating revelation as a matter of contention, rejecting part of it, arguing with it rather than submitting to it. The parallels are consistent — 3:105: "And do not be like those who became divided and differed after the clear proofs had come to them"; 42:14: "And those who were given the Scripture did not differ except out of mutual envy, after knowledge had come to them."

What this verse does **not** condemn is the juristic disagreement of qualified scholars deriving rulings from the texts — which is unavoidable, because the texts are finite and cases are infinite, and which the tradition treats as a mercy rather than a catastrophe. The governing hadith, recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from 'Amr ibn al-'As, states that **when a judge exerts his judgment and is correct he has two rewards, and when he exerts his judgment and errs he has one reward.** The one who errs is rewarded — which settles the question of whether derivation-as-such is blameworthy. It is not.

For practice, the application is about **how** a person holds a difference, and it is a test that works on oneself.

There is a way of differing that submits: I hold this reading, I know qualified people hold another, I hold mine because of what I take the evidence to show, and I would let it go if shown better. That is the disagreement of the jurists, and it is rewarded.

And there is a way of differing that contends: I hold this position because it is mine, or my school's, or my party's, and the question is no longer what the text says but who wins. That is the *shiqaq* of this verse — and note that it can be entered by someone holding a **correct** position. The verse condemns the posture, not the error.

A person can check which one he is in by asking whether he would still want to be right if it turned out the other reading were better evidenced.

## Verse 177

**"Righteousness is not in turning your faces towards the east or the west. Rather, the righteous are those who believe in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Books, and the prophets; who give charity out of their cherished wealth to relatives, orphans, the poor, ˹needy˺ travellers, beggars, and for freeing captives; who establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and keep the pledges they make; and who are patient in times of suffering, adversity, and in ˹the heat of˺ battle. It is they who are true ˹in faith˺, and it is they who are mindful ˹of Allah˺."**

This is one of the great summary verses of the Qur'an, and it is the definition the surah has been building toward.

*Laysa al-birra an tuwallu wujuhakum qibala al-mashriqi wa al-maghribi* — "righteousness is not that you turn your faces toward the east and the west."

***BIRR*** is comprehensive goodness — right belief, right action and right character together. The lexicographers connect it to *barr*, the open land, because *birr* is expansive goodness rather than a single virtue.

**And the opening negation lands with real force given where it falls.** The surah has just spent nine verses — 142 to 150 — commanding the change of the qiblah and defending it in detail. And now, five verses after the last of them, it says: righteousness is **not** which way you face.

That is not a retraction. It is the correction of a misreading that the surah itself anticipated. The qiblah is an obligation and it matters. It is not the substance. The obligation is the form; righteousness is the person.

And note the phrase: *al-mashriqi wa al-maghrib* — the east and the west — the same phrase as 2:115 ("to Allah belong the east and the west") and 2:142. The same words that established that all directions belong to God here establish that no direction constitutes righteousness.

*Wa lakinna al-birra man amana billahi wa al-yawmi al-akhiri wa al-mala'ikati wa al-kitabi wa al-nabiyyin* — "but righteousness is [the quality of] one who believes in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Book and the prophets."

**Note the grammar, which is doing real work.** It does not say *an tu'minu* — "that you believe." It says *man amana* — **he who believes**. Righteousness is predicated of a *person*, not of a set of acts. *Birr* is a state of someone, and the deeds that follow are its description rather than its definition.

The five articles named here: Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Book (understood as the category), and the prophets. The fuller creed is at 2:285, which closes the surah: "All of them have believed in Allah and His angels and His Books and His messengers." And 4:136 states the same five with the messengers added.

**And there is a precision here that is commonly muddled and should be corrected.** The familiar "six articles of faith" are not all in this verse — the verse names five. The sixth, belief in the divine decree (*qadar*), comes from the hadith of Jibril, recorded by Muslim from 'Umar, in which the Prophet ﷺ defined *iman* as: "that you believe in Allah, His angels, His Books, His messengers, the Last Day, **and that you believe in the divine decree, its good and its evil**." Five from this verse, the sixth from the Sunnah.

*Wa ata al-mala 'ala hubbihi dhawi al-qurba wa al-yatama wa al-masakina wa ibna al-sabili wa al-sa'ilina wa fi al-riqab* — "and gives wealth, in spite of his love for it, to relatives, orphans, the needy, the traveller, those who ask, and for [freeing] slaves."

***'ALA HUBBIHI*** — and the crux is what the pronoun refers to. The majority of the commentators read it as his love **of the wealth**: he gives while the wealth is dear to him. Others read it as his love **of Allah**: he gives out of love for Him. Both are held, and both are true of a real giver — the second being the motive and the first being the cost.

**And the cost is the point.** The definitive statement is 3:92: "Never will you attain righteousness [*al-birr*] until you spend from that which you love." The same word, the same condition. Giving that costs nothing is not the giving this verse describes.

Six recipients are named: relatives, orphans, the needy, the traveller (*ibn al-sabil* — the one cut off from his resources while away from home), those who ask, and *fi al-riqab* — "in [freeing] necks," the manumission of slaves. On that last category: the classical ruling is the freeing of slaves, which the religion both regulated and made a major act of expiation; contemporary scholarship discusses how the category applies where chattel slavery no longer exists, including to prisoners and to modern forms of bondage, and that discussion is live and unresolved.

**And note carefully that this list is not the zakah taxonomy.** This is a list of charitable giving generally. The eight categories of zakah are at 9:60, and the two lists overlap without being identical. That distinction has been maintained throughout this commentary — as at 2:43, where it was noted that the zakah ruling is not carried by any single verse.

*Wa aqama al-salata wa ata al-zakata* — "and establishes the prayer and gives the zakah."

*Wa al-mufuna bi-'ahdihim idha 'ahadu* — "and those who fulfil their covenant when they make one." *'Ahd* is the surah's running word — the covenant of 2:27, 2:40, 2:83, 2:100, 2:124. It closes here, in the definition of righteousness.

*Wa al-sabirina fi al-ba'sa'i wa al-darra'i wa hina al-ba'si* — "and the patient in hardship, in affliction, and at the time of conflict."

And note that these three pick up the five categories of 2:155. *Al-ba'sa'* — hardship, poverty, the hunger of 155. *Al-darra'* — affliction, harm, illness, the loss in *al-anfus*. *Hina al-ba's* — the time of battle, the fear. The patience of 155–157 is restated here as a component of righteousness.

*Ula'ika alladhina sadaqu* — "those are the ones who were truthful." And *wa ula'ika humu al-muttaqun* — "and those are the God-conscious."

**And note the bookend, which is the structural key to the whole surah.** Verse 2 opened: *hudan lil-muttaqin* — a guidance for the God-conscious. The surah named its audience and did not define them. Here, at 177, it defines them: the one who believes, gives what he loves, prays, pays, keeps his word and endures. That is who the guidance is for.

For practice, this verse is used as a self-test, and it should be — but three things in it are more useful than the list itself.

The first is the **negation**. Righteousness is not the direction you face. Which means: whatever external you are most invested in — your denomination, your school, your method, your dress, your politics, your party within the community — is not righteousness. All of it may be lawful and some of it may be obligatory. None of it is the substance, and the verse says so at the point where the surah has just finished arguing about the most sacred external there is.

The second is the **grammar**: *birr* is a person, not a checklist. A person can perform every item on this list and be far from it, because the verse is describing what a certain kind of person does rather than prescribing a set of acts to assemble. That reframes how to read it — not as a set of tasks but as a description to recognise yourself in or not.

And the third is ***'ala hubbihi***, which is the one item in the list that cannot be faked. Belief can be professed, prayer can be performed, charity can be given publicly and covenants can be kept from prudence. But giving from what you love is not reproducible by someone who is not actually giving. That is why 3:92 singles it out as the condition for attaining *birr*.

## Verse 178

**"O believers! ˹The law of˺ retaliation is set for you in cases of murder— a free man for a free man, a slave for a slave, and a female for a female. But if the offender is pardoned by the victim’s guardian, then blood-money should be decided fairly and payment should be made courteously. This is a concession and a mercy from your Lord. But whoever transgresses after that will suffer a painful punishment."**

In simple terms: prescribed for you is retaliation in cases of killing — the free for the free, the slave for the slave, the female for the female. But whoever is pardoned something by his brother, then [let there be] pursuit with fairness and payment to him with excellence. That is a lightening from your Lord and a mercy. And whoever transgresses after that, for him is a painful punishment.

*Ya ayyuha alladhina amanu kutiba 'alaykumu al-qisasu fi al-qatla* — "O you who believe, prescribed for you is *qisas* in cases of killing."

***QISAS*** is from the root meaning to follow and to trace: the punishment **follows** the crime, exactly. It is a bounded, equivalent, legally-administered response — and the distinction between it and revenge is the whole of the verse.

Revenge is private, unlimited and escalatory. *Qisas* is a legal claim: exact, bounded to the equivalent, and — critically — a **right belonging to the victim's party which they may waive**. A person taking revenge cannot be said to be waiving anything; he is satisfying himself. The existence of the waiver is what makes *qisas* a legal institution rather than a sanctioned vendetta.

On the ruling: for intentional killing, the majority position across the schools is that the heirs choose between retaliation, blood-money (*diyah*), or pardon, with or without compensation. A narration recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Abu Hurayrah states that whoever has a relative killed is to be given the choice between two things: blood-money or retaliation.

*Al-hurru bi-al-hurri wa al-'abdu bi-al-'abdi wa al-untha bi-al-untha* — "the free for the free, the slave for the slave, the female for the female."

And this is one of the most discussed clauses in the Qur'an, and the first thing to say about it is what the transmitted reports say it was addressing.

**The pre-Islamic practice was disproportionate retaliation.** The reports describe tribes refusing to accept one life for one life — insisting on killing several for one, or a person of higher standing for one of lower standing. On that reading, which is the one the majority of the commentators held, **this verse is a limitation rather than an expansion**: it establishes equivalence where the custom had been escalation.

The jurists then differed, and continue to differ, on the specific equivalences the clause raises — whether a free person is subject to retaliation for killing a slave, whether a man for a woman, whether a Muslim for a non-Muslim living under protection, and whether a parent for a child. **These are genuine and well-known disagreements within the classical schools and they should be reported as disagreements rather than resolved in a general commentary.** The principle the jurists worked from is 5:45: "And We ordained for them therein a life for a life, an eye for an eye..."

*Fa-man 'ufiya lahu min akhihi shay'un* — "but whoever is pardoned something by his brother..."

**And here is the most remarkable thing in the verse.** At the exact moment the blood is hottest, the Qur'an calls the killer **the brother** of the one with the right to kill him.

The commentators read *akhihi* as brother in religion where both are Muslim, and as brother in humanity otherwise — and both readings are transmitted. The function of the naming is not descriptive. It is to place the relationship before the claim at the moment the claim is at its most absolute.

*Fa-ttiba'un bi-al-ma'rufi wa ada'un ilayhi bi-ihsan* — "then [let there be] pursuit with what is fair, and payment to him with excellence."

**And note that both parties are addressed.** The one owed must pursue the blood-money fairly, *bi-al-ma'ruf* — without extortion or impossible demands. And the one paying must pay *bi-ihsan* — with excellence, promptly and without stinting. The law does not merely settle the dispute; it legislates the manner of both sides.

*Dhalika takhfifun min rabbikum wa rahmatun* — "that is a lightening from your Lord and a mercy."

***Takhfif*** — a lightening, a reduction — and the commentators differed on what it is a reduction **from**. Some held: from the pre-Islamic practice of disproportionate retaliation. Others: from what was prescribed for the previous communities, for whom retaliation was the sole option without blood-money. Both readings are transmitted, and the ambiguity does not affect the ruling.

*Fa-mani i'tada ba'da dhalika fa-lahu 'adhabun alim* — "and whoever transgresses after that, for him is a painful punishment."

**And this closes the loophole, which is the whole point of the clause.** Once the matter has been settled — by pardon, or by acceptance of blood-money — any further violence is a fresh crime. The verse anticipates the thing that actually happens: a family accepts the payment and then takes revenge anyway. That is the *i'tida'* named here.

For practice, three things.

The first is the **sanctity of life**, which is the frame the tradition places around all of this. Recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim: the blood of a person who testifies that there is no god but Allah and that Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah is not lawful except for one of three — a life for a life, the married person who fornicates, and the one who leaves his religion and abandons the community. The exception proves the rule: life is inviolable, and the taking of it is the gravest matter in the law after associating partners with God.

The second is *min akhihi*, and its application is broader than murder. The verse's method is to name the relationship **before** the claim, at the moment the claim is overwhelming. Every dispute a person is in has a relationship underneath it that the claim is threatening to obliterate. The verse's order — relationship first, claim second — is a method, and it is the opposite of the order most conflicts are actually conducted in.

And the third is the closing. No private revenge after settlement. That is a hard ruling and it is the thing that distinguishes a legal system from a feud. A person who has accepted the settlement has taken the remedy the law gave him; going beyond it is not justice but a second crime.

## Verse 179

**"There is ˹security of˺ life for you in ˹the law of˺ retaliation, O people of reason, so that you may become mindful ˹of Allah˺."**

In simple terms: and for you in retaliation is life, O people of understanding, that you may be God-conscious.

*Wa lakum fi al-qisasi hayatun* — "and for you in *qisas* is **life**."

**This is one of the most concentrated sentences in the Qur'an, and the rhetoricians have singled it out for centuries.**

*Qisas* is death. And the verse says that in it is **life**. The paradox is deliberate and it is the argument.

Three readings of how it works are transmitted, and they are all true:

**(a) Deterrence.** The one considering murder knows that his own life is the price, and refrains. The execution of one killer protects many potential victims.

**(b) The end of the feud.** The pre-Islamic vendetta consumed many lives for one. A bounded, equivalent, legally-satisfied claim stops the cycle at one. This is probably the most historically immediate sense: the institution of *qisas* replaced a practice that cost far more lives than it settled.

**(c) The satisfaction of the claim.** A wrong that is answered exactly is finished. A wrong that is left to the aggrieved party to answer privately is not.

**The tradition's rhetorical literature compares this verse to the pre-Islamic Arabic saying *al-qatlu anfa li-al-qatl*** — "killing reduces killing" — and argues that the Qur'anic formulation surpasses it, on the grounds that the Arab proverb names the outcome as *less killing* while the Qur'an names it as **life**. The difference is between a reduction in a negative and the assertion of a positive, and the second is the stronger and more truthful statement.

*Ya uli al-albab* — "O you who possess understanding." *Albāb* is the plural of *lubb*, the core or kernel of a thing, and hence the mind.

**And note the thread running through this whole stretch of the surah.** 2:164: signs for *qawmin ya'qilun*, a people who reason. 2:170: their fathers *la ya'qiluna*, did not reason. 2:171: *la ya'qilun*. 2:179: *ya uli al-albab*. The surah has been insisting on the reasoning faculty for twenty verses, and here it addresses the reasoners directly — because this is the one place in the batch where the Qur'an gives a **stated reason** for a legal ruling.

That matters for *usul al-fiqh*. The *'illah* — the effective cause — of retaliation is here named as the preservation of life. A ruling whose purpose is stated can be reasoned about in light of that purpose, and the tradition has done exactly that.

*La'allakum tattaqun* — "that you may be God-conscious."

For practice, this short verse gives the principle that governs how a penal ruling is to be understood and applied.

**The purpose of the law is life.** Not retribution for its own sake, not the satisfaction of the state, not vengeance — life. And that means any application of a penal provision is to be measured against whether it serves that purpose. A penal system that produces more death than it prevents has inverted the verse. A penal system administered without the possibility of the pardon the previous verse established has discarded half of it.

And the second application is the address itself. *Ya uli al-albab.* The verse is addressed to people who think — which means the law of God is not presented here as something to be accepted without understanding. It is presented with its reason given, and the reader is called upon to follow the reason. That is an unusual and significant thing, and it says something about who the religion thinks it is talking to.

## Verse 180

**"It is prescribed that when death approaches any of you—if they leave something of value—a will should be made in favour of parents and immediate family with fairness. ˹This is˺ an obligation on those who are mindful ˹of Allah˺."**

In simple terms: prescribed for you, when death approaches one of you, if he leaves behind wealth, is the bequest to the parents and near relatives, according to what is fair — an obligation upon the God-conscious.

*Kutiba 'alaykum idha hadara ahadakumu al-mawtu* — "prescribed for you, when death approaches one of you..."

Note the framing immediately: the religion legislates **the deathbed**. Not the death, and not the estate in the abstract, but the moment a person understands that he is dying. There is a whole attitude toward mortality in that framing, and it is not denial.

*In taraka khayran* — "if he leaves behind *khayr*." The commentators read *khayr* here as wealth. And the jurists differed on how much: some held the bequest is prescribed only for one leaving a substantial estate, others for anyone leaving anything. That is a genuine disagreement and it is why the schools differ on the threshold.

*Al-wasiyyatu lil-walidayni wa al-aqrabina* — "the bequest to the parents and near relatives."

**And here is the crux, and it is one of the classic disputed cases in the whole of the law.**

The verse prescribes a bequest **to parents and near relatives** — who are precisely the people to whom the later verses of inheritance (4:11–12) assign fixed shares. And the Sunnah contains the statement: **"Indeed Allah has given to every person entitled to a right his right. So there is no bequest to an heir"** — recorded by Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi, al-Nasa'i, Ibn Majah and Ahmad from Abu Umamah al-Bahili, with its grading discussed among the specialists.

So: is verse 180 abrogated?

**The disagreement must be reported rather than settled.**

**The majority position** is that the obligation to bequeath to heirs is abrogated — by the inheritance verses, and/or by the hadith — so that a bequest to an heir is not valid without the consent of the other heirs, and the obligation is replaced by the fixed shares.

**A minority position** holds that the verse is not abrogated but specified (*mukhassas* rather than *mansukh*): the bequest remains prescribed for near relatives who do **not** inherit — those excluded by a nearer heir, or relatives who do not inherit for another reason. On this reading the verse was never about heirs as such.

**An intermediate position** holds that the *obligation* is abrogated while the *recommendation* remains.

All three positions are held by qualified scholars, and this is one of the cases that shows why the whole subject of abrogation has to be handled with care — a point made at 2:106, where it was noted that the number of claimed instances of abrogation is itself disputed, that al-Suyuti's figure of roughly twenty reduces to a handful under scrutiny, and that no claim of abrogation is accepted by the jurists without proof of both the text and the sequence.

The governing hadith on the **amount** is recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas, who said: the Prophet ﷺ came to visit me while I was ill, and I asked whether I might bequeath all my wealth — he said no. Two-thirds? No. A half? No. A third? He said: **"A third, and a third is a lot."**

And the governing hadith on **promptness** is recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Ibn 'Umar: **"It is not fitting for a Muslim who has something to bequeath to spend two nights without having his will written and kept with him."**

*Bi-al-ma'rufi* — "according to what is fair." And on fairness among those one provides for, the hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from al-Nu'man ibn Bashir: his father brought him to the Prophet ﷺ having given him a gift, and the Prophet ﷺ asked whether he had given the same to all his children, and when he said no, he said: **"Fear Allah and be just among your children."**

*Haqqan 'ala al-muttaqin* — "an obligation upon the God-conscious." And note the closing word, which is the same as verse 177's: the *muttaqun*. The surah defined them at 177 and here addresses them again at the deathbed.

For practice, three things.

The first is the **practical ruling**, stated in the terms the majority of the schools hold: a bequest is valid up to one-third of the estate; it may not go to an heir except with the consent of the other heirs; it is recommended for one who has wealth, especially toward relatives who do not inherit; and the fixed shares of 4:11–12 govern the remainder. A person preparing a will should consult a qualified scholar in his own school and jurisdiction rather than proceeding from a general summary.

The second is the **fairness condition**, and it is where most of the actual harm in this area occurs. *Bi-al-ma'ruf* rules out both favouritism and vindictiveness: a bequest that advantages one child over another without cause, or that disinherits someone out of resentment, fails the condition. The hadith of al-Nu'man ibn Bashir is the standard the tradition applies, and the emotion it addresses — a parent's wish to provide especially for one child — is exactly the ordinary case.

And the third is the **deathbed framing**, which is the verse's quietest and most useful gift. *Idha hadara ahadakumu al-mawtu* — when death approaches one of you, the prescribed act is to settle your affairs justly. The religion does not treat the approach of death as a time for denial or for panic. It treats it as a time for a specific piece of work. The two-nights hadith extends that: the will is not something to write when dying but something to have written, so that dying does not arrive into disorder.

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

Three movements, and the join between the second and third is the argument.

**Hearing and eating (171–173).** The parable of the call that is heard as noise, with the three closures named and the non-reasoning given as their consequence (171). Then the turn to the ordinary: eat the good provision, and give thanks — with gratitude established as the proof that worship is His alone (172). Then the dietary law: four prohibitions only, with the sea and the organs excepted, pork alone given a stated reason, and the exception of necessity built into the same sentence with the two conditions that generate the maxim that necessity is measured by its extent (173).

**The Book and those who sell it (174–176).** Those who conceal it for a small price consume fire — literally, the price they took is the fire (174); and the *tazkiyah* they withheld is withheld from them. They bought misguidance with guidance and punishment with forgiveness, and are described with the very word the surah used for the steadfast — patient, but upon the Fire (175). And the reason: the Book came with the truth, so disputing it is a distant schism — with the necessary distinction drawn between that and the rewarded disagreement of scholars deriving rulings (176).

**The definition and the law (177–180).** Righteousness is not a direction; it is a person who believes, gives what he loves, prays, pays, keeps his word and endures — and those are the *muttaqun* the surah named in its second verse (177). Then retaliation: bounded, equivalent, waivable, with the killer called the victim's brother at the moment of the claim, and with both parties instructed in their manner (178). Then the paradox that resolves it: in retaliation is life, addressed to those who reason, with the purpose of the law stated (179). And finally the deathbed bequest, fair and bounded, with the abrogation question reported as the genuinely disputed case it is (180).

Three threads run through the batch. First, **the form is never the substance** — the direction is not righteousness (177), the hearing is not comprehension (171), the invocation is not worship (172), and the legal externals are the shell of something else. Second, **every ruling is given with its mercy inside it** — the exception of necessity in the same sentence as the prohibition (173), the pardon and the fair payment in the same verse as the retaliation (178), life named as the purpose of the death penalty (179), and the one-third limit on the bequest (180). And third, **the surah keeps returning to the reasoning faculty** — *ya'qilun* at 164, *la ya'qiluna* at 170, *la ya'qilun* at 171, and *ya uli al-albab* at 179, where the law is finally given with its reason stated and the reader called to follow it.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given. This is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and supplying precise references from memory would mean inventing them. Where the commentators and jurists differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved: the two readings of the simile in verse 171; the referent of *'ala hubbihi* in verse 177; what *takhfif* in verse 178 is a reduction from; and the abrogation of verse 180, where three distinct positions have been given and the majority identified.

Three areas have been deliberately left open rather than adjudicated, and the reason should be stated. The equivalences in verse 178 — whether a free person for a slave, a man for a woman, a Muslim for a protected non-Muslim — are matters of genuine and continuing disagreement within the classical schools, and a general commentary that resolved them would be manufacturing a consensus that does not exist. The question of mechanically-slaughtered meat is a live contemporary dispute among qualified bodies, and no general reader should be given false confidence in either direction from a summary. And the abrogation of verse 180 is one of the classic disputed cases, reported here in the terms the dispute actually takes.

Transmitted reports have been flagged as reports throughout, and the gradings of the narrations on the sea, on the two bloods, and on "no bequest to an heir" have been noted as discussed rather than asserted.

Four boundaries have been observed. On verse 171: the simile has been given in both transmitted readings rather than one being presented as the reading. On verse 173: the *innama* has been used to answer scrupulosity as well as laxity, since the invention of prohibitions is as much a corruption of the religion as the disregard of them. On verse 176: the necessary distinction has been drawn between the blameworthy dispute against revelation and the rewarded disagreement of qualified scholars deriving rulings, since the verse is routinely used to condemn the second as though it were the first. And on verse 178: the *min akhihi* naming has been given its full weight, and the closing clause against private revenge after settlement has been stated as the thing that distinguishes a legal institution from a feud.
