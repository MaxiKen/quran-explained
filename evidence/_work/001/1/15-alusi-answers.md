### ALŪSĪ'S ANSWERS TO THE SIXTEEN ARGUMENTS

- `1:1.0` · lesson · Ibn Wahbān's verse (in Alūsī): if one forgets the basmalah in every rakʿah he prostrates for forgetfulness, since its obligation is "the saying of most"; Ghunyat al-Mutamallī: that is the safer course, "and I say so", against Qāḍī Khān, the author of al-Khulāṣah and others — the truth is more worthy of following; and to call part of this the pen's excess is the extreme of excess and bigotry without mastery. [alusi¶17, alusi¶18.1]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · Against the first argument (Umm Salamah): the form al-Rāzī gives differs from al-Bayḍāwī's and from the hadith books; Abū Mulaykah's hearing from Umm Salamah is not established, and even granting that they were contemporaries the wording is not found in the well-known sources — perhaps he narrated by meaning from the reports that follow. [alusi¶18.1]
- `1:1.0` · hadith · Reports Alūsī gives in its place: Abū ʿUbayd, Aḥmad and Abū Dāwūd — the Prophet used to cut his recitation verse by verse ("bismillāh…", "al-ḥamdu li-llāh…", "al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm", "mālik yawm al-dīn"); Ibn al-Anbārī and al-Bayhaqī — he paused after each; Ibn Khuzaymah and al-Ḥākim — he counted the basmalah one, "al-ḥamdu…" two, "al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm" three, "mālik yawm al-dīn" four, and said "thus 'iyyāka naʿbudu…'", gathering five fingers; al-Dāraquṭnī — he counted in the Arab way, counted the basmalah and did not count "ʿalayhim". [alusi¶18.1-18.2]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · Alūsī: the first two reports can be read as describing how the Prophet recited the Qurʾān generally, naming some verses as an example; they prove only that it is a verse — conceded — of the Qurʾān, not that it is of al-Fātiḥah; the third shows he recited it in prayer and counted it a verse for pausing on it — also conceded: it is the first verse of the Qurʾān, "al-ḥamdu…" the second, and so on to the fifth, when the fingers were gathered and the speech ended. [alusi¶18.2-18.3]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · The fourth report may mean he sometimes recited so, in prayer or out of it; he counted the basmalah and did not drop it, for its obligation in prayer and the care owed it elsewhere, for its great secrets and fine thoughts — so many Ḥanafīs make the prostration of forgetfulness obligatory on one who leaves it — and in this he removed the supposition that it is not Qurʾān because it is used to open letters and affairs. [alusi¶18.3]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · He did not count "ṣirāṭa alladhīna anʿamta ʿalayhim" or pause on it but joined it, that once, to show it is allowed; closeness of verse lengths, like clauses, is liked and uneven endings do no harm ("in crowds", 110:2, against "the victory"); a described noun in one verse and its description in another has precedent; so the report proves only that the basmalah is a Qurʾān verse — granted by both sides — and that it is of al-Fātiḥah is "to pick thorns of the qatād tree with the hand", beyond reach. [alusi¶18.3-18.4]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · Against the second (Abū Hurayrah): al-Ṭabarānī, Ibn Mardawayh and al-Bayhaqī have "'al-ḥamdu li-llāhi rabbi l-ʿālamīn' is seven verses, 'bismillāh…' one of them; it is the seven oft-repeated, the great Qurʾān, Umm al-Qurʾān, Fātiḥat al-Kitāb"; al-Dāraquṭnī has "when you recite al-ḥamd, recite 'bismillāh…', for it is Umm al-Qurʾān…, and 'bismillāh…' is one of its verses". Alūsī reads the first as: "al-ḥamdu…" to the end is seven verses (the Ḥanafī view), and "the basmalah is one of them" means like one of them in being a Qurʾān verse, to remove the listeners' doubt; the second as encouragement to recite the basmalah first — admitting this takes a figurative sense but keeps the opening literal. [alusi¶19.1-19.2]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · The third argument only proves the basmalah is Qurʾān, as al-Rāzī himself grants and the Ḥanafīs do not dispute. [alusi¶19.2]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · The fourth argument's reports: the first, second, third, sixth (and it is weak) and eighth do not prove the aim, and the Ḥanafīs say what they do show; the fourth is a statement of Ibn ʿAbbās only — not conceded to be the Prophet's, since ijtihād is possible, though the sound view is that a verse is known by the lawgiver's instruction, as a sūrah is — which is why "Alif Lām Mīm" is counted a verse wherever it occurs and "Alif Lām Mīm Rā" is not; the Ḥanafīs call the basmalah an independent verse, and Ibn ʿAbbās by ijtihād made it a verse of al-Fātiḥah, or he spoke from instruction as he supposed it to be. [alusi¶19.2-19.3]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · The fifth report Alūsī doubts in this wording — perhaps it is al-Dāraquṭnī's; he does not rely on al-Rāzī in hadith, who is not among its memorisers and changes things when he narrates by meaning, and he does not have al-Thaʿlabī's tafsīr to check; the seventh lacks the polish and eloquence of the Prophet's speech — "the most eloquent speaker of ḍād" — and whoever has practised the hadiths is sure it is forged; were it sound the Shāfiʿīs would have rested on it alone or put it before their other proofs, and would that al-Rāzī had given its chain. [alusi¶19.3]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · Against the fifth argument: it is not conceded that obligation at the start of al-Fātiḥah makes it a verse of it, and the use of "Read in the name of your Lord" is very weak on grounds clearer than the sun. [alusi¶19.3-19.4]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · The sixth argument is the strongest proof that the basmalah is Qurʾān, but not that it belongs to al-Fātiḥah; those who deny it is Qurʾān answer it in ways sound nature and a straight mind do not accept — fairness is half of religion and submission to the truth a trait of the believers; the seventh is for the Ḥanafīs, not against them; the eighth needs mountains moved — weakening Abū Ḥanīfah's saying — before it stands. [alusi¶19.4]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · The ninth is the same as the fifth; and "every matter of importance…" proves nothing, since al-Fātiḥah is part of a prayer opened by the takbīr joined to the intention, which is its pillar; were the prayer called cut off for lacking the basmalah, so would bowing and prostration, where a servant is nearest to his Lord — each a matter of importance — if not opened with it; so one thinks well of al-Rāzī's religion that he said the basmalah at the start of his prayer, at bowing, prostration and every move. [alusi¶19.4-19.5]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · The tenth does not stand against the Ḥanafīs, who have told their school; the eleventh, at most, shows that Muʿāwiyah, having left an obligation without the prostration of forgetfulness, repeated the prayer to be free of defect — they gave him time to see whether he would repair it by prostration; and their objection was no stranger than their objecting to his leaving a posture, for al-Shāfiʿī, as al-Rāzī quotes, reports they cried "O Muʿāwiyah, you stole from the prayer — where is 'bismillāh…', where is the takbīr at bowing and prostration?", and he repeated it with both; what remains is the audibility, and the discussion of it is hidden now. [alusi¶19.5-19.6]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · The twelfth: obligation does not imply being a part; and arguing that it was obligatory on the Prophet because it was on the prophets before him, by "those are the ones God guided, so follow their guidance" (6:90), is, Alūsī says, ignorance of tafsīr and of the reports of the Bringer of glad tidings; the thirteenth is no help and has what its sisters have; the rest, like many before, serve only to blacken paper and waste breath. [alusi¶19.6]
- `1:1.0` · hadith · Alūsī adds that some of what al-Rāzī says is contradicted by what Muslim and others narrate from Abū Hurayrah, the ḥadīth qudsī "I divided the prayer between Me and My servant…", which makes "iyyāka naʿbudu…" the middle and puts three verses before it for God and three after it for the servant — so the basmalah is not of al-Fātiḥah and the verses are seven without it, though it does not deny that the basmalah is Qurʾān. [alusi¶19.6-19.7]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · This report is sounder than al-Thaʿlabī's — "I do not put a Thaʿlabī before Muslim" — and than al-Sijistānī's; and whenever a trustworthy narrator differs from one more trustworthy by an addition or omission his hadith is shādhdh, and this is not a matter of negation against affirmation, as thought by one with no footing in this science. [alusi¶19.7]

- `1:1.0` · lesson · Alūsī: to read "half" in the ḥadīth qudsī as half in meaning or kind is unfair — that is a figurative sense with no need and no indication — and to keep it literal by praise and supplication is belied by the counting; the objection that the report turns on al-ʿAlāʾ, whom Ibn Maʿīn weakened, "fattens nothing and satisfies no hunger": the trustworthy are many, and criticism is not always preferred to validation — only where the validators are not very many, and here they are. [alusi¶19.8]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · That the division concerns what is peculiar to al-Fātiḥah, the basmalah being shared, is against the apparent sense and not required by wisdom, since to the opponent the basmalah is its noblest part; that "prayer" means part of the prayer's recitation is nothing — the fitting part has a beginning and an end of its own, the second exists and the first is so on the Ḥanafī view; and al-Fātiḥah is a sūrah like al-Kawthar and al-Mulk. [alusi¶19.8]
- `1:1.0` · hadith · The Prophet said, in Abū Hurayrah's report, that the first sūrah is three verses and the second thirty, and had his listeners stand at them, and did not count the basmalah; had he counted it independent the number would rise, and as a part it would have been reported; the one who affirms must show it — "and where is it?". [alusi¶19.8-19.9]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · To make it a verse of al-Fātiḥah by itself and part of a verse elsewhere is arbitrary; al-Rāzī's "not far — 'al-ḥamdu li-llāh…' is a verse at one place and part of a verse at another, as in 'and the close of their prayer…' (10:10)" is, Alūsī says, far — a false analogy, since the reason for being a part is there and absent here. [alusi¶19.9]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · Many sūrahs came down without a basmalah and it was joined to them afterwards, as the sound hadith on the start of revelation shows, which makes it unlikely to be a verse or part of a verse of a sūrah; the report that it was the first thing revealed is not strong — the established first revelation is another — and sits badly with the report that the Prophet wrote "bismika Allāhumma"; and were it conceded it would not harm the Ḥanafīs. [alusi¶19.9]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · Alūsī's conclusion: the belief that the basmalah is part of no sūrah is almost innate to one who trusts his own sense; it is an independent verse of the Qurʾān; whoever has seen the hadiths should not hesitate over its being Qurʾān or deny that reciting it is obligatory and call it a sunnah — "by God, were the earth filled with gold for me I would not take that view, though I could justify it"; the hadith books are full of what contradicts it, and the obligation is what is sound to him from the Imām (Abū Ḥanīfah), whose silence on so grave a matter, on which a prayer's validity or completeness and rulings of divorce, oath and conditional release may turn, is not to be supposed. [alusi¶19.9-19.10]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · Saying it silently in loud prayers does not show it is only a sunnah: holding it obligatory does not conflict with saying it silently in following the Messenger. [alusi¶19.10]
