### ALLĀH: A NAME WITHOUT A ROOT, OR DERIVED

- `1:1.0` · language · Al-Khalīl and a group: Allāh is a proper name specific to God, with no derivation, like Zayd and ʿAmr for men; another group says it is derived. [baghawi¶8]
- `1:1.0` · language · Derived from alaha, ilāhah, "to worship" — Ibn ʿAbbās read "wa-yadharaka wa-ālihataka" (7:127) as "your worship" — so He alone deserves worship; or from ilāh, as in "no god was with Him; else each god would have gone off with what he created" (23:91). [baghawi¶8]
- `1:1.0` · language · Al-Mubarrad: from the Arabs' "alihtu ilā fulān", "I found rest in him" — creatures find rest in Him and calm in remembering Him; or "I fled to him", with a line of verse for each sense. [baghawi¶8-9]
- `1:1.0` · language · Or the origin is "wilāh", the wāw turned to hamza as in wishāḥ/ishāḥ, from walah — creatures are bewildered toward Him, fleeing to Him in hardship and taking refuge in need as a child toward its mother; or from walah as the loss of reason at the loss of someone dear. [baghawi¶10]

- `1:1.0` · language · Saʿdī: Allāh is the one worshipped (al-maʾlūh), the one who deserves to be singled out for worship because of the attributes of divinity — the attributes of perfection. [saadi¶2]
- `1:1.0` · language · Maʿārif: Allāh is the greatest and most comprehensive of the divine names, said by some to be the Great Name (al-ism al-aʿẓam), on which, by a hadith, a prayer is granted when it is uttered — reports differ about which name that is; it refers to the Essence and so is given to no one else, which is why it has no dual or plural: the name of the one Reality that holds every attribute of perfection, creator and sustainer, unique and peerless. [maarif¶18]
- `1:1.0` · language · The study draft: "Allāh" is, for some, a unique word with no root, and for others derived from al-Ilāh, "the Divine" (Ṭb); the Qurʾān shows the pre-Islamic Arabs already knew it — of the pagans, "Were you to ask them 'Who created you?' they would surely say 'God'" (43:87; cf. 29:61, 63; 31:25; 39:38) — though its supreme sovereignty was not accepted by most idol-worshippers in the Age of Ignorance (3:154; 5:50; 33:33; 48:26). [initial¶3]
- `1:1.0` · language · The study draft, citing al-Ghazzālī (Divine Names): Allāh is both the most universal Name and the most specific, since it can describe no being but God while other Names may; some call it the All-Encompassing Name, holding all the Names and Attributes (Bq; see 112:1c). [initial¶3]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · The study draft: the order of the three Names can allude to the levels of Divinity — Allāh the Essence, al-Raḥmān the unity of the Attributes, al-Raḥīm the unity of the acts (K). [initial¶3]
- `1:1.0` · language · Ibn Kathīr: Allāh is the name of the Lord, the Exalted; it is said to be His Greatest Name, since it is the one the other names describe — "He is God, besides whom there is no god, the Knower of the unseen and the seen, the Raḥmān, the Raḥīm … the King, the Holy …" (59:22-24); "to God belong the most beautiful names, so call on Him by them" (7:180); "call upon God or call upon al-Raḥmān" (17:110). [kathir¶1.42-1.44]
- `1:1.0` · hadith · The Two Ṣaḥīḥs, Abū Hurayrah: "God has ninety-nine names, one hundred less one; whoever counts them enters Paradise" — the names are listed in reports by al-Tirmidhī and Ibn Mājah, which differ in places (Ibn Kathīr). [kathir¶1.44]
- `1:1.0` · athar · Ṭabarī: "Allāh" means, as reported from Ibn ʿAbbās, "He to whom everything turns in devotion (yaʾlahuhu) and whom every creature worships"; his report (no. 141), through Abū Kurayb, ʿUthmān ibn Saʿīd, Bishr ibn ʿUmārah, Abū Rawq and al-Ḍaḥḥāk: "Allāh is the One of divinity and of being worshipped over all His creation"; the editor calls the chain weak, and al-Suyūṭī gives it, with a later report, to Ibn Jarīr and Ibn Abī Ḥātim. [tabari¶52-53]
- `1:1.0` · language · Asked whether the verb "faʿala yafʿalu" gave a root from which the name was built, Ṭabarī says: not by hearing from the Arabs but by inference. That ulūhiyyah is worship and the ilāh the worshipped is shown by the Arabs' agreement that "taʾallaha fulān" is right of one described as worshipping and seeking what is with God — as in Ruʾbah's line, "the praise of the beauties be God's — they glorified and said 'to God we belong' at my devotion (taʾallahī)", meaning my worship and my seeking God by my work. [tabari¶54-59]
- `1:1.0` · language · Taʾallūh is tafaʿʿul from "alaha yaʾlahu", which means "he worshipped God", and a verbal noun has come from it which shows the Arabs used "faʿala yafʿalu" with no addition. [tabari¶60]
- `1:1.0` · athar · Ibn ʿAbbās read "wa-yadharaka wa-ilāhataka" (7:127) as "your worship", saying Pharaoh was worshipped and did not worship — two reports through Sufyān ibn Wakīʿ (from his father, Nāfiʿ ibn ʿUmar and ʿAmr ibn Dīnār; and from Ibn ʿUyaynah, ʿAmr and Muḥammad ibn ʿAmr ibn al-Ḥasan); the editor calls both weak for Sufyān's sake, whose copyist corrupted his reports, and notes that the usual reading is "ālihataka", while "ilāhataka" is attributed to Ibn Muḥayṣin and al-Ḥasan, to ʿAlī, Ibn Masʿūd and Ibn ʿAbbās (Ibn Khālawayh), and with Anas and others (Abū Ḥayyān). [tabari¶61-64]
- `1:1.0` · athar · Ṭabarī: ʿAbdullāh and Mujāhid read it so too — "wa-ilāhataka" meaning "your worship" (al-Qāsim, al-Ḥusayn ibn Dāwūd, Ḥajjāj, Ibn Jurayj, Mujāhid); so ilāhah, as Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid explained it, is the verbal noun of "alaha Llāha fulān ilāhatan", as "ʿabada Llāha ʿibādatan" — which shows that "alaha" means "worshipped" and "ilāhah" is its verbal noun. [tabari¶65-66]
- `1:1.0` · language · Asked what to say, if "alahahu" may be said of the worshipper, to report that God has that due from His servant, Ṭabarī says there is no report, but by analogy with the report from the Prophet — the forged ʿĪsā report again, "Allāh is the God of the gods" (no. 145) — one should say "God alaha the servant and the servant alahahu", and the Arabic "Allāh" has "al-ilāh" as its origin. [tabari¶67-69]
- `1:1.0` · language · Asked how that can be, given their different wording, Ṭabarī says as "lākinna huwa Llāhu rabbī" (18:38) is originally "lākin anā huwa Llāhu rabbī" — and the line "you glance at me as if to say I am guilty and you hate me, but (lākinna) you I do not hate": the hamzah of "anā" is dropped, its nūn meets the silent nūn of "lākin" and they merge; so "Allāh" is originally "al-ilāh": the hamzah, the first letter of the word, drops, and the lām, its middle letter, meets the extra lām that came with the extra alif, silent, and they merge into one doubled lām. [tabari¶70-73]
- `1:1.0` · language · Qurṭubī, twentieth issue: "Allāh" is the greatest and most comprehensive of His names, so that some scholars said it is God's Greatest Name and that none else was called by it — hence it takes no dual or plural; it is one of two readings of "do you know any namesake for Him?" (19:65), i.e. one named by His name "Allāh"; it is the name of the truly Existent, who gathers the attributes of divinity, is described with the attributes of lordship and is alone in true existence — "there is no god but He"; it was said it means the One who deserves to be worshipped, or the Necessary Existent who has always been and always will be, the sense being one. [qurtubi¶25]
- `1:1.0` · language · Twenty-first: whether the name is derived or a proper name for the Essence is disputed; many scholars hold it derived, and differ on its origin: Sībawayh reports from al-Khalīl that it is originally "ilāh", of the form fiʿāl, the alif and lām replacing the hamzah — as al-nās is originally unās; or the origin is "lāh", the alif and lām added for magnification — Sībawayh's choice — with the line "O God, your cousin — you have not surpassed me in lineage, nor are you my judge to rule me". [qurtubi¶25-27.1]
- `1:1.0` · language · Al-Kisāʾī and al-Farrāʾ: "bismillāh" is "bism al-ilāh" with the hamzah dropped and the first lām merged into the second into one doubled lām, as in "lākinnā huwa Llāhu rabbī", meaning "lākin anā", as al-Ḥasan read it. [qurtubi¶27.1]
- `1:1.0` · language · Then it was said to come from "walaha", to be bewildered — walah is loss of reason; a man is walih, a woman wālihah, and "muwallah" water is water let loose in deserts — so minds are bewildered and lost over the truths of God's attributes and in thinking of knowing Him; on this the origin of "ilāh" is "walāh", the hamzah in place of a wāw as in ishāḥ for wishāḥ and isādah for wisādah, reported also from al-Khalīl (Qurṭubī). [qurtubi¶27.1]
- `1:1.0` · athar · Al-Ḍaḥḥāk: God is called "ilāh" because creatures take refuge in Him (yataʾallahūn) in their needs and plead to Him in their hardships; al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad: because they "yaʾlahūn" or "yaʾlihūn" to Him, two dialects. [qurtubi¶27.1-27.2]
- `1:1.0` · language · Or from elevation: the Arabs said "lāhā" of everything high, and when the sun rose they said "lāhat"; or from "alaha l-rajul", he devoted himself to worship — thus "wa-yadharaka wa-ilāhataka" (7:127) in that reading, Ibn ʿAbbās and others saying "your worship"; so "Allāh" means the One aimed at in worship, and the monotheists' "lā ilāha illā llāh" means "no object of worship but God", the "illā" meaning "other than", not an exception (Qurṭubī). [qurtubi¶27.2]
- `1:1.0` · language · Some claim the origin is the hāʾ, the pronoun for the absent: people found Him in the natural disposition of their minds and pointed to Him with the pronoun, then added the lām of ownership, since they knew He is the creator and owner of things, making "lahu", then the alif and lām for magnification (Qurṭubī). [qurtubi¶27.2]
