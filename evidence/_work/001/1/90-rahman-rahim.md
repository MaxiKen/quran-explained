### AL-RAḤMĀN AND AL-RAḤĪM: ONE MEANING OR TWO

- `1:1.0` · athar · Ibn ʿAbbās: two delicate (raqīqān) names, one more delicate than the other. [baghawi¶11]
- `1:1.0` · language · Some say the two are one in meaning, like nadmān and nadīm, "possessor of mercy", the second following the first to draw hopeful hearts; al-Mubarrad: favour upon favour, grace upon grace (Baghawī). [baghawi¶11]
- `1:1.0` · language · Others distinguish: al-Raḥmān is general, "the provider in this world" to all creation; al-Raḥīm is particular, the one who pardons in the next world, for the believers alone — hence the prayer "O Raḥmān of this world and Raḥīm of the next". [baghawi¶11]
- `1:1.0` · language · So others than God may be called raḥīm but not raḥmān: al-Raḥmān is general in meaning and particular in wording, al-Raḥīm general in wording and particular in meaning. [baghawi¶11]
- `1:1.0` · language · Mercy is God's will of good for its people; or leaving the punishment of one who deserves it and giving good to one who does not — an attribute of the essence on the first view, of action on the second. [baghawi¶11]

- `1:1.0` · language · Saʿdī: the two names show that God has vast, great mercy that took in everything and covered every living thing, which He decreed for the God-fearing who follow His prophets and messengers — they have mercy absolutely, and the rest have a share of it. [saadi¶2]
- `1:1.0` · language · Maʿārif: al-Raḥmān is one whose mercy is common to all and reaches the whole universe and all that will be created; al-Raḥīm one whose mercy is perfect in every way. [maarif¶24]
- `1:1.0` · ruling · Maʿārif: so al-Raḥmān is God's alone and no creature may be called by it — there is no dual or plural of it, as of "Allāh" (Qurṭubī); but raḥīm may be used of a man, as the Qurʾān does of the Prophet, "gentle and very merciful toward the believers" (9:128). [maarif¶24]
- `1:1.0` · ruling · Maʿārif: hence those who shorten ʿAbd al-Raḥmān or Faḍl al-Raḥmān to "Raḥmān" do what is not permissible and sin. [maarif¶25]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · Maʿārif: of all the Beautiful Names only these two are named here, both from the root raḥmah, showing the all-pervading perfection of mercy; the creation and upkeep of the universe have no motive but to show God's mercy — He needed none of it and none could compel Him. [maarif¶26]
- `1:1.0` · language · Maʿārif quotes Rūmī: "There was nothing — neither our being nor our claim to be; it was Thy mercy that heard our unsaid." [maarif¶27-30]
- `1:1.0` · language · Ibn Kathīr: both are derived from raḥmah, but al-Raḥmān carries more meanings of mercy than al-Raḥīm; a statement of Ibn Jarīr indicates agreement on this. [kathir¶1.45]
- `1:1.0` · hadith · Al-Qurṭubī's proof of the derivation, in Ibn Kathīr: al-Tirmidhī, graded ṣaḥīḥ, from ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf — "God said: I am al-Raḥmān; I created the raḥim and derived a name for it from My name; whoever joins it, I join him, and whoever cuts it, I cut him" — "a text that indicates derivation"; the Arabs denied the name al-Raḥmān out of ignorance of God and His attributes. [kathir¶1.45]
- `1:1.0` · language · Al-Qurṭubī, in Ibn Kathīr: it was said both mean the same, like nadmān and nadīm, as Abū ʿUbayd held; Abū ʿAlī al-Fārisī: al-Raḥmān, God's alone, covers every kind of His mercy, and al-Raḥīm is what reaches the believers — "He is ever Raḥīm to the believers" (33:43); Ibn ʿAbbās: two soft names, one softer than the other. [kathir¶1.46]
- `1:1.0` · athar · Ibn Jarīr, from al-ʿAzramī: "He is al-Raḥmān to all creation and al-Raḥīm to the believers" — hence God names the istiwāʾ with al-Raḥmān, "al-Raḥmān rose over the Throne" (25:59, 20:5), to show His mercy encompasses all creation, and "He is ever Raḥīm to the believers" (33:43); they said this shows al-Raḥmān has the broader scope of mercy toward creation in both lives while al-Raḥīm is for the believers alone. [kathir¶1.46-1.47]
- `1:1.0` · hadith · Yet, Ibn Kathīr adds, there is a supplication "Raḥmān of this world and the next, and Raḥīm of both". [kathir¶1.47]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · The name al-Raḥmān is God's alone — 17:110; "did We ever appoint gods to be worshipped besides al-Raḥmān?" (43:45) — and when Musaylimah the Liar called himself the Raḥmān of al-Yamāmah, God exposed him: he is named "the Liar" wherever he is mentioned and became a byword for lying in town and desert; so God first named His own exclusive name, Allāh, and described it with al-Raḥmān, which no one else may use — only Musaylimah and his followers did (Ibn Kathīr). [kathir¶1.47-1.48]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · As for al-Raḥīm, God has described others with it — "kind and merciful (raḥīm) to the believers" (9:128); He has also described people with other of His names, such as "hearing and seeing" (76:2); some of God's names are used for others and some belong to Him alone — Allāh, al-Raḥmān, al-Khāliq, al-Rāziq (Ibn Kathīr). [kathir¶1.48-1.49]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · So God began the tasmiyah with His name Allāh and described Himself as al-Raḥmān, softer and more general than al-Raḥīm; the most honourable names come first, as here (Ibn Kathīr). [kathir¶1.50]
- `1:1.0` · hadith · Umm Salamah: the Prophet's recitation was slow and clear, letter by letter — "bismillāhi l-raḥmāni l-raḥīm", "al-ḥamdu li-llāhi rabbi l-ʿālamīn", "al-raḥmāni l-raḥīm", "mālik yawmi l-dīn" — and a group of scholars read so, while others joined the tasmiyah to al-ḥamd. [kathir¶1.50]
- `1:1.0` · language · Ṭabarī: al-Raḥmān is a faʿlān form from raḥima and al-Raḥīm a faʿīl form from it; the Arabs often build nouns from faʿila yafʿalu on faʿlān — ghaḍbān from ghaḍiba, sakrān, ʿaṭshān — so raḥmān from raḥima; and raḥīm, though the middle letter of faʿila is kasrah, because it is praise: the Arabs carry noun forms of praise or blame to faʿīl whether the verb's middle letter is kasrah or fatḥah — ʿālim and ʿalīm from ʿalima, qādir and qadīr from qadara — not built on their verbs, whose form would be fāʿil; were al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm on their verbs' forms they would be "al-rāḥim". [tabari¶74-75]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · Ṭabarī's objection: if both names come from raḥmah, why repeat, when one conveys the meaning of the other? His answer: it is not as you suppose — each word has a meaning the other does not convey. [tabari¶76-78]
- `1:1.0` · language · From Arabic: those who know the Arabs' dialects agree that "al-raḥmān" departs further from the verb's noun-patterns than "al-raḥīm", and agree that a noun departing further from its base pattern describes one more excellent than one built on its base, when the naming is praise or blame — so "al-Raḥmān" carries more meaning than "al-Raḥīm" (Ṭabarī). [tabari¶79]
- `1:1.0` · athar · From reports, the interpreters differ (Ṭabarī). First, al-ʿArzamī, through al-Surī ibn Yaḥyā and ʿUthmān ibn Zufar (his no. 146): "al-Raḥmān is to all creation, al-Raḥīm to the believers." The editor notes that Ibn Kathīr quotes it, that al-Surī is "truthful", that this al-ʿArzamī (Muḥammad ibn ʿUbaydillāh) is very weak — Aḥmad: "his hadith is worth nothing" — not his uncle ʿAbd al-Malik, a trustworthy Tābiʿī who died in 145 and could not have been heard by ʿUthmān ibn Zufar (d. 218); and that the spelling is ʿArzamī, the zāy after the rāʾ, the reverse in Ṭabarī's and Ibn Kathīr's printing being a slip. [tabari¶80-81]
- `1:1.0` · hadith · Report no. 147, with the same two chains as no. 140: the Prophet said, "ʿĪsā ibn Maryam said: al-Raḥmān is the Raḥmān of the next world and this one, and al-Raḥīm is the Raḥīm of the next world"; the editor: two weak chains, as explained under nos. 140 and 145. [tabari¶82]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · Ṭabarī: these two reports show a difference between naming God "Raḥmān" and naming Him "Raḥīm" and a difference in the words' meaning, though they differ about the difference — one makes it this world, the other the next. [tabari¶83]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · Which is sounder? Ṭabarī: both can be right, and no one should ask which is sounder. By "Raḥmān" God is described by mercy general to all His creation, by "Raḥīm" by mercy particular to some of them, always or in some states; so the particularity in "Raḥīm" does not change, whether in this world, the next or both. [tabari¶84]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · God specially favoured the believers in this world with gentle help — success in obedience, faith in Him and His messengers, following His command and avoiding disobedience — from which He forsook the polytheist and unbeliever; and prepared for believers in the next world lasting bliss and clear triumph; so He gave the believers more of His mercy in both worlds, besides the bounty and kindness He gave them and the unbelievers alike in this world — provision, clouds subdued to rain, plants from the earth, healthy bodies and minds, countless gifts both share (Ṭabarī). [tabari¶85]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · So our Lord is Raḥmān to all His creation in this world and the next and Raḥīm particularly to the believers in both; what covers all in this world, whereby He is Raḥmān, is His countless blessings — "if you count God's blessing you cannot count it" (14:34, 16:18). [tabari¶86]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · In the next world, what covers all of them, whereby He is Raḥmān to them, is His treating them alike in His justice and decree: He wrongs no one the weight of an atom, multiplies a good deed and gives a great reward from Himself, and pays each soul in full what it earned (Ṭabarī). [tabari¶87]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · What He gave the believers alone in this world, whereby He is Raḥīm to them — "He was ever Raḥīm to the believers" (33:43) — is the gentle help in their religion; and in the next world, the bliss and honour prepared for them alone, beyond what hopes reach (Ṭabarī). [tabari¶88-89]
- `1:1.0` · athar · The other saying: Ibn ʿAbbās (no. 148, through al-Ḍaḥḥāk): "al-Raḥmān is a faʿlān form from raḥmah, and it is Arabic speech; al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm — the gentle, kind to whom He wishes to show mercy, and the distant, severe toward whom He wishes to be harsh; so are all His names." The editor: Ibn Kathīr quotes it, the chain is weak (nos. 137, 141), and al-Durr al-Manthūr's "toward whom He wishes to double the punishment" looks like a copyist's or printer's change. [tabari¶90-91]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · Ṭabarī: Ibn ʿAbbās's interpretation shows that what makes our Lord Raḥmān is what makes Him Raḥīm, though "Raḥmān" has a meaning "Raḥīm" lacks, since he made Raḥmān the gentle toward the one He is gentle with and Raḥīm the kind toward the one He is kind to. The report from the Prophet and al-ʿArzamī is closer to the sense than this, though both agree that Raḥmān has a meaning Raḥīm lacks. [tabari¶92-93]
- `1:1.0` · athar · The third saying: ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī, through ʿImrān ibn Bakkār al-Kalāʿī, Yaḥyā ibn Ṣāliḥ and Abū l-Azhar Naṣr ibn ʿAmr al-Lakhmī of Palestine (no. 149): "It was al-Raḥmān; when al-Raḥmān was cut out (ikhtuzila) of His name it became al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm." Ṭabarī's reading: al-Raḥmān was a name of God by which none of His creatures was called; when Musaylimah the liar took it — "cut it" from His names for himself — God told that His name is "al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm", to set His name apart from the one who took His names, since none is called by both together but He; some creatures are called raḥīm or raḥmān, never both. The editor: al-Suyūṭī gives it to Ṭabarī alone; ʿAṭāʾ ibn Abī Muslim is trustworthy though some imams weaken him, and often narrates mursal from Companions; for Abū l-Azhar no biography is found beyond al-Dūlābī's note. [tabari¶94-96]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · Ṭabarī: ʿAṭāʾ's saying is not unsound in meaning: God may have specified Himself by both names together to set Himself apart from His creation, so that His servants know from their mention together that He alone is meant, besides the meaning each holds that the other lacks. [tabari¶96-97]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · Ṭabarī answers "some of the foolish" who said the Arabs did not know "al-Raḥmān" and that it was not in their language — hence the polytheists' "And what is al-Raḥmān? Shall we prostrate to what you order us?" (25:60) — as if it were impossible for polytheists to deny what they knew true, or as if he had not read "those to whom We gave the Book know him as they know their sons" (2:146), yet they denied him; and a pagan's line, "may that girl strike her half-breed; may al-Raḥmān, my Lord, cut off her right hand". The editor's notes call that claim, still repeated today, answered by Ṭabarī, and reject al-Shinqīṭī's charge that the line is forged, since the next line refutes it. [tabari¶98-100]
- `1:1.0` · language · Salāmah ibn Jandal al-Saʿdī: "You hurried against us, we against you; and whatever al-Raḥmān wills, He binds and releases" (the editor: Ibn Sallām gives his name as "Salāmah ibn Jandal ibn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān", a further reply to al-Shinqīṭī, and corrects the manuscripts' "al-Ṭuhawī" to "al-Saʿdī"). [tabari¶101-102]
- `1:1.0` · language · Others — the editor says Abū ʿUbaydah, in Majāz al-Qurʾān — said al-Raḥmān means "possessor of mercy" and al-Raḥīm "the one who shows mercy", and that the Arabs may derive two words from one with one meaning, from breadth of speech, as nadmān and nadīm, citing a line of Burj ibn Mushir al-Ṭāʾī on a drinking companion (nadmān). [tabari¶103-104]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · Ṭabarī's reply: he distinguishes the two in sense — "al-Raḥmān is the possessor of mercy, al-Raḥīm the one who shows mercy" — though leaving their meanings unexplained, then illustrates with two words of one meaning, going back to what he had made two meanings; the possessor of mercy is the one for whom mercy is established as an attribute, while the rāḥim is described as one who will show it, has shown it or is showing it, which does not show mercy is an attribute as "possessor of mercy" does; so where is "al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm", on his reading, in two words derived from one with one meaning? A saying without a firm basis shows its flaw. [tabari¶105-107]
