### LABĪD'S LINE AND "THE NAME OF PEACE"

- `1:1.0` · lesson · Ṭabarī takes up an objection from those who say the name is the named — Labīd's line to his daughters, "to the year; then the name of peace upon you two (ilā l-ḥawli thumma smu l-salāmi ʿalaykumā); and he who weeps a whole year has made his excuse" (the editor adds that he said it when his daughters wished him long life, telling them to mourn and recite what they knew). A leading scholar of the Arabs' language — the editor says Abū ʿUbaydah, in Majāz al-Qurʾān — read it as "then peace upon you", holding that "the name of peace" is "peace". [tabari¶26-31]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · Ṭabarī's answer: were that valid, one could say "I saw the name of Zayd", "I ate the name of food", "I drank the name of drink", which all the Arabs refuse; so their consensus shows the reading of Labīd's line is false, and the claim that "name" could be added and annexed to "peace" because "the name of the named is the named itself". [tabari¶32]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · Those who hold it are asked whether they allow "I ate the name of honey" for "I ate honey", as they allow "the name of peace upon you" for "peace upon you"; if they say yes they leave the Arabs' tongue and allow what all Arabs call an error; if no, they are asked the difference, and whatever they say of one binds them to the same of the other. [tabari¶33-34]
- `1:1.0` · language · Ṭabarī: Labīd's line has two senses, neither the one reported: first, "al-Salām" is one of God's names, so he meant "then hold to the name of God and His mention, and leave my remembrance and weeping over me", in the way of urging — the noun is in the nominative because the urging word was delayed; the Arabs do this when they delay the urging and put forward what is urged on, though they may also put it in the accusative; as the line "O drawer of my bucket, hold it (dūnakā)…". [tabari¶35-38]

- `1:1.0` · language · The second sense: "then my naming God upon you" — as one says of something that pleases him, "the name of God be on you", to guard it from harm: "then the name of God be upon you from evil". Ṭabarī finds the first sense the better fit; the editor says it is clearly so, for Labīd had told his daughters to mourn him from his death until the year turned, so a farewell greeting after the year makes no sense, he having left them a full year before — rather he prays for them or tells them to stop: "he who weeps a whole year has made his excuse". [tabari¶39-42]
- `1:1.0` · lesson · Ṭabarī: to the one who reads Labīd's line as "then peace upon you" it is said: do you think our two senses, or one of them, allowed, or something other? If he says no, he shows the measure of his knowledge of the turns of Arabic speech and spares his opponent debating him; if yes, he is asked for his proof that his reading is right and not what he allows as possible — "and there is no way to that". [tabari¶43-46]
