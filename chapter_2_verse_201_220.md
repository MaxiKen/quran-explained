## Verse 201

Verse 201 says: *"Yet there are others who say, "Our Lord! Grant us the good of this world and the Hereafter, and protect us from the torment of the Fire.""*

In simple terms, the verse gives the perfect prayer: some people ask their Lord for the good of this world and the good of the Hereafter, and for protection from the torment of the Fire.

The verse continues the contrast begun in 2:200. Divided into two kinds of people at the end of the hajj: one group prays only for this world, and the other group prays for both. The worldly asker says, "Our Lord! Grant us in this world" — and has no share in the Hereafter. The comprehensive asker says the prayer of this verse — the good of this world, the good of the Hereafter, and the protection from the Fire. The verse sets the two supplications side by side: the one that is limited, and the one that is complete.

"Grant us the good of this world" — the *hasanah* of the world. The commentators explain the "good" of the world as the comprehensive good: the lawful provision, the good health, the beneficial knowledge, the righteous spouse, the pious children, the safety, the contentment of the heart, the ease of the worship. Ibn Kathir notes that *hasanah* here is one of the most comprehensive words of the supplication: it gathers every good of the world without the enumeration, and the believer asks the Lord for the whole — not the wealth only, not the health only, but the good that combines the worldly good with the well-being of the faith.

"Good of the Hereafter" — the *hasanah* of the hereafter: the mercy, the forgiveness, the easy accounting, the salvation, the Paradise, the pleasure of Allah, the highest ranks, the company of the prophets, the protection from the terrors of the Day. The verse's prayer asks for both: the world without losing the hereafter, and the hereafter without forgetting the world. The believer is not the one who renounces the world, nor the one who is drowned in it; the believer asks the good of both, and the verse's prayer is the balance of the two.

"Protect us from the torment of the Fire" — the third request, the shield. The verse's *qina* — protect, shield, save — covers the whole: the prayer asks the good of the world and the good of the hereafter, and then the protection from the worst. The believer prays the positive (the good of both) and the negative (the protection from the Fire). The Fire's torment is the foil of both goods: the one who is saved from the Fire and given the good of both worlds has the complete answer.

The Sunnah confirms the verse. Al-Bukhari recorded that Anas ibn Malik said: the supplication the Prophet made most often was, "Our Lord! Grant us the good of this world and the good of the Hereafter, and protect us from the torment of the Fire" (also in Muslim). Imam Ahmad recorded the story of the man who was ill: he said he used to pray, "O Allah! Whatever punishment You have saved for me in the Hereafter, give it to me in this life." The Prophet said: "Glory be to Allah! You cannot bear it. You should have said: Our Lord! Grant us the good of this world and the good of the Hereafter, and protect us from the torment of the Fire" — and the man began reciting this supplication and was cured (also in Muslim).

In modern life, the verse is the standard of the believer's prayer and the believer's wish. The modern person prays for the specific: the job, the house, the exam, the healing, the success. The verse's prayer does not forbid the specific; it teaches the comprehensive. The believer asks the good of both worlds — the lawful success of this world, and the mercy of the next — and the shield from the Fire. The verse is the prayer that covers every need: the *hasanah* is the umbrella of all the good, the world's and the hereafter's; and the protection from the Fire is the prayer of the salvation. The believer who asks the world's good without the hereafter's good prays half a prayer; the verse's prayer is the whole.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 202

Verse 202 says: *"It is they who will receive a ˹heavenly˺ reward for the good they have done. Surely Allah is swift in reckoning."*

In simple terms, the verse gives the answer to the prayer: those who ask this way will receive their share of what they have earned, and Allah is swift in reckoning.

The verse connects directly to 2:201. The one who prayed the comprehensive prayer receives the comprehensive reward. The verse's *nasib* — the share, the portion, the allotment — the grammatical force: the reward of what they have earned. The reward is not the gift of the arbitrary; it is the reward of the good they have done — the share of the earning, the portion of the deed. The verse's *kasabu* — they have earned, they have done, they have acquired — the reward answers the work: the prayer of the good receives the share of the good.

"Heavenly reward for the good they have done" — the verse's *nasib min al-ma kasabu* — the share of what they earned. The commentators note: the verse's reward is the answer to both the du'a and the deed. The prayer of the two worlds is the good; and the share of the good is the reward — in this world (the answered du'a, the good granted) and in the hereafter (the Paradise, the mercy, the pleasure). Ibn Kathir: the ayah gives the outcome of the comprehensive prayer — they will receive their share of what they earned in both worlds.

"Surely Allah is swift in reckoning" — the verse's *sari' al-hisab* — swift in the reckoning. The phrase appears several times in the Qur'an (3:19, 13:41, etc.) with two connected senses. First: the swiftness of the Day — Allah's reckoning of all creation at the Day gathers the accounting of all, without the delay, without the confusion, without the partial. The Lord reckons the whole with the completeness that the human accounting cannot reach. Second: the swiftness of the answer — the reward of the good answers quickly; the good is not lost in the world's accounting; the One swift in reckoning repays the prayer and the deed.

The verse's close ties the prayer to the justice. The one who prays the comprehensive prayer and does the good receives the share of the good, and the reckoning is in the hands of the One who is swift in the reckoning — the One who does not miss a deed, and who pays the share completely. The verse's message: the prayer is answered; the good is rewarded; the accounting is certain, swift and just.

In modern life, the verse is the confidence of the believer. The modern person fears that the good is lost — the kindness unrecorded, the effort unnoticed, the prayer forgotten, the deed gone with the years. The verse says: no. The One swift in reckoning has recorded it; the share of the good is with the Lord; and the One who is swift in reckoning also answers the faithful prayer. The believer does the good and makes the du'a of the two worlds, and leaves the share, the portion, the accounting to the One swift in reckoning. The verse is the end of the believer's anxiety: no good is lost, no prayer is wasted, and the reckoning belongs to the One who is swift and just.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 203

Verse 203 says: *"And remember Allah during ˹these˺ appointed days. Whoever departs swiftly on the second day is not sinful, neither are those who stay behind ˹till the third—seeking additional reward˺, so long as they are mindful ˹of their Lord˺. And be mindful of Allah, and know that to Him you will ˹all˺ be gathered."*

In simple terms, the verse commands the remembrance in the appointed days and gives the choice of departure: the pilgrims are to remember Allah in these numbered days, and may leave on the second day or stay for the third without sin, so long as they are mindful of their Lord — for to Him all will be gathered.

"Remember Allah during these appointed days" — the verse's *ayyam ma'dudat* — the numbered, appointed days: the days of tashriq, the three days after the day of sacrifice (the 11th, 12th and 13th of Dhul-Hijjah). Ibn Kathir, from Ibn Abbas (via Miqsam): the appointed days are the days of tashriq — the day of the sacrifice and the three days after; and the known days (2:197) are the first ten days of Dhul-Hijjah. The verse's command: the remembrance of Allah during these days — the takbir after the prayers (Allahu Akbar), the dhikr at the throwing of the pebbles, the remembrance at the slaughtering, the remembrance in general. The days are the days of the remembrance: the Prophet said, "The days of tashriq are days of eating, drinking and remembering Allah" (Ahmad and Muslim, from Nubayshah al-Hudhali) — and the days of tashriq and the day of Arafah are the days of the Eid of the Muslims (Ahmad, from 'Uqbah ibn 'Amr).

"Whoever departs swiftly on the second day is not sinful, neither are those who stay behind till the third, seeking additional reward" — the verse's choice: the pilgrim at Mina may depart on the 12th (the second day of tashriq) after the throwing, or may stay for the 13th. Ibn Kathir (from the hadith of Ibn 'Umar/'Abd al-Rahman ibn Ya'mar): the days of Mina are three; whoever hastens in two days, no sin upon him; and whoever delays, no sin upon him. The verse's point: the choice is given, and both are lawful — but the condition is the clause that follows.

"So long as they are mindful of their Lord" — the verse's condition: *liman ittaqa* — for the one who is mindful. The choice is not the convenience without the condition: the departure is lawful and the staying is lawful, so long as the days are spent in the mindfulness — the taqwa. Maududi: the number of days at Mina is not the matter of importance; the intensity of devotion during the days is the matter. The verse's *liman ittaqa* qualifies both the hastening and the delaying: the one who leaves on the second day mindful, and the one who stays for the third mindful — both are within the verse, and both are rewarded.

"And be mindful of Allah, and know that to Him you will all be gathered" — the verse's close: the taqwa and the gathering. The hajj ends with the mindfulness and the knowledge of the return: the rites of Mina, the throwing, the remembrance, the departure, and the gathering — to the Lord. Ibn Kathir: the verse follows the departure of the people at the end of the hajj, when they return to their regions after the rituals — remember: to Him you will be gathered.

In modern life, the verse teaches the worship's extension and the worship's condition. The days of the hajj end with the days of the remembrance; the festival is not only the day of the sacrifice but the days of the dhikr, the eating, the drinking, the gratitude. And the verse's condition is the modern lesson: the choice — the early departure or the later — is judged by the mindfulness; not the number of days, but the quality of the days. The verse's message: the worship's days are the days of the remembrance, and the worship's measure is the taqwa, and the end of the worship is the departure to the One to whom all are gathered.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 204

Verse 204 says: *"There are some ˹hypocrites˺ who impress you with their views regarding worldly affairs and openly call upon Allah to witness what is in their hearts, yet they are your worst adversaries."*

In simple terms, the verse describes the hypocrite: the one whose talk about worldly matters impresses you, who calls Allah to witness what is in his heart, and who is in truth the fiercest of opponents.

The verse turns from the believers' end of the hajj (2:200–203) to the exposure of the hypocrites. The verse's *yu'jibuka qawluhu* — his speech pleases you, impresses you, charms you — the speech of the hypocrite: eloquent, smooth, persuasive, and impressive in the matters of this world. The verse's description: the one whose speech pleases, whose views impress, whose words are polished — and whose heart is the opposite. Ibn Kathir: as-Suddi said these verses were revealed about al-Akhnas ibn Shuraiq al-Thaqafi, who came to the Prophet and announced his Islam while his heart concealed otherwise; and Ibn Abbas narrated that they were revealed about some of the hypocrites who criticized Khubayb and his companions killed in the incident of Raji' — the opinion of Qatadah, Mujahid, ar-Rabi' ibn Anas and others. The verse is the general description of the hypocrite, with the examples of the revelation.

"Openly call upon Allah to witness what is in their hearts" — the verse's *yushhidu Allah* — he calls Allah to witness what is in his heart: the oath, the solemn claim, the appeal to the divine witness — "Allah knows I love you", "Allah knows I am sincere", the invocation of the Highest Witness in the defense of the inner. The verse's irony: the one who calls Allah as witness to the hidden, while the hidden is known to Allah; the appeal to the witness of the heart is the very thing the heart denies. Ibn Kathir: the ayah indicates that such persons are deviant in the tongue, evil in the deeds — their words fabricated, their belief wicked, their works immoral.

"Yet they are your worst adversaries" — the verse's *alad al-khisam* — the most quarrelsome of the opponents, the fiercest of the adversaries. The one whose speech pleases and whose oath is solemn is the most dangerous: the adversary is not the one who fights openly but the one whose tongue is the cover of the enmity. The verse's *alad* — the stubborn, the quarrelsome, the skilled in the dispute: the one who argues with the words and the oaths, who pleases with the appearance and fights with the hidden. Ibn Kathir quotes the hadith: the signs of the hypocrite are three — when he speaks he lies, when he promises he breaks it, and when he disputes he is lewd and foul (al-Bukhari and Muslim).

In modern life, the verse is the warning of the words and the warning of the adversaries. The modern person lives in the age of the speech: the media, the campaign, the profile, the interview, the eloquent presentation, the impressive views — and the verse says: the speech may please, the views may impress, and the heart may be the opposite. The verse is not the call to suspicion of everyone; the verse is the call to the discernment — the weighing of the words, the looking beyond the oaths, the recognition that the pleasing speech and the call to the divine witness can be the cover of the enmity. And for the believer: the verse is also the self-examination — the speech is the mirror of the heart; the one who guards the speech is the one whose heart is not the cover of the enmity. The verse's message: the tongue is not the proof of the heart; the deeds, the fruits, the allegiance are the proof; and the worst adversary is the one whose speech dresses the enmity.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 205

Verse 205 says: *"And when they leave ˹you˺, they strive throughout the land to spread mischief in it and destroy crops and cattle. Allah does not like mischief."*

In simple terms, the verse exposes the hypocrisy in action: when he turns away, he strives in the land spreading corruption and ruining crops and cattle — and Allah does not like corruption.

The verse completes the portrait of 2:204: the one whose speech pleases and whose heart is the enmity — and then, when he turns away, the actions begin. The verse's *tawalla* — he turned away, he left, he withdrew — the moment of the departure from the Prophet's presence, from the assembly, from the observation; the verse's *sa'a* — he strives, he exerts, he moves with the effort — the mischief in the land, the corruption, the destruction. Ibn Kathir: when the hypocrite turns away, he strives in the earth for the corruption — the mischief in the land, the destruction of the crops, the offspring. The portrait of the divided life: the pleasing speech in the assembly, the destructive striving in the earth.

"Destroy crops and cattle" — the verse's *yuhtika al-harth wa al-nasl* — destroys the crops (the harvest, the tillage) and the offspring (the cattle, the progeny, the descendants). Ibn Kathir: the hypocrite has no motive in this life but to cause mischief and to destroy the crops and the offspring — what the animals produce and what the people depend on for their livelihood; Mujahid said: if the hypocrite strives for the mischief in the land, Allah prevents the rain from falling and thus the crops and the offspring perish. Tanwir al-Miqbas (Ibn Abbas): the crops — the stacks of grain — by burning them, and the cattle — by killing them (the Islamic Studies tradition reports the incident of Akhnas passing by the Muslims' crops and livestock and later burning the crops and slaughtering the livestock). The verse's *harth* and *nasl* carry the wide meaning: the livelihood and the generations, the wealth and the people — the destruction of both.

"Allah does not like mischief" — the verse's *Allah la yuhibb al-fasad* — Allah does not like the corruption, the mischief, the ruin. The verse's close is the divine verdict on the whole: the pleasing speech, the solemn oath, the hidden enmity, the striving in the earth, the destruction of the crops and the cattle — the Lord does not like the fasad. Ibn Kathir: Allah does not like those who possess these characteristics or those who act like this. The verse's verdict: whatever the world's assessment of the eloquent destroyer, the measure of the Lord is the liking of the fasad or the absence of it — and the Lord does not like the mischief.

In modern life, the verse is the test of the public persona and the modern corruption. The modern world has the refined speakers of the destruction: eloquent on the platform, charming in the interview, and destructive in the policies, the speculation, the environmental ruin, the fraud, the sowing of the division — the crops and the cattle in the modern forms: the livelihoods destroyed, the economy ruined, the generations harmed, the environment wrecked. The verse says: the Lord does not like the mischief — whatever the eloquence. And the verse is the believer's self-examination: when I leave the assembly, when I am not observed, what do I strive for? The test of the character is the time away from the observation; the verse's message: the one who pleases with the speech and destroys in the actions is the hypocrite of every age, and the Lord knows and does not like the mischief.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 206

Verse 206 says: *"When it is said to them, "Fear Allah," pride carries them off to sin. Hell will be their proper place. What an evil place to rest!"*

In simple terms, the verse shows the hypocrite's response to advice: when told to fear Allah, pride carries him into sin; Hell is enough for him — an evil resting place.

The verse continues the description of the hypocrite through three verses (204–206): the pleasing speech, the destructive striving, and now the response to the advice. When the hypocrite is told "Fear Allah" — the call to the taqwa, the admonition, the advice — the *izzah* — the pride, the arrogance, the sense of the self-importance — seizes him and carries him off to sin. Ibn Kathir: when the hypocrite, who deviates in his speech and deeds, is advised and commanded to fear Allah, refrain from his evil deeds and adhere to the truth, he refuses and becomes angry and outraged, as he is used to doing evil. The verse's *akhadhat-hu al-'izzah bi al-ithm* — the pride took him with the sin — the pride and the sin together: the pride carries him, the sin is the destination, and the advice becomes the fuel.

"Pride carries them off to sin" — the verse's psychology: the admonition should produce the humility and the return; the hypocrite's admonition produces the pride and the persistence. The *'izzah* here is the false glory — the ego that cannot accept the admonition, the self that refuses the correction, the stubbornness that clothes itself in the honor. The verse's *bi al-ithm* — with the sin, by the sin, on account of the sin: the pride and the sin are bound; the one who is proud in the face of the admonition is the one who is carried to the sin. The verse's portrait: the advice, the pride, the sin — the three moments of the hypocrite's response.

"Hell will be their proper place" — the verse's *fahasbuhu jahannam* — so Hell suffices him, is enough for him: the fire is the sufficient punishment. Ibn Kathir: the fire is enough punishment for the hypocrite. The verse's force: the one who refuses the admonition with the arrogance does not need the additional punishment; the Hell is enough. The verse's *hasbuhu* — sufficient — the sufficiency of the punishment: the pride that carried him to the sin has carried him to the place where the fire suffices.

"What an evil place to rest!" — the verse's *wa labi'sa al-mihad* — what an evil resting place, the worst of the beds, the worst of the cradles. Ibn Kathir compares it to 22:72 — "Shall I tell you of something worse than that? The Fire of Hell which Allah has promised to those who disbelieved, and worst indeed is that destination!" The verse's *mihad* — the resting place, the bed, the abode — the irony: the one whose pride carried him seeks the rest; and the rest — the *mihad* — is the fire; the evil of the place to rest.

In modern life, the verse is the mirror of the human response to the advice. The modern person is advised — the health, the finances, the relationships, the faith — and the response is the test: the humility of the correction or the pride of the refusal. The verse's warning: the pride carries; the advice becomes the fuel of the sin; and the one who refuses with the arrogance is the one whose resting place is the evil. The verse's message: the admonition is the mercy — the one who receives it with the humility is saved by it; the one who receives it with the arrogance is carried by it to the sin. And the verse's close: the Hell is enough — the believer does not need the sign of the punishment; the warning itself is the mercy, and the humility before the admonition is the beginning of the salvation. The believer takes the admonition as the mercy, not the insult; the pride that refuses it is the pride that chooses the evil resting place.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 207

Verse 207 says: *"And there are those who would dedicate their lives to Allah's pleasure. And Allah is Ever Gracious to ˹His˺ servants."*

In simple terms, the verse contrasts the sincere believer: some people give themselves wholly in pursuit of Allah's pleasure, and Allah is gracious to His servants.

The verse completes the contrast of 2:204–206 with its opposite. The hypocrite: the pleasing speech, the hidden enmity, the destructive striving, the pride at the admonition, the Hell as the place. The sincere: the one who sells himself — the verse's *yashri nafsahu* — he sells, he dedicates, he gives his own self — seeking the pleasure of Allah. Ibn Kathir: after Allah described the evil characteristics of the hypocrites, He mentioned the good qualities of the believers. The verse's contrast: the hypocrite sells the appearance for the world, and the believer sells the whole self for the pleasure of Allah.

"Dedicate their lives to Allah's pleasure" — the verse's *yashri nafsahu ibtigha' mardat Allah* — he sells his self, seeking the pleasure of Allah. The *shira'* — the sale, the exchange, the trade: the believer's trade of the self — the life, the effort, the desires, the comfort — for the pleasure of the Lord. The verse's *ibtigha'* — seeking — the seeking is the motive: not the fame, not the compensation; the pleasure of Allah is the sought. The verse's description is the description of the complete dedication: the one who gives the self — in the jihad, in the service, in the sacrifice, in the obedience — and the exchange is the pleasure of the Lord.

"And Allah is Ever Gracious to His servants" — the verse's *Allah ra'uf bi al-'ibad* — Allah is kind, gracious, compassionate to the servants. The close is the answer to the trade: the one who gives the self for the pleasure of the Lord is met by the kindness of the Lord. Ibn Kathir: the ayah indicates that Allah is kind and merciful to His servants, and this kindness is seen in the acceptance of the sacrifice, the multiplication of the reward, the ease of the path, the love of the giver. There is a well-known tradition linked to this verse — the sacrifice of the believers who gave their lives: the verse's point — the one who sells the self for the pleasure of Allah is received by the One who is Ever Gracious; the trade is never the loss.

The tafsir notes also the specific occasion: the verse was revealed, according to some accounts, concerning the believers who sacrificed — the companions who went out, the martyrs of the cause, the one who gives the soul. The general reading: the verse describes the pattern of the believer — the self for the pleasure; and the close — the divine graciousness.

In modern life, the verse is the standard of the complete giving. The modern person calculates the exchange: what is given for what is gained. The verse's exchange: the self for the pleasure of Allah — the life for the Lord. The verse is not the call to the neglect of the life or the abandonment of the duties; the verse is the call to the dedication — the work, the family, the community, the worship, the service — with the self given to the pleasure of the Lord, not to the world's pleasure. And the believer's comfort: the One Ever Gracious to the servants rewards the dedication — the trade is the best trade. The hypocrite of the previous verses seeks the world's pleasure with the appearance and the oath; the sincere believer seeks the Lord's pleasure with the self; and the Lord's answer is the graciousness.

## Verse 208

Verse 208 says: *"O believers! Enter into Islam wholeheartedly and do not follow Satan's footsteps. Surely he is your sworn enemy."*

In simple terms, the verse commands the complete entry into Islam: the believers are commanded to enter Islam wholly, completely, without reservation, and not to follow Satan's footsteps — for he is their sworn enemy.

The verse's command: *udkhulu fi al-silm kaffah* — enter into Islam completely, wholly, entirely — *kaffah* — all together, altogether, completely, without the division. Ibn Kathir: Allah commands His servants who believe in Him and have faith in His Messenger to implement all of Islam's legislation and law, to adhere to all of its commandments, as much as they can, and to refrain from all of its prohibitions. The verse's *silm* — peace, submission, Islam — according to the consensus of the earlier commentators, from Ibn Abbas, Mujahid, Tawus, ad-Dahhak, 'Ikrimah, Qatadah, as-Suddi and Ibn Zayd, means Islam. The verse's command: the full entry, not the partial — the acceptance of the whole, not the acceptance of the part with the reservation of the rest.

"Enter into Islam wholeheartedly" — the verse's *kaffah* — the whole, the complete. Maududi: you should not divide your life into different compartments so as to follow Islam in one and discard it in the other — your thoughts, your theories, your culture, your manners, your dealings, your efforts should in every aspect of life be subordinated to Islam. The verse's context — Ibn Kathir (from Ibn Abbas, Mujahid etc.): the verse was addressed especially to the believers from the People of the Scripture — those who believed in Allah but still followed some parts of the Torah and the previous revelations, and the command: enter Islam perfectly, embrace the legislation of the religion of Muhammad entirely, and avoid abandoning any part of it. The verse's universal reading: the command to the believers of every age — the whole religion, not the selective Islam.

"And do not follow Satan's footsteps" — the verse's *la tattabi'u khutawat al-shaytan* — do not follow the footsteps, the traces, the steps of Satan. The verse's image: the footsteps — the small steps, the gradual, the stages — the shaytan does not call to the great sin at once; he calls to the step, the small concession, the first inch, and from the step to the step. Ibn Kathir compares to 24:21 — "And if not for the grace of Allah upon you and His mercy, no one among you would have ever become pure. But Allah purifies whoever He wills. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing" (the same command: do not follow the footsteps of Satan). The verse's warning: the complete entry and the refusal of the gradual steps; the partial acceptance is the footstep of the shaytan.

"Surely he is your sworn enemy" — the verse's *innahu lakum 'aduwwun mubin* — he is a clear, manifest, evident enemy. The verse's close: the warning is grounded in the enmity — not the suspicion, not the theory, but the manifest enemy who seeks the destiny of man. The verse's force: the sworn enemy calls; the complete entry is the refusal.

In modern life, the verse is the standard against the selective religion and the gradual compromise. The modern believer is tempted by the compartmentalization: the faith in one part of the life, the world in the other; the accepted doctrine with the rejected practice; the Islam of the mosque and the Islam of the market. The verse says: enter wholeheartedly — the whole. And the modern believer is tempted by the gradual — the small concession, the one step, the minor compromise — and the verse says: do not follow the footsteps. The verse's message: the complete entry is the command; the shaytan works by the steps; and the enemy is the sworn enemy — the one who is known, and the one whose steps are to be refused. The believer walks the whole path and refuses the first step.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 209

Verse 209 says: *"If you falter after receiving the clear proofs, then know that Allah is indeed Almighty, All-Wise."*

In simple terms, the verse warns of the backsliding: if you slip after the clear proofs have come to you, then know that Allah is the Almighty, the All-Wise.

The verse follows the command of 2:208: enter wholeheartedly — and the warning: do not falter after the clear proofs have come. The verse's *zalaltum* — you slipped, you faltered, you strayed, you fell into the error — the slip after the establishment of the proof: not the doubt of the seeker before the evidence, but the slip after the clarity. Ibn Kathir: "Then if you slide back after the clear signs (the Prophet and this Qur'an and Islam) have come to you, then know that Allah is All-Mighty, All-Wise." The verse's *ba'd ma ja'atkum al-bayyinat* — after the clear proofs have come to you — the *bayyinat*: the clear proofs, the manifest evidences, the signs — the revelation, the Prophet, the miracles, the truth made clear. The verse's warning: the slip after the proof is the gravest slip; the error before the evidence is the search; the slip after the evidence is the rejection.

The verse's *zalaltum* — the slippage, the deviation, the falter — and the warning of the consequence. The verse does not describe the slip as the sin beyond the forgiveness; the verse describes the posture: the one who knows the proof and then slips — the knowing slip. The verse's frame: the command to enter wholeheartedly (208), the warning against the slip (209), and the question of the next verse — the waiting for the signs (210). The passage: the full Islam, the firmness, and the alternative — the waiting for the signs that will not benefit.

"Then know that Allah is indeed Almighty, All-Wise" — the verse's *fa'lamu anna Allaha 'Aziz Hakim* — then know that Allah is Almighty, All-Wise. The close is the ground of the warning: the Almighty — the power to which the slip answers, the power that judges and enforces; the All-Wise — the wisdom that makes the proofs clear, the wisdom of the command, the wisdom of the accounting. Ibn Kathir: the verse means — know that Allah has the power over you and the wisdom in the affair; the one who slips after the proof is met by the Almighty and the All-Wise. The verse's double name: the power and the wisdom — the slip is not the accident of the random power; it is the answer to the proof, and the answer is from the Almighty and the All-Wise.

In modern life, the verse is the warning of the known truth. The modern person is not without the evidence: the proof of the Creator, the proof of the faith, the proof of the guidance, the proof in the revelation, the creation, the reason. The verse's warning: the slip after the proof. And the modern slippage takes the forms: the knowledge without the practice, the doctrine with the compromise, the faith with the rejection of the parts — the faltering after the clarity. The verse's message: the truth is clear; the firmness after the clarity is the response; and the one who knows the Almighty and the All-Wise knows also that the slip after the proof is met by the power and the wisdom — the power that judges, and the wisdom that has made the proof clear.

## Verse 210

Verse 210 says: *"Are they waiting for Allah ˹Himself˺ to come to them in the shade of clouds, along with the angels? ˹If He did˺, then the matter would be settled ˹at once˺. And to Allah ˹all˺ matters will be returned ˹for judgment˺."*

In simple terms, the verse challenges the waiting: are they waiting for Allah to come to them in the canopies of clouds with the angels, when the matter would be settled once and for all — and to Allah all matters return?

The verse answers the refusal of 2:209. The one who does not enter wholeheartedly and does not follow the proofs — what is he waiting for? The verse's rhetorical question: *hal yanzuruna* — do they wait for anything other than that Allah should come to them in the canopies of the clouds and the angels? The question exposes the waiting: the people of the rejection are not waiting for the reasonable — they are waiting for the impossible, the direct sight, the coming of the Lord with the angels — the final display, the sign that leaves no room. Ibn Kathir (from 25:25 and 89:21–22): the verse refers to the Day of Resurrection, when the heaven is rent asunder with the clouds and the angels are sent down — "And your Lord comes with the angels in rows."

The verse's *fi zulal min al-ghamam* — in the shades, the canopies of the clouds — and the angels. Ibn Kathir: the angels will descend on the shadows of the clouds, while Allah comes as He wills. The verse's mention of the clouds and the angels — the Day's manifestation, the coming of the Lord to judge the early and the late creation. The verse's point: the waiting for this is the waiting for the Day; and the Day is the judgment, not the opportunity. Ibn Abbas (Tanwir): the people of Mecca wait for nothing else than that Allah should come unto them on the Day of Judgment, but this coming is without any modality — *bila kayfa* — the coming that is affirmed in the belief, without the asking of the how. Ma'arif: the correct position — the coming of Allah belongs to the *mutashabihat*, the statements of hidden meaning; the policy of the majority of the Companions and the early scholars — believe in its truth and avoid the concern about how it happens, because it is beyond the human reason to find the reality; the creed of the Qur'an: "there is nothing like Him" (42:11).

"Then the matter would be settled at once" — the verse's *wa qudiya al-amr* — the matter would be decided, the case settled: when the Day comes and the Lord comes to judge, the matter is settled — the judgment pronounced, the reward and the punishment assigned, the door closed. Maududi: if that were done, nothing would remain to be tested and the very idea of man's success or failure would be meaningless; the whole point of man's being tested in this world is that he should believe in God without having seen Him and solely by virtue of His arguments. The verse's warning: the waiting for the sight is the waiting for the end; the belief in the unseen is the test, and the sight is the judgment.

"And to Allah all matters will be returned for judgment" — the verse's *ila Allah turja' al-umur* — to Allah all matters return, are referred: the final reference, the final return. Ibn Kathir: this is why Allah said — the reward according to the deeds, the good sees the good, the evil sees the evil. The verse's close: all matters — not only the one matter — return to the Lord.

In modern life, the verse is the rebuke of the faith-by-sight and the answer to the doubt. The modern person asks: why not the sign? why not the divine display? why not the clouds and the angels? The verse says: what you are waiting for is the end; the sight will come, and the sight will be the judgment, not the invitation. The believer's life is the faith in the unseen through the proofs — the belief in the arguments without the sight, in the guidance without the manifestation. And the one who demands the sight, the modern doubter who says "show me and I will believe", is answered by the verse: the show is the settlement; the matter decided; the door closed. The verse's message: believe now — through the proofs, in the unseen; the waiting for the sight is the waiting for the judgment, and all matters return to Allah.

## Verse 211

Verse 211 says: *"Ask the Children of Israel how many clear signs We have given them. And whoever trades Allah's favour—after receiving it—˹for disbelief˺ should know that Allah is indeed severe in punishment."*

In simple terms, the verse calls the Children of Israel to account: ask them how many clear signs they were given; whoever exchanges Allah's favour for disbelief should know that Allah is severe in punishment.

"Ask the Children of Israel how many clear signs We have given them" — the verse's *sal* — ask, demand, call to account — the addressing of the Prophet to the Children of Israel, with the rebuke and the reminder. Ibn Kathir: Allah mentioned that the Children of Israel were witnesses to many clear signs that attest to the truth of Moses regarding what he was sent with for them — the hand that became lit with light, the parting of the sea, the striking of the rock with the water flowing from it, the clouds that shaded them during the intense heat, the manna and the quails. The verse's *ayyat bayyinat* — the clear signs, the manifest proofs, the unmistakable evidences — the signs that were given with the abundance. The verse's question expects the answer: how many? — and the answer is the reminder of the abundance; and the abundance was met with the exchange.

"And whoever trades Allah's favour — after receiving it — for disbelief" — the verse's *yubaddil ni'mat Allah* — he changes, he exchanges, he trades the favor of Allah — after it has come to him. Ibn Kathir: so many among them changed Allah's favor by preferring disbelief to faith and by ignoring Allah's favors. The verse's *tabdil* — the change, the exchange, the substitution — the favor received and the favor traded: the favor that was meant to bind the gratitude was exchanged for the disbelief; the signs that were meant to confirm the faith were met with the change. The verse's parallel — 14:28 — "Have you not seen those who have changed the blessings of Allah into disbelief and caused their people to settle in the home of destruction?" — the Quraysh of the parallel: the favor of the Prophet and the Qur'an, exchanged for the disbelief.

"Should know that Allah is indeed severe in punishment" — the verse's *fa'inna Allaha shadid al-'iqab* — then indeed Allah is severe in punishment. The verse's close: the exchange is not the indifferent matter; the favor exchanged for the disbelief is answered by the severe punishment. The verse's frame: the abundance of the signs, the receiving of the favor, the exchange for the disbelief, and the severe punishment — the pattern of the history: the people who were given the most and exchanged the most; and the punishment that follows the exchange.

In modern life, the verse is the accounting of the favor. The modern person is given the favor: the health, the wealth, the knowledge, the opportunity, the faith itself. The verse asks: what is done with the favor? The verse's *tabdil* — the exchange — the modern forms: the blessing of the health wasted in the harm; the blessing of the wealth turned to the oppression; the blessing of the knowledge turned to the falsehood; the blessing of the faith exchanged for the convenience. The verse's message: the favor is the trust; the receiving is the responsibility; and the exchange — the blessing traded for the disbelief, the favor met with the rejection — is answered by the severity of the Lord. The believer counts the favors as the signs — and the signs of the Lord call to the gratitude, not the exchange.

## Verse 212

Verse 212 says: *"The life of this world has been made appealing to the disbelievers, and they mock the believers. Those who are mindful ˹of Allah˺ will rank above them on the Day of Judgment. And Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit."*

In simple terms, the verse explains the worldly display and the true ranking: the life of this world is made attractive to the disbelievers, and they mock the believers, yet the mindful will rank above them on the Day of Judgment — and Allah gives provision without measure.

"The life of this world has been made appealing to the disbelievers" — the verse's *zuyyina* — it is beautified, adorned, made appealing — the life of this world for the disbelievers. The verse's *zuyyina* is the divine beautification tested: the world is adorned — the disbelievers have the appearance of the world's goods, the success, the elegance, the power; and the believers have the appearance of the lesser. Ibn Kathir (and the parallel of 3:196 — the disbelievers walking freely in the land) — the world's adornment for the disbelievers is the test of the believers and the measure of the hereafter. The verse's frame: the taqwa is not measured by the world's goods; the world's adornment is given to the disbelievers as the trial, and the believers are not to be deceived by the adornment.

"And they mock the believers" — the verse's *yaskharun* — they mock, they deride, they ridicule — the believers: the poor, the weak, the emigrants, the ones without the world's goods. The mockery is part of the test: the world's adornment produces the mockery — the believers mocked for the faith, for the simplicity, for the patience, for the trust in the unseen. The verse's frame: the disbeliever mocks the believer while the believer's rank is the hereafter; the mocking does not change the rank.

"Those who are mindful of Allah will rank above them on the Day of Judgment" — the verse's *alladhina ittaqaw fawqahum yawm al-qiyamah* — those who are mindful will be above them on the Day of Judgment. The verse's reversal: the mockers in the world are the ones below on the Day; the mocked in the world are the ones above. Ibn Kathir: the disbelievers appear to be the winners in this world, but the believers are above them on the Day of Judgment. The verse's *fawq* — above — the ranking: the rank of the taqwa, the rank of the Day, the rank that the world's adornment cannot buy.

"And Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit" — the verse's *Allah yarzuqu man yasha' bi-ghayr hisab* — Allah provides for whoever He wills without reckoning, without count, without limit. The close: the provision is not by the world's measure; the Lord provides without limit. Ibn Kathir: in this world, the provisions are given to the believers and the disbelievers; the difference is the account of the hereafter. The verse's *bi-ghayr hisab* — without limit, without account — the provision of the Lord — and the believer's trust: the Lord's provision is not the measure of the world's adornment; and the Day's ranking is the measure of the taqwa.

In modern life, the verse answers the modern anxiety of the world's success. The modern person compares: the wealth, the success, the appearance — and the believer is mocked: the believer mocked for the faith, for the trust, for the limits, for the worship. The verse says: the world is adorned, the mockers are the world's measure, and the Day reverses the measure — the mindful are above. The verse is the believer's confidence: the world's goods are not the proof of the worth; the mockery is not the verdict; the provision is from the Lord without the limit; and the ranking is on the Day, by the taqwa.

## Verse 213

Verse 213 says: *"Humanity had once been one community ˹of believers before they lost faith˺. Then Allah raised prophets as deliverers of good news and as warners, and revealed to them the Scriptures in truth to judge among people regarding their disputes. And no one disputed the Scriptures except the very people who received them after clear proofs had come to them—out of jealousy. Then Allah, by His grace, has guided the believers to the truth regarding those disputes. And Allah guides whoever He wills to the Straight Path."*

In simple terms, the verse explains the history of the guidance and the disputes: humanity was one community; Allah sent prophets with good news and warnings and revealed the Scripture in truth to judge the disputes; only those who received it differed after the clear proofs, out of jealousy; then Allah guided the believers to the truth of those disputes and guides whom He wills to the Straight Path.

"Humanity had once been one community of believers before they lost faith" — the verse's *kana al-nas ummah wahidah* — mankind was one community. Ibn Kathir (from Qatadah): they all had the guidance, then they disputed and Allah sent the prophets. Maududi: the Qur'an proclaims that man began his life in the full light of the Truth — God revealed this Truth to the very first man He created — and the human race remained on the Right Way for some time and lived as one community. The verse's *ummah wahidah* — the one community — the original unity of the humanity on the guidance — and then the difference, the division, the dispute. The verse's history: the one community, the division, the prophets.

"Then Allah raised prophets as deliverers of good news and as warners" — the verse's *ba'ath al-nabiyyin mubashshirin wa mundhirin* — He sent the prophets, the bearers of the glad tidings and the warners. Maududi: the prophets were not sent to found separate religions in their own names and bring new religious communities into existence; they were sent to restore the one truth, to deliver the good news, to warn. The verse's *mubashshirin* — the glad tidings of the Paradise for the believers; *mundhirin* — the warnings of the Fire for the disbelievers. The prophets: the restorers, the deliverers, the warners of the one message.

"And revealed to them the Scriptures in truth to judge among people regarding their disputes" — the verse's *anzal ma'ahum al-kitab bi al-haqq* — He revealed with them the Book in truth, to judge among the people in what they differed. The Scripture's function: the judgment — the dispute resolved by the divine word. Ibn Kathir: the book was revealed to judge between the people concerning the matters of the difference. The verse's *bi al-haqq* — in truth — the revelation is the truth, and the judgment is the truth's judgment.

"And no one disputed the Scriptures except the very people who received them after clear proofs had come to them — out of jealousy" — the verse's *wa ma ikhtalafa fihi illa alladhina utuhu min ba'd ma ja'at-hum al-bayyinat baghyan baynahum* — and none differed concerning it except those who were given it, after the clear proofs had come to them, out of transgression, jealousy, envy among themselves. Ibn Kathir: the difference is not from the obscurity of the Scripture; the difference is from the envy — the *baghy* — the transgression of the heart, the jealousy of the recipients. The verse's diagnosis: the disagreement of the People of the Book was not the disagreement of the reason; it was the disagreement of the envy — the clear proofs came, and the envy divided.

"Then Allah, by His grace, has guided the believers to the truth regarding those disputes" — the verse's *fahada Allah alladhina amanu lima ikhtalafu fihi min al-haqq bi-idhnihi* — then Allah guided those who believed to the truth of what they differed in, by His permission, by His leave. The verse's answer: the disputes are resolved for the believers — not by the believers' own reasoning only, but by the guidance of Allah. The believers are guided to the truth of the disputes — the Qur'an, the Prophet, the guidance of the believers' community. And Allah guides whoever He wills to the Straight Path — the verse's close: the guidance is the Lord's.

In modern life, the verse is the answer to the sectarianism and the frame of the guidance. The modern world is divided: the religions divided, the sects divided, the interpretations divided, the believers divided. The verse says: the original humanity was one; the divisions came — and the divisions of the recipients, the envy; and the guidance is by the grace of the Lord — the believers guided to the truth of the disputes by the permission of the Lord. The verse is not the claim that every dispute is settled by the human opinion; the verse is the claim that the truth is with the Lord, and the believers are guided by the Lord's leave. The modern believer's frame: the one truth, the many divisions, the envy behind the divisions, and the guidance of the Lord for the believers to the Straight Path. The verse's message: the truth is not the invention; it is the guidance; and the guidance is the Lord's.

## Verse 214

Verse 214 says: *"Do you think you will be admitted into Paradise without being tested like those before you? They were afflicted with suffering and adversity and were so ˹violently˺ shaken that ˹even˺ the Messenger and the believers with him cried out, "When will Allah's help come?" Indeed, Allah's help is ˹always˺ near."*

In simple terms, the verse answers the assumption of the easy paradise: do you think you will enter Paradise without being tested as those before you were — afflicted with misery and hardship and shaken until the Messenger and those with him asked when Allah's help would come — and indeed Allah's help is near.

"Do you think you will be admitted into Paradise without being tested like those before you?" — the verse's *am hasibtum an tadkhulu al-jannah* — or do you think you will enter Paradise — the rhetorical rebuke: the assumption of the entry without the test. Ibn Kathir: victory only comes after succeeding in the trials. The verse's pattern: Paradise is not the automatic entry; the entry passes through the test. The verse's *lamma ya'tikum* — before it has come to you — while the trial has not yet come: the trial is the path; the test is the condition; the pattern of the believers before is the pattern of the test.

"They were afflicted with suffering and adversity and were so violently shaken" — the verse's *massat-hum al-ba'sa' wa al-darra' wa zulzilu* — they were touched by the poverty and the hardship, and they were shaken, convulsed, rocked. Ibn Kathir: the *ba'sa'* — the poverty, the destitution, the severe need; the *darra'* — the illness, the harm, the adversity; the *zulzilu* — the shaking, the convulsion, the violent trial — the fear of the enemy, the testing, the tremendous trial. The verse's list: the economic trial, the physical trial, the psychological trial — the complete shaking. The verse's *zulzilat* — the earthquake imagery: the foundation shaken, the stability tested, the faithfulness proven.

"That even the Messenger and the believers with him cried out, 'When will Allah's help come?'" — the verse's *hatta yaqul al-rasul wa alladhina amanu ma'ahu mata nasr Allah* — until the Messenger and those who believed with him said, when is the help of Allah? Ibn Kathir (from the hadith of Khabbab ibn al-Aratt): the believers came to the Prophet (in Mecca) and said: "O Messenger of Allah! Why do you not invoke Allah to support us? Why do you not supplicate to Allah for us?" — and the Prophet described the believers before: a man would be seized and a pit dug for him, and he would be sawed into halves from his head, and his flesh torn from his bones with an iron comb, and this would not turn him from his faith; and the Prophet said that Allah would bring the matter to its consummation, but the companions were too much in a hurry (al-Bukhari). And the hadith of the man: "Were you asking or supplicating to Allah about something?" — the teaching of the comprehensive du'a (2:201). The verse's point: the cry "when is the help of Allah?" is not the cry of the doubt; it is the cry of the earnest desire — the believers' desire for the early help, the longing for the deliverance. Ma'arif: the prophets and their followers reached the point of suffering where they cried out — not because of any doubt, but in the background of the promised help for which the time was left undetermined; the call was the earnest supplication; and Allah favors the earnest supplication of His servants.

"Indeed, Allah's help is always near" — the verse's *ala inna nasr Allah qarib* — indeed, the help of Allah is near. Ibn Kathir connects it to 94:5–6 — "So surely with hardship comes ease. Indeed, with hardship comes ease" — just as there is hardship, its equal of relief will soon arrive. The verse's close: the nearness of the help — not the delay of the help, but the nearness. And the nearness is known after the test: the test's shaking is the preparation of the nearness.

In modern life, the verse is the answer to the trial and the correction of the assumption. The modern believer assumes: the faith is the insurance — the entry, the ease, the success without the test. The verse says: the test is the path. And the modern believer suffers: the poverty, the hardship, the shaking, the persecution — and the temptation: "where is the help?" The verse says: the help is near; the shaking is the preparation; the pattern is the pattern of the believers before. And the verse's modern message: the trial does not mean the abandonment; the trial is the entry's gate; and the believer's cry — "when is the help?" — is the cry of the longing, and the answer — "the help is near" — is the certainty.

## Verse 215

Verse 215 says: *"They ask you ˹O Prophet in˺ what ˹way˺ they should donate. Say, "Whatever donations you give are for parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, and ˹needy˺ travellers. Whatever good you do is certainly well known to Allah.""*

In simple terms, the verse answers the question of the donation: they ask what they should give, and the answer is the categories — parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, and the needy traveller — for whatever good you do is known to Allah.

"They ask you, O Prophet, in what way they should donate" — the verse's *yas'alunaka madha yunfiqun* — they ask you what they should spend, what they should donate. Ibn Kathir (from Muqatil ibn Hayyan): the ayah was revealed about the voluntary charity; Ibn Abbas and Mujahid: they ask how they should spend, in what way they should give. The verse's answer: not the amount first, but the direction — the recipients. The verse's context: the companions asked about the spending — and the answer: the giving has the order of the nearness.

"Whatever donations you give are for parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, and needy travellers" — the verse's list: the parents, the near relatives, the orphans, the poor (the *masakin* — the needy, the destitute), and the wayfarer (the *ibn al-sabil* — the traveler in need, the one cut off from his means on the road). The verse's order: the parents first, then the relatives, then the orphans, the poor, the traveler. Ibn Kathir quotes the hadith: the one who most deserves the generosity is — "your mother, your father, your sister, your brother, then the nearest, the nearest" (the hadith recorded from the Prophet, with the order of the priority). The verse's *khayr* — the good — the charity of the good: not the charity of the show, but the good — the direction of the nearness: the parents, the family, the orphans, the poor, the traveler.

The verse's modern and timeless message: the charity begins at home, in the nearness — the parents who have given, the relatives, the orphans, the poor, the traveler. The verse does not forbid the far charity; the verse establishes the priority. The giving's order: the nearness as the fairness of the giving — the parents, the kin, the orphans, the poor, the wayfarer. Ibn Kathir: the verse's order — "spend it on these categories or areas." The verse's frame: the donation is not the random; the donation is the ordered, the near, the deserving.

The close: the good is known to Allah — the known, the recorded, the rewarded. The verse's confidence: the hidden charity, the quiet donation, the unregistered giving — known to the Lord. The verse's frame: the donation's direction (the nearness) and the donation's knowledge (the Lord's knowledge) — the believer gives in the right direction and leaves the accounting to the One who knows.

In modern life, the verse is the guide of the giving. The modern person asks: how much? where? what? — and the verse says: the direction first — the parents and the family, the orphans, the poor, the traveler. The modern giving is often the outward: the public donation, the recognized charity, the distant cause; and the verse's priority: the nearness — the parents, the relatives, the poor around. The verse's message: the charity begins at the nearest deserving; the good is the good of the direction and the intention; and the One who knows the good is the One who records the deed — whether given in the public or given silently, given to the parents or given to the traveler. The believer gives the good in the order of the nearness, and leaves the accounting to the All-Knowing.

## Verse 216

Verse 216 says: *"Fighting has been made obligatory upon you ˹believers˺, though you dislike it. Perhaps you dislike something which is good for you and like something which is bad for you. Allah knows and you do not know."*

In simple terms, the verse establishes the obligation and the wisdom: fighting is prescribed though it is hated; perhaps you dislike something good and like something bad, for Allah knows and you do not know.

"Fighting has been made obligatory upon you believers" — the verse's *kutiba 'alaykum al-qital* — fighting is prescribed, written, decreed upon you. Ibn Kathir: in this ayah, Allah made it obligatory for the Muslims to fight in jihad against the evil of the enemy who transgresses against Islam. The verse's *kutiba* — the prescribed — the same verb used for the fasting (2:183), the qisas (2:178), the will (2:180): the divine decree of the obligation. The verse's context: the command of the fighting came after the period of the patience in Mecca — the Muslims had been persecuted, they had emigrated, the persecution had persisted — and now the command: fighting is prescribed. The verse's *qital* — the combat, the fighting — in the way of Allah: the defense of the faith, the removal of the persecution, the establishment of the worship.

"Though you dislike it" — the verse's *wa huwa kurhun lakum* — while it is hateful to you, it is heavy upon you. Ibn Kathir: fighting is difficult and heavy on your hearts — it includes being killed, wounded, striving against the enemies and enduring the hardship of the travel. The verse's honesty: the command is not the command of the desire; the command is the command of the obligation — the human nature dislikes the danger, the hardship, the harm; and the divine command is not the command of the nature's preference. The verse's *kurh* — the dislike, the reluctance — the natural aversion to the death, the violence, the difficulty.

"Perhaps you dislike something which is good for you and like something which is bad for you" — the verse's general principle: *'asa an takrahu shay'an wa huwa khayrun lakum* — it may be that you dislike a thing and it is good for you; and it may be that you love a thing and it is bad for you. Ibn Kathir: fighting is followed by victory, dominance over the enemy, taking over their lands, money and offspring; and refraining from the jihad might lead to the enemy taking over the land and the government; and the negative: one might covet something, yet in reality it is not good or beneficial. The verse's principle: the human perception is not the divine measure; the dislike may be the good; the love may be the harm. The principle of the divine wisdom: the obligations are not the pleasures; the prohibitions are not the harms; the Lord's decree is not the human desire.

"Allah knows and you do not know" — the verse's *Allah ya'lam wa antum la ta'lamun* — Allah knows and you do not know. Ibn Kathir: He has better knowledge than you of how things will turn out to be in the end, and of what benefits you in this earthly life and the Hereafter. The verse's close: the ground of the wisdom — the one who knows the outcome prescribes; the one who does not know entrusts. The verse's command and the verse's trust: the obligation is accepted on the knowledge of the One who knows; the believer obeys the prescription of the All-Knowing.

In modern life, the verse is the standard of the trust in the divine wisdom. The modern person faces the prescription and the preference: the obligation, the hardship, the disliked path — and the temptation: the avoidance. The verse says: perhaps the disliked is the good; perhaps the liked is the harm; the knowledge is the Lord's. The verse teaches the believer's submission to the divine prescription: not the blind obedience, but the trusting obedience — the acceptance of the command of the One who knows the outcomes. And the verse's modern lesson: the believer's judgment of the "good" and the "bad" is the limited judgment; the believer's trust: the Lord knows; and the believer follows the knowledge of the One who knows.

## Verse 217

Verse 217 says: *"They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about fighting in the sacred months. Say, "Fighting during these months is a great sin. But hindering ˹others˺ from the Path of Allah, rejecting Him, and expelling the worshippers from the Sacred Mosque is ˹a˺ greater ˹sin˺ in the sight of Allah. For persecution is far worse than killing. And they will not stop fighting you until they turn you away from your faith—if they can. And whoever among you renounces their own faith and dies a disbeliever, their deeds will become void in this life and in the Hereafter. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.""*

In simple terms, the verse answers the question of the sacred month and sets the order of sins: fighting in those months is a great sin, but hindering people from the path of Allah, rejecting Him, and expelling the worshippers from the Sacred Mosque is greater; persecution is worse than killing; the enemies will not stop until they turn you from your faith — and whoever dies as a disbeliever has null deeds and the Fire forever.

"They ask you, O Prophet, about fighting in the sacred months" — the verse's context: the incident of Nakhlah. Ibn Kathir: the Prophet sent the first raiding party under 'Abdullah ibn Jahsh; the party found Ibn al-Hadrami (one of the disbelievers of Quraysh) and killed him, not knowing (or thinking) the day was in Rajab — the sacred month. The polytheists said to the Muslims: "You have committed murder in the sacred month." The companions conferred and were troubled. The verse answers: fighting in the sacred month is a great sin. The four sacred months — Rajab of Mudar, Dhul-Qa'dah, Dhul-Hijjah, Muharram (the Prophet's hadith in the last hajj: the four months, three consecutive and the fourth is Rajab of Mudar); Ma'arif: the verses and the hadith prove that fighting is haram in the four months; the prohibition of the initiation is forever, with the exception of the self-defense when the disbelievers themselves start the fighting (al-Jassas reported from Jabir: the Prophet did not fight in any of the sacred months until the fighting was started by the disbelievers).

"But hindering others from the Path of Allah, rejecting Him, and expelling the worshippers from the Sacred Mosque is a greater sin in the sight of Allah" — the verse's ranking: the sin of the sacred-month fighting is great, but the sins of Quraysh are greater. Ibn Kathir: if you killed during the sacred month, they have hindered you from the path of Allah and disbelieved in it; they also prevented you from entering the Sacred Mosque, and expelled you from it, while you are its people — the greater transgression with Allah than the killing you did among them. The verse's *sadd* — the hindering from the path; the *kufr* — the rejection; the *ikhraj* — the expulsion — the greater sins: the obstruction of the guidance, the disbelief, the expulsion of the worshippers.

"For persecution is far worse than killing" — the verse's *al-fitnah akbar min al-qatl* — the fitnah is greater than killing. Ibn Kathir: the fitnah — the persecution, the dispute, the trial — means trying to force the Muslims to revert from their religion and re-embrace the disbelief after they had believed — worse with Allah than killing. The verse's scale: the killing of the one man in the sacred month is not the greatest evil; the oppression, the persecution, the forced apostasy — the *fitnah* — is the greater. The verse's principle of the moral scale: the offense of the killing is measured against the offense of the persecution, and the persecution outweighs.

"And they will not stop fighting you until they turn you away from your faith — if they can" — the verse's warning: the enemies' aim is not the land, the wealth, the border; the aim is the faith — the turning away of the believers from the religion. Ibn Kathir: and they will never cease fighting you until they turn you back from your religion, if they are able. The verse's frame: the war against the Muslims is the war against the faith; and the aim is the *fitnah* — the turning away.

"And whoever among you renounces their own faith and dies a disbeliever, their deeds will become void in this life and in the Hereafter" — the verse's warning: the apostasy — the renunciation of the faith — and the death upon the disbelief — the deeds void. Ibn Kathir: the one who reverts from the religion after the clear signs — the deeds' worthlessness in this life and the hereafter; the one who dies as the disbeliever — the fire, the eternal. The verse's *habitat* — void, wasted, nullified — the deeds of the life: the good, the worship, the charity — nullified by the death upon the disbelief; the verse's close: the residents of the Fire, forever.

In modern life, the verse is the scale of the sins and the warning of the apostasy. The modern world measures the offense by the violence alone; the verse's scale: the persecution — the obstruction of the path, the rejection of the truth, the expulsion of the worshippers, the forced turning away — is the greater; the fitnah — the religious persecution — is worse than the killing. And the modern warning: the renunciation of the faith — the death upon the disbelief — the deeds void; the modern believer examines the faith's firmness under the pressure: the persecution, the mockery, the pressure to abandon — and the verse says: the Lord's scale weighs the persecution heavier, and warns the believer against the exchange. The verse's message: the faith is the very thing the enemies aim at, and the faithful are those who do not trade it.

## Verse 218

Verse 218 says: *"Surely those who have believed, emigrated, and struggled in the Way of Allah—they can hope for Allah's mercy. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful."*

In simple terms, the verse gives the hope of the believers who acted: those who believed, emigrated and struggled in the way of Allah can hope for Allah's mercy — and Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

The verse follows the warning of 2:217. The apostate's deeds are void (217) — and the believers who have believed, emigrated and struggled have the hope of the mercy (218). Ibn Kathir: the verse answered the companions of 'Abdullah ibn Jahsh who feared they had no reward after the sin of the sacred-month killing — and the verse: the believers, the emigrants, the mujahidin — they hope for the mercy of Allah. The verse's *alladhina amanu* — those who believed; *hajaru* — emigrated; *jahadu* — struggled, strove, fought — the three marks: the faith, the hijrah, the jihad — the faith in the heart, the migration from the persecution, the struggle in the way.

"Emigrated" — the verse's *hajaru* — the hijrah — the emigration from the land of the persecution to the land of the safety, the faith: the believers of Mecca who left their homes, their property, their families for the sake of the faith. Ibn Kathir (via the tradition): the emigrants to Abyssinia, the emigrants to Medina — the people who left the homeland for the religion. The verse's *hajaru* — the leaving for the Lord: the migration as the act of the faith — the one who left the comfort for the obedience, the home for the worship, the persecution for the freedom of the faith.

"And struggled in the Way of Allah" — the verse's *jahadu fi sabil Allah* — the jihad — the struggle in the way of Allah: the exerting of the utmost — the wealth, the self, the life — in the path of the Lord. Ibn Kathir: the verse's *jahadu* — the striving hard — fighting the enemy, spending, enduring, serving — the jihad in the broad sense. The verse's *fi sabil Allah* — in the way — the direction: the struggle for the Lord's cause, not the tribal, the national, the material. The verse's hope: *yarjun rahmat Allah* — they hope for the mercy — the hope of the believers who have the faith, the migration and the struggle.

"And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful" — the verse's close: the two names — the All-Forgiving, the Most Merciful — the mercy of the believers' hope. Ibn Kathir: Allah has greatly elevated their hopes of gaining what they had wished for. The verse's *ghafur* — the forgiveness of the errors (the Nakhlah incident, the mistakes, the sins); *rahim* — the mercy of the reward — the faith, the hijrah, the jihad, and the hope of the mercy.

In modern life, the verse is the hope of the believers in action. The modern believer — who believes, migrates, and struggles: the one who leaves the persecution for the freedom of the faith; the one who moves from the hostile environment for the practice; the one who struggles — in the work, in the service, in the defense of the truth — in the way of the Lord. The verse: they can hope for the mercy. The modern believer's hope: the faith and the action — the belief, the leaving, the struggling — the hope of the mercy, the forgiveness, the reward. The verse's message: the apostate's deeds are void (217), and the doer's hope is the mercy; the believer's acts — the belief, the migration, the struggle — are the acts that the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful answers with the hope.

## Verse 219

Verse 219 says: *"They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about intoxicants and gambling. Say, "There is great evil in both, as well as some benefit for people—but the evil outweighs the benefit." They ˹also˺ ask you what they should donate. Say, "Whatever you can spare." This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you ˹believers˺, so perhaps you may reflect"*

In simple terms, the verse answers the question of intoxicants and gambling and the second question of spending: there is great sin in both, and some benefit, but the evil outweighs the benefit; and they should give what they can spare — and the signs are made clear so that they may reflect.

"They ask you, O Prophet, about intoxicants and gambling" — the verse's *khamr* — wine, the intoxicants; *maysir* — the gambling, the game of the arrows, the gaming of the chance. Ibn Kathir: the ayah was the beginning of the process of prohibiting the khamr, not explicitly but implying the meaning. The verse's context: the companions asked about the wine and the gaming — the two practices of the Jahiliyyah: the wine with the pleasure and the intoxication; the *maysir* — the game of the gambling — the money and the livestock wagered on the arrow game. The verse's answer: the weighing — the evil and the benefit.

"There is great evil in both, as well as some benefit for people — but the evil outweighs the benefit" — the verse's *fihima ithm kabir wa manafi' lil-nas wa ithmuhuma akbar min naf'ihima* — in them is a great sin and benefits for the people, but the sin of them is greater than the benefit. Ibn Kathir: the harm of the khamr and the gambling affects the religion; as for the benefit, it is material — benefit for the body, digesting the food, the joyous sensation, and the financial benefit from the sale; and the gambling's earnings one uses to spend on his family; yet these benefits are outweighed by the clear harm that they cause which affects the mind and the religion. The verse's weighing: the benefit is the material, the partial; the sin is the great — the harm of the mind, the religion, the family, the society — and the evil outweighs.

The verse's gradual prohibition: Ibn Kathir — the ayah of 2:219, then 4:43 ("O you who believe! Do not approach prayer while intoxicated..."), then 5:90–91 ("O you who believe! Intoxicants, gambling, idols, and divining arrows are an abomination of Satan's handiwork — so avoid them... Satan only wants to cause enmity and hatred among you through them and to hinder you from the remembrance of Allah and prayer"), the final prohibition. The verse's *khamr* includes everything that intoxicates the mind — Umar's statement recorded by Ibn Kathir. The companions could not fully give up the wine after 2:219 — the process: the warning, the prohibition during the prayer, the final prohibition.

"They also ask you what they should donate. Say, 'Whatever you can spare'" — the verse's *al-'afw* — the surplus, the extra, what is beyond the needs. Ibn Kathir (from al-Hakam/Miqsam/Ibn Abbas): whatever you can spare above the needs of your family. The verse's answer: the donation is the surplus — the giving of what is beyond the need, not the giving of the necessity; the balanced giving, the moderate charity — the one who spends the surplus is the one who neither hoards nor ruins himself.

"This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you believers, so perhaps you may reflect" — the verse's close: the clarity of the signs and the purpose — the reflection. The verse's *la'allakum tatafakkarun* — so that you may reflect, think, consider — the reflection on the weighing: the evil and the benefit, the world and the hereafter (continuing into 220). The verse's frame: the clear revelations for the reflection; the believer reflects on the weighing of the divine law.

In modern life, the verse is the weighing of the goods and the evils. The modern person weighs: the pleasure vs the cost; the benefit vs the danger; the intoxicants, the gambling, the addictions, the vices — the modern forms: the alcohol, the drugs, the gambling of the casinos and the betting, the spending of the wealth. The verse's weighing: the evil outweighs; the benefit is the material, the partial; the sin is the great — the harm of the mind, the health, the family, the faith, the society. The verse's wisdom: the gradualism — the divine law teaches in stages: the warning, the limitation, the prohibition; the modern believer reflects on the weighing — the Lord's scale is not the benefit's scale; and the spending: the surplus — the giving beyond the need — the balanced charity. The verse's message: the believer weighs with the Lord's scale, reflects on the clear signs, and gives of the surplus.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 220

Verse 220 says: *"upon this world and the Hereafter. And they ask you concerning orphans. Say, "Improving their condition is best. And if you partner with them, they are bonded with you ˹in faith˺. And Allah knows who intends harm and who intends good. Had Allah willed, He could have made it difficult for you. Surely Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.""*

In simple terms, the verse completes the reflection and answers the question of the orphans: improving their condition is best; if you mix with them, they are your brothers in faith; Allah knows who intends harm and who intends good; and had He willed He could have made it hard for you — He is the Almighty, the All-Wise.

"Upon this world and the Hereafter" — the continued sentence from 219: the reflections — the intoxicants, the gambling, the spending, the orphans — for the reflection upon this world and the hereafter. Ibn Kathir: the verse connects the whole — the clear signs for the thought about the world and the hereafter; the weighing of the divine law is the weighing of both. The believer's reflection: the matters are not only the world's matters; they are the matters of the world and the hereafter — the law's aim: the good of both.

"And they ask you concerning orphans" — the verse's context: the companions of the revelation — when the verse of the inheritance and the orphan's property was revealed, the believers were troubled: the orphan's property was in the same houses, the same food, the same affairs; and the companions separated their food and need from the orphans — the burden of the separation. Ibn Kathir: the people asked — and the answer: the *islah* — the betterment, the improvement, the making right — is best; and the mixing is permitted if the brothers.

"Improving their condition is best" — the verse's *islah lahum khayr* — improving for them is better — the verse's answer: the improvement of the orphan's condition — the property, the upbringing, the education, the welfare — is the best. Ibn Kathir: the best thing is to work honestly in their property, and if you mix your affairs with theirs, then they are your brothers; and 6:152: "And do not approach the orphan's property except in the best way." The verse's *islah* — the making right — the trustee's duty: the development, the care, the protection — the orphan's property improved, not consumed.

"And if you partner with them, they are bonded with you in faith" — the verse's *in tukhalituhum fa-ikhwanukum* — if you mix your affairs with theirs, they are your brothers — the brothers in the faith. Ibn Kathir: they joined their food and drink with the food and drink of the orphans (after the verse, the companions shared); the hadith recorded by Abu Dawud, an-Nasa'i and al-Hakim: the Prophet's companions mixed their food with the orphans', and the question was answered: they are your brothers. The verse's *akhawan* — the brothers — the bond of the faith — the orphan is the brother of the trustee; the mixing is the brotherhood, the care, the partnership of the faith.

"And Allah knows who intends harm and who intends good" — the verse's *Allah ya'lam al-mufsid min al-muslih* — Allah knows the one who corrupts from the one who improves, the one who intends the harm from the one who intends the good. The verse's close: the trustee's intention is known to the Lord; the mixing — permitted — but the intention is judged: the one who mixes to consume the orphan's property, and the one who mixes to improve — known to the Lord.

"Had Allah willed, He could have made it difficult for you. Surely Allah is Almighty, All-Wise" — the verse's close: the lightness of the command — had the Lord willed, the law would have been the hardship; the law is the ease, the permission of the mixing, the allowance of the partnership with the protection. Ibn Kathir: He made it easy for you and allowed you to mix your affairs with the orphans' affairs in a way that is better — the Almighty and the All-Wise — the power and the wisdom: the law's ease is the wisdom's mercy, and the Almighty's decree is the All-Wise's mercy.

In modern life, the verse is the charter of the orphan's care and the trustee's intention. The modern world has the orphans: the children of the war, the poverty, the family breakdown; and the modern trustee — the guardian, the charity, the institution, the state — faces the question: how to handle the orphan's property, the mixing, the management. The verse says: improving is best; the mixing is permitted — the brothers; and the intention is known — the corruptor from the improver. The verse's modern lesson: the orphan's care is the improvement, not the consumption; the mixing with the orphan is the partnership of the faith and the brotherhood; and the Lord knows the intention. The verse's message: the Almighty and the All-Wise made the law easy — the trustee's duty is the *islah*, and the Lord's knowledge is the accounting.

- Al-Jalalayn, tafsir of 2:219–220 — the great sin from the fighting, cursing and swearing that ensue; the delight in wine and the effortless acquisition of money from gambling; the reply "that which is beyond your needs"; the orphans — the verse answered the sharing of food, drink and shelter; "they are your brothers" in the religion — safeguard their shares; Allah knows him who spoils their wealth from him who improves it; there is no doubt concerning the orphans' property (Ibn Abbas, Tanwir al-Miqbas). https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-219-sora-2.html and https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-220-sora-2.html
