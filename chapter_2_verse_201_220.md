## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:201

> Among them are those who say, “Our Lord, give us good in this world and good in the Hereafter, and protect us from the punishment of the Fire.”

**Commentary:**

### The Balanced Supplication

After describing people who ask only for worldly gain, the Qur’an presents the supplication of the balanced believer. *Rabbanā ātinā fī al-dunyā ḥasanah wa-fī al-ākhirati ḥasanah wa-qinā ʿadhāb al-nār* asks for good in both realms and protection from the Fire. It is one of the most comprehensive supplications in the Qur’an because it gathers immediate needs, eternal success, and rescue from the consequence of sin.

“Good in this world” includes sound faith, beneficial knowledge, lawful provision, health, safety, a righteous family, useful work, contentment, and the ability to obey Allah. It does not mean unlimited luxury or success by worldly standards. A smaller amount of wealth with blessing and lawful use may be better than abundance that corrupts the heart. The servant leaves the details to Allah’s wisdom.

### Good in the Hereafter

Good in the Hereafter is greater and more lasting. It includes forgiveness, acceptance, a secure resurrection, the shade of Allah’s mercy, entry into Paradise, and the honour of seeing Allah in the manner He grants to the people of Paradise. The believer asks for worldly benefit only within the horizon of the final return.

This balance corrects two extremes. One person despises every lawful worldly blessing and imagines that poverty itself is holiness. Another makes comfort, status, and consumption the whole purpose of life. Islam allows the servant to ask for health, marriage, work, safety, and relief while reminding him that none of these can replace faith and righteous action.

### Protection from the Fire

The final request, “protect us from the punishment of the Fire,” shows that the believer does not rely upon his deeds alone. He works, repents, and hopes in Allah’s mercy. Protection includes being saved from disbelief, hypocrisy, persistent sin, injustice, and a final state that leads to punishment. It also includes being forgiven for shortcomings that no human effort could fully avoid.

Fear of the Fire should not become despair. It directs the servant away from harm and toward repentance. The Prophet ﷺ frequently sought refuge from the Fire and taught his Companions to combine fear with hope. The believer asks for Paradise because he trusts Allah’s generosity and asks for protection because he knows his own weakness.

### The Supplication of a Pilgrim

The preceding verses discuss Hajj and the two groups of pilgrims: those whose prayers remain limited to the world and those who ask for both worlds. The supplication is especially appropriate during pilgrimage, when people gather before Allah and remember that worldly travel is a rehearsal for the journey to the Hereafter.

The Prophet ﷺ made this supplication often, as reported by Anas ibn Mālik in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. Its frequent use shows that balance is not a theoretical ideal. It belongs in daily prayer, after worship, during hardship, and whenever the servant must choose between immediate desire and lasting good.

### Good Is Not Always What We Expect

The word *ḥasanah* means good, beautiful, and beneficial. A person may ask for a particular job or relationship and receive something else that protects his faith and future. He may experience an illness that is painful but becomes a means of forgiveness and deeper dependence on Allah. The supplication asks Allah for goodness, not for the servant to control every form it must take.

This does not mean that the believer should call every harm good or refuse treatment and practical action. He seeks relief and uses lawful means while trusting Allah to determine the ultimate benefit. Goodness includes being guided to respond well when an unwanted event occurs.

### A Supplication for Individuals and Society

The plural language of the prayer can be used personally and communally. A believer asks for a safe home, but also for safe communities; for lawful wealth, but also for economic justice; for personal forgiveness, but also for the protection of people from oppression. Worldly good is not complete if it is built on another person’s misery.

A society that remembers this supplication will pursue education, healthcare, security, and honest livelihoods without making material progress an idol. It will also preserve prayer, family, charity, and accountability so that worldly development serves the Hereafter.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:200–202 on worldly-only requests and the balanced supplication.
- Qur’an 16:30–32 and 13:29 on good in this life, the Hereafter, and Paradise.
- Qur’an 28:77 on seeking the Hereafter without neglecting one’s lawful share of the world.
- Qur’an 3:8, 3:16, and 66:8 on asking for firm hearts, forgiveness, and protection from punishment.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the report of Anas that the Prophet ﷺ frequently made this supplication.
- For *ḥasanah*, the balance of worldly and eternal good, and protection from the Fire, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:201.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:202

> They will have a share of what they have earned, and Allah is swift in account.

**Commentary:**

### A Share of Their Deeds

The people who ask for good in this world and the Hereafter will have a share of what they earned. The phrase joins supplication to action. A believer does not merely repeat a beautiful prayer while refusing the obedience that gives it meaning. He asks Allah for good, then works through lawful means, worship, charity, patience, and repentance.

“Share” does not mean that the servant purchases Paradise by his deeds as though Allah were indebted to him. Deeds are a means, and acceptance and mercy are gifts from Allah. The Prophet ﷺ said that no one enters Paradise solely because of his deeds, but only through Allah’s mercy, while deeds remain the evidence of sincere faith and the means Allah has commanded.

### The Fruits of Intention and Effort

A person may receive worldly benefit from an act while another receives its lasting reward. Allah knows the intention, the effort, the obstacles, and the hidden sacrifices. Two people may give the same amount in charity, but the one who gives from little with sincerity may have a greater share than the one who gives from abundance to impress people.

The verse also teaches that intentions matter in ordinary life. Earning money to support family, studying to serve people, resting to regain strength for worship, and seeking treatment can all become rewarded when done lawfully and sincerely. The believer asks Allah to place blessing in both the action and its outcome.

### No One Is Cheated

Allah’s accounting is swift and complete. No good deed is lost, no private prayer forgotten, and no act of oppression overlooked. A person whose good work was ignored by people still has a share recorded with Allah. Likewise, a person who escaped human consequences for wrongdoing cannot escape divine accounting.

“Swift in account” does not mean that Allah needs time to calculate or that judgement is difficult for Him. It emphasizes His complete knowledge and power to bring every person to account without confusion. Millions can be judged without one case delaying another. His judgement is immediate when He wills and perfectly precise.

### Supplication and Responsible Action

The previous verse presents a prayer that includes both worlds. This verse tells the believers that they will receive according to what they earn. A person who asks for good health should follow responsible means; one who asks for lawful wealth should work honestly; one who asks for a righteous family should cultivate patience and good example; one who asks for Paradise should guard faith and obligations.

The principle is not a denial of grace. Allah may give without worldly cause, forgive a great sin, or open a door a person could not reach. But the servant should not use reliance as an excuse for laziness. Tawakkul is trust joined to obedience and appropriate effort.

### Accountability without Comparison

A believer should not measure his share by comparing himself constantly with others. Someone else’s wealth, marriage, health, or public recognition does not reveal his final rank. Allah distributes worldly gifts according to wisdom and gives eternal reward according to justice and mercy. The servant’s task is to fulfil his own trust.

This protects the heart from envy. The person who sees another receiving more asks Allah for blessing and focuses on his own deeds. He also avoids pride when his worldly condition is better, knowing that the real account is still before Allah.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:200–202 on balanced supplication and receiving a share of earned deeds.
- Qur’an 3:195, 9:120–121, and 16:96–97 on no good deed being lost and reward for sincere effort.
- Qur’an 18:49 and 99:7–8 on the complete record of deeds.
- Qur’an 53:39–41 on human effort and the full presentation of deeds.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith that deeds are judged by intentions and the hadith that entry into Paradise is by Allah’s mercy while deeds are the means of that mercy.
- For *naṣīb*, earning, intention, and Allah’s swift and complete accounting, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:202.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:203

> Remember Allah during the appointed days. Whoever hastens to leave in two days, there is no sin upon him; and whoever delays, there is no sin upon him—for whoever is mindful of Allah. Be mindful of Allah and know that you will be gathered to Him.

**Commentary:**

### The Appointed Days of Hajj

The appointed days refer to the days of *tashrīq* in the Hajj rites, especially the days when pilgrims remain at Minā, remember Allah, and stone the designated pillars. The exact legal details of the days and times are explained by the Sunnah and juristic tradition. The central command is clear: the pilgrim’s movement through the rites must be filled with *dhikr* rather than distraction, pride, or quarrelling.

The days are called appointed because Allah has designated their time. Sacred worship has a rhythm that the servant receives rather than invents. Pilgrims follow the established sequence, remain patient in crowds, and understand that a delay or early departure can be lawful when performed within the prescribed limits.

### Hasting or Delaying

The pilgrim may hasten after completing the appointed portion and leave Minā after two days, or delay and remain for an additional day. “There is no sin upon him” does not make the options meaningless; it removes the fear that one lawful choice is inherently blameworthy. The choice depends upon the pilgrim’s circumstances, the prophetic practice, and the guidance of qualified scholars.

Some people assume that staying longer is automatically more pious, while others rush in order to finish without care. The verse teaches that obedience is measured by following Allah’s allowance, not by creating a competition in visible hardship. A person should choose the sound option without belittling those who choose the other.

### Dhikr in the Rites

The pilgrim remembers Allah through takbīr, tahlīl, supplication, Qur’an, and awareness of the meaning of the rites. The stoning of the pillars recalls opposition to Satan and rejection of his commands; it is not an act of attacking a physical devil inside the pillars. The sacrifice, prayer, and movement through Minā connect the pilgrim to Ibrāhīm’s submission and the community’s shared covenant.

The Prophet ﷺ made abundant remembrance during Hajj and taught the Companions the words and times of the rites. The pilgrim should avoid inventing formulas that claim guaranteed effects or turning the rite into a loud contest that disturbs others. Dhikr is worship and should be accompanied by reverence.

### Taqwā Makes Both Options Good

The verse qualifies both hastening and delaying with “for whoever is mindful of Allah.” *Taqwā* is what transforms a legal option into worship. A person who leaves after two days with gratitude and care may be more sincere than one who stays longer to impress people. A person who delays to complete the rite calmly and assist the weak may be acting with greater taqwā than one who rushes from impatience.

Taqwā also controls treatment of other pilgrims. The person who pushes, insults, steals, or harms people cannot claim that physical completion alone proves acceptance. Hajj is a training in obedience, patience, and mercy.

### The Final Gathering

“Know that you will be gathered to Him” returns the pilgrim’s mind from Minā to resurrection. The enormous gathering of Hajj is a visible reminder of the final assembly, where every human being will stand before Allah without tribal privilege. The rites end, but the account continues.

This reminder gives meaning to the earlier commands about provision, promises, charity, and restraint. The pilgrim prepares for the road home, and every person prepares for the road to Allah. No delay in worldly life can prevent the final gathering, and no hidden deed will be absent from the account.

### Lawful Flexibility and Unity

The verse’s allowance demonstrates the flexibility of Islamic law within a clear framework. People differ in health, age, crowd conditions, transport, and responsibilities. Allah does not require every pilgrim to follow one personal timetable when the revealed options are broader. Unity comes from sharing the lawful rite, not from forcing identical preferences in every detail.

Pilgrims should therefore learn before travelling, follow reliable instructions on the ground, and avoid arguing over minor differences. The one who knows the law has a duty to teach gently rather than shame others.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:196–203 on the Hajj rites, sacrifice, remembrance, and the appointed days.
- Qur’an 22:28 and 33:41–42 on remembrance during sacred rites and in all circumstances.
- Qur’an 3:97 and 22:27 on the gathering of pilgrims and the universal call to Hajj.
- Qur’an 59:18 and 18:47–49 on remembering the final gathering and the presentation of deeds.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on the days of Minā, the takbīr during tashrīq, and the Prophet’s instruction to take the Hajj rites from him.
- For *ayyām maʿdūdāt*, *tashrīq*, hastening or delaying at Minā, and the qualification of taqwā, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:203.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:204

> Among the people is one whose speech about worldly life impresses you, and he calls Allah to witness what is in his heart, while he is the fiercest of opponents in dispute.

**Commentary:**

### Attractive Speech Is Not Proof of Sincerity

The verse describes a person whose words appear convincing in public. His speech about worldly life may be polished, emotional, and persuasive, and he may invoke Allah as a witness to his sincerity. Yet his inner reality is different: he is the most intense of opponents in dispute—*aladd al-khiṣām*. The warning is against judging character solely by eloquence, religious vocabulary, or public claims.

The Qur’an does not condemn beautiful speech. A truthful person may speak well, and calling Allah to witness can be genuine when it arises from reverence. The danger is using sacred language as a shield against examination. A person who repeatedly says “Allah knows my heart” while cheating, intimidating, or refusing correction turns a true statement into a cover for false conduct.

### Speech about Worldly Life

The phrase *yuʿjibuka qawluhu fī al-ḥayāti al-dunyā* can indicate speech that impresses people concerning worldly matters or speech that presents a pleasing image of this life. The individual may promise prosperity, protection, reform, or success. He knows how to identify people’s hopes and fears, and he presents himself as their advocate.

The Qur’an teaches that claims must be tested against conduct. Does the speaker fulfil promises, protect the weak, admit mistakes, and accept lawful accountability? Or does his pleasant speech disappear when he gains power? The believer listens carefully but does not surrender judgement to performance.

### Invoking Allah as a Witness

Calling Allah to witness increases the seriousness of speech. If the claim is true, it should make the person more truthful and cautious. If it is false, the invocation becomes an act of impiety and manipulation. The Qur’an warns against using oaths to conceal betrayal (16:91–94) and commands believers to speak words that are straight and responsible (33:70–71).

A person should therefore avoid casual religious oaths. He should not say “By Allah” to win a dispute, pressure a buyer, silence a victim, or make an uncertain claim appear absolute. Truth does not need theatrical sanctification.

### The Fiercest Opponent

*Aladd al-khiṣām* describes someone who is highly skilled and relentless in disputation. He may change the subject, attack the person rather than the evidence, exploit technicalities, and continue arguing after the truth is clear. The problem is not firmness in defending a right. The problem is using argument to defeat truth and preserve ego.

The Prophet ﷺ warned against the most hated person to Allah being the quarrelsome disputatious one, in the authentic reports of Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. The hadith does not forbid asking questions or seeking justice. It condemns debate that is fueled by hostility and has no sincere desire to reach what is right.

### Words and Deeds Must Meet

The following verse reveals the person’s conduct: when he turns away, he strives through the land to spread corruption and destroy crops and livestock. The sequence exposes the gap between speech and action. A person’s true priorities appear when he gains authority or leaves the audience that admired him.

This principle helps evaluate public leaders, teachers, activists, businesspeople, and even ourselves. Beautiful words should be supported by transparent records, consistent treatment of people, and willingness to accept scrutiny. A Muslim does not invent accusations merely because he dislikes a speaker, but he also does not ignore clear evidence because the speaker is charming.

### The Danger of Manipulative Leadership

Manipulators often present themselves as devoted to the people while creating division, exploiting resources, or demanding personal loyalty. They may use religious language to resist accountability and call criticism an attack on faith. The Qur’an exposes this pattern so that communities will value integrity over charisma.

Leaders should welcome consultation, disclose conflicts of interest, keep promises, and make decisions through justice. Followers should avoid treating a popular speaker as infallible. The best speech is that which leads people to Allah and remains consistent with mercy and truth.

### Guarding One’s Own Tongue

The verse is not only about famous hypocrites. Ordinary believers can fall into the same habit in family arguments, online discussions, and business disputes. A person may use tears, oaths, or religious quotations to appear innocent while refusing to acknowledge the harm he caused. Self-examination asks whether our speech invites truth or merely seeks victory.

The cure is *ikhlāṣ*, sincerity, and *ṣidq*, truthfulness. Before speaking, a person asks whether the words are necessary, accurate, and beneficial. When corrected, he says “You may be right” when appropriate. This humility is stronger than winning a dispute.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:204–206 on impressive speech, corrupt action, arrogance, and the consequence of rejecting advice.
- Qur’an 16:91–94 and 33:70–71 on truthful promises, careful oaths, and straight speech.
- Qur’an 49:6 and 49:11–12 on verifying reports and avoiding mockery, suspicion, and backbiting.
- Qur’an 31:18–19 on humility in conduct and measured speech.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith describing the hated quarrelsome disputant and the warning against false oaths and deceptive speech.
- For *aladd al-khiṣām*, speech that impresses, and the distinction between sincere debate and manipulative argument, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:204.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:205

> When he turns away, he strives throughout the land to spread corruption there and destroy crops and livestock. Allah does not love corruption.

**Commentary:**

### Reality Revealed after the Audience

The person described in the previous verse appears impressive while speaking, but when he turns away—*tawallā*—his hidden priorities become visible. He moves through the land creating *fasād*, corruption, and destroys crops and livestock. The verse contrasts public image with public consequence. A person may claim concern for prosperity and people, yet leave behind damaged livelihoods and suffering.

“Turns away” can mean leaving the Prophet’s presence, turning away from the truth, or obtaining authority and then acting without accountability. The exact historical referent is discussed by the exegetes, but the moral pattern is general. Words must be judged by what a person does when praise is absent and power is available.

### Fasād in the Land

*Fasād* is disorder, corruption, and the undoing of what is sound. It includes violence, economic exploitation, environmental destruction, broken institutions, spreading lies, and policies that ruin the conditions of life. Crops and livestock are mentioned because they are the foundations of food security and livelihood. Destroying them harms families far beyond the immediate act.

The Qur’an repeatedly condemns those who claim reform while creating corruption (2:11–12), and it links corruption to human actions on land and sea (30:41). A person cannot call a project successful if it produces profit for a few while poisoning water, destroying farms, or making communities unsafe.

### Food and Livelihood as Trusts

Crops and animals are gifts from Allah and trusts placed under human stewardship. The Qur’an permits people to benefit from them but prohibits waste and abuse. Farmers, traders, officials, and consumers all have responsibilities. A farmer should avoid needless cruelty and destructive practice; a business should not contaminate food or manipulate markets; officials should protect land and water; consumers should avoid waste when others face hunger.

The Prophet ﷺ taught mercy toward animals and praised planting and cultivation that benefits people and creatures. Environmental care is not an optional modern decoration on Islam. It follows from recognizing Allah as Creator and humanity as a responsible steward rather than an absolute owner.

### Corruption Includes Speech and Policy

The verse follows a description of persuasive speech, so corruption is not limited to physical destruction. False reports can destroy reputations, dishonest financial claims can ruin businesses, and inflammatory rhetoric can turn neighbours against one another. A leader who spreads suspicion may destroy the social crop from which cooperation grows.

A believer examines both visible and delayed consequences. Some corruption looks like development at first, while its harm appears years later in disease, displacement, debt, or family breakdown. Consultation with experts, honest data, and concern for the vulnerable are part of responsible decision-making.

### Allah Does Not Love Corruption

The closing statement is categorical: Allah does not love *fasād*. He may allow corrupt people time and temporary success as a test, but allowance is not approval. Wealth and power are not evidence that a project is blessed. The final measure includes justice, truth, and the rights of people and creation.

This should make believers courageous in opposing corruption while remaining just. They do not accuse without evidence or destroy property in the name of reform. They document wrongdoing, advise appropriately, support lawful accountability, and protect people from harm.

### Reform Must Be Real

Some people answer criticism by claiming that their actions are reform, as in 2:11–12. The Qur’an teaches that reform is known by its fruits: justice, safety, truth, mercy, and the preservation of rights. A slogan of reform cannot sanctify oppression. Nor can opposition to a corrupt person justify corruption by the critic.

The Prophet ﷺ built reform through trustworthy leadership, consultation, charity, fair markets, reconciliation, and protection of the weak. The method matters as much as the claim.

### Personal Application

The verse can be applied to the smaller sphere of family and work. A person may speak gently before elders but spread conflict after leaving. He may promise to improve an institution while destroying its staff, resources, or reputation. He may win an argument yet leave relationships poisoned. Taqwā requires asking whether our actions produce *iṣlāḥ* or *fasād*.

Repentance is possible. A person who has caused harm should stop, acknowledge it, restore what can be restored, and work for repair. The next verse warns about the arrogance that prevents this response.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:204–206 on the contrast between impressive speech and corrupt conduct.
- Qur’an 2:11–12, 7:56, and 30:41 on corruption, false claims of reform, and harm on land and sea.
- Qur’an 6:141 and 16:10–11 on crops, provision, and avoiding waste.
- Qur’an 28:77 on seeking the Hereafter without spreading corruption in the land.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on mercy to animals, planting, and removing harm from the path.
- For *fasād*, crops and livestock, and the contrast between claims and consequences, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:205.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:206

> When it is said to him, “Be mindful of Allah,” pride drives him further into sin. Sufficient for him is Hell—what an evil resting place.

**Commentary:**

### The Reaction to Advice

The previous verses described impressive speech followed by corruption. Here the person is confronted with a simple moral reminder: “Be mindful of Allah.” Instead of reflecting, he is seized by *ʿizzah*, a prideful sense of honour or power, and continues in sin. The verse exposes a decisive test of character: how does a person respond when someone reminds him of accountability?

A sincere person may feel embarrassed or initially defensive, but he listens, checks the evidence, and corrects himself if necessary. The arrogant person treats advice as humiliation. He is more concerned with protecting his image than with stopping the harm he is causing. Pride converts a reminder into an insult and makes repentance appear beneath him.

### Taqwā as a Moral Appeal

“Be mindful of Allah”—*ittaqi Allāh*—does not mean “submit to my personal authority.” It is an appeal to a higher Judge. The adviser and the advised are both servants of Allah. A person who uses the phrase to control people unjustly can also be wrong, but the one who hears a sincere reminder should not reject it merely because it challenges his status.

*Taqwā* includes awareness of Allah’s knowledge, fear of His displeasure, hope in His mercy, and protection from sin. It asks the powerful person to remember the rights of the weak and the wealthy person to remember the poor. It places every human role beneath divine accountability.

### Pride Produces Further Sin

“Pride drives him further into sin” suggests that arrogance is not passive. It pushes the person to defend himself through more wrongdoing: he may lie to cover a lie, punish the adviser, destroy evidence, or recruit followers to praise him. What began as a moral failure becomes a system of corruption because the person refuses correction.

The Prophet ﷺ described *kibr* as rejecting truth and looking down on people. The hadith, recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, makes clear that good clothing or appearance is not pride. Pride is a disease of the heart revealed by the refusal to accept truth and the contempt for those considered inferior.

### Advice, Justice, and Wisdom

The verse does not command people to confront every wrongdoer publicly in a way that creates greater harm. Advice should be sincere, informed, and proportionate. The Prophet ﷺ taught that religion is sincere counsel, and he taught methods of correcting wrong according to ability, knowledge, and likely consequence.

A leader may need private counsel before public accountability; a crime may require reporting rather than informal advice; an abusive person may need firm boundaries and legal intervention. The goal is to stop sin and protect people, not to give the adviser an opportunity to display superiority.

### Hell as a Consequence

“Sufficient for him is Hell” is a warning that a person who refuses every reminder and persists in harming others is preparing his own destination. The expression is not approval of the punishment; it is a declaration that Hell is an adequate consequence for deliberate arrogance and corruption. The phrase “evil resting place” contrasts with the false security the person enjoyed in worldly power.

The Qur’an repeatedly joins pride with rejection of truth. Iblīs refused Allah’s command because he considered himself superior, and Pharaoh’s arrogance led him to oppress an entire people. Pride can therefore be personal, religious, political, or intellectual.

### A Warning to the Advised and the Adviser

The immediate warning is to the arrogant offender, but believers must examine themselves. A person may accept advice when it is general but reject it when it names his habit. He may accuse the critic of envy, ignorance, or disloyalty without investigating. Taqwā means asking whether some portion of the reminder is true.

Advisers must also avoid arrogance. Correcting another person does not prove that one is better. The Prophet ﷺ taught gentleness and concealed private faults when concealment served reform. The best counsel begins with concern for Allah and the person, not with the desire to win.

### Repentance before Stubbornness Hardens

The door of repentance remains open while life remains. A person who recognizes pride should say, “I was wrong,” restore the rights he violated, and seek forgiveness. Apology is not humiliation before people; it is strength before Allah. Communities should encourage accountability and reform rather than rewarding those who never admit fault.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:204–206 on speech, corruption, advice, pride, and punishment.
- Qur’an 7:12–18, 16:22–23, and 40:35 on arrogance, Iblīs, and rejecting signs.
- Qur’an 31:18–19 and 49:11–12 on humility, speech, and social ethics.
- Qur’an 39:53 and 25:70 on repentance even after grave wrongdoing.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith defining pride as rejecting truth and despising people; Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Muslim, reports on sincere counsel and gentleness.
- For *ʿizzah*, *kibr*, *taqwā*, and the psychological progression from pride to further sin, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:206.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:207

> And among the people is one who sells himself, seeking the pleasure of Allah. Allah is Kind to His servants.

**Commentary:**

### The Opposite of Arrogant Speech

The previous verses described someone whose speech impresses people but whose actions spread corruption. This verse presents a contrasting servant who “sells himself” for Allah’s pleasure. The metaphor of sale means that he gives his life, wealth, comfort, reputation, and desires in exchange for Allah’s acceptance. Unlike the person who trades guidance for a small worldly price, this believer makes the highest possible exchange.

The phrase is not a call to despise life or to seek death recklessly. It describes sincere sacrifice in obedience: enduring hardship, defending the oppressed within lawful limits, spending wealth, leaving sin, serving people, and preferring Allah’s command over the ego. The servant’s life remains a trust from Allah, not property he may destroy without right.

### Seeking Allah’s Pleasure

The motive is *ib'tighāʾ marḍāt Allāh*, seeking the pleasure of Allah. Actions that appear heroic can be corrupted by the desire for fame, revenge, political power, or admiration. The verse directs the heart to the unseen audience whose approval matters eternally. A private act of sacrifice for Allah may be greater than a public act celebrated by people.

Sincere intention does not make an unlawful act lawful. A person cannot claim to seek Allah’s pleasure through suicide, killing innocents, theft, or oppression. Allah’s pleasure is sought through the means He has permitted and the moral boundaries He has revealed. The Prophet ﷺ taught that deeds are judged by intentions, but a good intention does not cleanse a forbidden deed.

### The Example of Suhayb and Others

Classical exegetes mention reports connecting the verse to Companions who sacrificed wealth and homeland for faith, including Ṣuhayb al-Rūmī, and to the general qualities of sincere believers. Historical reports differ over the immediate occasion, but the verse’s meaning is not restricted to one person. It praises every servant who makes the right exchange for Allah.

The early believers left homes, status, and possessions in Makkah, endured persecution, and built a community in Madīnah. Their sacrifice was not an escape from responsibility. It created safety, worship, brotherhood, and justice. The verse therefore honours sacrifice that produces life and reform, not destruction for its own sake.

### Selling the Self through Daily Obedience

Most believers will not face the dramatic circumstances of the first Muslims, but the same exchange occurs daily. A worker refuses a bribe, a student refuses cheating, a spouse apologizes, a parent sacrifices sleep for a child, a person leaves a forbidden relationship, or a wealthy Muslim gives quietly to the poor. These acts trade immediate comfort for Allah’s pleasure.

The believer also sells his ego when he accepts correction. The person who lets go of the need to win an argument, forgive an insult, or share credit has not become weak. He has transferred ownership of his desires to Allah’s guidance.

### Allah Is Kind to His Servants

The closing name *Raʾūf*—Kind or Tenderly Compassionate—assures the servant that Allah does not waste sincere sacrifice. People may overlook the cost, misunderstand the motive, or exploit generosity, but Allah knows. His kindness appears in guiding the person to sacrifice, strengthening him during it, protecting him from excess, and rewarding him beyond what he gave.

Allah’s kindness also means that He does not require a servant to abandon every lawful blessing. The Prophet ﷺ balanced worship with sleep, family, work, and recreation. A person who destroys his health, neglects dependants, or refuses lawful rest has not necessarily made the best exchange. Kindness includes respecting the rights Allah has placed upon the body and family.

### Sacrifice and Justice

The verse belongs near passages about corruption, arrogance, and public harm. It contrasts self-giving with self-serving leadership. The righteous person gives himself to protect others; the corrupt person uses others to protect himself. This distinction is visible in how power is exercised. A leader who bears hardship for the people is closer to the verse than one who demands sacrifice while preserving his own comfort.

The Qur’an praises those who give others priority even when they themselves are in need (59:9). But sacrifice must not be demanded from the vulnerable by those who refuse their own duty. Charity, service, and defence are noble when freely and lawfully undertaken, not when imposed through manipulation.

- Qur’an 3:92 and 59:9 on spending what one loves and giving others priority.
- Qur’an 9:111 on the metaphorical “transaction” with Allah and the reward of sincere believers.
- Qur’an 4:29 and 17:33 on preserving life and not treating sacrifice as permission for self-destruction.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith “Deeds are only by intentions,” and the hadith on Allah’s kindness and reward for sincere deeds.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:208

> O believers, enter into Islam completely and do not follow the footsteps of Satan. Surely he is a clear enemy to you.

**Commentary:**

### Entering Islam Entirely

The believers are commanded to enter into *al-silm* or *al-islām* completely—*udkhulū fī al-silm kāffah*. The phrase means embracing the religion as a whole rather than selecting only the beliefs, rituals, or ethics that fit personal preference. Islam is submission to Allah in creed, worship, character, family life, commerce, justice, and public responsibility.

“Completely” does not mean that a believer becomes instantly perfect. Human beings learn gradually, sin, repent, and grow. It means that the direction of submission is comprehensive: no part of life is declared permanently independent of Allah’s guidance. A person may struggle with a command while believing it is true and working toward obedience; he must not make his struggle a principle that rejects revelation.

### The Whole Revelation Belongs Together

A person cannot claim Islam while severing worship from ethics. Prayer with fraud, fasting with cruelty, charity with arrogance, or public religious speech with private abuse indicates that the message has been divided in practice. The Qur’an joins faith and action repeatedly, and the Prophet ﷺ embodied the connection between devotion and good character.

The command also prevents selective reading. Some people accept verses about mercy but reject justice, or quote rules while ignoring the compassion and wisdom that govern them. Entering Islam completely means reading the Qur’an as a coherent revelation, returning disputed matters to Allah and His Messenger, and allowing clear principles to correct personal bias.

### Do Not Follow Satan’s Steps

The warning about Satan’s footsteps repeats 2:168–169. Satan rarely begins by asking a person to reject everything at once. He invites a small exception, a convenient reinterpretation, a private compromise, or a division between “spiritual” and “practical” life. The believer watches the direction of the path.

A person may begin by delaying prayer for work, then treating prayer as optional, then deciding that worship has no relevance to public life. Another may begin by excusing a small lie and gradually build a business on deception. The cure is to return quickly, close the first door, and ask Allah for firmness.

### Islam and Human Diversity

Entering Islam completely does not require every Muslim culture to look identical. The Sharīʿah contains fixed acts of worship and moral boundaries, while allowing diversity in language, food, clothing, art, architecture, and customary practice when these do not violate revelation. Complete submission means evaluating culture by Islam, not erasing all legitimate culture.

Likewise, Muslims differ in knowledge and spiritual strength. The community should teach patiently and support gradual growth without turning gradual learning into permanent rejection. The Prophet ﷺ taught people according to their capacity while making the complete goal clear.

### Faith, Law, and Character

Islam addresses the heart through tawḥīd, the body through worship, society through justice, and the soul through purification. The believer enters the whole path by accepting that Allah has the right to guide every sphere. This protects against the modern tendency to keep religion as a private identity while allowing desire or market power to govern everything else.

It also protects against harshness. Complete Islam includes the Prophet’s mercy, his fulfilment of treaties, his patience with learners, his care for neighbours, and his protection of the weak. A person who applies isolated rules while abandoning the prophetic ethic has not entered the revelation completely.

### A Collective Command

The verse addresses the community in the plural. Muslims must help one another enter Islam fully through education, good example, justice, and supportive institutions. A society that makes lawful living impossible through corruption or exploitation pushes people toward compromise. Community leaders should make worship accessible, protect the vulnerable, and teach without humiliation.

Each individual remains responsible. No group can claim to have entered Islam completely while its members excuse injustice because a leader commands it. The whole community submits to Allah, and every leader remains a servant.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:168–208 on Satan’s footsteps, lawful life, self-sacrifice, and comprehensive submission.
- Qur’an 4:125 and 16:89–90 on submitting oneself to Allah and the comprehensive guidance of revelation.
- Qur’an 5:2–3 and 6:162–163 on completing faith through worship, cooperation, and directing life to Allah.
- Qur’an 17:53 and 49:13 on good speech, mercy, and diversity under taqwā.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith “The religion is ease,” the hadith on the branches of faith, and reports of the Prophet’s gradual teaching and complete character.
- For *silm*, *islām*, *kāffah*, and the meaning of entering the religion wholly without demanding instant perfection, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:208.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:209

> If you slip after clear proofs have come to you, then know that Allah is Exalted in Might and Wise.

**Commentary:**

### A Warning after Clarity

After commanding believers to enter Islam completely, Allah warns against slipping back toward Satan’s steps after clear proofs have come. The image of slipping—*zalaltum*—suggests a loss of footing. A person may know the path, become careless, follow a tempting detour, and then find himself moving away from obedience. The verse does not deny the possibility of sin in a believer; it warns against turning a lapse into a deliberate direction.

“Clear proofs”—*al-bayyināt*—include revelation, the prophetic explanation, and evidence that removes a person’s excuse for rejecting what Allah has made clear. The more clearly a truth is known, the more serious it is to abandon it for desire or pressure. At the same time, a question about an ambiguous matter is not the same as rejecting a decisive command.

### Allah Is Mighty and Wise

Allah is *ʿAzīz*, Exalted in Might, and *Ḥakīm*, Wise. His might means that no one can defeat His command or escape His judgement. His wisdom means that His legislation, warning, and mercy are placed with perfect knowledge. The closing names create both fear and trust: the servant cannot treat Allah’s limits casually, and he can trust that the limits are not arbitrary.

The verse’s warning is therefore not a threat from an unjust ruler. Allah knows the evidence each person received, the pressures he faced, and the sincerity of his response. His might guarantees accountability; His wisdom guarantees that accountability will be fair.

### Slipping Is Often Gradual

Satan’s footsteps are small and sequential. A person may begin by postponing a prayer, consuming doubtful wealth, mocking a command, or following a flattering teacher. Each step weakens the heart’s sensitivity. The believer should not wait until a serious sin has become a lifestyle before seeking help.

Practical protection includes regular Qur’an, prayer, repentance, trustworthy companionship, and asking knowledgeable people when confused. The Prophet ﷺ taught that hearts can be affected by repeated actions and that the most beloved deeds are those done consistently. Stability is built through small acts of obedience.

### Clear Proofs and Intellectual Honesty

The verse does not require a person to suppress genuine uncertainty. Islam values questions and study. A person who does not understand an issue should ask, research, and suspend judgement until he learns. The danger is using “I am still thinking” as a permanent disguise for refusing a clear truth, or using a confident claim to hide that one has not studied.

Scholars distinguish decisive evidence from matters open to interpretation. This distinction preserves both certainty and humility. The believer accepts what is clear, follows qualified scholarship in complex matters, and avoids making personal preferences into divine commands.

### The Possibility of Return

A slip is not the same as a final fall. The Qur’an repeatedly calls those who sin to repent and repair. Allah’s might is joined elsewhere to His forgiveness, and His wisdom includes giving servants opportunities to return. The person who notices his deviation should stop, seek refuge from Satan, confess the wrong, restore rights, and resume the path.

Repentance should not be delayed because a person feels unworthy. Satan wants the sin followed by despair. Allah wants the servant to return. The warning of this verse and the mercy of the next passages should be read together.

### A Community that Helps People Stand

Believers should not take pleasure in another person’s slip. They advise, protect, and encourage return while maintaining necessary boundaries. Teachers should explain proof clearly, families should support those trying to change, and institutions should create routes for correction rather than rewarding concealment.

This does not mean ignoring harm. Justice may require accountability, and protecting victims comes first. But accountability should aim at truth and reform, not permanent humiliation. Allah is both Mighty and Wise, and believers seek to reflect those qualities in their response.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:168–209 on Satan’s footsteps, complete submission, clear proofs, and the danger of slipping.
- Qur’an 3:102–105 and 47:25–28 on holding fast to Allah and the consequences of turning away after guidance.
- Qur’an 39:53 and 66:8 on repentance and returning to Allah sincerely.
- Qur’an 16:43 and 49:6 on asking qualified people and verifying information.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith on consistent deeds, repentance, and the changing condition of hearts.
- For *zalal*, *bayyināt*, *ʿizzah*, and *ḥikmah*, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:209.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:210

> Are they waiting for Allah to come to them in canopies of clouds, with the angels, and for the matter to be decided? To Allah all matters return.

**Commentary:**

### A Rhetorical Question of Delay

After warning believers not to slip once clear proofs have arrived, the Qur’an asks what the rejecters are waiting for. Do they require the final scene of judgement before they will acknowledge the truth? The question exposes a destructive habit: postponing faith until the unseen becomes visible and accountability is no longer avoidable.

Revelation, conscience, creation, and the signs of history already provide evidence. Waiting for the Day when the matter is decided is not intellectual caution; it is an attempt to delay responsibility. Once the final realities are unveiled, belief will no longer be the voluntary response of an unseen test. The opportunity for repentance will have ended.

### The Canopies of Clouds and the Angels

The verse describes a scene of the Hereafter in which the angels appear and Allah’s command is manifested. The imagery conveys majesty, awe, and the approach of final judgement. The Qur’an uses language that human beings can understand while reminding them that the realities of the unseen exceed the limitations of worldly perception.

The expression “Allah comes” must be affirmed and understood in a manner befitting Allah, without likening Him to creation, imagining a bodily movement, or asking how. The mainstream Ahl al-Sunnah affirm what revelation affirms *bilā kayf*, without modality. Some classical exegetes explain the phrase as the coming of Allah’s command, judgement, or manifestation of His power. These interpretive approaches share a rejection of human physical comparison. The verse is about the certainty and majesty of divine judgement, not an invitation to create an image of Allah.

### The Final Decision

“For the matter to be decided” means that judgement is issued and the truth becomes manifest. The disputing parties will no longer be able to hide behind slogans, inherited identity, or the authority of leaders. Allah’s decision separates truth from falsehood, the sincere from the hypocritical, and the oppressed from the oppressor.

The final decision also gives moral meaning to present patience. People may suffer while corruption appears to triumph, but no matter is lost. Allah will bring every hidden deed to light and restore rights. Those who delay repentance should remember that the final decision is not merely a theological event; it is the moment when every choice receives its true consequence.

### All Matters Return to Allah

“To Allah all matters return”—*turjaʿ al-umūr*—means that all affairs ultimately belong to His authority and judgement. People may manage property, institutions, governments, and disputes, but no created power is independent. The phrase invites reliance and accountability at once. The believer entrusts outcomes to Allah while fulfilling his duty; the oppressor should fear that every abuse returns to the One who sees it.

The return also corrects worldly fragmentation. Human beings divide life into religion, politics, economics, and private desire as though Allah governed only one part. The Qur’an teaches that every matter returns to Him. Worship, law, family, wealth, and public conduct remain under His knowledge and moral judgement.

### Do Not Wait for the Unseen to Become Sight

A person who says, “I will believe when I see the angels or the punishment,” misunderstands the test. Faith concerns the unseen supported by signs and revelation (2:3). Seeing the final reality after death does not earn the same faith as trusting Allah before the veil is lifted.

This does not require irrational belief. The Qur’an calls people to evidence, reflection, and honest inquiry. It warns only against demanding a type of proof whose arrival would erase the opportunity to choose. The wise person responds to guidance while the door is open.

### Theological Humility

The verse contains realities that human beings cannot fully picture. Muslims affirm revelation, avoid denial, and avoid speculative detail. They do not describe Allah as a body, place Him within creation, or claim knowledge of the unseen beyond what He revealed. At the same time, they do not dismiss the verse simply because its reality exceeds ordinary experience.

This humility is a general rule in studying the unseen: affirm what Allah and His Messenger ﷺ taught, deny likeness to creation, and leave the modality to Allah. The purpose of the verse is guidance and warning, not philosophical imagery.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:208–210 on complete submission, slipping after clear proofs, and the final judgement.
- Qur’an 6:158, 16:33, and 89:22–23 on the arrival of the final signs and the Day of judgement.
- Qur’an 2:3 and 53:35–38 on belief in the unseen and the limits of human knowledge.
- Qur’an 18:49 and 99:7–8 on every deed returning to its owner and being displayed.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on the gathering, angels, and the realities of the Hereafter; the principles of affirming divine attributes without asking how are established in the early Sunni tradition.
- For the clouds, angels, “coming,” and *turjaʿ al-umūr*, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:210.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:211

> Ask the Children of Israel how many clear signs We gave them. Whoever exchanges Allah’s favour after it has come to him, then surely Allah is severe in punishment.

**Commentary:**

### Remembering the Signs

The verse asks the Children of Israel to remember how many clear signs Allah gave them. These signs include deliverance from Pharaoh, the splitting of the sea, manna and quails, the cloud of shade, water from the rock, the victories and guidance granted through their prophets, and the clear revelation sent to them. The question is not seeking information Allah lacks. It is an indictment of forgetfulness and ingratitude.

A sign—*āyah*—is both a miracle and an indication. It should direct the heart to the One who gave it. The problem was not that the signs were too few; it was that many people treated repeated favours as temporary conveniences and returned to disobedience once danger passed.

### Favour Creates Responsibility

Allah’s favour—*niʿmah*—is not simply comfort. It includes guidance, rescue, provision, knowledge, and the opportunity to serve. When a community receives these gifts, its responsibility increases. It must preserve the revelation, act justly, teach the next generation, and respond gratefully.

The Qur’an repeatedly reminds Banū Isrāʾīl of their favours while also acknowledging that some among them were righteous and others were not. The warning is therefore not racial or collective. It addresses a historical pattern of receiving guidance, violating covenant, and claiming privilege while neglecting obedience. Muslims are equally accountable when they receive the Qur’an and Sunnah but treat them as decoration.

### Exchanging Favour for Loss

“Exchanges Allah’s favour” means trading guidance for something lesser. A person may exchange truth for political influence, revelation for social approval, worship for wealth, or justice for group loyalty. The language of exchange connects this verse with the earlier warnings about selling the Book for a small price (2:174–175).

The exchange can happen through neglect as well as active denial. A blessing may be lost when a person uses it against its purpose. Knowledge becomes a curse if it produces arrogance, wealth becomes a curse if it funds oppression, and authority becomes a curse if it protects corruption. Gratitude preserves a blessing by directing it to Allah’s obedience.

### The Severe Punishment

Allah is severe in punishment because deliberate ingratitude after clear signs is a serious betrayal. This severity does not contradict His mercy. He gave signs, reminders, prophets, and opportunities to repent. Punishment follows persistent refusal, not a single moment of weakness or an honest question.

The phrase is a warning rather than a reason for believers to celebrate another community’s punishment. Muslims should learn the principle and fear for themselves. The Qur’an says that Allah does not change a blessing granted to a people until they change what is within themselves (8:53). A community that wants its blessings preserved must maintain gratitude and justice.

### Signs and the Human Heart

A miracle alone does not force submission. Pharaoh saw signs and still became arrogant; the Qur’an describes him and his people as rejecting them inwardly despite recognizing them (27:14). Signs clarify evidence, but pride can make a person reinterpret or conceal it. This is why spiritual purification matters alongside knowledge.

The servant asks Allah for a heart that recognizes blessings before they disappear. He remembers past rescue, thanks Allah for present guidance, and does not demand a new miracle every time obedience becomes difficult.

### A Warning for Muslims

Muslims have received the greatest sign in the Qur’an, along with the Prophet’s example and countless daily favours. They must not assume that membership in the ummah makes them immune from the verse. If they conceal revelation, divide its teachings, abandon justice, or use religious language for worldly gain, they are exchanging favour for loss.

The proper response is remembrance, gratitude, repentance, and service. A person who sees a blessing in his faith should make it available through good character and truthful teaching rather than using it to humiliate others.

- Qur’an 8:53 and 16:112 on preserving or losing blessings through gratitude and conduct.
- Qur’an 14:7 on gratitude and increase; Qur’an 27:14 on knowing a sign while rejecting it through arrogance.
- Qur’an 2:174–175 and 3:187 on exchanging or concealing revelation for worldly gain.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on the miracles of earlier prophets and the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, and the hadith on the blessings of guidance and the duty of gratitude.
- For *āyāt*, *niʿmah*, the history of Banū Isrāʾīl, and exchanging favour for punishment, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:211.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:212

> The life of this world has been made appealing to those who disbelieve, and they ridicule the believers. But those who are mindful of Allah will be above them on the Day of Resurrection, and Allah gives provision to whom He wills without measure.

**Commentary:**

### The Attraction of the World

The verse explains why people may exchange guidance for worldly gain. The life of this world is made attractive to those who disbelieve. Wealth, status, pleasure, comfort, and immediate success can appear so complete that a person forgets death and judgement. The Qur’an does not say that worldly beauty is unreal or that believers may not enjoy lawful blessings. It warns against allowing temporary attraction to define the whole meaning of life.

The verb “made appealing”—*zuyyina*—can describe both a test in Allah’s creation and the work of Satan and desire. The world becomes dangerous when its decoration hides consequences. A person sees the profit but not the injustice that produced it, the pleasure but not the addiction that follows, or the applause but not the emptiness of living for approval.

### Ridiculing the Believers

When believers refuse a profitable sin or challenge a dominant culture, they may be mocked as naïve, backward, weak, or impractical. The verse acknowledges this social pressure. The disbelievers’ ridicule is not proof that the believers are inferior. It often reveals the discomfort of people who do not want their priorities questioned.

Believers must not respond with arrogance or mockery of their own. The Qur’an forbids one people ridiculing another and teaches dignified speech (49:11). Firmness means maintaining faith and justice without becoming intoxicated by a sense of superiority.

### Above Them on the Day of Resurrection

The worldly hierarchy will be reversed on the Day of Resurrection. People who appeared powerful may be exposed as spiritually bankrupt, while servants who were mocked for faith are honoured by Allah. Being “above” them refers to rank, security, and reward in the Hereafter, not a command for believers to seek domination over people in this life.

The promise is not for every person who uses a religious identity. It is for those who are *muttaqūn*, mindful of Allah in belief and conduct. A believer who claims Islam while oppressing others cannot rely on the verse as a guarantee. Taqwā is proven through truth, patience, and justice.

### Provision without Measure

Allah gives provision—*rizq*—without measure. This can mean abundant worldly provision, unexpected spiritual gifts, or reward beyond calculation in the Hereafter. The phrase also comforts the believer who loses worldly status for refusing falsehood. Allah’s storehouses are not limited by the approval of a market, ruler, or audience.

Provision is not always a sign of divine favour. The disbelievers may receive great wealth as a test, while a believer may be poor yet rich in faith and dignity. The Qur’an asks people to distinguish temporary distribution from final honour. Allah gives according to wisdom and will, and no lawful sacrifice is lost.

### The World Is a Test, Not an Enemy

Islam does not teach hatred of the created world. The earth, family, food, work, and beauty are signs and gifts. The problem is *dunyā* when it becomes the ultimate object of love and fear. A believer uses the world as a means to the Hereafter, earning lawfully, supporting family, helping the poor, and cultivating knowledge.

The Prophet ﷺ lived simply but accepted lawful gifts, married, traded, governed, and cared for society. He taught that wealth in itself is not blameworthy; what matters is how it is acquired, what it does to the heart, and how it is spent.

### Responding to Ridicule

A believer responds to mockery with patience, clarity, and good character. He does not abandon a command to gain acceptance, but he does not turn every disagreement into a fight. If ridicule becomes harassment or violence, he may seek protection and justice. Patience never requires accepting abuse without lawful remedy.

Communities should support those who face pressure. Children, converts, minorities, and people leaving harmful environments may need companionship and practical help. The promise of Allah’s provision is lived partly through believers who become means of support for one another.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:211–212 on exchanging guidance, worldly attraction, ridicule, taqwā, and divine provision.
- Qur’an 3:14–15 and 57:20 on the decoration and temporary nature of worldly life.
- Qur’an 49:11–13 on avoiding ridicule and measuring honour through taqwā.
- Qur’an 28:77 and 89:15–17 on using worldly provision rightly and not equating wealth with honour.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on the Prophet’s simple life, his patience under ridicule, and the true value of wealth and generosity.
- For *zuyyina*, *dunyā*, *rizq*, and being above others in the Hereafter, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:212.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:213

> Humanity was one community, then Allah sent prophets as bearers of good news and warnings, and He sent down with them the Book in truth to judge between people concerning what they differed over. No one differed over it except those who were given it, after clear proofs had come to them, out of jealous hostility among themselves. Then Allah guided the believers to the truth concerning what they differed over, by His permission. Allah guides whom He wills to a straight path.

**Commentary:**

### Humanity’s Original Unity

The statement that humanity was one community—*kāna al-nāsu ummatan wāḥidah*—has been explained by the early exegetes in related ways. It may refer to their original unity upon a simple recognition of Allah before disputes multiplied, or to their being one undivided human community before revelation and social differences required detailed guidance. The broader lesson is clear: division is not humanity’s highest destiny, and revelation came to restore truth amid competing claims.

Unity in the Qur’an does not mean that all people must share one culture or that every disagreement is blameworthy. It means unity upon truth, justice, and the recognition of Allah. Human diversity in language, nation, and custom can be a sign of Allah (30:22). What destroys community is arrogant rivalry, oppression, and the refusal to accept correction.

### Prophets Bring Good News and Warning

Allah sent prophets as *bashīrīn* and *mundhirīn*: bearers of good news and warners. Good news encourages faith, repentance, mercy, Paradise, and the honour of obedience. Warning protects people from disbelief, injustice, and the consequences of refusing truth. A message with only warning may produce despair; one with only promise may produce complacency. Prophetic teaching balances hope and fear.

The prophets share one essential call—to worship Allah alone—while the details of law can differ according to time and community. Muslims believe in all of them and do not insult one messenger to honour another. The Prophet ﷺ described the prophets as brothers with the same father but different mothers, meaning one faith with different legal forms, in the authentic reports of Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim.

### The Book Judges Disputes

Allah sent the Book in truth so that it might judge between people in their disagreements. Revelation is not merely a collection of private spiritual inspirations. It establishes a criterion for belief, worship, ethics, contracts, family rights, and public justice. When people disagree, they return to Allah’s Book and the prophetic explanation rather than allowing power or popularity to decide.

The Book does not erase the need for human judgement. Scholars interpret language and apply principles to changing circumstances. Courts examine evidence and protect procedure. But all these efforts remain accountable to revelation. A judge who accepts a bribe or a leader who uses scripture to protect himself has betrayed the Book’s purpose.

### Difference after Clear Proofs

The verse says that some people differed only after clear proofs came to them. This is not a condemnation of every difference in interpretation. Honest scholars may disagree over evidence that is probabilistic, language that admits more than one reading, or new circumstances. The blameworthy dispute is the one that arises after clarity and is driven by *baghy*, jealous hostility and transgression.

Such rivalry may be caused by envy of another community’s status, fear of losing authority, desire for wealth, or refusal to accept that a truth has come through a person outside one’s group. The result is not merely a theoretical disagreement. People conceal proofs, distort texts, and mobilize followers against one another.

### Guidance in Disagreement

Allah guides believers to the truth in what people differ over, by His permission. This guidance is a gift, but believers seek it through sincerity, knowledge, consultation, prayer, and willingness to be corrected. A person who begins a discussion determined to defeat an opponent may possess information yet miss guidance.

The verse does not promise that believers will agree on every secondary matter. It promises that Allah can guide them to the truth and to proper conduct in disagreement. They may say, “Allah knows best,” respect legitimate scholarship, and preserve brotherhood while remaining firm on clear principles.

### The Causes of Division

The Qur’an identifies both ignorance and hostility as causes of division. Ignorance is addressed through teaching; hostility requires purification of the heart. A community can possess libraries and institutions while still fighting because envy has made knowledge a weapon. The remedy includes sincere intention, justice toward opponents, and recognition that Allah’s guidance is not a personal possession.

The Prophet ﷺ warned that disputation can lead people astray when it is not based on sincere learning. He taught mercy, consultation, and returning matters to the Qur’an and Sunnah. A community should create spaces where questions are safe, evidence is examined, and disagreement does not become a reason for humiliation.

### A Straight Path through Human Diversity

The straight path does not erase legitimate diversity. It directs diversity toward worship, justice, and mutual recognition. Muslims can differ in language, legal school, architecture, food, and social custom while sharing the testimony, prayer, Qur’an, and prophetic model. They must distinguish these permissible differences from rivalry that fragments the faith.

Allah guides whom He wills, and His will is joined to wisdom and justice. The servant does not claim that his group owns guidance. He asks Allah to guide him and acts upon the truth he knows.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:214

> Or do you think that you will enter Paradise without experiencing trials like those who came before you? They were touched by poverty and hardship and were shaken until the messenger and those who believed with him said, “When will Allah’s help come?” Surely Allah’s help is near.

**Commentary:**

### Paradise Is Not Reached without Testing

The verse corrects an expectation that faith should remove every hardship. Believers may hope for Allah’s protection, but they should not assume that Paradise is earned without trial. The path of the prophets included persecution, poverty, exile, illness, grief, and opposition. Testing reveals sincerity, purifies the heart, strengthens reliance, and distinguishes a living commitment from a claim made only when life is comfortable.

The question “Do you think?” is an invitation to examine assumptions. A person may say he is ready to sacrifice, but hardship reveals what he truly values. The Qur’an does not promise that every believer will face the same trial or that suffering itself guarantees Paradise. It teaches that tests are part of the path and must be met with faith, patience, and lawful action.

### Poverty and Hardship

The previous generations were touched by *baʾsāʾ* and *ḍarrāʾ*. The first includes poverty, deprivation, and external hardship; the second includes illness, injury, and painful affliction. These words cover both social conditions and personal suffering. The believer is permitted to seek food, medical care, shelter, employment, and protection. Patience does not require refusing help or romanticizing poverty.

The community also bears responsibility. When people are tested by hunger or displacement, other believers must provide material aid rather than simply telling them to be patient. Zakah, charity, fair policy, and protection of the oppressed are practical expressions of faith.

### They Were Shaken

“They were shaken”—*zulzilū*—describes intense fear and pressure. The trial disturbed their outward security and tested their inward certainty. The verse does not condemn them for feeling fear. Even prophets experienced anxiety in dangerous circumstances. What matters is that they remained turned toward Allah and waited for His help rather than abandoning faith or becoming unjust.

The statement “When will Allah’s help come?” is not necessarily a complaint against Allah. It can be the cry of people who long for relief while still trusting the promise. Yaʿqūb grieved and complained to Allah; Ayyūb called upon Allah in illness; the Prophet ﷺ sought help in battle. Honest lament before Allah is different from accusing Him of injustice.

### Help Is Near

Allah’s answer is immediate: His help is near—*alā inna naṣr Allāhi qarīb*. Nearness does not always mean that relief arrives according to the servant’s timetable. Help may come through victory, a change in circumstances, protection from sin, unity of the believers, forgiveness, or reward in the Hereafter. Allah may delay the visible outcome while strengthening the people who will carry it.

The promise gives hope without prescribing a date. Impatience can lead people to unlawful shortcuts, betrayal, or despair. Taqwā means taking means, persevering, and trusting that no trial is outside Allah’s knowledge.

### Trials Do Not Prove Abandonment

A believer facing illness, unemployment, persecution, or grief may wonder whether Allah has rejected him. The verse shows that the best servants were tested. Hardship is not automatically punishment, and comfort is not automatically approval. The meaning depends upon the person’s response and Allah’s wisdom.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that the people most severely tested are the prophets, then those nearest to them in righteousness, according to Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī. This does not make people seek pain. It teaches that difficulty can accompany honour and spiritual growth.

### The Community in Trial

The verse uses the messenger and believers together. Trials are faced collectively through leadership, mutual support, consultation, and prayer. A righteous leader does not hide hardship from the people or demand sacrifices he will not share. Believers encourage one another, distribute resources, protect the vulnerable, and avoid rumours that deepen fear.

The Prophet ﷺ built resilience through prayer, brotherhood, lawful planning, and trust. The community’s spiritual language was matched by action: sheltering migrants, feeding the poor, defending the oppressed, and fulfilling covenants.

### A Pattern for Personal Crisis

When an individual is shaken, he can divide the crisis into duties: maintain the obligatory prayer as able, seek expert and medical help, speak honestly, protect dependants, avoid unlawful relief, and ask Allah for patience. He should not compare his trial with another person’s or assume that weakness makes him a hypocrite.

Healing can be gradual. A person may say, “When will Allah’s help come?” while continuing to seek it through lawful means. The promise of nearness gives him permission to hope again each day.

- Qur’an 12:86 and 21:83–84 on lament and supplication directed to Allah.
- Qur’an 94:5–6 and 65:2–3 on relief, ease, and provision with hardship and taqwā.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:215

> They ask you what they should spend. Say, “Whatever good you spend should be for parents, close relatives, orphans, the needy, and the traveller. Whatever good you do, surely Allah knows it.”

**Commentary:**

### Asking about the Right Use of Wealth

The question concerns what should be spent—*mādhā yunfiqūn*—and the answer directs attention not only to the amount but to the people who have claims. Charitable spending is not merely an expression of generosity; it is a social responsibility. Allah names parents, relatives, orphans, the needy, and travellers because wealth should reach those whose protection, provision, or temporary circumstances make them vulnerable.

The verse does not mean that every voluntary donation must always be distributed in precisely this order regardless of circumstance. It establishes priorities and reminds believers to notice needs close to them rather than pursuing distant public praise while neglecting dependent parents or struggling relatives.

### Parents and Relatives

Parents often deserve support because of their lifelong care and because old age, illness, or poverty can make them dependent. Relatives combine the right of need with the bond of kinship. Supporting them can be both charity and maintaining *ṣilat al-raḥim*, the connection of family ties. Yet family connection must not become nepotism or a reason to deny assistance to non-relatives who are in greater danger.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that charity to a relative can carry the reward of charity and maintaining kinship. The giver should still avoid reminding the recipient of the favour or using money to control family members. Support should preserve dignity and encourage wellbeing.

### Orphans, the Needy, and Travellers

Orphans—*yatāmā*—need care because they have lost a parent or guardian and may lack someone to defend their rights. The Qur’an strongly warns against consuming their property or treating them harshly (4:2; 93:9). Giving to an orphan includes food, education, shelter, emotional support, and careful guardianship of any inheritance.

The needy—*masākīn*—may have some resources but not enough for basic life. Their poverty can be hidden, and they may avoid asking. A compassionate community learns to identify needs without humiliating people. The traveller—*ibn al-sabīl*—may be wealthy at home but stranded without access to his property. Help is based on present need, not on stereotypes about the person’s overall status.

### What Is Good Spending?

“Whatever good you spend” includes lawful money, time, food, clothing, expertise, and effort. A person may provide transport, teach a skill, pay a medical bill, or protect someone from abuse. The word *khayr* reminds us that spending should produce real benefit and not support harm, vanity, or corruption.

The source of the wealth matters. Charity from theft does not purify the thief, and a donation that buys political silence may be a form of bribery. Allah accepts what is good and given with sincere intention. The Prophet ﷺ taught that the upper hand is better than the lower hand, while also encouraging moderation and responsibility toward dependants.

### Balancing Near and Wider Duties

Islam does not teach a person to give away what his family needs and then call the resulting hardship piety. The Prophet ﷺ advised beginning with those for whom one is responsible and leaving dependants secure. Obligatory support, debt repayment, and the rights of workers come before optional public generosity.

After fulfilling immediate duties, believers look outward. A relative may need help, but so may a refugee, disaster victim, student, or neighbour of another faith. Allah’s categories establish compassion, not a narrow circle of tribal preference. The best spending reaches people with justice and wisdom.

### Allah Knows Every Good Deed

The final statement assures the giver that no act is lost. People may not know about a quiet payment, a meal delivered at night, or time spent caring for an orphan. Allah knows the amount, intention, sacrifice, and benefit. This protects charity from dependence upon human applause.

It also warns the giver not to corrupt the act after giving through reminders, insults, or public humiliation. The Qur’an says that charity is cancelled when accompanied by injury and boasts (2:264). The gift belongs to Allah’s knowledge even when the recipient cannot repay it.

- Qur’an 4:2, 4:36, and 93:9–10 on protecting orphans and honouring vulnerable people.
- Qur’an 2:264–274 on sincere charity, hidden giving, and avoiding harm to recipients.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:216

> Fighting has been prescribed for you, though it is hateful to you. Perhaps you dislike something while it is good for you, and perhaps you love something while it is bad for you. Allah knows, and you do not know.

**Commentary:**

### A Difficult Duty

This verse acknowledges the human dislike of fighting. Violence brings fear, injury, bereavement, displacement, and moral danger. The Qur’an does not romanticize war or pretend that a believer naturally enjoys it. Fighting may become necessary to defend a community from aggression and persecution, but it remains a severe and regulated duty rather than a source of entertainment or pride.

The wording “has been prescribed” must be read with the limits in the surrounding verses: fighting those who fight, no transgression, acceptance of cessation, and proportional response (2:190–194). It does not command individuals to attack people because of their faith. It establishes a community’s responsibility to defend life and religious freedom when lawful conditions are met.

### Dislike Does Not Decide What Is Good

People often judge good and bad by immediate emotion. They dislike discipline, truthful correction, sacrifice, or a difficult responsibility, yet it may protect them. They love comfort, fame, revenge, or easy profit, yet it may corrupt them. Allah sees consequences beyond the present moment, while human knowledge is partial.

The verse is not asking believers to call every painful event good without reflection. It teaches humility before Allah’s knowledge. A person seeks the best course through revelation, consultation, expertise, and prayer, then accepts that the outcome may differ from his preference. What is good in one circumstance may be harmful in another; the judgement requires wisdom.

### The Ethics of Necessary Fighting

When fighting is genuinely prescribed, believers must maintain moral limits. They protect non-combatants, honour covenants, avoid torture and mutilation, and stop when aggression ends. The dislike of war can help preserve these limits by preventing the heart from becoming addicted to violence. Courage is needed, but hatred is not the measure of courage.

A leader may have to make a painful decision to defend people, yet he remains responsible for alternatives, proportionality, and the welfare of civilians. A soldier may be brave while still grieving and hoping for peace. Islam does not require emotional celebration of bloodshed.

### Human Preference and Divine Knowledge

The final statement—“Allah knows, and you do not know”—is a foundation of *tawakkul*. Human beings know what they can observe, but not every consequence of an action. A person may be prevented from a desired outcome because Allah knows it would harm his faith or family. Another may be required to endure a burden because it will protect many people or cultivate a virtue.

This does not cancel reason or consultation. The Qur’an praises deliberation and commands believers to prepare strength, and the Prophet ﷺ consulted his Companions and used practical strategy. Reliance on Allah means acting with knowledge available to us while submitting the unseen result to Him.

### No Excuse for Blind Obedience

The verse should not be used by rulers or commanders to suppress criticism. A claim that “Allah knows” cannot make an unjust war righteous. The cause must still be examined through Qur’anic limits, evidence, lawful authority, and the rights of people. Human beings may be wrong about what they call necessary.

Likewise, an individual cannot use the verse to follow a charismatic leader into violence without proof. The command is addressed within a revealed legal framework, not as a blank cheque for anyone who claims divine backing.

### The Personal Application

The verse applies beyond armed conflict. A person may dislike prayer at its time, honest financial accounting, apologizing, caring for a sick relative, or ending a harmful habit. He may love praise, debt-funded luxury, or an unhealthy relationship. Allah’s guidance may require what the ego dislikes and forbid what it wants.

The believer asks for wisdom rather than assuming that every desire is a reliable guide. He distinguishes a lawful preference from a command and does not blame himself for natural dislike of hardship. Obedience is shown by what he chooses after recognizing the truth.

### Balancing Fear and Courage

The verse prepares the community for the difficult commands in the following passage about fighting in the sacred month. Believers may fear social consequences, death, or uncertainty, but they are reminded that Allah’s knowledge is complete. Courage is not the absence of fear; it is disciplined action under Allah’s command, without abandoning mercy and justice.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that the strong believer is better and more beloved to Allah than the weak believer, while there is good in both, and he encouraged seeking what benefits, asking Allah for help, and not surrendering to helpless regret. Strength in Islam includes moral and spiritual strength, not merely physical force.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:217

> They ask you about fighting in the sacred month. Say, “Fighting in it is a grave matter. But barring people from Allah’s way, disbelief in Him, preventing access to al-Masjid al-Ḥarām, and expelling its people from it are graver in the sight of Allah. Persecution is graver than killing.” They will continue fighting you until they turn you away from your religion, if they are able. Whoever among you turns back from his religion and dies while disbelieving, his deeds have become worthless in this world and the Hereafter, and they are the companions of the Fire; they will remain there forever.

**Commentary:**

### The Question about a Sacred Month

The verse responds to a question about an incident in which fighting occurred during a sacred month. The sacred months carry a restriction on warfare, and the believers did not dismiss that sanctity. Allah says clearly that fighting in the month is a grave matter. The answer then places the incident within the larger history of persecution: preventing people from Allah’s way, rejecting Him, blocking access to the Sacred Mosque, and expelling its inhabitants were graver offences.

The Qur’an is not making the sacred month meaningless. It is exposing selective outrage. The opponents focused on one violation while ignoring the persecution, expulsion, and religious obstruction they had practised. Justice requires considering the entire pattern of aggression rather than using one rule as a shield for a greater campaign of harm.

### Sacred Time and Proportional Justice

The four sacred months are protected times, and Muslims must not initiate wrongdoing in them. If an enemy uses that protection to attack or to continue expelling people, the community may defend itself within the limits of 2:190 and 2:194. The response remains proportionate and directed toward ending aggression.

The principle is relevant beyond the calendar. A treaty, sanctuary, humanitarian pause, hospital, or protected status cannot be abused as a platform for attacking others. If protection is violated, the answer is lawful defence and restoration of safety, not abandonment of every rule.

### The Graver Offences

The verse lists offences worse than the fighting being questioned: blocking guidance, disbelief, preventing access to the Sacred Mosque, and expelling its people. These acts attack faith, liberty, home, and the right to worship. *Fitnah* here again refers to persecution and coercion that makes people suffer for their religion and drives them from their homes.

The phrase does not mean that every non-Muslim person is guilty of these acts. The context concerns a hostile group and its conduct. The Qur’an separately commands justice and kindness toward peaceful non-Muslims (60:8). A believer must identify the actual aggressor rather than generalize guilt to an entire religion, ethnicity, or population.

### Persisting in the Attempt to Turn Believers Back

“They will continue fighting you until they turn you away from your religion, if they are able.” This describes the active hostility of those who sought to force Muslims away from Islam. It does not command Muslims to assume that every neighbour secretly wants to convert them or to treat ordinary disagreement as war. The conditional “if they are able” portrays a determined persecuting force, not a universal psychological claim about all outsiders.

The believer protects his faith through knowledge and worship, but he also honours the freedom of others. Coercion cannot create īmān, and the Qur’an prohibits forcing religion. Defence against coercion is not the same as imposing belief.

### Turning Back and Dying in Disbelief

The final section describes apostasy joined to dying in disbelief. “His deeds have become worthless” refers to the final state of a person who abandons faith and never returns before death. It does not mean that every sin erases all good deeds or that a person who struggles, doubts, or commits a grave act is automatically outside Islam. The Qur’an distinguishes final rejection from repentance and return.

The verse’s warning is serious because faith is the foundation that gives deeds their direction. A person should protect it through knowledge, prayer, good companionship, and asking Allah for firmness. The Prophet ﷺ taught that hearts change and frequently made supplication for steadfastness.

### Deeds in This World and the Hereafter

The phrase “in this world and the Hereafter” has been interpreted to include the loss of honour, protection, and the legal consequences associated with leaving the Muslim community in the historical setting, as well as the loss of reward in the Hereafter if death occurs upon disbelief. Application of worldly legal rulings requires due process, knowledge, legitimate authority, and distinction between public treason, private doubt, coercion, and sincere questioning.

Ordinary Muslims must not investigate hearts or take punishment into their own hands. The verse warns the living and directs them toward faith, while Allah judges each person’s final state with perfect knowledge.

### Hope in Return

Even in this severe warning, the door of repentance remains open while life remains. The next verse praises those who believe, emigrate, and strive. The Qur’an repeatedly invites the person who has turned away to return. A believer who fears for his faith should not despair; he should seek knowledge, renew the testimony, leave harmful influence, and ask Allah for mercy.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:218

> Surely those who believe, emigrate, and strive in Allah’s cause may hope for Allah’s mercy. Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Commentary:**

### Three Qualities of Hope

After warning about turning away from faith, the Qur’an describes those who may hope in Allah’s mercy: they believe, emigrate, and strive in His cause. The verse joins inner conviction to costly action. Hope is not a vague optimism; it is grounded in faith and expressed through sacrifice and effort.

*Īmān* is trust in Allah and acceptance of His revelation. *Hijrah* is leaving a place, condition, or influence that prevents obedience when a lawful path of departure exists. *Jihād* means striving and exerting effort for Allah’s sake, including striving against one’s own desires, seeking knowledge, serving the vulnerable, and, under lawful authority and strict limits, defending a community from aggression. It cannot be reduced to violence or used to sanctify terrorism.

### Emigration for Faith

The first Muslims emigrated from Makkah to Madīnah because persecution obstructed worship and threatened their lives. They left homes, property, family networks, and familiar status for Allah. The Qur’an honours the Muhājirūn and the Anṣār for this sacrifice and for the brotherhood that followed.

Hijrah has a continuing spiritual meaning. A believer may need to leave a sinful environment, an abusive situation, dishonest employment, or a social circle that repeatedly destroys faith. Physical relocation is not always possible or required. The principle is to move toward obedience while taking wise steps and protecting dependants.

A person should not abandon responsibilities impulsively or create harm while claiming hijrah. The Prophet ﷺ taught that deeds are judged by intentions, and the jurists consider ability, safety, contracts, family rights, and the nature of the danger. Allah’s mercy is sought through wise obedience, not dramatic gestures.

### Striving in Allah’s Cause

*Jihād* literally conveys striving and exertion. The broadest daily struggle is to obey Allah, resist sin, speak truth, acquire beneficial knowledge, and serve people. A parent who patiently raises children, a student who pursues knowledge, a reformer who resists corruption, and a person who fights addiction can all be striving for Allah when the intention and means are sound.

Armed fighting is a restricted category, not the definition of every use of the word. It requires a just cause, legitimate authority, ability, and observance of the prohibitions in 2:190–194. The Prophet ﷺ forbade killing non-combatants, treachery, and transgression. No individual or group may use this āyah to declare private war.

### Hope Rather Than Presumption

The believers “hope”—*yarjūna*—for Allah’s mercy. Hope is not a guarantee based on self-satisfaction. The servants believe, sacrifice, and still recognize their need for forgiveness. They do not say, “Our deeds have purchased Paradise.” They ask Allah to accept the little they offer and to cover their mistakes.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that no one enters Paradise solely through his deeds but through Allah’s mercy. This teaching protects the striving person from pride and the sinner from despair. Work is required, but acceptance remains a gift.

### Mercy for Those Who Leave for Allah

Emigration can involve loneliness, financial difficulty, discrimination, or the grief of leaving loved ones. Allah’s mercy includes companionship, provision, new opportunities, forgiveness, and reward beyond what was lost. The Qur’an promises that those who emigrate for Allah will find many places of refuge and abundance (4:100).

Communities receiving migrants have a duty to welcome and support them. The Anṣār were praised for loving those who emigrated to them and preferring them over themselves (59:9). A community that celebrates hijrah but exploits migrants has missed the verse’s ethics.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:219

> They ask you about intoxicants and gambling. Say, “In both is great sin and some benefits for people, but their sin is greater than their benefit.” They also ask what they should spend. Say, “Whatever is beyond your needs.” Thus Allah makes the revelations clear to you so that you may reflect.

**Commentary:**

### Acknowledging Benefit without Approving Harm

The verse addresses *khamr*, intoxicants that cloud the mind, and *maysir*, gambling and games of chance in which wealth is gained through speculation and another person’s loss. It acknowledges that people may perceive benefits in them: pleasure, temporary escape, social excitement, commercial income, or a chance of profit. Acknowledging an apparent benefit does not make the practice lawful. Allah states that the sin and harm are greater.

This was part of the Qur’an’s gradual education concerning intoxicants. The final prohibition in 5:90–91 makes the ruling explicit and commands believers to avoid them. The progression taught the community to understand the moral and social consequences before the final boundary was established.

### The Harm of Intoxicants

Intoxication clouds *ʿaql*, the faculty by which a person understands, restrains desire, and fulfils responsibility. It can lead to violence, neglect of prayer, family breakdown, accidents, health damage, debt, exploitation, and addiction. The temporary pleasure does not cancel these harms. A person who says that he can control an intoxicant should consider the wider effect on people around him and on those who become dependent.

The prohibition extends to producing, selling, transporting, serving, and promoting intoxicants when these activities support their consumption. The Prophet ﷺ cursed multiple parties connected to wine in the hadith recorded by Sunan Abī Dāwūd and Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī. The principle is to close the commercial path to a known harm.

### The Harm of Gambling

*Maysir* transfers wealth through chance, rivalry, and the hope of gaining without productive work. It can include betting, lotteries, casinos, wagering on games, and modern forms of speculative play that exploit addiction. Even when a winner receives money, the system depends upon others losing and encourages a cycle of hope, debt, and resentment.

The Qur’an later says that gambling creates enmity, hatred, and distraction from remembrance and prayer (5:91). Games can be harmless when no unlawful stake or deception is involved. The problem is making chance a mechanism for consuming wealth and cultivating greed.

### The Benefit Is Not a Licence

People often reason that if a forbidden thing has any benefit, it must be permissible. The verse teaches the opposite method: moral judgement considers the whole balance of benefit and harm, not the existence of a single advantage. Medicine may contain a substance with a harmful side effect and be governed by necessity and expert judgement, but recreational intoxication is not justified by the claim that it helps a person relax.

The final ruling on khamr and maysir protects individuals and society from industries that profit from weakness. It also prevents the wealthy from shifting the harm onto families, workers, and communities while keeping the visible profit.

### What to Spend

The second question concerns charitable spending: “What should we spend?” The answer, *al-ʿafw*, has been explained as what is beyond one’s needs, what can be given with ease, or what remains after fulfilling legitimate obligations. This does not command a person to neglect dependants, debts, health, or necessary provision. It teaches generosity without irresponsibility.

A person begins with obligatory support and rights, then gives from what Allah has enabled. The best charity may be regular and modest rather than dramatic and financially destructive. The Prophet ﷺ taught that the best charity is what a person gives while he still has need and that one should begin with those for whom he is responsible.

### The Connection between Intoxication and Spending

The two questions are placed together because both concern how people treat resources and judgement. Intoxicants and gambling consume wealth without producing wholesome benefit, while charity directs wealth toward people and purposes that preserve life. One feeds addiction and rivalry; the other cultivates mercy and social stability.

A believer examines not only what he spends but what his spending strengthens. Supporting a harmful industry, paying a bribe, or wasting food can contradict gratitude even when the amount is small. Allah asks for responsible stewardship.

### Reflection and Moral Education

Allah makes the revelations clear so that people may reflect—*yatafakkarūn*. The Qur’an educates the heart through explanation, gradual legislation, consequences, and reminders. Reflection should lead to action: abandoning intoxicants, refusing gambling, seeking treatment for addiction, supporting affected families, and giving wealth with wisdom.

A person who is already dependent should seek medical, psychological, spiritual, and community support. Repentance includes stopping the harm, repairing rights, and removing access where possible. Shaming an addict can deepen the problem; mercy includes firm help toward recovery.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:220

> About this world and the Hereafter. They ask you about orphans. Say, “Improving their condition is best. If you mix your affairs with theirs, then they are your brothers.” Allah knows the corrupter from the reformer. Had Allah willed, He could have made things difficult for you. Surely Allah is Exalted in Might and Wise.

**Commentary:**

### The Larger Horizon of Spending

The opening words connect this verse to the balanced supplication for good in this world and the Hereafter and to the question of charitable spending. Muslims are not asked to abandon the world; they are asked to order it toward the Hereafter. Wealth, family, work, and care for vulnerable people become part of worship when guided by Allah’s commands.

The verse then focuses on orphans, whose vulnerability makes the ethics of wealth especially important. An orphan—*yatīm*—has lost a father or responsible protector while still young. Such a child may possess property inherited from parents, making him vulnerable to guardians who control the wealth before the child can defend himself.

### Reform Is the Best Response

“Improving their condition”—*iṣlāḥ lahum khayr*—includes protecting the orphan’s property, educating and nurturing the child, providing food and shelter, treating him with affection, and preparing him for responsible adulthood. It is better than treating the child as a burden or isolating him from ordinary family life out of excessive fear of financial contact.

The Qur’an elsewhere commands guardians to test orphans until they reach marriageable age and sound judgement, then hand over their property (4:6). This shows that care includes physical maturity and financial competence. A guardian should not retain control merely because the orphan has reached an age at which he can manage his own affairs.

### Mixing Affairs with Integrity

“If you mix your affairs with theirs, then they are your brothers.” Some guardians separated their food and property so strictly that ordinary care became difficult, fearing that any shared expense would be sinful. Allah clarifies that separate accounting is not always required if mixing is genuinely for the orphan’s welfare. The orphan is a brother or sister in humanity and faith, not an object of suspicion or an opportunity for profit.

Mixing is lawful only with integrity. A guardian may combine food, household expenses, or business arrangements when he can distinguish the orphan’s rights and prevent exploitation. Clear records, fair valuation, independent oversight, and transparent accounts protect both the child and the guardian. If mixing creates confusion or temptation, separation is safer.

### Allah Knows the Intention and the Result

“Allah knows the corrupter from the reformer.” Human beings may not know whether a guardian’s complicated arrangement is honest or fraudulent. Allah knows the intention, the details, the benefit to the child, and the hidden enrichment of the adult. This is a warning against disguising consumption as care, but also a comfort to the sincere guardian who acts responsibly despite suspicion.

The statement does not make intention alone enough. A person who says he wants to help while repeatedly losing the orphan’s money through negligence is not a reformer merely because he feels affectionate. Good intention must be joined to sound administration and measurable protection.

### Do Not Consume Orphans’ Wealth

The Qur’an gives severe warnings against consuming orphan property unjustly (4:2, 4:10). A guardian with sufficient means should not take compensation from the orphan’s wealth; a poor guardian may take what is reasonable for necessary service, according to the legal conditions. He must not borrow without record, mix assets to conceal losses, or delay returning property when the child becomes capable.

Modern guardianship may involve bank accounts, investments, school fees, medical costs, and legal documents. The principles remain: preserve the principal, seek safe and lawful growth, document every transaction, and place the child’s welfare above the guardian’s convenience.

### Brothers, Not Objects

The word “brothers”—*ikhwānukum*—gives an ethic of dignity. Orphans need more than property management. They need stable affection, education, protection from abuse, and a sense of belonging. The Prophet ﷺ warned that the person who does not show mercy to children lacks the mercy he needs, and he praised the one who cares for an orphan.

Care should not erase the child’s history or force gratitude. The orphan has rights to identity, safety, and truthful information appropriate to his age. Guardians and institutions must listen to children and create safe ways to report mistreatment.

### Ease in the Law

“Had Allah willed, He could have made things difficult for you.” The verse shows that Islamic law is not designed to make honest guardians impossible. Allah knows that families share meals, homes, labour, and resources. He permits reasonable mixing when the purpose is reform, while maintaining the prohibition of exploitation.

Ease is not negligence. The concession is a mercy for people sincerely trying to care for orphans, not a loophole for those who use the child’s wealth. The guardian combines ease with taqwā, documentation, and accountability.

### A Community Responsibility

Orphan care is not left to private goodwill alone. The community should support guardians, supervise charitable institutions, protect children from trafficking and abuse, and establish transparent systems for inheritance and foster care. Zakah and voluntary charity can fund education, health, housing, and legal protection.

A society that praises orphan sponsorship but allows corruption in orphanages has not fulfilled *iṣlāḥ*. Reform is measured by the child’s safety and flourishing, not by the institution’s public image.
