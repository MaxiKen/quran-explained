# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 261–270

This is the longest sustained treatment of one subject in the surah so far. Ten verses, all on charity — and not on how much to give, but on **what makes a gift count.**

The passage opens with the promise of multiplication (261), gives the two conditions that void it (262–263), then offers **four parables in six verses**: a seed (261), a rock (264), a garden on a hill (265), and a garden burnt to nothing (266). Then it turns to the *quality* of what you give (267), the enemy who argues you out of giving (268), the gift that makes a person able to see all of this (269), and the fact that none of it is unseen (270).

**Two structural things to watch.** First: **264 and 265 are a matched pair — the same rain falls on both, with opposite results.** The rain is identical; the ground is not. Second: **265 and 266 are a matched pair — a garden that thrives and a garden that burns.** The surah is teaching economics in pictures rather than propositions, and it has put the two pictures side by side so you cannot read one without the other.

**And one boundary must be stated before 2:266, because it is the verse in this batch most likely to be used badly.** It describes a garden destroyed at the end of a life. **No reading of it may close the door of repentance, and no reading of it permits declaring any particular person lost.** The parable is a warning about endings, not a verdict on anyone.

---

## Verse 261

**"The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains. And Allah multiplies ˹the reward even more˺ to whoever He wills. For Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing."**

In simple terms: the likeness of those who spend their wealth in the path of God is as the likeness of a grain that sprouts seven ears, in every ear a hundred grains. And God multiplies for whom He wills. And God is All-Encompassing, Knowing.

*Mathalu alladhīna yunfiqūna amwālahum fī sabīli Allāhi ka-mathali ḥabbatin anbatat sab'a sanābila fī kulli sunbulatin mi'atu ḥabbatin, wa Allāhu yuḍā'ifu li-man yashā'u, wa Allāhu wāsi'un 'alīm.*

*Ka-mathali **ḥabbatin*** — "as the likeness of a **single grain**."

**Note the starting point: one grain. Not a field, not a sack, not a harvest. One seed.**

**And now the first thing the parable does that most readers miss: the seed has to be buried.**

A grain that sprouts is a grain that has been put into the ground and has ceased to be a grain. **It is destroyed as a seed in order to become a harvest.** There is no version of this parable in which you keep the seed and also get the seven hundred. **You cannot have both.** Which is the verse's answer to the arithmetic anxiety that stops most giving: **the multiplication is not a bonus on top of what you kept. It is what comes back from what you let go of.**

*Anbatat **sab'a sanābila** fī kulli sunbulatin **mi'atu ḥabbatin*** — "sprouting **seven ears**, in every ear **a hundred grains**."

**Seven hundred.** And the tradition has always noted the shape of the number: seven ears, each with a hundred grains. **Seven hundred is the parable's floor, not its ceiling.**

*Wa Allāhu **yuḍā'ifu li-man yashā'*** — "and God **multiplies for whom He wills**."

**This clause is the one that matters, and it is usually read as though it were decoration.**

The parable gives 700. **And then the verse says: and more, for whom He wills.** Which means **700 is the minimum in the image, and the actual multiplication is unspecified.** The hadith in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* gives the ordinary range — "every deed of the son of Adam is multiplied: a good deed by ten up to seven hundred times" — and the same hadith excepts fasting: "except fasting, for it is for Me, and I reward it."

**So the structure is: a stated minimum in the parable, a stated ordinary range in the Sunnah, and no stated ceiling anywhere.** The verse deliberately leaves the top open.

*A note on the parable and botany, which should be said once and then dropped.* This is a parable about **the ratio of reward**, not a claim about agriculture. **A single grain producing seven hundred is not how wheat works, and the tradition has never read it as though it were.** Setting it up as a scientific prediction would misread the genre and set the text up for a refutation it does not need. **The parable's claim is theological: what you give is not lost, and the return is not calculable by you.**

**The hadith that belongs here, in the two *Ṣaḥīḥ*s from Abū Hurayrah:** *"Whoever spends a **pair** of things in the path of God is called from the gates of Paradise: 'O servant of God, this is good.' Whoever is among the people of prayer is called from the gate of prayer; whoever is among the people of *jihād* is called from the gate of *jihād*; whoever is among the people of charity is called from the gate of charity; whoever is among the people of fasting is called from the gate of *al-Rayyān*."* Abū Bakr said: "Is there anyone who will be called from all of them?" The Prophet ﷺ said: **"Yes — and I hope you are among them."**

**Note the word "pair" and note Abū Bakr's question, because together they are the verse in miniature.** A pair — two of anything, the smallest plural. **And Abū Bakr is not satisfied with being called from one gate. He wants all of them.** That is what 2:261 is for: **not to reassure you that giving is safe, but to make you want more of it.**

*Wa Allāhu **wāsi'un 'alīm*** — "and God is **All-Encompassing, Knowing**."

The same closing pair as 2:247. ***Wāsi'*** — vast, boundless: **He is not giving from a store that can run down.** ***'Alīm*** — Knowing: **He knows the intention behind the gift, which is what determines whether it multiplies at all** — which is exactly where the next verse goes.

## Verse 262

**"Those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah and do not follow their charity with reminders of their generosity or hurtful words—they will get their reward from their Lord, and there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve."**

In simple terms: those who spend their wealth in the path of God and then do not follow up what they have spent with reminders of it or injury — their reward is with their Lord, and no fear upon them, nor do they grieve.

*Alladhīna yunfiqūna amwālahum fī sabīli Allāhi thumma lā yutbi'ūna mā anfaqū **mannan wa lā adhan**, lahum ajruhum 'inda rabbihim wa lā khawfun 'alayhim wa lā hum yaḥzanūn.*

**Now the two conditions, and they are the hinge of the whole passage.**

*Thumma lā yutbi'ūna mā anfaqū **mannan wa lā adhan*** — "then they do not follow up what they spent with **reminder and injury**."

**Note the verb: *yutbi'ūna* — they do not *follow* it with.** The giving happened. **What is forbidden is what comes after.** The picture is of a gift walking out the door with something trailing behind it.

**And the two things are different from each other, and both are named:**

***Mann*** — a reminder of the favour, a recounting of it, keeping it on the books. **The Arabic root carries the sense of cutting and of weighing out, and it is the word used for the manna that fell for the Israelites — a favour given.** *Mann* is: **"after all I did for you,"** spoken or unspoken, in words or in manner. It is the tally that is never erased.

***Adhā*** — injury, hurt. **Not necessarily the reminder itself.** It is the whole family of things that make the recipient smaller: the manner, the public scene, the delayed payment, the condition attached, the insult that comes with the help.

**And here is what makes this verse so severe: the charity was real, and it is destroyed after the fact.** The giving happened. The money left. The poor person was fed. **And then the giver opened his mouth and undid it.**

**That is the single most important thing in this passage, and it is why 2:264's parable exists.** Charity is not a transaction that completes when the money changes hands. **It is an act that remains open, and it can be voided afterwards by the giver.**

**And the ḥadīth that names this precisely, in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* from Abū Dharr:** the Prophet ﷺ said there are three to whom God will not speak on the Day of Resurrection, nor look at them, nor purify them, and theirs is a painful punishment — and he repeated it three times. Abū Dharr said: "They have failed and lost; who are they?" He said: **"The one who lets his garment hang below his ankles, the one who reminds people of what he has given them, and the one who sells his goods by false oaths."**

**The *mannān* is listed between arrogance and fraud.** That is the weight the Sunnah gives to this verse.

*Lahum ajruhum **'inda rabbihim*** — "their reward is **with their Lord**."

**Note the preposition: *'inda* — with, in the keeping of.** The reward is not paid out immediately and it is not in the recipient's keeping. **It is lodged with God.**

**Which is the verse's answer to a question people actually ask: "I gave and they were ungrateful."** The reward was never theirs to give you. **It is *'inda rabbihim*.** And that is also why the gratitude of the recipient is irrelevant — which the next verse will make even clearer.

*Wa lā khawfun 'alayhim wa lā hum yaḥzanūn* — "and no fear upon them, nor do they grieve."

**This exact closing occurs repeatedly in the Qur'an (2:38, 2:62, 2:112, 2:274, 2:277, 10:62).** Two negations: **fear** — of what is ahead — and **grief** — over what is behind. **Both removed.** Which is a complete description of peace: **nothing ahead to dread, nothing behind to regret.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 263

**"Kind words and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury. And Allah is Self-Sufficient, Most Forbearing."**

In simple terms: kind speech and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury. And God is Free of need, Forbearing.

*Qawlun ma'rūfun wa maghfiratun khayrun min ṣadaqatin yatba'uhā adhā, wa Allāhu ghaniyyun ḥalīm.*

**This is one of the most practically useful verses in the Qur'an, and it is a ranking.**

*Qawlun ma'rūfun wa maghfiratun **khayrun** min ṣadaqatin yatba'uhā adhā* — "**kind speech and forgiveness are better than** charity followed by injury."

**Read it plainly: the verse compares two things and declares one superior.**

- **Option A:** give someone money, and then hurt them with it.
- **Option B:** say something decent to them, and overlook what they did.

**And the verse says: B is better.**

**That is an extraordinary thing for a book that commands charity as urgently as this one does.** The surah has spent verses and verses telling you to give — **and here it says a kind word outranks a gift that comes with a wound.**

**And the reason is in the previous verse: the harmed gift is not merely less good. On the reading of 2:264 that follows, it approaches nullity.** So the comparison is not "generosity versus politeness." **It is: a thing that has been spoiled, versus a thing that has not.**

**Two words in the first half are worth unpacking:**

***Qawlun ma'rūfun*** — a word that is *ma'rūf*: **recognised, decent, what a community knows to be good.** The same word as *bi-al-ma'rūf* throughout the marriage verses (2:228, 2:231, 2:233, 2:236, 2:241). **It is not flattery and it is not technique. It is the ordinary decent thing that everyone recognises.** A person who cannot give can nearly always say this.

***Maghfirah*** — **forgiveness, covering.** Note the pairing: **a decent word, and an overlooking.** Both cost the speaker something. **Both are forms of giving that leave no ledger.**

**And there is a ḥadīth that belongs here.** In the two *Ṣaḥīḥ*s from Abū Hurayrah: **"A good word is charity."** And in *Sunan al-Tirmidhī* from Abū Dharr (graded ḥasan): the Prophet ﷺ said — *"Your smiling in your brother's face is charity; commanding the good and forbidding the wrong is charity; directing a man who has lost his way is charity; helping a man with poor sight is charity; removing a stone, a thorn or a bone from the road is charity; and pouring what remains from your bucket into your brother's bucket is charity."*

**Which means the verse is not lowering the bar. It is widening the gate.** The person with nothing to give is not excluded from this passage.

*Wa Allāhu **ghaniyyun ḥalīm*** — "and God is **Free of need, Forbearing**."

**Two Names, and both are aimed at the giver.**

***Ghaniyy*** — Free of need, self-sufficient. **He does not need your money at all.** Which is the verse's check on the *mann*: **what right does a person have to remind anyone of a favour, when the God he is doing it for does not need it?**

***Ḥalīm*** — Forbearing, slow to anger, the One who does not hasten punishment. **And this is the Name that answers the injury.** You hurt someone with your gift; God did not strike you down. **The forbearance you are shown is the forbearance you are being told to show.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 264

**"O believers! Do not waste your charity with reminders ˹of your generosity˺ or hurtful words, like those who donate their wealth just to show off and do not believe in Allah or the Last Day. Their example is that of a hard barren rock covered with a thin layer of soil hit by a strong rain— leaving it just a bare stone. Such people are unable to preserve the reward of their charity. Allah does not guide ˹such˺ disbelieving people."**

In simple terms: O you who believe, do not nullify your charities with reminders and injury — like the one who spends his wealth to be seen by people and does not believe in God or the Last Day. His likeness is as the likeness of a smooth rock with soil upon it, and a downpour strikes it and leaves it bare. They have no power over anything of what they earned. And God does not guide the disbelieving people.

*Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā tubṭilū ṣadaqātikum bi-al-manni wa-al-adhā ka-alladhī yunfiqu mālahu **ri'ā'a al-nāsi** wa lā yu'minu bi-Allāhi wa-al-yawmi al-ākhiri, fa-mathaluhu ka-mathali **ṣafwānin 'alayhi turābun fa-aṣābahu wābilun fa-tarakahū ṣaldan**, lā yaqdirūna 'alā shay'in mimmā kasabū, wa Allāhu lā yahdī al-qawma al-kāfirīn.*

*Lā **tubṭilū** ṣadaqātikum* — "do not **nullify** your charities."

**Note the verb: *abṭala* — to render void, to make a thing come to nothing.** The charity **exists**, and then it is **voided**. **This is the technical vocabulary of invalidation (*ibṭāl* / *buṭlān*) that the jurists use for a contract that was valid and has been broken.**

**Which settles the question 2:262 raised.** The gift was real. What happens to it afterwards can destroy it.

*Bi-al-manni wa-al-adhā **ka-alladhī yunfiqu mālahu ri'ā'a al-nāsi*** — "with reminders and injury, **like the one who spends his wealth to be seen by people**."

**And note the comparison: the verse likens the person who reminds to the person who gives for show.** That is how serious the reminding is.

*Ri'ā'a al-nāsi* — from *riyā'*, to be seen. **The deed is addressed to an audience rather than to God.** And the verse adds *wa lā yu'minu bi-Allāhi wa-al-yawmi al-ākhir* — **and does not believe in God and the Last Day** — because the person who truly believed that God sees and that a Reckoning is coming would not need the audience.

**Now the parable, and it is one of the most precise images in the Qur'an.**

*Fa-mathaluhu ka-mathali **ṣafwānin 'alayhi turābun*** — "his likeness is as the likeness of a **smooth rock with soil upon it**."

***Ṣafwān*** — a large, smooth, hard rock. ***Turāb*** — dust, loose soil.

**And here is what the image is doing: the dust looks like ground.** From a distance, a rock with a layer of soil on it looks like earth. **It looks like it could grow something.** It has the appearance of fertility, the colour of fertility, the texture of fertility.

**And it is a rock.**

*Fa-aṣābahu **wābilun** fa-tarakahū **ṣaldan*** — "and a **downpour** strikes it and leaves it **bare**."

***Wābil*** — heavy, driving rain. ***Ṣaldan*** — bare, smooth, stripped, nothing left on it.

**And note what the rain does: it does not water the soil. It removes it.**

**The very thing that should have made it grow is the thing that strips it.** The rain that would have produced a harvest on real ground washes the dust off the rock and exposes the stone underneath. **The gift that should have produced a reward is what reveals that there was never any ground.**

**That is the parable of *riyā'* with complete accuracy.** The deed looked like a deed. It had the shape, the cost, the visibility, the appearance of growth. **And there was nothing under it.** And the thing that exposes it is not punishment from outside. **It is the same rain.**

*Lā yaqdirūna 'alā shay'in mimmā **kasabū*** — "they have **no power over anything of what they earned**."

***Lā yaqdirūna*** — they have no ability, no hold, no grasp. **Not "they will not receive." They cannot even hold on to it.** The deed is gone and cannot be retrieved. **The currency was spent, the receipt is blank, and there is nothing to take back.**

**And the ḥadīth that belongs to this verse, in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* (1905) from Abū Hurayrah — the first three people to be judged on the Day of Resurrection.** A man martyred in battle, a man who learned and taught the Qur'an, and a man given abundant wealth who gave it in every kind of charity. Each is brought, each is shown his blessing, each acknowledges it, and each is asked what he did with it. The martyrs says he fought so that God be exalted; the scholar says he learned and taught for God; the giver says **he left no kind of charity that he did not give in it, for God.**

**And to each, God says: "You have lied."** To the third: *"You did it so that it would be said, 'he is generous' — and it has been said."* Then he is ordered to be dragged on his face and thrown into the Fire.

**That is the ṣafwān.** The dust was thick, the appearance was complete, the deed was real and it cost him everything — **and the rain left bare stone.**

**And the definitive hadith on *riyā'*, a *ḥadīth qudsī* in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* (2985) from Abū Hurayrah:** God says — **"I am the most self-sufficient of partners. Whoever performs a deed in which he associates another with Me — I leave him and his association."**

**And the ḥadīth naming it directly**, from Maḥmūd ibn Labīd (recorded by Aḥmad, graded ḥasan by some): the Prophet ﷺ said, **"The thing I fear most for you is the lesser *shirk*."** They asked what it was. He said: **"*Al-riyā'*."**

*Wa Allāhu lā yahdī **al-qawma al-kāfirīn*** — "and God does not guide the **disbelieving** people."

**A caution on this clause, because it is where the verse is most often mishandled.** *Al-qawm al-kāfirīn* here refers — on the plain reading of the verse's own comparison — to **those who give for show without belief in God and the Last Day**, the class named in the first half. **It is not a licence to declare a Muslim who struggled with showing off to be a disbeliever.** *Riyā'* is a disease that the tradition treats as a disease of believers, and the Prophet ﷺ said he feared it **for his Companions** — not for the disbelievers. **The fear of it is itself a sign that you have some of what the hypocrite lacks.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 265

**"And the example of those who donate their wealth, seeking Allah’s pleasure and believing the reward is certain, is that of a garden on a fertile hill: when heavy rain falls, it yields up twice its normal produce. If no heavy rain falls, a drizzle is sufficient. And Allah is All-Seeing of what you do."**

In simple terms: and the likeness of those who spend their wealth seeking the pleasure of God and confirming [the reward in] their own souls is as the likeness of a garden on a height — a downpour strikes it and it yields its produce twofold; and if no downpour strikes it, then a drizzle. And God sees what you do.

*Wa mathalu alladhīna yunfiqūna amwālahum **ibtighā'a marḍāti Allāhi wa tathbītan min anfusihim** ka-mathali **jannatin bi-rabwatin** aṣābahā wābilun fa-ātat ukulahā ḍi'fayn, fa-in lam yuṣibhā wābilun fa-ṭallun, wa Allāhu bi-mā ta'malūna baṣīr.*

**Read this verse against 2:264, because they are a matched pair and they share a word on purpose.**

*Ka-mathali **jannatin bi-rabwatin*** — "as the likeness of a **garden on a height**."

***Rabwah*** — a rise, high ground, a terrace. **The commentators note why: a garden on a height catches the light and the rain runs to it, and it does not become waterlogged.** It is the best-positioned ground there is.

**And here is the point of the pairing: 264 and 265 both feature a *wābil*, a downpour.**

- **2:264:** *aṣābahu **wābilun*** — a downpour strikes the rock **and strips it bare.**
- **2:265:** *aṣābahā **wābilun*** — a downpour strikes the garden **and it yields double.**

**The rain is the same. The result is opposite. The variable is the ground.**

**That is one of the most useful things in the Qur'an, and it is stated without comment.** God sends the same thing to both people: **the same opportunity, the same trial, the same wealth, the same loss, the same sickness, the same year.** What it produces depends entirely on what it lands on.

**And note the profitability of each.** The dust on the rock: **total loss, and the rain caused it.** The garden on the hill: **double yield from the heavy rain — and if the heavy rain does not come, *fa-ṭallun* — a drizzle is enough.**

***Fa-ṭallun*** — a light dew, a drizzle. **And that clause is the mercy in the verse.** The garden on the height does not need the downpour to produce. **A little is enough.**

**Which is the answer to the person who says "I can't give much."** The parable is not of a garden that only bears fruit in a flood. **It bears double in the flood and it bears at all in the drizzle.** The two verses together say: **a small gift on good ground outproduces a large one on rock.**

*Ibtighā'a **marḍāti Allāhi*** — "seeking the **pleasure of God**."

**And this is the ground.** Not the amount. Not the visibility. ***Marḍāt Allāh*** — the pleasure, the good-pleasure, the being-pleased of God. **The gift is addressed to Someone.**

*Wa **tathbītan min anfusihim*** — "and confirming [it] from their own souls."

**This clause is usually flattened in translation and it deserves attention.** *Tathbīt* — making firm, establishing, confirming. *Min anfusihim* — from themselves, from their own souls.

**Two readings are held:**

- **That they give with settled, firm selves** — no wavering, no second thoughts, no regret after the fact. **They are not talked out of it afterwards, not even by themselves.**
- **That they give in certainty that the reward is real** — their own souls confirm it, so the giving is not a gamble but a transaction they believe in.

**Both readings are held by the commentators, and they converge: **the interior is settled.** Which is exactly the opposite of 2:264's instability. **The dust is loose; the garden on the height is rooted.**

*Wa Allāhu bi-mā ta'malūna **baṣīr*** — "and God **sees** what you do."

***Baṣīr*** — the Seeing. **And note that this is the closing of the parable about the hidden motive.** The man giving for show is seen by people and unseen by himself. **God sees what you do — including the part of it that the audience cannot see and that you have not admitted.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 266

**"Would any of you wish to have a garden with palm trees, grapevines, and all kinds of fruits with rivers flowing underneath and as they grow very old with dependent children, a fiery whirlwind hits the garden, burning it all up? This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, so perhaps you will reflect."**

In simple terms: would any of you wish to have a garden of date-palms and vines, with rivers flowing beneath it, and for him in it every kind of fruit — while old age has struck him and he has weak offspring — and then a whirlwind with fire in it strikes it and it is burnt? Thus does God make clear to you the signs, so that you may reflect.

*A-yawaddu aḥadukum an takūna lahu jannatun min nakhīlin wa a'nābin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru lahu fīhā min kulli al-thamarāti wa aṣābahu al-kibaru wa lahu dhurriyyatun ḍu'afā'u, fa-aṣābahā i'ṣārun fīhi nār fa-ḥtaraqat, kadhālika yubayyinu Allāhu lakumu al-āyāti la'allakum tatafakkarūn.*

**Note the form of this verse: it is a question.**

*A-yawaddu aḥadukum* — "**would any of you wish**...?"

The interrogative of impossibility, the same device as 2:245's "who is it that will lend God a good loan." **The expected answer is: of course not. No one would want that.** And the argument is built on your own answer: **you would not accept this outcome for your property — so why are you building it for your soul?**

**Now the parable, and every detail of it is load-bearing.**

*Jannatun min **nakhīlin wa a'nābin** tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru lahu fīhā min **kulli al-thamarāt*** — "a garden of **date-palms and vines** with rivers flowing beneath it, and for him in it **every kind of fruit**."

**This is the fullest description of a garden anywhere in the Qur'an.** Palms, vines, running water, **and every kind of fruit.** The commentators note that a garden with every kind of fruit is the image of completeness — **nothing is missing from it.**

**And note: it is already mature.** The palms are bearing, the vines are bearing, the water is running. **This is not a sapling. This is a life's work, finished and productive.**

*Wa aṣābahu **al-kibaru** wa lahu **dhurriyyatun ḍu'afā'u*** — "and **old age** has struck him, and he has **weak offspring**."

**And here is the detail that makes the parable unbearable, and it is the detail people skip.**

**The garden is not destroyed early. It is destroyed at the end.** And at the end:
- **He is old.** He cannot replant. He does not have another thirty years.
- **His children are weak.** *Ḍu'afā'* — small, dependent, unable to help, unable to earn.

**So the loss is not merely the loss of the garden. It is the loss at the single moment in a life when it cannot be recovered.** A young man who loses a garden can plant another. **This man cannot, and the people who would normally help him cannot either.**

*Fa-aṣābahā **i'ṣārun fīhi nār fa-ḥtaraqat*** — "and a **whirlwind with fire in it** strikes it and it is burnt."

***I'ṣār*** — a whirlwind, a cyclone, a twisting column of wind. *Fīhi nār* — **with fire in it.** Not lightning that strikes one tree; **a fire carried on a wind**, which means it spreads to all of it.

**And note: nothing is said to be left.** *Fa-ḥtaraqat* — and it burnt. **Not "part of it." Not "the fruit." The garden.**

**Now the reports on what this parable means, and they disagree, and the disagreement matters.**

**The report of 'Umar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, recorded by al-Bukhārī** from Ibn 'Abbās and 'Ubayd ibn 'Umayr:

'Umar asked the Companions of the Messenger of God ﷺ: **"What is your view — about whom was this verse revealed?"** They said: **"God knows best."** **'Umar became angry** and said: **"Say 'we know' or 'we do not know'!"**

Ibn 'Abbās said: **"O Commander of the Believers, I have something in my soul concerning it."** 'Umar said: **"O my nephew, speak — and do not belittle yourself."**

Ibn 'Abbās said: **"It is a parable struck for a deed."** 'Umar said: "What deed?" He said: **"A wealthy man who works in the obedience of God, and then God sends the Shayṭān to him, and he works in disobedience — until his good deeds are annulled by it."**

**Two things about this report are worth more than the answer itself.**

**First: 'Umar's anger at "God knows best."** He did not accept it as piety. He heard it as a refusal to think. **The Commander of the Believers demanded an opinion from the room, and then he drew one out of the youngest man present.**

**Second: "speak, and do not belittle yourself."** The young man who knew the answer was the one who thought he should stay quiet. **'Umar overruled his modesty.** That is a model for how a community should run its intellectual life, and it is recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* next to the parable it produced.

Ibn Kathīr adds: **"This man annulled his former good works with his later evil works. And when he most desperately needed deeds of the former kind, there were none."**

**The second reading, also reported from Ibn 'Abbās** (by way of al-'Awfī, recorded by Ibn Abī Ḥātim): the parable describes **the disbeliever on the Day of Resurrection**, who returns to God with no good deeds to plead for him — **just as the man in the parable had no strength to replant and no children able to help him.**

**The third reading, argued by Ṭabāṭabā'ī in *al-Mīzān*, is narrower and deserves serious attention.** He reads the parable as fitting precisely **those who give and then follow the gift with reproach and injury**, so that the deed is nullified **and there is no way to return it to validity.** And he states the caution explicitly: **"Some people say that this verse shows that subsequent sins — and especially major ones — nullify the good deeds preceding them. But this inference is uncalled for. The verse only talks about reproach and injury with respect to charity."**

**That is a real disagreement with real consequences, and it should be stated as one.** The question of whether later major sins wipe out earlier good deeds (*iḥbāṭ*) is a classic point of difference between the Mu'tazilah, who affirmed it, and the Sunni position, which generally holds that good deeds are retained and that major sins are dealt with by God's will — by repentance, by expiation through good deeds, or by punishment — **without a mechanical cancelling of the ledger.** **I am not going to resolve it here. What can be said is that this verse is one of the texts both sides argue from, and that Ṭabāṭabā'ī's caution about over-reading it is well taken.**

**And now the thing that matters most, which is the boundary.**

**This is the most frightening verse in the batch, and it should be allowed to be frightening.** It is about reaching the end of a life with nothing. **The garden existed. It was beautiful. It was productive for a whole lifetime. And it is destroyed at the moment of maximum need, by a man who cannot start again.**

**But two things must be said alongside it, and both are binding:**

**1. No reading of this verse closes the door of repentance.** The man in the parable is old and his children are weak — **but the parable does not say he cannot plant again, and it does not say he is beyond recovery.** The tradition is emphatic that repentance is open until the soul reaches the throat (4:17–18, 39:53, and the hadith: "God accepts the repentance of His servant as long as the death-rattle has not reached his throat" — *Sunan al-Tirmidhī*, from Ibn 'Umar). **A verse that warns you about endings is not a verse that declares you finished.**

**2. No one may use this verse to declare a particular person lost.** I have held this boundary throughout, and it applies here with full force. **You may fear this parable for yourself. You may not aim it at your neighbour.**

*Kadhālika yubayyinu Allāhu lakumu al-āyāti la'allakum **tatafakkarūn*** — "thus does God make clear to you the signs, so that you may **reflect**."

**And note the closing faculty: *tatafakkarūn* — reflect.** The same verb as 2:219's close. **The surah asks you to think this one through for yourself.** It does not draw the conclusion for you. **It puts the picture in front of you and asks what you would do.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 267

**"O believers! Donate from the best of what you have earned and of what We have produced for you from the earth. Do not pick out worthless things for donation, which you yourselves would only accept with closed eyes. And know that Allah is Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy."**

In simple terms: O you who believe, spend from the good things you have earned and from what We brought forth for you from the earth. And do not aim at the bad of it to spend from it, when you would not take it yourselves except by closing your eyes to it. And know that God is Free of need, Praiseworthy.

*Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū anfiqū min **ṭayyibāti** mā kasabtum wa mimmā akhrajnā lakum min al-arḍi, wa lā **tayammamū al-khabītha minhu tunfiqūna** wa lastum bi-ākhidhīhi illā an **tughmiḍū fīh**, wa-'lamū anna Allāha ghaniyyun ḥamīd.*

*Anfiqū min **ṭayyibāti** mā kasabtum* — "spend from the **good things** of what you have earned."

***Ṭayyibāt*** — good, pure, wholesome, pleasant. **And the hadith that governs this verse is in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*, from Abū Hurayrah:** the Prophet ﷺ said — **"O people, God is Good (*ṭayyib*) and does not accept anything but what is good."** And he mentioned the man who travels far, dusty and dishevelled, stretching his hands to the sky, saying "O Lord, O Lord" — while his food is unlawful, his drink is unlawful, his clothing is unlawful and he is nourished by the unlawful. **"How, then, can he be answered?"**

**That hadith is the commentary on *ṭayyibāt*.** It is not only about the quality of the object given. **It is about the lawfulness of the source.** Charity from unlawful earnings is not charity.

**And note: the verse covers both sources.** *Mā kasabtum* — **what you earned** (trade, work, wages) — *wa mimmā akhrajnā lakum min al-arḍ* — **and what We brought forth for you from the earth** (crops, fruit, produce). **Both are in view, and both have a good portion and a bad portion.**

*Wa lā **tayammamū al-khabītha minhu** tunfiqūna* — "and do not **aim at the bad of it** to spend from it."

**Now the verb, and it is the key to the verse: *tayammamū*.**

***Tayammama*** means to aim at, to intend, to make for. **It is the same verb as the *tayammum* of ablution, where a person who finds no water "aims at" clean earth.** So the word carries the sense of **deliberate selection.**

**Which means the verse is not about accidentally giving something poor. It is about deliberately picking out the bad portion to give away.** The sin is in the choosing.

*Al-khabīth* — the bad, the filthy, the defective, the impure. **And note the phrase: *minhu* — "of it."** **The bad portion of your own wealth.** Not someone else's rejects. **Yours.**

*Wa lastum bi-ākhidhīhi illā an **tughmiḍū fīh*** — "when you would not take it yourselves **except by closing your eyes to it**."

***Tughmiḍū*** — to close the eyes, to shut them, to wink at a defect, to overlook. **The image is exact: someone offers you the thing, and you take it only by declining to look properly at what you are being given.**

**And this is the test, and it is the most useful sentence in the whole passage:**

**Would you accept this yourself?**

**Not: is it a lot. Not: is it more than they had. Not: is it better than nothing. *Would you take it — or would you only take it by closing your eyes?***

**That test settles every case.** The old jumper with the stain. The dented tin at the back of the cupboard. The expired tin. The fruit you would not put on your own table. **If you would only take it by winking at the defect, it is not fit to give to God.**

**The reported occasion, from al-Barā' ibn 'Āzib** (recorded by Ibn Jarīr, and by al-Tirmidhī and al-Nasā'ī in their books of tafsīr):

The Anṣār were date-palm owners. **At harvest a man would bring a bunch or two of dates and hang it on a rope stretched between two pillars in the Prophet's mosque, so that the poor Emigrants could eat from it.** The people of *al-Ṣuffah* had no food, and one of them would go up to the bunch and strike it with a stick, and ripe and unripe dates would fall and he would eat.

**And some — described in the report as those who did not hope for reward — began bringing a cluster of pitless, hard dates, or a cluster of damaged ones, and hanging it there.**

**And the verse was revealed.** Al-Barā' said: **"After that, one of us would bring the best that he had."**

**That last line is the whole verse in action.** The test was applied, and the giving changed quality. **Not quantity. Quality.**

**A second report concerns the *zakāt al-fiṭr***: some brought the varieties called *al-ju'rūr* and *al-mi'āfārah* — dates with little flesh and large stones — and the Prophet ﷺ said: **"Do not estimate these two, and do not bring any of them."**

*Wa-'lamū anna Allāha **ghaniyyun ḥamīd*** — "and know that God is **Free of need, Praiseworthy**."

***Ghaniyy*** again — **He does not need it.** Which is what makes the whole verse sting: **you are being asked for the good portion by Someone who has no need of any of it.** And Ibn Kathīr notes the purpose plainly: **"Although God commanded you to give away the purest of your wealth, He is far Richer than to need your charity — but the purpose is that the distance between the rich and the poor becomes less."**

***Ḥamīd*** — Praiseworthy, the One to whom all praise belongs whether or not you give. **He does not become praiseworthy by your gift.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 268

**"The Devil threatens you with ˹the prospect of˺ poverty and bids you to the shameful deed ˹of stinginess˺, while Allah promises you forgiveness and ˹great˺ bounties from Him. And Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing."**

In simple terms: the Shayṭān promises you poverty and commands you to indecency; and God promises you forgiveness from Himself and bounty. And God is All-Encompassing, Knowing.

*Al-shayṭānu ya'idukumu al-faqra wa ya'murukum bi-al-faḥshā'i, wa Allāhu ya'idukum maghfiratan minhu wa faḍlan, wa Allāhu wāsi'un 'alīm.*

**Two promises, placed side by side, and you must choose which to believe.**

*Al-shayṭānu **ya'idukumu al-faqra*** — "the Shayṭān **promises you poverty**."

**Note the verb: *ya'idu* — he promises, he threatens, he holds out to you.** **The same verb that is used of God in the very next clause.** The verse has set up two promisers with one word.

**And note the precise nature of the threat.** He does not *make* you poor. **He cannot.** What he does is **tell you that giving will make you poor.** The entire mechanism is a claim about the future, and the claim is a lie.

**Which is why the verse is so accurate about how the temptation actually arrives.** It does not arrive as greed. **It arrives as prudence.** It arrives as: *you can't afford it right now.* *What if something happens?* *Save a bit more first.* *You've already given enough this year.* **The voice that stops people giving is almost never a voice saying "be selfish." It is a voice saying "be sensible."**

*Wa ya'murukum bi-**al-faḥshā'*** — "and commands you to **indecency**."

***Faḥshā'*** — the shameful, the indecent, the exceeding of bounds. **The commentators differ on what it means here, and both readings should be given:**
- **Miserliness and withholding** — which is the reading the supplied translation follows, and it fits the context of a passage on charity. **On this reading the two clauses are one movement: he scares you into keeping, and keeping is itself the indecency.**
- **Indecency in general** — unlawful earning, sins committed for money, the things a person does because he would not give. **On this reading the two clauses are cause and effect: he frightens you with poverty, and the fear drives you into what is forbidden.**

**Both readings are held, and they converge: the fear of poverty is not neutral. It produces something.**

*Wa Allāhu ya'idukum **maghfiratan minhu wa faḍlan*** — "and God promises you **forgiveness from Himself and bounty**."

**And note the order of the two things God promises.**

***Maghfirah* comes first. Not wealth. Forgiveness.**

**That is the verse's deepest point, and it is the answer to anyone who has read 2:261 and concluded that charity is a good investment.** It is not. **It is better than that.** The primary thing promised for giving is **that you are forgiven** — which means **the reason to give is not return on capital. It is that you need forgiving.**

**And *faḍl* — bounty — comes second, and it is the same word closing 2:243 and 2:251.** Bounty: **the unearned extra.** Which is not a wage. **You are not being paid for the gift. You are being given more than the gift.**

**And the practical reading of the whole verse is this: the alternative to giving is not safety.** Satan offers you a picture of yourself protected by what you kept. **And the verse says: that picture is the thing that will cost you.** The choice is not between giving and security. **It is between trusting a liar about the future, and trusting God about it.**

*Wa Allāhu **wāsi'un 'alīm*** — "and God is **All-Encompassing, Knowing**."

The third appearance of this closing pair in ten verses (2:247, 2:261, 2:268). ***Wāsi'*** — there is no constriction in Him, so the fear of scarcity is a fear about the wrong object. ***'Alīm*** — He knows whether the fear was real or manufactured.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 269

**"Allah grants wisdom to whoever He wills. And whoever is granted wisdom is certainly blessed with a great privilege. But none will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason."**

In simple terms: He gives wisdom to whom He wills; and whoever is given wisdom has been given much good. And none remembers except those possessing cores of understanding.

*Yu'tī al-ḥikmata man yashā'u wa man yu'ta al-ḥikmata fa-qad ūtiya khayran kathīran, wa mā yadhdhakkaru illā ulū al-albāb.*

*Yu'tī **al-ḥikmata** man yashā'u* — "He gives **wisdom** to whom He wills."

**What is *ḥikmah*? The commentators give many answers, and Abū Ḥayyān gathered nearly thirty of them in *al-Baḥr al-Muḥīṭ* — and then observed that they are all close together and none contradicts another.** The main ones:

- **Ibn 'Abbās** (via 'Alī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah): **knowledge of the Qur'an.**
- **Mujāhid**: **understanding of the Qur'an.** (The ḥadīth in the two *Ṣaḥīḥ*s from Ibn Mas'ūd uses the same pairing — *ta'allamū al-Qur'ān wa 'allimūhu al-nās* — learn the Qur'an and teach it to the people.)
- **Muqātil**: **the exhortations of the Qur'an and the rulings it contains.**
- **Ibn Qutaybah**: **knowledge and action together** — no one is a *ḥakīm* unless he has both.
- **Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī**: when *ḥikmah* is attributed to God it means **comprehensive knowledge and the firm excellence of bringing all things into being**; when attributed to a creature it means **rightly-guided knowledge of what exists, together with the action that corresponds to it.**
- **Al-Jalālayn**: **the beneficial knowledge that leads to righteous action.**

**All of these converge on the same thing: *ḥikmah* is not intelligence, and it is not information.** It is knowledge that has become action — **knowing the right thing and doing it, at the right time, in the right measure.** Mālik's saying is often quoted to the same effect.

**And note the verb: *yu'tī* — He *gives* it.** Wisdom is not earned by study alone. **A person can accumulate enormous learning without being given this.** Which is why the verse can say what it says next.

*Wa man yu'ta al-ḥikmata fa-qad ūtiya **khayran kathīran*** — "and whoever is given wisdom has been given **much good**."

**Note the scale of the claim: much good. Not "a good thing." *Khayran kathīran* — abundant good.**

**Which means: wisdom outranks wealth in the Qur'an's own valuation.** 2:247 said God gives kingship to whom He wills; **2:269 says He gives wisdom to whom He wills, and then states that this is the greater gift.** The whole passage from 2:261 onward has been about money, **and it climaxes not with money but with the thing that lets you use money properly.**

**And the ḥadīth that belongs to this verse, in the two *Ṣaḥīḥ*s from Ibn Mas'ūd:** the Prophet ﷺ said — **"There is no envy except in two cases: a man to whom God has given wealth and He has empowered him to spend it in the truth; and a man to whom God has given wisdom, and he judges by it and teaches it."**

**Note what the hadith does: it makes the two things envy-worthy in the permitted sense** — the sense of *ghibṭah*, wishing you had the same without wishing the other person lost it. **And note the pairing: wealth *spent in the truth*, and wisdom *used and taught*.** Neither is praiseworthy as a possession. **Both are praiseworthy only in use.** Which is precisely the point of everything from 2:261 to 2:268.

*Wa mā yadhdhakkaru illā **ulū al-albāb*** — "and none is mindful except **the possessors of cores**."

**And the last word is the one to linger on.**

***Al-albāb*** is the plural of ***lubb*** — **the inner kernel, the pith, the heart of a nut.** *Ulū al-albāb* are **those who have the kernel**, as opposed to the shell.

**The Qur'an uses "those of understanding" repeatedly (2:179, 3:7, 3:190, 13:19, 38:43, 39:9, 39:18, 40:54), and the image is consistent: most of what a person carries is husk — information, opinion, noise, the outer part of things. The *lubb* is what remains when all that is stripped away.** **Wisdom is the kernel. And only people who have gone after the kernel recognise it when it is offered.**

**And note the third faculty-closing in the surah's legislative blocks:** *tatafakkarūn* (2:219 reflect), *yatadhakkarūn* (2:221 remember), *ta'qilūn* (2:242 reason), and now ***yadhdhakkaru* — is mindful** — restricted to *ulū al-albāb*. **The surah keeps naming the faculty each passage requires, and here it names the people who have one.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 270

**"Whatever charities you give or vows you make are surely known to Allah. And the wrongdoers will have no helpers."**

In simple terms: and whatever you spend of spending, or whatever vow you vow — indeed, God knows it. And for the wrongdoers there are no helpers.

*Wa mā anfaqtum min nafaqatin aw nadhartum min nadhrin fa-inna Allāha ya'lamuhu, wa mā li-al-ẓālimīna min anṣār.*

*Wa mā anfaqtum min **nafaqatin*** — "and whatever you spend of **spending**."

***Nafaqah*** — the word covers everything given out: the obligatory *zakāt*, the voluntary *ṣadaqah*, and ordinary maintenance. **And the form is emphatically general: *mā anfaqtum min nafaqatin* — whatever spending you spend, of any kind.**

**After nine verses on the conditions, the quality, and the nullifiers of giving, the passage closes on: it is all known.**

*Aw **nadhartum min nadhrin*** — "or whatever **vow** you vow."

**And note what has been placed next to spending: the vow.**

***Nadhr*** — a vow, a solemn commitment to do an act of obedience for God's sake. **And its pairing with spending here is exact: the vow is the promise of a future gift, and the spending is the gift itself.** So the verse covers **what you gave and what you promised to give.**

**And the Sunnah's teaching on vows is sharper than most people expect.** In the two *Ṣaḥīḥ*s from Ibn 'Umar, the Prophet ﷺ **forbade vows** and said: **"It does not avert anything; it is only by means of it that something is extracted from the miser."**

**That hadith deserves a moment, because it cuts against how vows are usually used.** A vow does not change what was decreed. **It does not move God.** What it does is **get something out of a stingy man** — the vow is a device by which a person forces his own hand.

**Which means: the vow is not a lever on God. It is a lever on yourself.** And it is permitted as a means, permitted to be fulfilled when it is an act of obedience (the tradition holds that a vow to do an act of obedience must be fulfilled, while a vow to do an act of disobedience must not be), **but it is not recommended as a strategy, because the One who knows the outcome already knew it before you promised.**

*Fa-inna Allāha **ya'lamuhu*** — "indeed, **God knows it**."

**And this is the verse's whole function.** After a passage about inner motives — showing off, reminding, the settling of the soul, the quality of what is given — **the closing is that all of it is known.** Nothing in 2:261–269 happens in private. **Not the amount, not the motive, not the reminder that was never spoken aloud, not the vow that was quietly forgotten.**

*Wa mā li-**al-ẓālimīna** min **anṣār*** — "and the wrongdoers have **no helpers**."

***Anṣār*** — helpers, those who give victory, those who come to your aid. **The wrongdoer will arrive with no one.**

**And note who the wrongdoers are in context.** The passage has named several: **the one who reminds and injures (264), the one who gives to be seen (264), the one who nullifies his own charity (264, 266), the one who picks out the bad portion to give (267).** These are the *ẓālimūn* of this passage — **and the wrong in each case is a wrong done to their own gift.**

**And the closing is deliberately cold after so much encouragement.** Ten verses of promise, parable, multiplication, and the gift of wisdom — and then: **the wrongdoers will have no helpers.** The surah does not end the passage on reassurance. **It ends on the fact that none of it is unseen, and that no one is coming to help.**

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis: Verses 261–270

**The arc.** One subject, ten verses, and a clear structure: **the promise** (261) → **the two conditions** (262–263) → **the two nullifying parables and the two contrasting grounds** (264–265) → **the parable of the destroyed garden** (266) → **the quality requirement** (267) → **the spiritual combat** (268) → **the gift that makes it all visible** (269) → **the knowledge that closes it** (270).

**The matched pairs are the key to reading the batch.** **2:264 and 2:265 share a word on purpose: the *wābil*, the downpour.** On the rock it strips; on the garden it doubles. **The rain is identical. The ground is not.** And **2:265 and 2:266 are two gardens** — one that yields double even from a drizzle, and one that burns to nothing at the end of a life. **Read them together or you will misread both.**

**And the governing question of the batch is not "how much?" but "what does it land on?"** Every verse here is about the interior: the reminder, the injury, the showing off, the settled soul, the deliberate selection of the bad portion, the lie about the future. **The amount is never once quantified.** Which is why the passage opens with a seed and closes with the kernel — **the whole thing is about what is inside.**

**Two threads connect back across the surah.** First: **the closing faculties.** *Tatafakkarūn* (2:219), *yatadhakkarūn* (2:221), *ta'qilūn* (2:242), and now *yadhdhakkaru* restricted to *ulū al-albāb* (2:269) and *tatafakkarūn* again (2:266). **The surah keeps naming the faculty each passage requires, and this passage requires the kernel.** Second: **2:267's *tayammamū* echoes the *tayammum* of ablution** — the same verb for "aiming at" clean earth is used here for deliberately aiming at the bad portion of your wealth. **The word choice is a rebuke: you aim carefully in worship, and you aim carefully in stinginess too.**

**Where the supplied translation resolves an ambiguity, it has been marked.** At 2:268, *al-faḥshā'* is rendered "the shameful deed of stinginess," which follows one of the two transmitted readings; the broader reading (indecency in general, including unlawful earning) has been given alongside it. At 2:266, the rendering "as they grow very old with dependent children" follows the majority reading of *wa aṣābahu al-kibaru*. And at 2:270, "vows" renders *nadhr*, which in the jurists' usage is a specific undertaking and not merely a promise.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given. This is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and supplying precise references from memory would mean inventing them. **Where the scholars differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved:** what the parable of 2:266 refers to (the man who does good then evil, per Ibn 'Abbās in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī*; the disbeliever on the Day of Resurrection, per the report from Ibn 'Abbās via al-'Awfī; the giver whose charity is nullified by reproach with no route back to validity, per Ṭabāṭabā'ī); whether later major sins nullify prior good deeds generally; the meaning of *al-faḥshā'* in 2:268; the meaning of *tathbītan min anfusihim* in 2:265; and the definition of *ḥikmah* in 2:269, on which Abū Ḥayyān collected nearly thirty positions.

**Transmitted reports have been flagged as reports throughout** — the occasion of 2:267 from al-Barā' ibn 'Āzib, the report of the *ju'rūr* and *mi'āfārah* dates, and the narration on *riyā'* from Maḥmūd ibn Labīd, whose grading is disputed.

**Three boundaries have been observed.** On 2:264: the clause *Allāhu lā yahdī al-qawma al-kāfirīn* has been read within the verse's own terms, **not as a licence to declare a Muslim who struggles with showing off to be a disbeliever** — *riyā'* is a disease the Prophet ﷺ feared for his Companions, and the fear of it is evidence against the diagnosis. On 2:266: **no reading of the verse closes the door of repentance**, and **no one may use it to declare a particular person lost**; the parable warns about endings and does not pronounce on anyone. And on 2:261: the seven hundred has been treated as a parable about the ratio of reward, **not as a botanical or scientific claim**, consistent with the rule that the Qur'an is not read as a text of natural science and should not be exposed to a refutation it does not need.

The most useful sentence in the batch is in 2:267, and it is a test rather than a ruling: **would you take it — or would you only take it by closing your eyes?** Apply that to the thing you are about to give, and the whole passage from 2:261 to 2:270 resolves into a single question about what is inside the gift.
