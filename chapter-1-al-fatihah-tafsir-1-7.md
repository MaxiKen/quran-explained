# Surah al-Fatihah (The Opening) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 1–7

We are now entering Surah al-Fatihah, the seven verses with which the Qur'an opens and which every Muslim recites in every unit of every prayer. In the arrangement of the Qur'an it is the first surah; in meaning it is a summary of the whole Book. The early scholars called it *Umm al-Qur'an* (the Mother of the Qur'an) and *Umm al-Kitab*, because it gathers the Qur'an's three great concerns — correct belief about Allah, correct worship of Him, and the returning to Him for judgement — into a few lines. The Prophet ﷺ called it *as-Sab' al-Mathani*, "the seven oft-repeated verses," and said that nothing like it had been sent down in the earlier scriptures. Most scholars hold that it was revealed in Makkah, though a number of early authorities said it was revealed in Madinah, and some said it was revealed twice — once in Makkah and once in Madinah — which is why it can be described with both contexts. Whatever the exact timing, it was revealed as a *du'a*: a prayer that Allah Himself taught His servants, which is why a servant recites it standing before his Lord and Allah answers him verse by verse. The ten verses we are about to examine — in this surah's case, all seven — move from praising Allah, to acknowledging His mercy and His judgement, to declaring exclusive worship and reliance, and finally to asking for the one thing without which nothing else can be set right: guidance.

---

## Verse 1

**"In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful."**

In simple terms, Allah teaches His servants to begin everything with His name, and He opens His Book by describing Himself as the fountain of mercy. Before a single command or story is given, the reader is told who is speaking: not a distant force, not a harsh master, but Allah — the One whose mercy is His most prominent relationship with His creation.

The verse opens with the phrase *bismi* (in the Name). The small attached particle *bi-* carries the sense of seeking help and blessing "with" or "by means of" something, so the phrase means: I begin this action seeking help and blessing through the name of Allah. Grammarians of Basra and Kufa long discussed why the short connecting vowel of *ism* (name) is dropped in writing; the Basran grammarians explained it as an omission made permanent through the sheer frequency of the phrase, while the Kufans accounted for it differently. The practical point is unaffected: the phrase is so central to a Muslim's life that the language itself shortened it through constant use.

The word *ism* (name) itself was derived by the Basran grammarians from *al-sumuww*, meaning loftiness and elevation — a name is what raises a thing into notice and makes it known — while the Kufan grammarians derived it from *al-wasm*, meaning a mark or sign, since a name is the sign by which a thing is recognised. Both derivations point to the same function: a name makes the one named present to the mind. So when a believer says "in the name of Allah," he is not uttering a protective formula or a magical syllable; he is consciously placing Allah before his own mind and acknowledging that whatever he is about to do happens by Allah's permission and help.

*Allah* is His proper name, the name that belongs to none other. Most linguists derive it from *ilah* (a deity, one who is worshipped), with the definite article fused into the word so that it means "the One who alone deserves worship." It is the name that gathers every attribute of perfection. Reports speak of a single "greatest name" of Allah, and later scholars collected many opinions about which name it is — some said it is *Allah* itself, others *al-Hayy al-Qayyum* (the Ever-Living, the Self-Sustaining), others *Dhu'l-Jalali wa'l-Ikram*, and some held the knowledge of it is not fixed to one word at all. We cannot settle that question with certainty, and it is not necessary to; what is certain is that *Allah* is the name no one else may bear and the name with which every act of worship is opened.

The two names that follow, *ar-Rahman* and *ar-Rahim*, share the root *r-h-m*, the same root as *rahm* (the womb) — the organ of the most instinctive, unconditional mercy we know. Both are intensive forms in Arabic, but they intensify in different ways. *Ar-Rahman*, on the pattern *fa'lan*, suggests vastness, fullness and overflow; *ar-Rahim*, on the pattern *fa'il*, suggests a settled attribute, mercy continuously bestowed. The classical commentators drew a distinction that has become standard: *ar-Rahman* describes the mercy that reaches every creature in this life — believer and unbeliever, human and animal — while *ar-Rahim* describes the mercy specially reserved for the believers in the next. Al-Qurtubi and others support this with the Qur'an's own words: "My mercy encompasses everything" (7:156), and "He is to the believers especially merciful" (33:43). A second, related point they made: *ar-Rahman* is a name used only for Allah and cannot be applied to a human being, whereas *ar-Rahim* can be, as when Allah describes His Prophet as "kind and merciful to the believers" (9:128). This is historically illustrated by an incident recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari at the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah: when the Prophet ﷺ dictated "In the name of Allah, the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful," the Qurayshi negotiator Suhayl ibn 'Amr objected, saying, "As for *ar-Rahman*, by Allah I do not know what it is," and asked him to write "In Your name, O Allah" instead. The pagan Arabs knew the word *Allah*, but this intensive divine name of mercy was unfamiliar to them — which shows how deliberately the Qur'an placed mercy at its very threshold.

A note on where this verse sits: the scholars differed on whether the Basmalah is an independent first verse of al-Fatihah or a verse revealed to separate the surahs from one another. The reciters of Kufa and Imam al-Shafi'i counted it as the first verse of al-Fatihah; Imam Abu Hanifa and the Madinan and Basran counts regarded it as a verse of the Qur'an placed at the head of each surah but not part of al-Fatihah itself; Imam Malik's well-known position was that it is not a verse of the Qur'an except within the letter of Sulayman in Surah al-Naml (27:30). This is why some counts reach seven verses by including the Basmalah and others by splitting the closing passage of the surah. In practice these differences produced differences in the prayer: the Shafi'is recite the Basmalah aloud in audible prayers, while the Hanafis, Malikis and Hanbalis recite it silently, and the Malikis generally do not recite it in the obligatory prayer at all. Every one of these positions rests on evidence and on the practice transmitted from the early community, so none of them should be treated as a violation of the Sunnah. It is also worth noting that Surah al-Tawbah is the only surah that does not begin with the Basmalah; the commentators explained this by saying that al-Tawbah was revealed with a declaration of ultimatum and warfare, and by a report attributed to 'Uthman that al-Anfal and al-Tawbah were so closely paired that they were once called "the two companions," so the Basmalah was not written between them.

Now bring this into our own lives. The Sunnah turned this verse into a habit that marks the whole day. The Prophet ﷺ taught a child eating his first meal with the adults: "Say *Bismillah*, eat with your right hand, and eat from what is nearest to you." In the authentic narration recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Ibn 'Abbas, he taught that if a man says, when he approaches his wife, "In the name of Allah — O Allah, keep Satan away from us and keep Satan away from what You provide us," and a child is decreed for them, Satan will never harm that child. There are also well-known narrations about mentioning Allah's name when entering and leaving the house, and a widely circulated report — reported by Abu Dawud and others, and disputed in its chain, with some scholars grading it sound on account of its multiple routes and others, including al-Albani, grading it weak — that "every important matter that does not begin with the name of Allah is cut off from blessing." We should be honest about that grading, but its content is supported by the whole spirit of the Sunnah, which begins acts with Allah's name.

So what does this mean for you, concretely? It means that before you type the difficult email, sign the contract, open the business, sit the exam, drive the car, or walk into a conversation that could go badly, you pause for one second and say "in the name of Allah." That pause does three things at once: it reminds you that the outcome is not ultimately in your hands, it invokes the mercy of the One who can make the outcome better than you planned, and it drags the act out of heedlessness into worship. The verse is teaching a frame of mind, not a formality. And notice that the name invoked is a name of mercy: whatever you are beginning, you are beginning it in the hands of the Most Merciful.

## Verse 2

**"All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds,[1]"**

In simple terms, the servant now says: every kind of praise, at every time, in every place, belongs to Allah alone, because He is the One who brought everything into being, sustains it moment by moment, and brings it to its completion. He is not the God of one tribe, one people or one planet; He is the Lord of every order of creation that exists.

The first word deserves careful attention. *Al-hamdu* is praise, but not exactly the same as *al-shukr*, thankfulness. The scholars of language distinguished them: *shukr* is gratitude shown in return for a favour received, while *hamd* is praise given for a quality or perfection that the praised one possesses — whether or not that perfection has reached you personally. That is why you can praise a person for generosity you never benefited from, but you can only thank him for what he actually gave you. And because *hamd* is praise for inherent perfection, all of it belongs to Allah: every perfection anyone has ever praised, in any language, is ultimately His, since He is the one who gave it. The definite article *al-* at the start of *al-hamdu* adds a second layer: not "some praise" but "all praise," comprehensively. So the phrase is not a polite opening; it is the most complete statement of acknowledgement a creature can make.

Then the word *Rabb*, usually rendered "Lord." It is far richer than "owner" or "master," though it includes both. Its root is *r-b-b*, from *tarbiyah*: to nurture, to rear, to bring something along gradually, stage by stage, until it reaches its completion. A *rabb* is therefore the one who owns a thing, governs it, and cares for it until it becomes what it was meant to be. Al-Tabari and al-Qurtubi bring early explanations along these lines: the *Rabb* is the master who is obeyed, the owner who disposes, and the nurturer who sets His creation right. That means the Qur'an introduces Allah not as a distant creator who wound up the universe and left, but as the One actively sustaining every creature at every instant. When you say "Lord of all worlds," you are confessing that your next heartbeat, your child's growth, the rain on your farm and the orbit of a planet are all being continuously held by the same care.

*Al-'alamin* is the plural of *'alam*, "a world" or "an order of creation" — everything by which Allah is known, since the root carries the sense of a sign or mark. The early authorities differed, helpfully, on its scope. A report from Ibn 'Abbas explains it as the jinn and mankind; Sa'id ibn Jubayr and others said the same. Others, including reports from Qatadah and from Abu Sa'id al-Khudri, widened it to everything in existence besides Allah — all the "throngs" of creation, which is why the term is plural rather than singular. A third view, associated with grammarians such as al-Farra' and al-Zajjaj, held that *'alamin* refers specifically to beings endowed with intellect — the angels, the jinn and the humans — because a "world" properly means a community of moral agents. These views are not really in conflict; they describe the same word at different levels of specificity, and the safest reading is the broadest: every order of created existence known to us and unknown to us has Allah as its Lord. Modern writers sometimes rush from this plural to claim that the Qur'an predicted a multiverse or parallel universes. We should be careful here. The verse says nothing about the structure of physical cosmology; it affirms that whatever worlds exist, of whatever kind, are of one Lord. That affirmation is true regardless of what cosmology turns out to be correct, and it should not be made to depend on a currently fashionable scientific hypothesis.

Why does the surah begin with praise rather than with a request? Because gratitude is the etiquette of asking. A person who praises first has recognised that the One he is about to petition has already given him more than he can count; the request then comes from humility rather than entitlement. The Sunnah makes the value of this phrase concrete. In Sahih Muslim, the Prophet ﷺ said that purification is half of faith, and that *al-hamdu lillah* fills the scales — meaning the immense scale of deeds on the Day of Resurrection is filled by these few words when they are said with presence of heart. And in the hadith qudsi recorded by Muslim from Abu Hurayrah about the division of the prayer, Allah says: when the servant says *al-hamdu lillahi rabbi'l-'alamin*, He responds, "My servant has praised Me." The entire Umayyad-era and later tradition of commenting on this surah rests on that hadith: al-Fatihah is not merely recitation, it is a conversation in which every line has an answer.

There is a deeper lesson here that is easy to miss. Praise is the opposite of complaint. Human beings are extraordinarily quick to notice what is missing and slow to notice what is present, which is why modern psychology has had to "discover" gratitude practice and demonstrate — with real, if modest, evidence — that deliberately counting blessings improves mood, sleep and resilience. The Qur'an placed that practice at the head of its Book and attached to it a promise that it fills the scale. In your own life, this verse asks you to notice: the body that works, the mind that is clear, the roof, the food, the people you love, the faith you did not earn by your own cleverness. Saying *al-hamdu lillah* in the morning is not a formality to get through; it is the deliberate re-orientation of a heart that would otherwise spend the whole day measuring its life by what it lacks.

## Verse 3

**"the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful,"**

In simple terms, this verse repeats and reaffirms what the first verse said: Allah is the One whose mercy is vast beyond measure and who constantly bestows that mercy on His creation. The repetition is deliberate, and it tells you that mercy is not one attribute among many in this surah — it is the lens through which everything else in it should be read.

The first question a careful reader asks is: why say it twice? The scholars answered in several complementary ways, and none of them makes the repetition redundant. First, repetition in Arabic rhetoric is a recognised device for emphasis (*tawkid*), and placing the two names of mercy at the very beginning of the Qur'an and then again as the second verse of al-Fatihah signals that this is the headline of the whole revelation. Second, al-Razi and others noted that the two places carry slightly different work: in the Basmalah the names of mercy bless the act of beginning; here they explain *why* Allah deserves the praise of verse 2. He is to be praised because He is merciful, and because His lordship over the worlds is exercised through mercy rather than through mere power. Third — and this is the point the hadith makes vivid — in the prayer, each phrase is a separate address with a separate response. In the hadith qudsi of Sahih Muslim, when the servant says *ar-Rahmani'r-Rahim*, Allah says, "My servant has extolled Me." So the verse is not a stutter; it is a distinct station in the conversation.

The root *r-h-m* deserves a moment. From it come *rahmah* (mercy), *rahim* (the womb), and the whole vocabulary of tenderness in Arabic. The connection the classical commentators drew between mercy and the womb is not a pun; it is a lesson. The mercy a mother feels for her child, the most self-forgetting love most human beings ever witness, is a created, finite, borrowed fragment of the mercy of Allah. This is exactly what an authentic hadith in the two Sahihs says: "Allah created mercy as one hundred parts. He kept ninety-nine parts with Him and sent down one part to the earth, and it is because of that one part that creation shows mercy to one another, so much so that a mare raises her hoof over her foal for fear of harming it." If a hundredth part of His mercy produces every act of kindness ever done in human history, then the ninety-nine parts He has kept for the Day of Resurrection are beyond our imagination. A related authentic narration makes the practical link: "The womb (*al-rahim*) is a branch of *ar-Rahman*; whoever maintains it, Allah maintains him, and whoever cuts it, Allah cuts him off." Mercy in your family relationships is therefore not a separate chapter of the religion from mercy in theology; the same name governs both.

On the theological level, the early community was unanimous that Allah is described by mercy as He described Himself. Where later schools diverged was over *how*. The mainstream position — that of the early generations and of the Ash'ari and Maturidi tradition — is that mercy is a real attribute of Allah, affirmed without asking "how" (*bi-la kayf*), without likening it to the mercy of creatures and without denying it. The Mu'tazila, reasoning that an attribute of softness or inclination would imply change in God, reinterpreted Allah's mercy as His will to do good to His servants. The difference matters because it decides whether "Allah is merciful" is a description of who He is or merely a label for what He does. The Qur'an's own usage supports the mainstream reading: it speaks of a mercy that encompasses, that is decreed, that precedes wrath.

And there is the balancing truth that prevents this verse from being misused. Mercy in the Qur'an is never sentimentality. The same Allah who says "My mercy encompasses everything" also made that mercy conditional in its fullest form: "I will decree it for those who have taqwa, give zakah, and believe in Our signs" (7:156). The Prophet ﷺ said in a hadith qudsi recorded by al-Bukhari, "My mercy prevails over My wrath" — prevailing, not cancelling. So a person who hears "Most Merciful" and concludes that obedience is optional has taken hold of half a verse. The correct response to the divine mercy is hope that acts: hope that gets you out of sin rather than hope that keeps you comfortable in it. The Prophet ﷺ also said, in a hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim, "Allah says: I am as My servant thinks of Me" — which means the quality of your assumption about Allah shapes the quality of your relationship with Him.

Bring this into your own life now. A well-known narration reported by Abu Dawud and al-Tirmidhi — regarded as sound by a number of scholars — states: "The merciful are shown mercy by *ar-Rahman*. Be merciful to those on the earth and the One in the heaven will have mercy on you." This is the practical shape of the verse: the way to receive the attribute is to reflect it. Concretely, that shows up in how you speak to the person who made a mistake at work, whether you give the delivery rider a moment's grace, whether you forgive your sibling, whether you are harsh with your own children over a spilled cup, whether you allow yourself to be gentle with your own faults as you struggle to change them. Every act of mercy you extend is a claim on the mercy of the One whose name you have just pronounced — and every act of unnecessary harshness is a decision to live as though you did not need it.

## Verse 4

**"Master of the Day of Judgment."**

In simple terms, after telling us about His mercy, Allah tells us that He is the sole owner and king of the Day when every human being will be brought to account and every deed will be repaid in full. The surah deliberately places judgement immediately after mercy so that a believer lives with both hope and fear, never despairing and never careless.

Two well-known Qur'anic readings (*qira'at*) of the opening word exist, and both are authentically transmitted from the Prophet ﷺ. One reading is *Maliki yawmi'd-din*, with a long vowel — "Owner of the Day of Judgement" — and this is the reading of 'Asim and al-Kisa'i. The other is *Maliki yawmi'd-din*, with a short vowel — "King of the Day of Judgement" — and this is read by most of the other canonical reciters. These are not rival claims that could conflict; they are two facets of one truth, revealed as a mercy of expansion. "Owner" stresses that on that Day no one will possess anything, control anything, or dispose of anything except by His leave. "King" stresses that all authority, judgement and command will belong to Him alone, and no intercession will avail except by His permission. Al-Qurtubi and Abu Hayyan both point out that the two readings together close every escape route a person might imagine on that Day — the escape of ownership and the escape of influence.

The phrase *yawm al-din* is usually translated "the Day of Judgement," but the Arabic word *din* is doing more work than that. Its root carries the sense of requital, of returning like for like — the Arabs had a proverb, "as you judge, you will be judged" (*kama tadinu tudanu*), which uses this very root. *Din* also carries the sense of debt and of submission, which is why the same word means "religion": religion is the debt of obedience the creature owes, and the Day of *Din* is the Day that debt is settled. Al-Tabari's explanation, followed by most later commentators, is therefore that it is "the Day on which every soul is repaid in full for what it earned — good for good, evil for evil — with no injustice." So the verse is not only about a courtroom scene; it is about the moment when the moral structure of the universe, which so often looks crooked and unenforced in this life, is made straight and public.

Why is only this Day mentioned when Allah owns everything at all times? The scholars gave a beautiful answer: because in this world Allah has given human beings a delegated, temporary ownership and authority, so a person can say "my house," "my money," "my decision," and a king can rule. On that Day all of that is stripped away. The Qur'an states it plainly: "To whom belongs the dominion this Day? To Allah, the One, the Prevailing" (40:16), and "The dominion on that Day belongs to Allah; He will judge between them" (22:56). Al-Zamakhshari also drew attention to a grammatical subtlety: attaching the word "Owner/King" to a Day that has not yet arrived speaks of it as though it were already present and settled, an idiom that conveys certainty. The Resurrection is not a possibility the verse entertains; it is a fixed appointment.

There is an important ordering to notice. Mercy (verses 1 and 3) comes before judgement (verse 4), and in the arrangement of the surah, judgement comes before worship and the request for guidance (verses 5–7). The commentators, including al-Razi and Ibn Kathir, observed that this is the pedagogy of the Qur'an: the heart is opened by mercy, sobered by accountability, and only then asked for its worship and its trust. A person who knows only mercy becomes presumptuous; a person who knows only accountability becomes despairing or mechanical. The surah holds both together in four verses.

In the hadith qudsi of Sahih Muslim on the division of the prayer, when the servant reaches *Maliki yawmi'd-din*, Allah says: "My servant has glorified Me" — and in one version of the narration, "My servant has entrusted his affair to Me." That second wording is worth sitting with. Reciting this verse in prayer is itself an act of handing your case over to the only Judge who cannot be bribed, misled or exhausted, and whose judgement is never separated from His mercy.

What does accountability look like in ordinary modern life? Let's be genuinely practical. The verse trains what you might call an audit mentality, and it applies most sharply exactly where no human auditor will ever look. It applies to the invoice you could inflate because the client will never check. It applies to the expense claim, the tax return, the CV line that stretches the truth, the working hours you logged while scrolling, the exam you could pass by cheating with no realistic chance of being caught, the online review you were paid to write, the borrowed item you could quietly keep, the promise to a child you could forget. In every one of those situations, the calculation in this life is "probability of being caught," and it is often near zero. The verse announces a Day on which the probability is one. Al-Tirmidhi records the Prophet ﷺ saying that the feet of the son of Adam will not move on that Day until he is asked about five things: his life and how he spent it, his youth and how he used it, his wealth — where he earned it and where he spent it — and what he did with his knowledge. That is a job description for self-examination before the audit arrives: review your own ledger while reviewing is still possible.

## Verse 5

**"You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help."**

In simple terms, after praising Allah, the servant turns and addresses Him directly: we worship no one but You, and we turn to no one but You for the help that only You can give. This verse is the hinge of the surah and, in the view of many scholars, the verse around which the whole Qur'an turns, because it states both halves of Islam's central claim: that worship belongs to Allah alone, and that reliance belongs to Allah alone.

The first thing an attentive reader notices is the shift. Verses 1 to 4 spoke *about* Allah in the third person — "the Most Merciful," "Master of the Day of Judgment." Here the surah suddenly switches to "You." In Arabic rhetoric this is called *iltifat*, a turn of address, and al-Zamakhshari explained its effect here beautifully: the servant begins by praising his Lord as though describing someone absent, and then, having brought His perfection before his own heart, he turns to face Him and speak to Him directly. It is the movement from hearing about God to standing in His presence. If you have ever felt your prayer go flat, this is usually the missing element: recitation without the inner turn of address.

Then there is the word order. In normal Arabic, the object comes after the verb: *na'buduka* — "we worship You." Here, unusually, the object is pulled to the front: *iyyaka na'budu*. Arabic grammar treats fronting as a device of restriction, so the meaning becomes "You alone we worship" — not merely "we worship You (among others)." The same fronting is then repeated with the second verb, and the repetition of *iyyaka* is itself emphatic: exclusivity is being asserted twice, once for worship and once for reliance. So the verse is a double declaration of *tawhid*: no partner in worship, and no partner in ultimate reliance.

*Na'budu*, "we worship," comes from a root whose core sense is humility and lowliness, but worship in the Qur'an is not only humility. Ibn Taymiyyah gave the definition that became standard: worship is "a comprehensive term for everything that Allah loves and is pleased with, of words and deeds, outward and inward." Ibn al-Qayyim added that worship stands on two pillars that must meet: the utmost love of Allah and the utmost humility before Him. Love without humility produces casual familiarity; humility without love produces joyless servitude. The Qur'an's own definition of the purpose of creation sits behind all of this: "I did not create the jinn and mankind except to worship Me" (51:56). So this verse is not one religious duty among others; it is the reason you exist, being declared by you.

*Nasta'in*, "we seek help," from the root *'-w-n*. Note the sequence: worship is mentioned first, and the request for help second, even though in experience you need help *in order to* worship. The commentators explain the wisdom in several ways. Worship is the goal and help is the means, and goals are stated before means. Also, seeking help *for* worship places the request in its proper orbit: we are not asking for help in order to serve ourselves, we are asking for help in order to serve Him. And there is a subtle cure for pride here: by asking for help with the very act of worship, the servant confesses that even his worship is a gift, not an achievement he can boast of.

Notice also that both verbs are plural — "we worship," "we seek help." The servant does not say "I," even though he prays alone. The commentators gave several reasons: it is the language of the congregation; it identifies the worshipper with the whole community of believers so that he asks on their behalf and they, in effect, ask on his; and it is a protection against spiritual individualism. Islam did not give us a religion of private, self-designed devotion. Even in the prayer you perform in an empty room, you pray as a member of a body.

The Sunnah sharpens the meaning of the second half. A well-known narration reported by al-Tirmidhi from Ibn 'Abbas — graded sound by many scholars — records the Prophet ﷺ saying: "If you ask, ask Allah; and if you seek help, seek help from Allah." This must not be misunderstood as a ban on asking human beings for ordinary help. The Qur'an commands cooperation: "Cooperate with one another in righteousness and piety" (5:2), and the Prophet ﷺ accepted and gave ordinary help throughout his life. What the verse forbids is turning to anyone or anything other than Allah for what only Allah can do — for the relief that comes from the unseen, for the outcome that no one controls, for protection, provision in its ultimate sense, and for the ultimate good of your soul. The dividing line is simple and searching: if the thing you are relying on has no power of its own over your outcome, then relying on it as though it did is misplaced reliance (*shirk* in reliance) however religious it looks.

The sincerity condition follows directly. In the hadith qudsi of Sahih Muslim, when the servant says *iyyaka na'budu wa iyyaka nasta'in*, Allah says: "This is between Me and My servant, and My servant shall have what he asks." But worship is only worship if it is directed at Him. The Qur'an says, "They were not commanded except to worship Allah, sincere to Him in religion" (98:5), and the Prophet ﷺ warned, in a narration recorded by Ahmad and regarded as sound by several scholars, that the thing he feared most for his community was "the lesser shirk" — showing off, doing the deed so that people see it. Al-Fudayl ibn 'Iyad captured the whole matter in his famous reading of "that He might test you, which of you is best in deed" (67:2): the best deed is the one that is most sincere (*akhlas*) and most correct (*aswab*) — sincere meaning done purely for Allah, and correct meaning in accordance with the way of the Prophet ﷺ. Both conditions are needed; a deed performed with sincere intentions but in an invented form is not accepted, and a deed in the correct form but performed for an audience is not accepted either.

Now, what does this look like on a Monday morning? Worship, on this definition, is much wider than the prayer mat. Your work becomes worship when it is done honestly and well for the sake of Allah and within the limits He set; your parenting becomes worship; your study, your repayment of a debt, your patience with a difficult colleague, your restraint from the haram deal that everyone else is doing — all of it enters the category of *'ibadah* when it is directed to Him. And the request for help has its own daily form: the moment when a problem is bigger than your resources, and instead of spiralling into anxiety or manoeuvring, you say, "I need You for this," and then take the practical step that is actually in your hands. That combination — full reliance on Allah plus full use of the means He gave you — is exactly what this verse teaches. Trust in Allah is not passivity; it is work without the illusion that the work alone was sufficient.

## Verse 6

**"Guide us along the Straight Path,"**

In simple terms, having declared his worship and his reliance, the servant now makes his first request — and the request is not for money, health, victory or safety, but for guidance. He asks Allah to show him the straight road and to keep him on it, because guidance is the thing that makes every other gift useful and every other loss survivable.

The verb *ihdina* (guide us) opens a question that the scholars of theology treated at length, because the Qur'an uses "guidance" in two distinct senses. The first is the guidance of showing and directing — explaining the truth, calling to it, making the road visible. This is the guidance Allah gave to His Prophet and, through him, to the preachers and teachers of every generation: "And indeed you guide to a straight path" (42:52). The second is the guidance of enablement and success — opening the heart, inclining the will, actually causing a person to walk the road. This belongs to Allah alone: "Indeed, you do not guide whom you love, but Allah guides whom He wills" (28:56). Both are real, and both are needed. A person can have the road perfectly explained to him and still refuse to walk it; and a person can be sincerely willing and still need Allah to open the door. When you say *ihdina*, you are asking for the second through the first: show me the road, and make me someone who walks it.

That is why believers ask for guidance repeatedly even though they already believe. This request is recited in every unit of the prayer, so at least seventeen times a day, by people who were guided years ago. The reason is that guidance is not a one-time event but a continuous need: new situations, new temptations, new decisions, and above all the constant risk of a heart turning away. The Qur'an records the prayer of the firmly grounded believers in exactly these terms: "Our Lord, do not let our hearts deviate after You have guided us" (3:8). And the Prophet ﷺ, who had no possibility of deviation, frequently supplicated, in a narration recorded by al-Tirmidhi and regarded as sound: "O Turner of hearts, keep my heart firm upon Your religion." A related authentic hadith in Sahih Muslim describes the heart of a human being as lying between two fingers of the Most Merciful, turned however He wills. If the heart of the Prophet ﷺ needed that supplication, nobody else's heart is self-steering.

The word *as-sirat* means a clear, wide, well-trodden road. Lexicographers noted that some scholars of vocabulary considered the word to have entered Arabic from Greek or Latin *strata* (a paved road), while others insisted it is genuinely Arabic; in any case, by the time of the revelation it was ordinary, clear Arabic, and the Qur'an uses it in its plain sense. Al-Qurtubi explains that it is the road that is clear, open and unmistakable — not a hidden track that only specialists can find. *Al-mustaqim* comes from the root *q-w-m*, meaning to stand upright: the road with no crookedness in it, no deviation to the right or the left, the shortest route between where you are and where you need to be. A crooked road is longer and leads elsewhere; a straight road may be hard to walk but never misleads.

So what is the Straight Path? The commentators gave several answers which are really layers of one answer. The majority said it is Islam itself. Others said it is the Qur'an, which is called the road elsewhere. Others said it is the Sunnah, the way of the Prophet ﷺ — and this is where the hadith of the parable becomes important. In a narration reported by Ahmad and al-Tirmidhi from al-Nawwas ibn Sam'an — whose chain scholars disputed, some grading it sound and others weak — the Prophet ﷺ described a straight path with a wall on either side, with open doors in the walls covered by curtains, a caller at the head of the path saying "this is the straight path, do not deviate from it," and a caller above calling out whenever a person tries to lift a curtain, "Woe to you, do not open it, for if you open it you will go through." He then explained: the path is Islam, the walls are the limits of Allah, the open doors are the things Allah has prohibited, the caller at the head of the path is the Book of Allah, and the caller above is the admonition Allah has placed in the heart of every believer. Whether one accepts the chain or not, the picture it gives matches the Qur'an's own teaching precisely, and it identifies the practical components of guidance: the Book, the inner moral sense Allah created, and the boundaries that are not to be crossed.

There is a related thought worth mentioning carefully, because it is sometimes confused. In the Hereafter, the believers will cross a bridge called *as-Sirat*, stretched over the Fire, and this is established in authentic hadith in the two Sahihs. That is a different use of the word from the guidance asked for in this verse, which concerns this life. Some commentators nevertheless joined the two with a reflection rather than a claim: whoever walks Allah's straight path in this world will find the crossing on the Day of Resurrection made easy for him. That is a spiritual connection drawn from the shared word, not an interpretation the verse demands.

The practical reach of this verse is enormous, because "guidance" in the Qur'an covers both knowing the truth and being enabled to live it, and human beings need both in every area of life. Think about the decisions that actually distress people: which career to take, whether to marry this person, how to handle a betrayal, whether to stay in a country or leave it, how to raise a child in a culture that contradicts your values, how to earn a living without selling your integrity. The Sunnah gives a concrete practice for the specific decisions — the prayer of seeking guidance, *istikhara*, taught by the Prophet ﷺ and recorded by al-Bukhari, in which a person prays two units of prayer and then asks Allah to guide him to what is good in his religion, his livelihood and his affair, saying: "O Allah, if You know this matter is good for me, decree it for me and make it easy, and if You know it is bad for me, turn it away from me and turn me away from it, and decree for me the good wherever it is, then make me pleased with it." That final clause is the real fruit of the verse: guidance is not only being shown the right thing, it is being made content with it. And *istikhara* is not a substitute for thinking, consulting experienced people and weighing the options; it is what you do after you have done all of that, because the outcome still belongs to Allah.

## Verse 7

**"the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray.[2]"**

In simple terms, the servant now spells out what the Straight Path is by describing the people who walk it: it is the road of those Allah has favoured, and it is decisively not the road of those who earned His anger, nor the road of those who lost their way. Guidance in the Qur'an is not an abstract ideal; it is a road you recognise by the people who are already on it.

The verse grammatically continues the previous one as a description of the path, and it unfolds in three movements. The first is "the path of those You have blessed." The verb is *an'amta*, from the root *n-'m*, which conveys softness, ease and a pleasant life — a blessing that makes existence gentle. Note that the blessing is attributed to Allah directly and in the second person: it is not a path people earned by their cleverness, it is a path Allah granted. The Qur'an identifies exactly who these blessed people are in Surah al-Nisa: "And whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger — those will be with the ones upon whom Allah has bestowed favour: the prophets, the steadfast affirmers of truth, the martyrs and the righteous. And excellent are those as companions" (4:69). So the blessed are four ranks: the prophets who received revelation, the *siddiqun* who affirmed it with total conviction and without hesitation, the martyrs who gave their lives for it, and the *salihun* whose whole lives were set right by it. When you recite this verse you are asking to be placed in their company on the road, which is a request about your companions as much as about your creed.

The second movement is "not those You are displeased with." The Arabic is in the passive voice — literally, "those upon whom is anger" — and the commentators noted the effect of that construction. By not naming the One who is angry, the phrasing carries a kind of reverent dread: the anger is so unmistakably Allah's that naming Him is unnecessary, and the omission itself makes the threat more solemn. The third movement is "nor those who are astray." Al-Zamakhshari, Abu Hayyan and other grammarians discussed the precise function of *ghayri* here — whether it is an adjective qualifying "those You have blessed" or a substitute for it — and the extra *la* in *wa la-ddallin*, which is a particle of emphasis that reinforces the negation. The upshot for meaning is clear: the path is being defined as much by what it is not as by what it is.

The scholars then asked the obvious question: who are these two groups? The most widely transmitted answer identifies them with two religious communities. A narration reported by Ahmad and al-Tirmidhi from 'Adi ibn Hatim — a former Christian who became a Companion — records the Prophet ﷺ saying: "Those who have earned anger are the Jews, and those who are astray are the Christians." The chain of this narration was disputed: some scholars graded it sound and others weak, and we should be honest about that. However, its content is supported by the Qur'an's own characterisations elsewhere, and the great majority of classical commentators — including al-Tabari, al-Qurtubi and Ibn Kathir — explained the verse along these lines. Ibn Taymiyyah, in his commentary on this surah, drew out the underlying principle rather than the label: those who earned anger are the people who knew the truth and then abandoned it — knowledge without corresponding action — while those who are astray are the people who acted with devotion but on the basis of false belief — action without sound knowledge. The Qur'an itself supports both descriptions: it speaks of those to whom the truth came and who then rejected it after recognising it, and it speaks of a community that "exceeded the bounds in their religion" and followed their scholars in what had not been authorised.

This is where the most important caution is needed, because this verse is frequently misused. The two descriptions are moral and spiritual categories, not ethnic or communal verdicts. It is not legitimate to read this verse as a blanket condemnation of every Jew and every Christian in every time and place; the Qur'an itself commends among the People of the Book "a community standing, reciting the verses of Allah" and believing (3:113–115), and it states that those who believed, and the Jews, and the Christians and the Sabians — whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day and does righteousness — have their reward with their Lord (2:62). What the verse describes is a type, and that type can appear anywhere, including inside the Muslim community. A Muslim who knows the ruling, understands the evidence, and then sets it aside because it costs him something has taken on the characteristic of the first group. A Muslim who invents religious practices out of enthusiasm, or follows a charismatic teacher into clear error while sincerely devoted, has taken on the characteristic of the second. The verse is a mirror, not only a map of other people.

The verse also carries a therapeutic insight worth naming. Notice that the two failures are failures of the two faculties that make a human being. The first is a failure of the will: the intellect works, the truth is known, but desire or pride or tribal loyalty blocks submission. The second is a failure of the intellect: the will is willing, the devotion is real, but the belief underneath it is wrong, so all that energy is spent walking in the wrong direction. Sound religion needs both — correct knowledge and a submitted will. That is why the surah asks for guidance and not merely for information, and why the Prophet ﷺ paired the Book with his Sunnah as the two things that, held together, keep a community from either failure.

One further point on the recitation itself. The scholars are agreed that the word *Amin* — "O Allah, answer" — which is said at the end of this verse, is not part of the Qur'anic text; it is a supplication added by the Sunnah. An authentic hadith in the two Sahihs from Abu Hurayrah records the Prophet ﷺ saying: "When the imam says *Amin*, then say *Amin*, for the angels say *Amin*, and if a person's *Amin* coincides with the *Amin* of the angels, his previous sins are forgiven." So the surah ends with the whole creation — the servant, the congregation and the angels — joining in one word of request, at the one moment in the prayer when Allah has promised, in the hadith qudsi of Sahih Muslim, "and My servant shall have what he asks."

Finally, take this into your own life, because the verse is asking you a question you answer every day: whose road are you actually on? The path is recognised by its travellers, which makes your company a matter of creed and not merely of preference. The Prophet ﷺ said, in a narration reported by Abu Dawud and al-Tirmidhi and graded sound by a number of scholars, "A person is upon the religion of his close friend, so let one of you look carefully at whom he befriends." And in an authentic hadith in the two Sahihs, when a man asked about the Hour, the Prophet ﷺ asked him what he had prepared for it, and the man replied that he had not prepared much except that he loved Allah and His Messenger; the Prophet ﷺ said, "A person will be with those he loves." In a world where your companions now include the accounts you follow, the voices you listen to, the creators you imitate and the arguments you keep scrolling past, this verse asks you to audit them honestly. Are the people shaping your assumptions people of knowledge and action, or people of knowledge without action, or people of enthusiasm without knowledge? Then say *Amin* — and mean it.

## Synthesis of the Seven Verses

Read together, these seven verses trace the complete arc of a human being's relationship with his Creator. Verses 1 to 3 establish *who* Allah is: the One whose name blesses every beginning, to whom all praise belongs because He nurtures every world, and whose defining attribute is mercy. Verse 4 adds the balance that keeps mercy from becoming presumption: the same Lord is the sole owner of the Day on which every debt is settled. Verse 5 turns the whole address into a direct encounter and states the two claims that define Islam — worship for Him alone, reliance on Him alone. Verses 6 and 7 then make the one request that makes everything else possible: guidance to the straight road, the road of the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs and the righteous, and safety from the two ways human beings go wrong — knowing the truth and refusing it, or seeking the truth and losing the way. The surah therefore contains theology, worship, accountability and ethics in seven lines, which is precisely why it was named *Umm al-Qur'an*, the mother and sum of the Book. Its deepest lesson is structural: Allah taught His servants to ask for guidance before He taught them the detailed rulings, because a guided heart is the precondition for every other good, and the prayer itself is the answer to the request.

## Principal Sources Consulted

**Primary revelation and hadith**
- The Qur'an, for the cross-references cited (2:62; 3:8; 3:113–115; 5:2; 7:156; 9:128; 22:56; 27:30; 28:56; 33:43; 40:16; 42:52; 51:56; 67:2; 98:5)
- *Sahih al-Bukhari* — including the hadith qudsi "My mercy prevails over My wrath"; the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah and the objection to the name *ar-Rahman*; the division of mercy into one hundred parts; the hadith of *istikhara*; "a person will be with those he loves"; the hadiths on saying *Amin*
- *Sahih Muslim* — including the hadith qudsi on the division of the prayer (the central source for the structure of this surah's commentary); "There is no prayer for the one who does not recite the Opening of the Book"; "al-hamdu lillah fills the scale"; the heart between two fingers of the Most Merciful; the supplication before intimacy
- *Sunan* of Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi, al-Nasa'i and Ibn Majah, and the *Musnad* of Ahmad — for the narrations on the Basmalah, mercy to those on earth, "if you ask, ask Allah," the firmness of the heart, close friendship, and the parable of the straight path. Where chains are disputed, this has been stated in the commentary rather than concealed.

**Classical tafsir drawn upon**
- al-Tabari, *Jami' al-Bayan* — early opinions of the Companions and Tabi'un, especially on *Rabb*, *'alamin* and *yawm al-din*
- al-Baghawi, *Ma'alim al-Tanzil*; Ibn 'Atiyyah, *al-Muharrar al-Wajiz*; Ibn al-Jawzi, *Zad al-Musir*
- al-Zamakhshari, *al-Kashshaf* — rhetorical and grammatical points, especially *iltifat*, fronting for restriction, and the annexation in verse 4
- Fakhr al-Din al-Razi, *Mafatih al-Ghayb* — the theology of mercy, the wisdom of repetition, and the ordering of the surah
- al-Qurtubi, *al-Jami' li-Ahkam al-Qur'an* — legal questions including the status of the Basmalah and the recitation of it in prayer, and the meaning of *al-sirat*
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir al-Qur'an al-'Azim* — hadith-based explanation and the identification of the two groups in verse 7
- al-Baydawi, *Anwar al-Tanzil*; Abu Hayyan, *al-Bahr al-Muhit* — grammatical analysis and the discussion of *ghayri* in verse 7
- al-Shawkani, *Fath al-Qadir*; al-Alusi, *Ruh al-Ma'ani* — later synthesis and reports on the names of the surah

**Other works**
- Ibn Taymiyyah, *Tafsir Surat al-Fatihah*, and Ibn al-Qayyim, *Madarij al-Salikin* — on the nature of *'ibadah*, the two kinds of guidance, and the analysis of the two groups in verse 7
- al-Nawawi, *Sharh Sahih Muslim* and *al-Majmu'* — hadith commentary and fiqh of the Basmalah and *Amin*
- Works on the canonical readings, principally the tradition descending from Ibn Mujahid's *al-Sab'a* and al-Dani's *al-Taysir*, for the two readings of *Maliki / Maliki* in verse 4
- Arabic lexicography: al-Raghib al-Isfahani, *al-Mufradat fi Gharib al-Qur'an*; Ibn Manzur, *Lisan al-Arab*; al-Jawaliqi, *al-Mu'arrab* (for the discussion of the word *sirat*)

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, chains or publication details have been given, because this is a synthesized commentary rather than a line-by-line citation apparatus, and inventing precise references would be worse than omitting them. Where a narration's authenticity is disputed among the hadith scholars, that has been stated plainly in the text. Where a detail could not be established with sufficient certainty — for example the identification of a single "greatest name" of Allah, or the exact scope of the word *'alamin* in relation to modern cosmology — that uncertainty has been left visible rather than papered over. Modern academic scholarship on the Qur'an has been engaged with only where it bears directly on a verse (principally the lexical discussion of *sirat* and the question of reading cosmology into *al-'alamin*); the substance of this commentary rests on the Qur'an, the authentic Sunnah and the classical tafsir tradition.
