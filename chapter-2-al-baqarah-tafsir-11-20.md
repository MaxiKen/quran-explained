# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 11–20

We are continuing the long description of the hypocrites that began at verse 8, and these ten verses bring that description to its close. Verses 11 to 14 show the hypocrites in conversation — how they answer when they are corrected, and how they speak in the two different rooms they live in. Verses 15 and 16 give Allah's response to them: mockery returned upon themselves, and a trade that never profited. Verses 17 to 20 then turn from description to imagery, with two of the most famous parables in the Qur'an — a fire that is kindled and then extinguished, and a storm in which people stumble forward in flashes of lightning. Together the two parables answer the question the earlier verses raise: what is actually wrong with these people? The answer is that they have no light of their own. Everything they have is borrowed, intermittent and external, which is why their movement stops the moment the light goes out. After this passage closes with a declaration of Allah's power over all things, verse 21 turns to address all of humanity directly.

---

## Verse 11

**"When they are told, “Do not spread corruption in the land,” they reply, “We are only peace-makers!”"**

In simple terms, when someone advises these people and tells them to stop causing harm and disorder in society, they answer indignantly that they are not corrupting anything — they are the ones putting things right.

Notice first how the verse opens: *wa idha qila lahum*, "and when it is said to them." The verb is passive and the speaker is not named. That is deliberate. It is not only the Prophet ﷺ who says this to them; any believer who gives sincere advice says it, and the Qur'an leaves the saying open so that every reader recognises the situation. It is the normal, healthy reflex of a believing community: when it sees harm being spread, it says so.

The word used for the harm is *fasad*, from the root *f-s-d*, which means corruption, decay, rot and disorder — the opposite of *salah*, soundness and rightness. What was the corruption they were spreading? The classical commentators described it in several ways, and these are best read as layers rather than as rival answers. Some said it refers to their disbelief and hypocrisy, which is the root corruption. Some said it refers to their obstructing people from faith and discouraging them from the Messenger ﷺ. Some said it refers to their betraying the community and breaking agreements, including their secret dealings with the enemies of Madinah. And some pointed to their reviving of the old tribal feuds between the Aws and the Khazraj, the two Arab tribes of Madinah whose decades of bloodshed Islam had only just ended. No single specific occasion is firmly established for this verse, and we should not manufacture one; what is clear is that the corruption is social as well as personal.

That is why the verse says "in the *land*" (*fi al-ard*) rather than naming a place. Corruption is not a private vice; it spreads, and its damage lands on the whole community. The Qur'an consistently treats *fasad fil-ard* as one of the gravest categories of wrongdoing: it is the charge against those who wage war against the community in 5:33, and it is the description of what happens when human hands are let loose in 30:41 — "Corruption has appeared on land and sea because of what the hands of people have earned." The verse is therefore teaching something important about moral ecology: a person's corruption is never contained, because fitnah and disorder travel.

Then comes their reply, and it is one of the most psychologically accurate sentences in the Qur'an. *Innama nahnu muslihun* — "We are only reformers." The particle *innama* is a particle of restriction: they are not merely claiming to be reformers, they are claiming to be *nothing but* reformers, and they are denying the accusation entirely. The word *muslihun* comes from *s-l-h*, the very root that is the antonym of *fasad*. So the structure of the exchange is: you are corrupting; no, we are *the exact opposite*, we are fixing. They do not minimise, explain or apologise. They invert the charge and claim the moral high ground in the same breath.

There is an extra resonance in their choice of word. *Muslih* shares its root with *salah* (soundness, rightness) and with *salam* (peace, wholeness), and it is the same family from which *islam* and *muslim* come. They are claiming, in effect, to be the people of wholeness and peace. The Qur'an lets them say it and then answers in verse 12.

This is the mechanism by which corruption survives in every age, and it is worth naming plainly: the corruptor almost never experiences himself as a corruptor. He experiences himself as a realist, a protector, a reformer, a truth-teller. The community leader stirring up factionalism believes he is defending the community. The relative who carries stories between family members believes she is keeping people informed. The official taking bribes believes he is navigating a system that was already broken. The account that publishes half-truths about people believes it is doing accountability. In each case the self-description is the last thing to be surrendered, because it is the shield that makes the behaviour bearable.

Now consider what the verse teaches the person who gives the advice. The hypocrites were told, and they did not listen — but the telling was still right. Islam does not let a community go silent in the face of harm. The Prophet ﷺ said, in the well-known narration recorded by Muslim from Abu Sa'id al-Khudri: "Whoever among you sees an evil, let him change it with his hand; if he cannot, then with his tongue; and if he cannot, then with his heart — and that is the weakest of faith." The scholars of hadith and fiqh set clear conditions around acting on that: knowledge of what is truly wrong, wisdom in how it is addressed, and reasonable expectation that speaking will not produce a greater harm. But the default of silent acceptance is not an option.

And consider what the verse teaches the person receiving the advice, because that is where most of us actually live. The healthy response to "you are causing harm" is not to deny it reflexively but to look at the fruit: Is the thing I am doing producing order or disorder? Does it heal relationships or poison them? Does it make the community stronger or weaker? The verse insists that your self-description is not evidence. Reality is.

## Verse 12

**"Indeed, it is they who are the corruptors, but they fail to perceive it."**

In simple terms, Allah now corrects the record in the plainest possible terms: these people are not the reformers they claim to be — they are the corruptors — and the tragedy is that they do not realise it.

The verse opens with *ala*, a particle that Arabic uses to draw attention to what follows, the equivalent of "take note" or "truly." It signals that a decisive verdict is about to be given.

Then comes one of the most elegant pieces of refutation in the Qur'an. The verse is built to mirror exactly the sentence it answers, and then to reverse it. The hypocrites said: *innama nahnu muslihun* — "we are *only* the reformers," using a restrictive particle and an emphatic pronoun. The reply: *ala innahum hum al-mufsidun* — "truly it is *they* who are the corruptors," using the same emphatic structure (*innahum*, then the independent pronoun *hum*, then the definite noun). In the vocabulary of Arabic rhetoric, matching an opponent's phrasing in order to cancel it is a recognised and powerful device. The effect here is that their own sentence is handed back to them with the word replaced. You claim to be the reformers exclusively; the reality is that you are the corruptors exclusively. Every element you used to strengthen your claim now strengthens the verdict against you.

The closing clause repeats something we have already heard in this passage: *wa lakin la yash'urun* — "but they do not perceive it." This is the same verb that closed verse 9. Repetition within a few verses is never filler in the Qur'an; it is a diagnosis. The defining deficit of this group is not ignorance of the law, not lack of intelligence, and not lack of opportunity. It is a failure of awareness. They genuinely do not see what they are doing to the community or to themselves.

Why not? Several reasons are visible in the passage itself. Their deception has become self-deception — they have lied so consistently that the lie now feels like the truth. Their self-interest supplies a constant, plausible justification for whatever they were inclined to do anyway. And their corruption is not a series of lapses but a settled orientation, and an orientation is invisible from the inside, because it is the lens through which you see rather than something you look at.

There is a major principle embedded here that deserves to be stated explicitly, because it protects the reader from two opposite errors. The Qur'an does not accept a person's own account of himself as the final word. The claim to be a reformer, a believer, a sincere person, is a claim — and claims are tested against what they produce. At the same time, this does not authorise anyone to stand in judgement over another person's hidden intentions, because that is knowledge no one possesses. The balance the scholars drew is this: we judge by what is apparent, we leave the hidden heart to Allah, and we measure outward claims against outward fruit.

This is precisely why the verse is unsettling for a believer rather than merely comforting. It is easy to read verses 8 to 12 as being about "them" — the hypocrites of Madinah — and to feel safely outside the description. But the mechanism the verse describes is one that any human being can enter: doing real harm while sincerely believing you are doing good. The community organiser who destroys reputations in the name of justice. The parent who controls an adult child's life in the name of protection. The colleague who undermines someone in the name of standards. The person who spreads a rumour in the name of concern. In each case the label is *islah* and the fruit is *fasad*.

So the practical discipline this verse teaches is a specific habit: measure your actions by their results and by revelation, never by your own description of them. Concretely, that means doing three things. First, ask what the actual effect of what you are doing is on the people around you — not what you intend, but what is happening. Second, let yourself be corrected by someone you trust, and treat the sting of correction as a mercy rather than an attack, because the hypocrites' failure began with refusing the correction. Third, ask Allah for honesty about yourself, because awareness of one's own corruption is not something a person can manufacture by willpower; it is something given. The early Muslims used to supplicate for exactly this, and the Prophet ﷺ himself, who had no corruption in him, constantly asked Allah to keep his heart firm.

## Verse 13

**"And when they are told, “Believe as others believe,” they reply, “Will we believe as the fools believe?” Indeed, it is they who are fools, but they do not know."**

In simple terms, when these people are invited to believe the way the believers believe, they answer with contempt — "should we become like those fools?" — and Allah responds that the real fools are the ones saying it, even though they do not know it.

The invitation is *aminu kama amana al-nas* — "believe as the people have believed." Who are "the people" (*al-nas*) here? The majority of commentators explained that it means the believers — that is, "believe as the believers believe." Some specified the Companions of the Prophet ﷺ, and some narrowed it further to the Muhajirun and the Ansar. The important point is not the exact reference but the nature of the invitation: they were being called to an ordinary, shared faith, the same faith ordinary people had accepted — and they found that insulting.

Their answer is *a-nu'minu kama amana al-sufaha'*. The opening is a questioning particle, but rhetoricians note that a question of this shape in Arabic often carries the meaning of rejection and contempt rather than genuine enquiry: "are we to believe...? Of course not." They are not asking; they are sneering.

The word they use is *al-sufaha'*, the plural of *safih*. The root *s-f-h* conveys spilling and pouring out, and the *safih* is the person whose words and deeds spill out without the restraint of judgement. Lexicographers defined *safah* as lightness of intellect and weakness of judgement, and the classical commentators glossed *al-safih* as the one who cannot distinguish what benefits him from what harms him. Who were they calling fools? The believers. And there is a strong strand in the commentaries, supported by what the Qur'an reports elsewhere, that the sting of the word was aimed particularly at the poor, the weak and the former slaves among the believers. The Qur'an records exactly this reflex from the elites who rejected earlier messengers: the chiefs of Nuh's people said, "We do not see anyone following you except the lowest of us, those who follow you without reflection" (11:27). The same attitude appears in Surah al-An'am, where the Qurayshi chiefs wanted the poor Companions sent away from the Prophet's gathering (6:52–53). The pattern is a constant in revelation: the powerful do not reject the message on the grounds of evidence; they reject it on the grounds of the company it keeps.

Which means the accusation of foolishness was never really an intellectual judgement. It was a status claim dressed as one. "People of our standing do not follow that." And it is worth noticing how durable that move is: the sneer is the cheapest available substitute for an argument, and it works precisely because it shifts the ground from "is this true?" to "who believes this?"

Then the verse answers, and again with the same mirroring we saw in verse 12: *ala innahum hum al-sufaha'u wa lakin la ya'lamun* — "truly it is they who are the fools, but they do not know." Their own word is returned to them with the same restrictive construction. The exclusive claim they made about others is applied to them.

Notice, though, that the closing verb has changed. Verse 12 ended with *la yash'urun* — "they do not perceive." This verse ends with *la ya'lamun* — "they do not know." The commentators and rhetoricians paid attention to this variation, and the most natural reading of it is that the two verses describe two different failures: verse 12 concerns their perception of their own corruption, which is a matter of awareness, while verse 13 concerns their judgement about who is foolish, which is a matter of knowledge. We should not build doctrine on the distinction, but the variation is a good example of how precisely the Qur'an varies its closing notes.

The deeper lesson is in the definition. If the *safih* is the person who cannot tell what benefits him from what harms him, then foolishness has nothing to do with social standing, education or sophistication. It is a failure of calculation about ultimate outcomes. And by that definition the verdict is unavoidable: the person who exchanges the eternal for the temporary, who trades the approval of the Creator for the approval of people who will be dust, has made the worst available trade. That is exactly what verse 16 will say in commercial language. So the fool is not the poor, simple believer; the fool is the person who has seen the offer and declined it.

This verse speaks with unusual directness to the modern reader, because contempt for religious commitment is one of the defining social reflexes of the contemporary world. The assumption that belief is what simple, uneducated, provincial people do, and that sophistication consists in having outgrown it, is everywhere — in universities, in media, in boardrooms, in the algorithmic culture of the internet. The verse relocates the question entirely. It does not ask how smart you are. It asks whether you can calculate what benefits you. A person of towering intellect who cannot answer that question is, in the Qur'an's vocabulary, a *safih*.

And there is a warning here for believers in the other direction: do not adopt the sneer. When a Muslim begins describing other Muslims' commitment with the vocabulary of the culture that despises it — "extreme," "backward," "that's so village" — he has taken the sufaha's side in the argument while still calling himself a believer. The company of the believers is an honour, and the Qur'an's own measure of a person has nothing to do with his bank balance or his accent. In a narration recorded by Muslim from Abu Hurayrah, the Prophet ﷺ said: "How many a dishevelled, dusty person, driven away from doors, who if he were to swear an oath by Allah, Allah would surely fulfil it for him." That is the Qur'an's valuation of a human being, and it is the opposite of the valuation in this verse.

## Verse 14

**"When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.” But when alone with their evil associates they say, “We are definitely with you; we were only mocking.”"**

In simple terms, these people have two entirely different languages. In front of the believers they profess faith. In private with their own circle they announce that they were never on the believers' side at all and that their public faith was a joke.

This verse describes what hypocrisy looks like as a daily practice rather than as a doctrine. The verb in each half is instructive. With the believers it is *laqu* — "they meet," a chance or general encounter; they say *amanna*, "we believe," the plain confession of faith. With the others it is *khalaw ila* — a phrase built on the verb meaning to be empty or alone, so the sense is "when they retreat to be by themselves with..." There is a deliberate contrast between the open encounter and the closed room.

The word translated here as "evil associates" is *shayatinihim* — "their devils." The commentators explained this in two ways that complement each other. The majority held that it means their leaders and chiefs in disbelief: the human ringleaders whose company they keep, whose authority they accept, and in whose presence they drop the pretence. Others understood it literally as the devils from among the jinn, the evil companions who whisper to them and whose suggestions they follow. A third reading combines both. The Qur'an itself supports the idea of human devils: "And so We have appointed for every prophet an enemy — the devils of mankind and jinn, inspiring one another with adorned speech in delusion" (6:112). The word *shaytan* in Arabic denotes any rebellious, insolent being, whether from the jinn or from humankind.

The possessive suffix is worth pausing over: not "the devils" but "*their* devils." The commentators drew a sobering conclusion from it — every group has the devils appropriate to it. A person does not simply fall into bad company in general; he acquires the particular devils, human or otherwise, that suit the particular way he has turned away. The company you keep in private is not an accident; it is a symptom and then a cause.

Notice also what they say in that room. *Inna ma'akum* — "indeed we are with you." The sentence is built for emphasis: the particle *inna*, and a fronted predicate. This is not casual agreement; it is a sworn declaration of solidarity. And then: *innama nahnu mustahzi'un* — "we were only mocking." The word *istihza'* is mockery, derision, treating something as a joke. So their public profession of faith was not merely tactical; it was done *in a spirit of contempt*. They were not grudgingly going along; they were enjoying it.

The progression through this passage is worth tracking, because it is a real psychology rather than a list of slogans. At verse 8 they make a false claim. At verses 9 and 10 they deceive themselves and their hearts sicken. At verses 11 to 13 the corruption becomes active and the contempt surfaces in how they talk about the believers. Here at verse 14 the contempt is spoken out loud in the safety of their own circle. Mockery is where the disease becomes visible.

It is worth asking why mockery specifically. In most cases, mockery is not the response of someone who is confidently right; it is the response of someone who has been made uncomfortable. A person who is genuinely untroubled by a message ignores it. The person who needs to ridicule it in private, who needs his friends to laugh with him, is discharging a pressure — the pressure of having been near something that accused him and not being able to shake it off. The sneer is how a group gives each other permission to stop feeling.

For the believer who is on the receiving end, this verse is also a consolation, and the Qur'an gives it repeatedly. "Messengers before you were certainly mocked, but those who ridiculed them were engulfed by the very thing they mocked" (6:10). And to His own Prophet ﷺ, Allah said: "Indeed, We are sufficient for you against the mockers" (15:95). Being mocked for faith is not a sign that something has gone wrong; it is the normal experience of everyone who has carried this message.

Now take this into our own lives, because this verse is one of the most searching in the passage. The diagnostic question it poses is simple: which room are you actually from? A person's public statements are cheap; what he says among the people whose approval he actually wants is the truth about him. In modern terms, that is the group chat, not the feed. It is what you say about your religious colleague when he leaves the room. It is whether the jokes you laugh at are at the expense of things you claim to hold sacred. It is the version of you that appears when you are with the people you are trying to impress.

And there is a second, sharper application. Many people live exactly this two-room life without ever having decided to. They are one person at the mosque and another with their friends. One person with their family and another online. One person in the meeting and another in the car afterwards. They are not plotting anything; they are simply adapting to every audience, and the adaptation has become so automatic that neither version feels like a lie. That is the early stage of the condition this verse describes, and it is far more common than open hypocrisy.

The remedy the Qur'an and Sunnah give is to collapse the two rooms into one. Live so that there is no version of yourself you would be ashamed to have repeated. Choose company whose presence makes you more rather than less like the person you claim to be. And take seriously the warning the Prophet ﷺ gave in a narration recorded by Abu Dawud and al-Tirmidhi: "A person is upon the religion of his close friend, so let one of you consider carefully whom he befriends." If the people you are with only ever bring out the version of you that mocks good things, that tells you something important about the room you are sitting in.

## Verse 15

**"Allah will throw their mockery back at them, leaving them to continue wandering blindly in their defiance."**

In simple terms, Allah responds to their mockery by turning it back on them, and He gives them respite to wander further and further in their rebellion.

The first half of the verse contains one of the classic theological difficulties of the Qur'an, and a faithful commentary has to face it rather than glide past it: *Allahu yastahzi'u bihim* — "Allah mocks them." Mockery, as a human act, is contemptible: it is deriding someone to make yourself feel superior. How can it be attributed to Allah?

The answer the classical tradition gives rests on a well-known device of Arabic rhetoric, and understanding it unlocks a whole class of Qur'anic verses. The device is called *mushakala* or *muqabala*: a word is repeated in response to a word of the same form, but the meaning it carries in the response is the one appropriate to the responder, not the one appropriate to the original speaker. The Qur'an uses it repeatedly. "They plan, and Allah plans — and Allah is the best of planners" (8:30). "They forgot Allah, so He forgot them" (9:67). "They seek to deceive Allah, while He is the one who deceives them" (4:142). And here: they mock, and Allah mocks them.

So what does it mean? It means Allah repays mockery with its reality. He exposes them, humiliates them, lets their scheme collapse onto them in such a way that they become the object of the ridicule they were trying to inflict, and makes them a lesson. It is not the derision of one creature over another; it is the just recompense of the Creator, who cannot be mocked and who is not diminished by what they said. Ahl al-Sunnah affirm the verb exactly as Allah used it, without asking how, while denying any resemblance between the divine attribute and the blameworthy human one. The Mu'tazila, as they did with the sealing of hearts in verse 7, preferred to read it as "He requited them with the punishment of mockery" — a reading close in conclusion but framed so as not to attribute the act to Allah at all. The difference in framing is real; the practical meaning is nearly the same.

The Qur'an shows us what that repayment looks like in Surah al-Hadid, in a passage the commentators have long paired with this one. On the Day of Resurrection the hypocrites will call out to the believers, "Wait for us, that we may borrow some of your light," and a wall will be set up between them with a gate — inside it is mercy and outside it is punishment. They will be told, "Were we not with you?" and they will say, "Yes, but you tempted yourselves and you waited and you doubted, and wishful thinking deluded you until the command of Allah came" (57:13–15). They mocked faith in private; on the Day when it matters, they will be mocked by the very light they spent their lives pretending to have.

The second half of the verse is quieter and, in some ways, more frightening: *wa yamudduhum fi tughyanihim ya'mahun* — "and He extends them in their transgression, wandering blindly." The verb *madda* means to lengthen, to stretch out, to give more rope. The image some commentators drew on is of leaving a tethered animal a longer rope so that it grazes further and further out. What is being described is respite: Allah does not strike them immediately. He lets them go deeper.

The word *tughyan* comes from a root meaning to overflow and burst beyond limits — the same root that gives us *taghut*, and the same verb the Qur'an uses for water bursting its banks in Surah al-Haqqah, "when the water overflowed" (69:11), and for Pharaoh, "Go to Pharaoh, for indeed he has transgressed" (20:24). So the picture is of a rebellion that has overflowed its banks, and of people being swept along further into it.

And *ya'mahun* — wandering blindly. The lexicographers distinguished carefully here: *'amaha* is confusion and loss of direction, the blindness of insight and orientation, whereas *'ama* is the blindness of the physical eye. The Qur'an is not saying they are stupid. It is saying they are lost.

Theologically, this is the same territory as the sealing in verse 7, and the same principles apply: the extension is a consequence of their own sustained choice, and it is one of the ways Allah's decree operates rather than an arbitrary act imposed on the innocent. The scholars have a precise term for it — *istidraj*, letting a sinner sink deeper while outwardly succeeding. The Qur'an states it plainly: "Those who deny Our signs — We will lead them on step by step from where they do not know. And I give them respite; indeed, My plan is firm" (7:182 and 68:45). A narration reported by Ahmad and others from 'Uqbah ibn 'Amir captures the same idea in Prophetic words, that when you see Allah giving a person what he loves of this world while he persists in disobeying Him, that is *istidraj* — and the Prophet ﷺ is reported to have recited 7:182 at that point. The chain of that narration has been discussed by hadith scholars, so it should be cited with that awareness, but its content is established by the Qur'an itself.

Now this is one of the most practically important ideas in the whole passage, because it overturns a conclusion almost everyone draws by instinct. The visible prosperity of people who are openly, confidently corrupt is not evidence that Allah is pleased with them. It may be the opposite. Pharaoh was never more powerful than in the moments before he drowned. In our own time, when wealth, platform and impunity are openly displayed by people who mock everything sacred, the Qur'an's reading of that success is not envy and not confusion; it is *madd* — more rope.

And the individual version matters more than the public one. If you are persisting in something you know is wrong and nothing bad is happening to you — the business is fine, the relationship is fine, nobody has found out — the correct emotional response is not relief. It is alarm. Ease during unrepented sin is either a veil being lifted later or a rope being lengthened now. The right use of that ease is to spend it on repentance while it is still being offered, because respite is only merciful if it is used.

## Verse 16

**"They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance. But this trade is profitless, and they are not ˹rightly˺ guided."**

In simple terms, these are the people who deliberately exchanged guidance for misguidance — and the exchange brought them no profit whatsoever, because they were never truly guided in the first place.

Everything about this verse is built on a single metaphor, and the metaphor is doing precise work. The verb is *ishtaraw*, "they purchased," and with the preposition *bi-* it means they bought one thing at the price of another: they bought misguidance with guidance. Guidance is what they paid; misguidance is what they received.

Why describe the choice as a commercial transaction? Because a transaction implies things that a simple statement of belief or disbelief would miss. It implies agency — nobody accidentally completes a trade. It implies valuation — they looked at both items and judged misguidance to be worth more. It implies a price actually paid — they gave up something real. And it implies an expected return — they made the trade because they thought it would profit them. All four of those are true of the hypocrites. They were not swept along. They calculated.

Notice, too, what they bought. It is not nothing. Misguidance (*dalalah*) is a positive acquisition: a set of justifications, an identity, a community, a way of life, a position in society. People rarely abandon faith into a vacuum; they abandon it *for* something. The hypocrites bought a social position, freedom from obligation, access to two communities, safety with the powerful. The Qur'an's point is that they made a purchase, not an escape.

The next clause is where the Arabic contains a small, deliberate detail: *fa-ma rabihat tijaratuhum* — "so their trade did not profit." The subject of the verb is not the people but the *trade*. *Tijarah* is feminine in Arabic, and the verb agrees with it: the transaction itself failed to yield a return. The commentators noted the point of this. It is not simply that they lost; it is that the exchange itself was unproductive, which closes off the escape route of good intentions. A person can say "I meant well" about a loss that came from bad luck. He cannot say it about a structure that had no profit in it by design.

And the verse closes with *wa ma kanu muhtadin* — "and they were not guided." The construction is the continuous past, which here carries the sense that they were never in a state of being guided at all. The form of the word matters too: *muhtadin* is the derived form that means receiving guidance, accepting it, letting yourself be guided. So the closing statement is not merely "they ended up lost"; it is that they were never people who received guidance — their outward claim to it was hollow from the beginning.

The Qur'an returns to this commercial metaphor several times in this surah alone, and reading them together is clarifying. In 2:86: "Those are the ones who have bought the life of this world at the price of the Hereafter." In 2:90: "Miserable is that for which they sold their souls." In 2:175: "Those are the ones who have purchased error for guidance and punishment for forgiveness." And then there is the most important one, because it is the same metaphor turned upwards — 9:111: "Indeed, Allah has purchased from the believers their lives and their wealth in exchange for Paradise." Everyone in the Qur'an is trading. The question is never *whether* you are making a deal; it is *what you are buying, what you are paying, and from whom*.

That reframing is enormously useful, because it relocates faith from the category of taste to the category of exchange. People do not choose misguidance because they have been defeated in an argument. They choose it because it looks like it pays. Which means that the answer to someone who has made a bad trade is not only more evidence; it is also showing him that the return he was promised does not arrive.

Make it concrete. The young professional who trades honesty for a promotion: the promotion arrives, and the sleep does not. The woman who trades her faith for a social circle that would mock it: the circle accepts her, and she discovers she is only safe there as long as she agrees with them. The community leader who trades principle for access and funding: the access arrives, and the respect does not. The person who trades the pleasure of Allah for the approval of strangers online: the numbers arrive, and the emptiness deepens. In every case the trade was real, the price was paid, and *ma rabihat tijaratuhum*.

So the discipline this verse teaches is to run the profit test honestly. Whatever you are being asked to give up for the sake of something you want, ask two questions. First: what exactly am I paying, and is it recoverable? Guidance, integrity, time, relationships and the Hereafter are not recoverable. Second: what am I actually getting, and do the people who already have it report that it delivered? If the answer to the second question is uncertain, the trade is a bad one, whatever it costs.

And there is a word of mercy hidden in the verse's logic. If their trade failed, it follows that a different trade is available — the one in 9:111, where Allah is the buyer and the price is everything you cannot keep anyway. The failure of the first transaction is not the end of the story for anyone still breathing.

## Verse 17

**"Their example is that of someone who kindles a fire, but when it lights up all around them, Allah takes away their light, leaving them in complete darkness—unable to see."**

In simple terms, Allah now gives a parable for these people: they are like someone who lights a fire in the dark; the moment it blazes and shows him everything around him, the light is taken away and he is left in deeper darkness than before, unable to see anything.

The Qur'an strikes this parable deliberately, and it will defend the practice of parables later in this surah at verse 26 — "Surely Allah does not shy away from using the parable of a mosquito or what is even smaller." The method matters: a parable does not argue with you, it shows you something you could not otherwise see about yourself.

Look closely at the details, because each one carries meaning. *Istawaqada naran* — "he kindled a fire." The verb is a derived form that conveys setting about the kindling, making an effort to start it. The initiative is his. Whatever light he has, he struck it himself.

Who is the man in the parable, and what is the fire? The commentators offered several readings, and they are complementary. The most commonly transmitted one is that the man is the hypocrite and the fire is the outward faith he displayed: by declaring Islam he obtained light and benefit in this world — safety, belonging, standing, the protection of the community — and then that light was taken from him, at death or at the Resurrection, leaving him in a darkness worse than if he had never had it. Others said the fire is the truth and guidance that reached him and lit up his surroundings, and which he then lost by rejecting it. Others read the parable more generally: it pictures anyone who has been near the light and then loses it. All of these readings share a single insight, and that insight is the point of the parable: the light was borrowed and external. It was a fire burning beside him, not a lamp lit inside him.

Then: *fa-lamma ada'at ma hawlahu* — "when it illuminated all around him." Note that the light shows him *what is around him* — his surroundings, his situation, the people near him. It gives him orientation and comfort. The hypocrites had exactly this: the Qur'an was being revealed around them, the community was flourishing around them, and the outward faith gave them a place in the light.

There is a grammatical feature here that grammarians and rhetoricians have long discussed. The parable is told in the singular — "the one who kindled," "around *him*" — and then abruptly returns to the plural: "Allah took away *their* light and left *them* in darkness." Two explanations are offered, and both are reasonable. One is that the singular is used to make the picture vivid and individual — you see one man in the dark — and then the plural returns because the condition belongs to the whole group. The other is grammatical: the relative pronoun "the one who" may be intended collectively, so the return to the plural is natural. Either way, the effect is that the parable individualises the condition before generalising it: it is *you*, and it is *all of them*.

*Dhahaba Allahu bi-nurihim* — "Allah took away their light." This raises the same question that arose in verse 7: if these people never truly believed, what light was there to take away? The answer the commentators gave is that the light removed is the benefit they were deriving from the outward faith — the safety, the standing, the worldly advantage, and above all the possibility of guidance that had been placed within their reach. They had the light of the message arriving to them, the light of the natural disposition (*fitrah*) within them, and the light of a believing community around them, and by rejecting it they lost it. The removal is attributed to Allah because He creates all outcomes, including this one, and as in verse 7 the order is: they turned away first, and the taking away followed.

*Wa tarakahum fi zulumatin la yubsirun* — "and He left them in darknesses, unable to see." Note the plural: *zulumat*, darknesses, not a single darkness. The commentators read the plural as layers — the darkness of doubt, the darkness of hypocrisy, the darkness of envy, the darkness of desires, the darkness of a lie they can no longer distinguish from the truth. This plural is systematic in the Qur'an, and the pattern is one of the most beautiful things in its vocabulary: Allah "brings them out of darknesses into light" (2:257), plural darknesses and a single light. He "made the darknesses and the light" (6:1). The logic is exact: truth is one, and error is countless, because there is only one way to be right and an indefinite number of ways to be wrong.

Now, what should a modern reader take from this? The most important and most uncomfortable lesson is that proximity to light is not the same as possessing it. The hypocrites had more exposure to revelation than almost anyone in history — they lived in Madinah, they heard the Qur'an as it was revealed, they sat with the Prophet ﷺ, they prayed in the congregation. And it did them no good, because it never became theirs.

That is a direct warning to anyone who has grown up surrounded by Islam. Being born into a Muslim family, having a Qur'an on the shelf, knowing the phrases, having Muslim friends — that is a fire burning *beside* you. It is real warmth and real light, and it is also entirely removable. It becomes yours only when belief moves from inheritance to conviction and practice moves from environment to choice. This is why the children of practising households sometimes drift further than converts: the convert bought the light; the other inherited it and never checked whether it was plugged in.

It is also a warning about a subtler condition: knowing a great deal about the religion while being untouched by it. The Qur'an warns elsewhere about those who carry knowledge and do not act on it, and the parable applies with full force — a scholar whose knowledge is a fire lighting up everything around him while his own heart is dark.

Finally, note the shape of the parable: light, benefit, sudden loss, total darkness, and no ability to see. The worst part is not the darkness. It is that they had seen. The person who never had the light and the person who had it and lost it are not in the same condition, and the Qur'an is telling us which one is worse.

## Verse 18

**"They are ˹wilfully˺ deaf, dumb, and blind, so they will never return ˹to the Right Path˺."**

In simple terms, having given the parable, the Qur'an states the condition plainly: these people are as good as deaf, dumb and blind, and for that reason they do not come back.

The three words in Arabic — *summun, bukmun, 'umyun* — come one after another with no connecting "and" between them. Rhetoricians noted the effect: omitting the conjunction makes the qualities arrive all at once, as though they are not three separate defects but a single compounded condition.

The first thing to establish is that this is not a description of physical disability, and the Qur'an's language elsewhere proves it. These people could hear perfectly well — they heard the Qur'an and understood it well enough to mock it in private. The description is functional: they do not hear the truth *as if* they were deaf, they do not utter it or utter any good *as if* they were dumb, and they do not perceive the signs *as if* they were blind. The same triad appears in 7:179: "They have hearts with which they do not understand, eyes with which they do not see, and ears with which they do not hear."

Note also the order, which is not arbitrary. The sequence traces the path by which guidance normally reaches a person: you hear the message, you affirm it with your tongue, and then the signs become visible to you. All three doors are closed, so there is no route in.

It is worth putting this verse next to verse 7 for a moment, because the overlap is deliberate. There, the disbelievers had their hearts and hearing sealed and a covering placed over their sight. Here, the hypocrites are described as being deaf, dumb and blind. Different routes, same destination — which is one of the quiet arguments of this whole opening passage: open defiance and concealed disbelief end up in the same place.

The final clause raises a difficult question: *fa-hum la yarji'un* — "so they do not return." Does this mean these particular people will never repent, full stop? The commentators differed, and the difference matters enormously for how the verse is used. Some read it as a statement of fact about these people's final state: they will not return to faith. Others read it as a description of the condition: in the state they have reached, with the faculties by which a person returns disabled, they are not returning — which is a statement about the trajectory, not a seal on the future. The second reading is the one most commentators settled on, and it is the one that must be taught, for two reasons.

The first reason is textual: the verse attributes the failure to a condition they have brought on themselves, and the condition is described in the language of consequence, not of an arbitrary decree. The second reason is far more important: reading this verse as closing the door on any living person would contradict the whole tenor of the Qur'an. "Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of Allah. Indeed, Allah forgives all sins" (39:53). And in 4:17–18, repentance is accepted up until the moment a person is actually dying. The door is open until the soul reaches the throat. What this verse says is not that the door is locked; it is that a person in this condition is not walking towards it.

That distinction is the difference between a verse that drives a reader to despair and a verse that drives him to hurry. The correct response to reading about a hardened heart is not "could that be me, and is it too late?" but "could that be me, and therefore let me turn now, while turning is still possible." No one can know another person's final state, and no one should be written off — a point already made in the commentary on verses 6 and 7.

Now consider what the condition looks like from the inside, because it is not exotic. A person becomes deaf by deciding in advance that he is not going to be told anything. He curates his input so that nothing he disagrees with ever reaches him. A person becomes dumb by never saying the true thing when it costs him — he has the opinion and swallows it, in the meeting, in the family gathering, at the dinner with friends, until the silence becomes who he is. A person becomes blind by scrolling past everything that might convict him, by training his attention on what is trivial until the significant is invisible. None of those require a decision to reject God. They only require a long series of small refusals to use the faculties for their purpose.

The contrast the Qur'an draws elsewhere is the one to hold onto. In a narration recorded by al-Bukhari from Abu Musa al-Ash'ari, the Prophet ﷺ said: "The example of the one who remembers his Lord and the one who does not is like the example of the living and the dead." The organs are the same; the difference is whether they are being used for what they were made for. That is why the believer's constant prayer is not for new faculties but for the preservation and right use of the ones he has.

## Verse 19

**"Or ˹those caught in˺ a rainstorm from the sky with darkness, thunder, and lightning. They press their fingers into their ears at the sound of every thunder-clap for fear of death. And Allah encompasses the disbelievers ˹by His might˺."**

In simple terms, the Qur'an offers a second parable: these people are like travellers caught in a violent downpour out of the sky, in darkness, with thunder and lightning — cramming their fingers into their ears at every crash for fear of being struck dead, while Allah has the disbelievers entirely surrounded.

The verse opens with *aw* — "or" — which raises a question the commentators addressed directly: is this a second parable for the same group, or a parable for a different group? The majority held that both parables describe the hypocrites, the first showing them losing their light and the second showing them terrified and paralysed. Some held that the second parable belongs to the disbelievers, largely because the verse closes with "Allah encompasses the *disbelievers*." The two readings are closer than they look, because hypocrisy is a species of disbelief, and the terms overlap. It is also possible to read the "or" as offering an alternative likeness for the same condition. What is certain is that both parables are describing the experience of a person who is in the presence of something from Allah and cannot bear it.

The word for the rain is *sayyib*, a heavy downpour, from a root meaning to pour and flow. A number of early commentators identified the rain with the Qur'an itself — revelation coming down from heaven, carrying both mercy and warning. On that reading, which is widely transmitted, the elements map precisely onto the revelation: the darknesses are the disbelief, doubt and confusion in the heart of the hearer; the thunder is the warnings and threats in the Qur'an — the verses about death, the grave, the Fire and the Reckoning; and the lightning is its clear signs and proofs that momentarily show a person where he is. Other commentators took the picture more literally as a storm, with the elements simply standing for terror and confusion. Both readings are held, and the first is the more detailed.

Note the accuracy of the vocabulary. The Qur'an uses three distinct words for three distinct phenomena: *ra'd* for the sound of thunder, *barq* for the flash of lightning, and *sawa'iq* for the destructive bolts that kill. These are not loose synonyms, and their precision is part of why the parable works.

Then comes the gesture that has made this verse famous: *yaj'aluna asabi'ahum fi adhanihim* — "they put their fingers into their ears." The commentators lingered on the detail that it is *fingertips*, not whole hands or palms. Why? Because a person cramming his whole hand into his ear could not move or flee; the fingertip is what you use when you are desperate but still trying to get somewhere. It is the gesture of someone overwhelmed — not calmly rejecting, but frantically trying not to hear. That detail is psychologically exact. The hypocrites are not portrayed as cool sceptics weighing evidence. They are portrayed as people in a panic, plugging their ears.

And from what? *Mina al-sawa'iq hadhara al-mawt* — from the thunderbolts, in fear of death. Here is the irony the Qur'an builds into the picture: they are terrified of being struck dead by the storm, and entirely untroubled by the thing that actually destroys a human being — dying in a state of rejection. Fear of the creature while ignoring the Creator, terror of physical death while walking towards spiritual ruin. The Qur'an returns to this inversion again and again, because it is one of the primary signs of a disordered heart.

The verse closes: *wa Allahu muhitun bi-al-kafirin* — "and Allah encompasses the disbelievers." *Muhit* comes from the root meaning to surround and enclose completely, and the point is inescapability: there is no route out of the storm, because the One whose signs terrify them is the One who has them enclosed. That is why the attribute is mentioned here rather than the more general statement that He encompasses everything; the context is one of threat and of there being no exit.

One clarification is needed, because this phrase is sometimes misunderstood. "Encompassing" is not spatial. Ahl al-Sunnah are unanimous that Allah is not inside creation or surrounding it in a physical sense; *ihata* here is encompassing in knowledge, power, authority and grasp. Nothing about them is outside His knowledge, and nothing about them is beyond His reach. To read it as spatial containment would be to attribute to Allah the properties of bodies, which the creed rejects.

Now bring the parable into the present, because it is one of the most transferable images in the Qur'an. The first thing to notice is *what kind of response* theirs is. They do not argue with the thunder. They do not produce a counter-theory of meteorology. They cover their ears. That is an accurate picture of how most rejection of religion actually works: not as a refutation but as an avoidance. Very few people reject faith because they lost an argument. They reject it because they stopped listening — they left the room, changed the channel, unfollowed the account, avoided the friend who made them uncomfortable, kept the Qur'an on a high shelf. The plug is not intellectual; it is behavioural.

The second thing to notice is what the storm reveals. The same downpour falls on everyone. For the believer, revelation is rain that revives dead ground — the Qur'an uses that image explicitly elsewhere. For the hypocrite, the very same rain is darkness, noise and terror. The difference is not in the revelation; it is in the receiver. This is why the Qur'an can say in verse 26 of this surah that the same message leaves many astray and guides many, and why it describes itself as "a healing and mercy for the believers" while it "increases the wrongdoers in nothing but loss" (17:82). The same water that grows a garden drowns a man standing in the wrong place.

And the third thing: the terror underneath. Modern rejecters of religion often present as bored, amused or superior. The Qur'an's reading is that underneath the boredom is fright — a person who has heard about death and accountability and has decided that the only available strategy is not to think about it. That is a diagnosis worth taking seriously, because it changes how you speak to such a person. You do not argue him out of a position he has not reasoned his way into. The goal is to get him to take his fingers out of his ears, and that is usually a work of patience and kindness rather than of debate.

## Verse 20

**"It is as if the lightning were about to snatch away their sight. Whenever lightning strikes, they walk in its light, but when darkness covers them, they stand still. Had Allah willed, He could have taken away their hearing and sight. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything."**

In simple terms, the parable continues: the lightning is so blinding that it nearly tears their sight away. Every time it flashes they manage a few steps forward; when the darkness returns they freeze where they are. And the verse then makes clear that this is not a defect in their equipment — if Allah had willed, He could have removed their hearing and sight altogether, and nothing is beyond His power.

*Yakadu al-barqu yakhtafu absarahum* — "the lightning almost snatches away their sight." The verb *yakhtafu* means to snatch or seize suddenly and violently, and the word *yakadu*, "almost," is doing something interesting. Why say it almost happens rather than that it happens? Rhetoricians observed that the near-miss is more frightening than the event: it holds the person in suspense, and it conveys the violence of the contrast between the flash and the surrounding dark. Think of stepping from a pitch-black room into sudden bright light — the pain is not in the light itself but in the contrast.

And there is a paradox built into the image that is the real heart of the parable. Lightning is light. Light is what lets you see. But this light is so violent, against so much darkness, that it threatens to destroy sight altogether. That is exactly the condition of a person who is exposed to the truth and cannot bear it: the very clarity that should guide him is what he experiences as an assault. The truth does not gradually become unpersuasive to such a person; it becomes unbearable.

*Kullama ada'a lahum mashaw fihi* — "whenever it flashes for them, they walk in it." The word *kullama*, "whenever," marks repetition: flash after flash, each time a few steps. The phrase *ada'a lahum* carries a note of benefit — the light illumines *for them*, it serves them — and they walk *fihi*, in it, inside its circle. So there is movement, and there is even progress. But it is borrowed progress, dependent on something outside them.

*Wa idha azlama 'alayhim qamu* — "and when it darkens over them, they stand." The contrast between *mashaw* (they walked) and *qamu* (they stood) is the whole point. Their movement is a pure function of the external flash. When the flash goes, they stop — not because the path has ended, but because they have nothing of their own to walk by.

So the rhythm of their life is: flash, a few steps, dark, freeze; flash, a few steps, dark, freeze. They are always in motion and never arriving. And the reason is stated by the parable itself: they have no light of their own. That is the answer to everything this passage has been describing since verse 8. The hypocrites could move when the community was watching, when the moment was emotional, when a sign was unmistakable — and then stop.

Put this beside the description of the believers on the Day of Resurrection, and the contrast is complete: "Their light will run ahead of them and on their right" (57:12) — a light that belongs to them, that travels with them, that does not switch off. That is what a person builds by living on the means rather than on moods.

Then comes the statement that reframes the whole parable: *wa law sha'a Allahu la-dhahaba bi-sam'ihim wa absarihim* — "and if Allah had willed, He could have taken away their hearing and their sight." This is a crucial sentence, and its direction is the opposite of what readers often assume. It is not a threat of what Allah might do; it is a reminder of what He has not done. Their hearing and their sight are intact. Their organs work. Their problem is not a defect in the equipment — it is what they choose to do with it. The verse is, in other words, a mercy statement disguised as a warning: you are not blind, so you have no excuse.

Theologically, this verse holds two truths in a single breath, and the tradition has always insisted on holding them together rather than choosing. Allah's will is absolute and effective — nothing happens outside it. And human beings are genuinely responsible for what they do with the faculties they have been given. The mainstream reading of Ahl al-Sunnah is that these two are not in competition: Allah created the human being with a real capacity and a real choice, and His will encompasses that choice rather than overriding it. The Mu'tazila, as elsewhere in this passage, read "if Allah had willed" as referring to compulsion, since on their principles Allah cannot will what He disapproves of. The difference belongs to the same family of disagreements already noted at verses 7 and 15.

The verse, and with it the whole opening passage of the surah, closes with *inna Allaha 'ala kulli shay'in qadir* — "Surely Allah is over everything competent." The closing attribute matches the content perfectly: the One who can remove the senses, can seal and can open, can leave astray and can guide, can give respite and can seize. Ending here is not incidental. After twenty verses about guidance, sealing, hypocrisy, mockery and darkness, the reader is left with the recognition that all of it is in the hands of the One who has power over everything — and that the only sane response is humility, vigilance, and the constant asking that the Prophet ﷺ taught: "O Turner of hearts, keep my heart firm upon Your religion."

Now, practically: this parable is the most honest description in the Qur'an of the stop-start religious life, and almost every believer recognises himself in it at some point. A Ramadan in which you prayed properly; a lecture that moved you; a funeral that sobered you; a moment of fear in which you made promises. You walked. And then the darkness came back and you stood still, waiting for the next flash.

The diagnosis the parable gives is not weakness of willpower; it is the absence of an internal light. A person who only moves when he is moved will always oscillate, because flashes are intermittent by nature. The cure is to convert external flashes into a stable internal lamp, and in practice that means exactly what the next verse commands: worship, sustained and ordinary, whether or not you feel anything. The person who prays only when it feels good is living lightning to lightning. The person who prays on the days when it means nothing to him is building a lamp. That is the difference between walking in flashes and having a light that runs ahead of you.

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

Verses 11 to 20 complete the portrait of the hypocrites that began at verse 8, and they do it in four movements. The first movement, verses 11 to 13, shows them in conversation with the community: corrected for spreading corruption, they claim to be the reformers; invited to believe, they call the believers fools. In both cases the Qur'an hands their own word back to them in the same grammatical shape, reversed. The second movement, verse 14, opens the door to the private room and shows the two-language life: profession with the believers, contempt with their own circle. The third movement, verses 15 and 16, gives Allah's response: mockery returned upon themselves, and a trade — guidance exchanged for misguidance — that produced no profit, because there was never any profit in it. The fourth movement, verses 17 to 20, abandons argument for imagery and gives two parables, the extinguished fire and the storm, which together answer the question the whole passage has been circling: what is actually wrong with these people? The answer is that they have no light of their own. The fire they benefited from was struck beside them, not inside them. The lightning they walked by came from outside and switched off. So their movement is real but intermittent, and they never arrive.

Three lessons carry forward into the rest of the surah. First, self-description is not evidence: the reformer, the believer, the sincere person is whoever the fruit shows him to be, and the terrifying possibility the passage holds out to every reader is that of corrupting while sincerely believing you are reforming. Second, no one arrives at this condition by a single decision: it is built out of small refusals — to listen, to speak the true thing, to see what is in front of you — until the refusal becomes the person. And third, respite is not approval: the ease of a life lived in contradiction is rope being lengthened, and its correct use is repentance while repentance is still possible. It is fitting that the passage ends by declaring Allah's power over everything, because the next verse, 21, opens with the address that follows naturally from it: "O humanity! Worship your Lord."
