## Verse 1

Verse 1 says: *Alif-Lãm-Mĩm.*

In simple terms, the verse opens the surah with three Arabic letters — alif, lam, mim — recited exactly as they are written, without a meaning available to the reader. This is the Qur'an's way of opening a chapter with a sign that is itself beyond ordinary explanation, and it is one of the most discussed features of the Qur'an in the whole history of its interpretation.

The letters are known to the classical scholars as the *huruf muqatta'at* — the "disjointed," "detached," or "cut-off" letters. They appear at the opening of twenty-nine surahs of the Qur'an, in various combinations: Alif-Lam-Mim, Alif-Lam-Ra, Ta-Sin-Mim, Ta-Sin, Ya-Sin, Ha-Mim, 'Ayn-Sin-Qaf, Qaf, Sad, Nun, and others. When all the repetitions are removed, the letters used in these openings number exactly fourteen — alif, lam, mim, sad, ra, kaf, ha, ya, 'ayn, ta, sin, ha, qaf, and nun. Their placement is not random: they are always at a surah's beginning and always in a fixed form, and the early community treated them as a serious feature of the revelation rather than a curiosity.

The classical exegetes divide into two broad camps on what these letters mean, and both approaches are worth understanding. The first camp — associated with the earliest generation, including Abu Bakr, 'Umar, 'Uthman, 'Ali, and Ibn Mas'ud as Ibn Kathir reports — holds that the meaning of these letters was deliberately withheld from human knowledge. On this view, Alif-Lam-Mim belongs to the *mutashabihat*: the verses whose precise meaning only Allah knows. The believer's response is the one the Qur'an itself prescribes in 3:7, a few verses ahead in this very surah: "We believe in it; all of it is from our Lord." The second camp — represented by linguists and by exegetes such as al-Tabari, al-Zamakhshari, and ar-Razi, following al-Mubarrad and others — sees a rhetorical and miraculous function. The letters are simply letters of the Arabic alphabet, the same letters the Arabs used every day in poetry, trade, and speech. The Qur'an opens with the alphabet itself and then issues the challenge: this Book is composed of your own letters, so produce a surah like it if you can. The letters are thus an announcement of the challenge of 2:23 and of the inimitability of the Qur'an.

For the modern reader, the important lessons are several. First, humility: no authentic prophetic explanation gives the full secret of these letters, and numerological claims, letter-counting speculations, and confident private interpretations should be treated with caution. Second, attention: the letters are deliberately placed where the reader is most alert — at the very beginning — and they are almost immediately followed by the answer to every question: "Allah! There is no god except Him" (3:2) and "He has revealed to you the Book in truth" (3:3). Third, faith before comprehension: the reader who demands that everything be explained before he believes has made his own mind the measure of revelation; Alif-Lam-Mim teaches the opposite — there are things whose wisdom belongs to Allah, and our response is trust.

The surah itself should also be located. Surat Al 'Imran — "The Family of 'Imran" — is a Madinan surah, revealed after the migration to Madinah. Ibn Kathir notes that it is clearly a Madinan revelation, evident from the fact that the first eighty-three ayat relate to the delegation from Najran that arrived in Madinah in the ninth year of the Hijrah (632 CE). The surah takes its name from the family of 'Imran — the father of Maryam (Mary), whose story occupies the middle of the chapter — and it weaves together three great themes: the Oneness of Allah, the truth of the Qur'an against the disputes of the People of the Book, and the story of the two battles of Badr and Uhud. Everything in the surah is built on the foundation laid in these first verses: Allah is One, His revelation is true, and the proof is in the history of His help.

## Verse 2

Verse 2 says: *Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him —the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining.*

In simple terms, the verse states the foundation of the whole surah and of the whole religion: there is no god worthy of worship except Allah — and He is the Ever-Living, the All-Sustaining. Everything that follows in the chapter — the revelation, the battles, the stories of Mary and Jesus, the disputes with the People of the Book — is the unfolding of this single claim.

The verse is the shahada of the surah's opening: *Allahu la ilaha illa Huwa* — "Allah! There is no god except Him." The Arabic formula works by negation and affirmation — it negates every object of worship and affirms the One. Ibn Kathir explicitly connects this verse to the greatest verse of the Qur'an: he notes that the meaning of "Allah! La ilaha illa Huwa, Al-Hayyul-Qayyum" was already explained in his treatment of Ayat al-Kursi (2:255), and the phrase appears there with the same two divine names. The opening of Al 'Imran is therefore a deliberate echo of the opening of Al-Baqarah's great verse: the same creed, in the same words, with the same names.

The two names at the end are the verse's substance. *Al-Hayy* — the Ever-Living — is the name of the One whose life is essential, without beginning or end, without dependence on anything, and without the weakness, sleep, or death that belongs to every living creature. The Qur'an says of Him (2:255): "Neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him." *Al-Qayyum* — the All-Sustaining — is the One who stands over every thing, who sustains and maintains all that exists, and upon whom everything depends for its existence from moment to moment. The two names are often paired in the Qur'an because they describe the two sides of divine reality: life that has no limit, and governance that has no interruption. Ibn Kathir, in his tafsir of Ayat al-Kursi, explains that *al-Qayyum* is "the One Who sustains and protects all that exists."

The theological point is important for the verses that follow. The surah is going to address the Christians of Najran, who had elevated 'Isa (Jesus) to divinity. Here, in the second verse, the Qur'an establishes the standard against which every claim of divinity is measured: the Creator alone is worthy of worship; the Ever-Living who does not die; the All-Sustaining who needs nothing. A being created in the womb, who ate, slept, and was subject to death, cannot be the Ever-Living — and that argument is made explicit in 3:6 and 3:59 a little later in this surah.

For the reader, the verse is also a practical anchor. The whole surah's battles and disputes, its warnings and its promises, all rest on this single sentence. The believer who keeps 3:2 in view reads every subsequent verse correctly: whatever happens in history — victory or defeat, doubt or dispute — the throne of the universe is held by the One who lives and sustains, and nothing occurs outside His dominion.

## Verse 3

Verse 3 says: *He has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book in truth, confirming what came before it, as He revealed the Torah and the Gospel*

In simple terms, the verse states the Qur'an's origin and its relation to the earlier scriptures: Allah revealed the Book to Muhammad in truth, it confirms what was revealed before it, and it stands in the same line as the Torah and the Gospel.

"He has revealed to you the Book in truth" — the verb *nazzala* (sent down, brought down) is the Qur'an's own word for revelation, and *bi al-haqq* (in truth, with the truth) is the mode of its descent. Ibn Kathir explains: "It is He Who has sent down the Book to you with truth" means revealed the Qur'an to you, O Muhammad, in truth — there is no doubt or suspicion that it is revealed from Allah. Allah revealed the Qur'an with His knowledge, and the angels testify to this fact; Allah is sufficient as a Witness. Al-Jalalayn renders the same point: the Qur'an is "enveloped by the truth, with veracity in what it announces." The phrase rules out every alternative explanation of the Book's source — poetry, sorcery, human invention, or Muhammad's own authorship — and declares that it arrived as truth, with truth, and on the truth.

"confirming what came before it" — the Arabic is *musaddiqan lima bayna yadayhi*, literally "confirming what is between its hands," that is, what preceded it in time. This is the Qur'an's relationship to the earlier revelation. Ibn Kathir explains that the previous divinely revealed Books were sent to the servants and Prophets of Allah; these Books testify to the truth of the Qur'an, and the Qur'an also testifies to the truth these Books contained — including the news and glad tidings of Muhammad's prophethood and the revelation of the Glorious Qur'an. The confirmation is mutual and two-way: the earlier scriptures announced him, and the Qur'an now confirms what was sound in them and corrects what had been distorted and disputed.

"as He revealed the Torah and the Gospel" — the verse names the two great scriptures of the People of the Book: the *Tawrah* (Torah) revealed to Musa, and the *Injil* (Gospel) revealed to 'Isa. Maududi offers an important clarification here. The Torah is generally taken to signify the first five books of the Old Testament, and the Injil the four Gospels of the New Testament, even though those books form only a part of them. Wherever authors and editors of the Bible have added anything of their own — elaboration or elucidation — it has become difficult to distinguish the original from the later human interpolations. Those with insight, however, can distinguish the original revealed fragments from the later additions; it is these scattered fragments of the original revealed Book which the Qur'an terms the Torah and confirms. This is the standard Muslim understanding of the Qur'an's confirmation: it does not confirm every word of the transmitted texts but the original revelation that lay in them and the truths they still contain — the Oneness of Allah, the prophethood of the messengers, and the glad tidings of the Prophet who would come after.

The verse thus establishes the Qur'an's place in a single continuous history of revelation. It is not a new religion invented in the seventh century; it is the latest installment of the one message: One God, prophets, and guidance — the same message of the Torah and the Gospel, now delivered in the language of the Arabs and with the final authority. This is exactly the argument the surah will make to the People of the Book: your own scriptures, which you read, announce this messenger; the Book confirms what you have, so there is no ground for the dispute and the envy.

## Verse 4

Verse 4 says: *previously, as a guide for people, and ˹also˺ revealed the Standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺. Surely those who reject Allah’s revelations will suffer a severe torment. For Allah is Almighty, capable of punishment.*

In simple terms, the verse completes the account of revelation: the Torah and the Gospel came previously as guidance for people; Allah also revealed the Standard — the criterion that separates truth from falsehood; and those who reject the revelations face a severe torment, because Allah is Almighty and capable of punishment.

"previously, as a guide for people" — the phrase *min qablu* (before, previously) attaches to the Torah and the Gospel of the previous verse. Ibn Kathir explains that the Torah and the Gospel were revealed before the Qur'an, as guidance to mankind. They were not merely documents for one tribe or one era; they were guidance for people, and the Qur'an describes them with the same word (*hudan*) it uses for its own guidance. The point is continuity: the earlier books were guidance for their peoples, and the Qur'an is guidance for all.

"and ˹also˺ revealed the Standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺" — the Arabic noun is *al-Furqan*, "the Criterion," "the Standard," the separator between truth and falsehood. Classical exegetes have explained the Furqan in several closely related ways. Ibn Kathir and the sundry notes treat it as the Qur'an itself, the decisive authority that distinguishes truth from falsehood, guidance from misguidance, right from wrong. Others understand it as the clear miracles and proofs of the Prophet that separated the truth he brought from the falsehood of his opponents; and others as the deliverance of Musa (as in the parting of the sea, 2:50, where the same word, *furqan*, describes the salvation of the Children of Israel). All of these readings agree on the essence: the Furqan is the decisive instrument by which Allah separates the true from the false, so that no one with a sound heart remains confused. The verse's point is that revelation did not stop at guidance; it provided judgment — the scale on which competing claims are weighed.

"Surely those who reject Allah's revelations will suffer a severe torment" — the verse turns from the revelation to its rejection. *Kafaru bi ayat Allah* — those who disbelieve in, reject, and cover the signs of Allah — will receive *'adhab shadid*, a severe, unshakeable torment. Ibn Kathir frames this as the warning against those who reject His ayat and defy His honorable Messengers and great Prophets. The wording is important: the torment is not for ignorance of the signs but for rejection of them — for seeing the truth and turning away, which is exactly the condition of those who read the earlier scriptures and then disputed the messenger.

"For Allah is Almighty, capable of punishment" — the verse ends with the pair *al-'Aziz* (the Almighty, the All-Mighty, the One whose will prevails over all) and *dhi intiqam* (the Owner of retribution, the One capable of punishment, who takes vengeance on those who defy Him). Ibn Kathir renders it "All-Able of Retribution." The two names answer a possible doubt: is it certain that the rejection of clear signs will be punished? Yes — because the One who sent the signs is Almighty (nothing can resist His will) and capable of exacting retribution (nothing escapes His grasp). The knowledge of the punishment is no threat to His mercy; it is a function of His justice and power.

Taken together, verses 3–4 give a compact theology of revelation: Allah reveals in truth (3:3), the revelation confirms what came before (3:3), it is guidance for people (3:4), it is a standard of judgment (3:4), rejection of it has a consequence (3:4), and the consequence is guaranteed by the Almighty (3:4). The rest of the surah — the disputes with the People of the Book, the story of the two armies, the questions about Jesus — is the working out of this opening.

## Verse 5

Verse 5 says: *Surely nothing on earth or in the heavens is hidden from Allah.*

In simple terms, the verse declares the absolute scope of divine knowledge: nothing on the earth and nothing in the heavens is hidden from Allah. Every secret, every intention, every unborn form, and every happening is fully known to Him.

The verse is short but comprehensive. *La yakhfa 'ala Allah* — "nothing is hidden from Allah" — is a double negation that produces the strongest positive claim: everything is manifest to Him. The phrase sets the two horizons, *fi al-ard* (on the earth) and *wa la fi al-sama'* (nor in the heavens): the whole of creation, upper and lower, contains no concealment from the Knower. Ibn Kathir states the point plainly: Allah has perfect knowledge of the heavens and the earth, and nothing in them is hidden from His watch. The knowledge described is not an acquisition but an attribute: He does not learn, discover, or compute; He knows without time, without effort, and without limit.

The verse sits deliberately between the revelation (3:1–4) and the shaping of the womb (3:6). It is the hinge: the One who revealed the Book with truth (3:3) is the One from whom nothing is hidden (3:5), and the One from whom nothing is hidden is the One who shapes humanity in the wombs (3:6). The order matters. Revelation is trustworthy because the Revealer knows all; the Qur'an's claims about the unseen — the Day of Judgment, the creation of man, the histories of past peoples — are made by the One who sees all of it. God is not a narrator of reports He has heard; He is a witness of what He describes.

There is also a subtle connection to the next verse. The shaping of the child in the womb — the gender, the features, the length, the character, the joy or misery of the soul — is precisely the kind of thing the ancients counted as the most hidden of all hidden things. A mother does not know what her womb carries; no physician in that era could see it; the secret of the unborn is the perfect image of the hidden. And the Qur'an says: even that is not hidden from Allah. The verse therefore prepares the reader for the claim of 3:6: the Shaper of the womb knows the hidden, and the Knower of the hidden is the One who shapes.

For the reader, the verse has a practical and a moral weight. Practically, it means that nothing in your life is truly private: no intention, no deed in the dark, no sin confessed or unconfessed, no fear, no hope — all are known to the One whose knowledge is complete. Morally, it means that the believer's standard of behavior is not the observer but the Omniscient. The person who lives as though only people can see him lives at the level of appearance; the person who lives knowing that Allah sees him lives at the level of truth. And for the anxious: the One who knows your weakness, your secret need, and the content of your heart is the source of guidance — so the hidden is not a threat to the believer; it is the opening of the prayer of 3:8–9, where the well-grounded in knowledge turn to the One who alone knows the realities and keep the hearts steady.

## Verse 6

Verse 6 says: *He is the One Who shapes you in the wombs of your mothers as He wills. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Almighty, All-Wise.*

In simple terms, the verse points to the most intimate evidence of Allah's power: He is the One who forms each of you in the wombs of your mothers, exactly as He wills — male or female, features, stature, all of it — and therefore He alone is worthy of worship: the Almighty, the All-Wise.

The verse is the direct sequel to the claim of 3:5 that nothing is hidden from Allah. From the statement of perfect knowledge, the Qur'an moves to the demonstration: the womb is the domain of the hidden, and the Shaper of the womb is Allah. *Yusawwirukum* — "He shapes you," "He forms you, gives you your form" — is from the root *s-w-r*, form, image, shape. Al-Jalalayn explains: "He it is Who forms you in the wombs as He will — as males or females, white, black, or otherwise" — and Tanwir al-Miqbas from Ibn 'Abbas gives the same catalogue: "short or tall, pretty or ugly, male or female, felicitous or damned." The shaping is both the biological structure and the very constitution of the person: Ibn Kathir says, "He creates you in the wombs as He wills, whether male or female, handsome or otherwise, happy or miserable."

The classical tafsir makes a crucial theological point in this verse. Ibn Kathir writes that this ayah refers to the fact that 'Isa, son of Mary, is a created servant, just as Allah created the rest of mankind. Allah created 'Isa in the womb of his mother and shaped him as He willed. Therefore, how could 'Isa be divine, as the Christians claim? 'Isa was created in the womb and his creation changed from stage to stage, just as Allah said in 39:6: "He creates you in the wombs of your mothers, creation after creation, in three veils of darkness." The argument from the womb is the argument against the divinity of Jesus: whatever the intensity of its miracle, the birth of Jesus was still a creation in a womb, a shaping by the Shaper — and the Shaper cannot be the shaped. This is precisely the location of the verse in the surah: Al 'Imran is the chapter of the family of 'Imran and the story of Mary and Jesus, and the Qur'an plants the argument before the story even begins.

Maududi draws two practical facts from the verse. The first: no being knows human nature as well as God does; it is thus imperative that man should depend on the guidance revealed by God, something man needs the most. The second: the Being who takes care of all of man's requirements, major and minor, from the time of conception onwards, will not fail to provide guidance for man's conduct in this life. The argument is from the greater to the lesser: the One who shaped you in the womb and has sustained you since conception, who set your provision and your term before you were born, is not going to leave you without instruction on how to live. The One who made the body provides the map for the life.

The closing of the verse is the creed again: *la ilaha illa Huwa* — there is no god except Him — *al-'Aziz al-Hakim* — the Almighty, the All-Wise. Ibn Kathir explains: He is the Creator and thus is the only deity worthy of worship, without partners; His is the perfect might, wisdom, and decision. The two names are the answer to the two possible doubts. *Al-'Aziz* (the Almighty): nothing can prevent Him from shaping as He wills, and nothing can resist His decree. *Al-Hakim* (the All-Wise): no shape is arbitrary — the form, the gender, the circumstance, and the timing all belong to a wisdom the creature cannot fully fathom. The Ruler of the womb is not a blind force; He is the Wise.

The reader's lesson is both credal and personal. Credally: the evidence of Allah's Oneness is written inside every person before birth. Personally: the facts of your own body — your features, your makeup, your circumstances, the family into which you were born — are not accidents but the shaping of the Wise. The believer is invited to look at his own origin: you did not choose your form, your nature, or your timing; the One who chose and shaped them is the only One worthy of being worshipped.

## Verse 7

Verse 7 says: *He is the One Who has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book, of which some verses are precise—they are the foundation of the Book—while others are elusive. Those with deviant hearts follow the elusive verses seeking ˹to spread˺ doubt through their ˹false˺ interpretations—but none grasps their ˹full˺ meaning except Allah. As for those well-grounded in knowledge, they say, “We believe in this ˹Quran˺—it is all from our Lord.” But none will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason.*

In simple terms, the verse describes two kinds of verses in the Qur'an and two ways of reading them. Some verses are precise and clear — they are the foundation of the Book. Others are elusive — they admit more than one meaning. People whose hearts are deviant seize on the elusive verses and use them to spread doubt and support false interpretations. But the full meaning of the elusive verses is known only to Allah. Those who are firmly grounded in knowledge respond differently: "We believe in it — all of it is from our Lord."

"some verses are precise—they are the foundation of the Book" — the *muhkamat* are the verses that are entirely clear, firmly established, precise. The Arabic *muhkam* comes from *ihkam*, firmness, solidity, making something secure. Ibn Kathir explains that the muhkamat are "entirely clear and plain, and these are the foundations of the Book which are plain for everyone." They are *umm al-Kitab* — the mother of the Book — the base to which everything else is referred when clarification is needed. Muhammad bin Ishaq described them as "containing proof of the Lord, immunity for the servants, and a refutation of opponents and of falsehood"; they cannot be changed or altered from what they were meant for. In practical terms the muhkamat include the verses of command and prohibition, of law and of creed — the clear rulings on what is allowed and forbidden, the declarations of Tawhid, the stories of the prophets, the promises and warnings — the verses that any reader can understand as the source of guidance.

"while others are elusive" — the *mutashabihat* are the verses that are not entirely clear, that resemble one another, that carry more than one possible meaning. Ibn Kathir: "not entirely clear for many, or some people" — they have several meanings, some that agree with the muhkam and some that carry other literal indications, although those meanings might not be desired. The mutashabihat include, in Ibn Kathir's enumeration, the abrogated ayat, parables, oaths, and matters that should be believed but are not matters of practice. Muhammad bin Ishaq adds that the unclear ayat "can (but must not) be altered and changed, and this is a test from Allah to the servants, just as He tested them with the allowed and prohibited things."

"Those with deviant hearts follow the elusive verses seeking ˹to spread˺ doubt through their ˹false˺ interpretations" — this is the heart of the verse. *Zaygh* in the heart means deviation, crookedness, a leaning away from the truth. Ibn Kathir: "those who are misguided and deviate from truth to falsehood." Why do the deviant prefer the mutashabihat? Because, he explains, "they are able to alter its meanings to conform with their false interpretation since the wordings of the mutashabihat encompass such a wide area of meanings. As for the muhkam ayat, they cannot be altered because they are clear and, thus, constitute unequivocal proof against the misguided people." They follow the elusive verses *ibtigha' al-fitnah* — seeking discord, sedition, doubt — and *ibtigha' ta'wilih* — seeking its interpretation, in the sense of forcing upon it the meaning that suits their innovation. Ibn Kathir gives the classic example: Christians might claim that 'Isa is divine because the Qur'an calls him a Spirit from Allah (Ruhullah) and His Word given to Mary, while ignoring the muhkam verses that declare him "only a servant" (43:59) and that his likeness before Allah is the likeness of Adam (3:59). The deviant method is the method of the proof-text torn from the context and set against the clear verses.

"but none grasps their ˹full˺ meaning except Allah" — here the classical sources have a famous and well-known difference of reading, and both are legitimate. One reading pauses after "except Allah": none knows the true ta'wil — the full, final reality and interpretation — of the mutashabihat except Allah, and the sentence then resumes with the well-grounded in knowledge as those who say, "We believe in it." This is the position favored by Ibn 'Abbas and Ibn Kathir. Ibn Kathir elaborates using the famous report of Ibn 'Abbas: tafsir is of four types — the tafsir that the Arabs know in their language; the tafsir that no one is excused of being ignorant of; the tafsir that the scholars know; and the tafsir that only Allah knows. The matters of the unseen belong to the fourth: the realities of the Hour, the details of the worlds beyond, the full reality of the divine attributes — these are ta'wil in the deepest sense, and only Allah knows them. The other reading, held by a number of scholars, takes "and those firmly grounded in knowledge" as joined to "except Allah" — meaning that the firmly grounded do know the interpretation of the mutashabihat as far as it is accessible, and it is specifically because they are grounded in knowledge that they say, "We believe in it." The practical conclusion of both readings is the same for the deviant: the verse forbids the forcing of interpretations on verses whose full meaning belongs to Allah, and commands belief in the whole Book.

"As for those well-grounded in knowledge, they say, "We believe in this ˹Quran˺—it is all from our Lord."" — the *rasikhun fi al-'ilm* are those firmly rooted, established, and settled in knowledge. Their response to the presence of the elusive in the Book is not to exploit it but to submit to the whole: *kullun min 'indi Rabbina* — all of it is from our Lord. Ibn Kathir: "all of it is from our Lord — both the muhkam and the mutashabih are true and authentic, and each one of them testifies to the truth of the other. This is because they both are from Allah, and nothing that comes from Allah is ever met by contradiction or discrepancy." The believer does not pit the clear verses against the unclear; the clear verses are the key to the unclear, and both are the Word of the One.

The verse closes with a description of who actually benefits: "none receive admonition except men of understanding" — *ulu al-albab*, the possessors of sound minds. Ibn Kathir: "those who have good minds and sound comprehension, understand, contemplate and comprehend the meaning in the correct manner."

The hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim and others (from 'A'ishah) confirms the warning. Ibn Kathir quotes it: the Messenger of Allah recited this verse and then said: "When you see those who seek the ambiguous verses of it, they are those whom Allah has named. Beware of them!" The verse is thus not merely information about the Book's structure; it is a standing warning against the method of proof-texting, against the use of ambiguity to create doubt, and a standing instruction that the only sound method is to refer the unclear to the clear and to believe the whole.

For the reader, three things follow. First, study the Qur'an in its clear verses: the framework of the religion — Tawhid, prayer, zakat, truthfulness, the prophetic stories, the promises and warnings — is all in the muhkamat. Second, when an obscure verse is quoted to prove a strange doctrine, return it to the muhkamat: no interpretation of a mutashabih verse can stand against a muhkam verse. Third, guard the heart: the difference between the deviant and the grounded is not intelligence but the condition of the heart — the deviant seeks the verse that supports his desire; the grounded says, "We believe; all of it is from our Lord."

## Verse 8

Verse 8 says: *˹They say,˺ “Our Lord! Do not let our hearts deviate after you have guided us. Grant us Your mercy. You are indeed the Giver ˹of all bounties˺.*

In simple terms, the verse gives the prayer of those who are firmly grounded in knowledge: Our Lord, do not let our hearts deviate after You have guided us; grant us mercy from You — for You are the Giver of all bounties.

The verse is the opening of the prayer of the *rasikhun* — the ones who, in the previous verse, said "We believe in it; all of it is from our Lord." Their intellectual submission is immediately converted into a prayer of spiritual dependence. Ibn Kathir explains: "Do not deviate our hearts from the guidance after You allowed them to acquire it. Do not make us like those who have wickedness in their hearts, those who follow the mutashabih in the Qur'an." The prayer is the antidote to the condition described in 3:7. The deviants are distinguished by the state of their hearts; therefore, the well-grounded pray for the state of their hearts to be protected.

The verb *tuzigh* (do not cause to deviate) is the same root as *zaygh* in 3:7 — the crookedness, the leaning away from truth. The prayer uses the very word of the warning: our Lord, do not make our hearts *zaygh*, do not let them veer, after You have guided us. The petition recognizes two truths at once: that guidance is a gift already received ("after You have guided us"), and that the gift's continuation is not automatic — it is sustained by Allah, and therefore it is the object of prayer. Al-Jalalayn: "do not cause them to incline away from the truth in their desire to interpret it... as You caused the hearts of those others to deviate — after You have shown us the way to it."

"Grant us Your mercy. You are indeed the Giver ˹of all bounties˺" — the request for mercy is the request for the *steadfastness itself*. The believer asks for something that is a mercy: the preservation of the heart in the truth. And the verse ends with the name *al-Wahhab* — the Bestower, the Giver without measure, the One who gives freely and repeatedly. Ibn Kathir renders the closing "Truly, You are the Bestower." The logic of the prayer is the logic of the divine name: if You are the One who gives without measure, then give us the mercy that keeps our hearts from deviating.

There is a deep point here about the psychology of the believer. The well-grounded in knowledge — the most learned, the most established — do not say, "We have understood, therefore we are safe." They say, "We have believed, and therefore we need You to keep us." Knowledge does not immunize against deviation; it increases the danger if the heart becomes proud. Every scholar, every student of the religion, every reader of this commentary, is included in the prayer: do not let my hearts deviate after You have guided me. The prayer is also the answer to one of the recurring fears of religious people: the fear of apostasy, of losing the faith, of the heart turning cold. The Qur'an does not answer that fear with self-reliance but with the prayer itself — the turning to the One who holds the hearts.

Maududi, in his treatment of the verses, notes the connection between the prayer and the verse that follows: the one who prays for the heart's steadiness and for mercy is the one whose horizon is the Day of Gathering (3:9). The prayer of the believers is framed by the Hereafter: they ask for the world of the heart's guidance and the world of the final gathering in one breath. The believer lives between two concerns — the state of his soul now and the meeting with his Lord then.

The practical instruction of the verse is the habit of prayer itself. The Qur'an does not merely describe the well-grounded; it teaches their speech as a model to be repeated: "Our Lord! Do not let our hearts deviate after You have guided us. Grant us Your mercy. You are indeed the Giver of all bounties." The reader is invited to make this prayer his own — to pray it in the context of study, when encountering the elusive verses, when hearing doubts, when the heart is tempted by the argument that supports a desire. The safeguard of the faith from the inside is the prayer for the faith.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 9

Verse 9 says: *Our Lord! You will certainly gather all humanity for the ˹promised˺ Day—about which there is no doubt. Surely Allah does not break His promise.”*

In simple terms, the verse completes the prayer of the well-grounded: Our Lord, You will gather all humanity for the Day of which there is no doubt — and Allah never breaks His promise.

The prayer has a structure. Verse 8 asks for the present state of the hearts — guidance sustained, hearts not deviating, mercy granted. Verse 9 moves to the future horizon — the gathering of all humanity on the Day of which there is no doubt. The two are connected: the one who is grounded in knowledge knows that everything he does now, every question he settles at the feet of the Book, every struggle against the deviants, ends at the gathering. Ibn Kathir explains: "Our Lord! Verily, it is You Who will gather mankind together on the Day about which there is no doubt" — the believers confess the resurrection, the assembly, the judgment. The word *jami'* (gatherer, gatherer of all) is an emphasis: not some, not one community, but *al-nas* — all humanity, the first and the last, from Adam to the last child born, all of them assembled.

"about which there is no doubt" — the phrase is the same used in 2:2 for the Book itself: *la rayba fihi* — there is no doubt in it. The Day is not a possibility, a myth, a symbol, or a metaphor; it is a certainty of the same order as the Book's own truth. The Qur'an treats the resurrection as an object of faith in the strict sense: believed not because the eye has seen it but because the One who reports it cannot lie. And the believers' prayer states it as fact: "You will certainly gather."

"Surely Allah does not break His promise" — the sentence is both a declaration and a reason. The prayer's confidence is grounded in the divine character: *la yukhlifu Allah al-mi'ad* — Allah does not fail His promise, He does not break the appointment. Ibn Kathir notes the conviction of the believers in the unbreakable promise. The promise of the gathering is the promise that the scale will be set right: every injustice, every martyrdom, every disputation over the Book, every victory of falsehood and every apparent defeat of truth will be weighed and settled. If Allah does not break His promise, then the Day is sure, and if the Day is sure, then the present life is not the final judgement.

The two verses of the prayer also answer the two great dangers of the readers whom the surah addresses. To the deviant who builds doubts on the ambiguous verses: the well-grounded answer is belief in the whole Book and the prayer for the heart; the debate is not won by dialectic, it is beneath it — everything will be settled at the gathering. To the believer who wonders at the prosperity of the disbelievers and the suffering of the believers (the very question of 3:10 and 3:14): the answer is the Day about which there is no doubt — the delays of justice in this life are not the absence of justice; the gathering is its completion.

The practical lesson: the believer frames his life by the Day. The well-grounded are those whose knowledge of the Book runs into the certainty of the meeting — whose prayer for the heart's stability and whose hope for mercy are both directed at the Day. Every verse of the Qur'an, every deed, every disagreement over the Book, every battle — Badr, Uhud, and the battles of ideas — all of it is history in transit toward the gathering which Allah has promised and which He does not break.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 10

Verse 10 says: *Indeed, neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah—and they will be the fuel for the Fire.*

In simple terms, the verse states a hard fact at the level of final justice: the wealth and the children of the disbelievers will not help them at all against Allah — they will themselves be the fuel of the Fire.

The verse confronts one of the constant puzzles of human experience: the prosperity of those who reject Allah. Wealth and children are the two most obvious measures of worldly success — the two goods that the Qur'an itself lists, in the very next section (3:14), as the things whose love has been made attractive to people. The disbelievers of Madinah, the Quraysh of Mecca, the Jews of the city, the great powers of the world — they had the treasure and the heirs, the armies and the lineage. The Qur'an does not deny the fact; it denies the inference. *Lan tughniya 'anhum* — they will not avail, will not help, will not render them independent and immune. Ibn Kathir emphasizes: "They will not avail them with Allah, or save them from His punishment and severe torment," and he cross-references 9:55 ("let not their wealth nor their children amaze you") and 40:52 (the Day when the excuses of the wrongdoers will profit them nothing).

The doctrine here is not that wealth and children are evil in themselves. The Qur'an calls them the adornment of the worldly life. The claim is narrower and harder: on the Day of account they are not currency. The man who stands before Allah with a fortune and a family of heirs stands there as an individual with his deeds; no one will buy his salvation with a qintar, and no child will transfer a reward or a punishment. Al-Jalalayn: "neither their riches nor their children will avail them against God — that is, against His chastisement." The test of wealth is not the possession but the use, and the disbeliever's wealth in this verse is not a weapon against Allah but the evidence of the charge: the goods were given of His bounty and were spent in defiance.

"and they will be the fuel for the Fire" — the phrase *waqud al-nar* is the wood, the kindling, with which the fire is fed. Ibn Kathir: "they will be the wood with which the Fire is kindled and fed," and he cites 21:98: "Certainly you and that which you worship besides Allah are but fuel for Hell." The image is exact and grim: a fire requires fuel; the denial of Allah is the fuel; the ones who rejected the signs, the messengers, the books, and the revelation will be consumed by the very fire their rejection kindled. Al-Jalalayn notes the reading *waqud* (fuel) as the established one, meaning "what the Fire will be fed by."

The verse also answers the question that haunts the believers in the surah: if Allah's promise is true (3:9), why do the rejecters flourish? The answer begins here: the measure of the flourish is the life; the measure of the fate is the Hereafter. The wealth and children of the disbelievers are real, and they are precisely not the currency of the final transaction. The apparent contradiction between the prosperity of the deniers and the justice of Allah dissolves on the Day, when the two goods appear as what they always were: an enjoyment, and them, the fuel.

For the reader there is a warning and a correction. The warning: do not measure the truth by the prosperity of its opponents. The correction: do not measure your own security by your own abundance. The believer is told in the same surah (3:14) that the whole set of worldly attractions — women, children, heaped-up treasures, horses, cattle, land — is merely the enjoyment of the worldly life, "but with Allah is the finest destination" (3:15). Wealth and children are trusts to be used for the cause of Allah; they are not the proof of Allah's favor, nor a substitute for the faith, nor a shield against the punishment. The fuel of the Fire and the Gardens of the mindful are both decided by one thing: the state of the heart toward Allah, which no amount of wealth or offspring can buy.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 11

Verse 11 says: *Their fate will be like that of the people of Pharaoh and those before them —they all rejected Our signs, so Allah seized them for their sins. And Allah is severe in punishment.*

In simple terms, the verse gives the historical pattern: the fate of those who reject the signs is the fate of the people of Pharaoh and of the nations before them — they rejected the signs, Allah seized them for their sins, and Allah is severe in punishment.

The Arabic word at the opening is *ka-da'b* — "like the custom, the practice, the behavior, the habit." Ibn Kathir collects the classical readings: Ad-Dahhak reported that Ibn 'Abbas said the meaning is "like the behavior of the people of Fir'awn"; the same interpretation came from 'Ikrimah, Mujahid, Abu Malik, and Ad-Dahhak. Other scholars read it as "like the practice, conduct, likeness of the people of Fir'awn." All the readings agree that *da'b* is practice, behavior, tradition, and habit. The verse is therefore not merely a comparison of punishment; it is a comparison of behavior — the disbelievers addressed in this surah behave the way Pharaoh's people behaved, and therefore they will end the way Pharaoh's people ended.

"they all rejected Our signs, so Allah seized them for their sins" — the causal chain is stated with perfect clarity: *kadhdhabu bi ayatina* — they denied, called lies, rejected Our signs — *fa-akhadhahum Allah bi dhunubihim* — so Allah seized them, took them, for their sins. Al-Jalalayn: "They denied Our signs; God seized them — He destroyed them — for their sins; this statement explains the previous one." The sequence is the law of divine dealing with nations: the signs are sent, the signs are clear, the denial is chosen, and the seizure follows. The word "seized" (*akhadha*) is exactly the word of power: a hand that takes, that grasps, that does not miss. And what is seized is for "their sins" — *bi dhunubihim* — not for accidents of history, not for tribal bad luck, but for the sins they committed, the rejection itself being the conclusive sin.

The people of Pharaoh are the standard example because their story is the most complete in the Qur'an: the signs of Musa before them — the staff, the hand, the plagues, the sea — were witnessed by Pharaoh and his court, and they refused. The nations before them — 'Ad, Thamud, and others, as Al-Jalalayn enumerates — rejected their prophets. The point of the verse is that this is not a one-off judgment but a system: "the same end the people of Fir'awn and the previous nations met, those who rejected the Messengers, the ayat, and proofs of Allah that they were sent with" (Ibn Kathir). The disbelievers, says Ibn Kathir, will not benefit from their wealth or offspring (3:10); rather, they will perish and be punished.

"And Allah is severe in punishment" — *shadid al-'iqab* — severe, intense, extreme in punishment. Ibn Kathir: "His punishment is severe and His torment is painful. None can escape Allah's grasp, nor does anything escape His knowledge. Allah does what He wills and prevails over all things; it is He to Whom everything is humbled, and there is no deity worthy of worship, nor any Lord except Him." The name is a balance: the same surah opens with the mercy of the revealer (3:3–4) and the mercy of the Giver (3:8); the severity of punishment stands beside the mercy as the guarantee that the evil is not indifferent — that the One who gives the signs also enforces them.

For the reader, the verse establishes a habit of reading history. The Qur'an does not read the past as a collection of curiosities; it reads the past as a collection of signs — patterns of cause and effect in the moral order of the world. The people of Pharaoh are not merely an ancient story; they are the archetype of the powerful who receive the clear signs and still refuse. Every passage of the surah — the Jews of Madinah who disputed the messenger, the Quraysh who met the Muslims at Badr, the Christians of Najran who argued about Jesus — is being read against this template. The lesson: no amount of ancient precedent (Pharaoh's splendor, his armies, his magicians, his claims to divinity) protected anyone; and no amount of current wealth will protect anyone either (3:10). The wages of the rejection of clear signs are the seizure of the one who rejects them.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 12

Verse 12 says: *˹O Prophet!˺ Tell the disbelievers, “Soon you will be overpowered and driven to Hell—what an evil place to rest!”*

In simple terms, the verse commands the announcement of the coming defeat: tell the disbelievers that soon they will be overcome and then gathered to Hell — and what an evil place to rest!

The verse is the first of the surah's direct prophecies of events that actually happened. *Satughlabun* — "you will be overcome, you will be defeated" — is a statement of the future, and the surah itself records the fulfillment: the battle of Badr (3:13) and later the expulsion of the Jews of Madinah. *Tuhsharun* — "you will be gathered, mustered" — the same gathering of 3:9, now applied to the disbelievers: gathered to Hell. The two clauses give the double fate: the defeat in this world and the gathering to Hell in the Hereafter.

Ibn Kathir gives the historical setting. Muhammad bin Ishaq recorded that 'Asim bin 'Umar bin Qatadah said that when the Messenger of Allah gained victory at the battle of Badr and went back to Madinah, he gathered the Jews in the marketplace of Bani Qaynuqa' and made the call to them — and it was about them that this ayah was revealed. Al-Jalalayn records the occasion: when the Prophet enjoined the Jews to enter Islam after his return from Badr, they said, "Do not fool yourself just because you killed a few men of Quraysh, inexperienced and knowing nothing about fighting," whereupon this was revealed. The verse is thus a reply to the arrogance of a people who had been watching the newly established Muslim community, who had the scriptures describing the coming prophet, and who still counted the Muslims as a temporary novelty. The reply is the prophecy: you will be defeated in this world (through being killed, taken captive, or made to pay the jizya, as actually happened) and gathered to Hell in the Hereafter.

"—what an evil place to rest!" — *bi'sa al-mihad* — "what an evil resting place, what a bad bed, what a miserable cradle." The word *mihad* (bed, resting place, cradle) is chosen with terrible irony. The disbelievers in 3:10 had wealth and children, the goods of this world as their bed of comfort; here they are told that their final bed, the place where they will rest, is Hell — and it is an evil bed. Al-Jalalayn: "an evil cradling! an evil resting place." The same phrase returns at the end of the surah in 3:197 ("what an evil place to rest"), closing the surah's treatment of the prosperity of the disbelievers: the brief enjoyment, then the evil bed.

The verse is also an image of the surah's method. The Qur'an does not merely argue against disbelief; it announces the outcomes of the argument in history. The announcement to the disbelievers — "soon you will be overpowered" — is a prophetic test: if the announcement was true, it was a sign; if it had failed, it would have destroyed the Prophet. It did not fail. Badr came within months, and the Jews of Bani Qaynuqa' were expelled. The reader is being shown that the Qur'an's news is verifiable: the victory promised in the surah to the cause of truth is the same victory that the surah's own narrative of Badr records.

For the believer, the verse is a lesson in the relationship between truth and power. The disbelievers' confidence rested on counts — the numbers of the Quraysh at Badr, the experience of the Jews in war, the wealth (3:10) and the armies (3:11). The verse announces the reversal: the power that counts is the power that decides the outcome, and it is not the count. And the believer draws the comfort: the same Allah who told the Prophet to announce the defeat of the deniers is the One who will accomplish it. The word "soon" is the word of the believer's hope as much as the disbeliever's warning — not long, not far, sooner than the deniers suppose.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 13

Verse 13 says: *Indeed, there was a sign for you in the two armies that met in battle— one fighting for the cause of Allah and the other in denial. The believers saw their enemy twice their number. But Allah supports with His victory whoever He wills. Surely in this is a lesson for people of insight.*

In simple terms, the verse points to the battle of Badr as a sign: two armies met — one fighting for Allah's cause, the other in denial; the believers could see the enemy was twice their number; yet Allah supported with His victory whom He wills — and this is a lesson for people of insight.

The verse is the historical demonstration of the prophecy of 3:12. Ibn Kathir: the two armies are the two camps that met in combat at Badr, one fighting in the cause of Allah — the Muslims — and the other in disbelief — the idolaters of Quraysh at Badr. The occasion is also framed: "There has already been a sign for you, O Jews, who said what you said — you have an ayat, meaning proof, that Allah will make His religion prevail, award victory to His Messenger, make His Word apparent and His religion the highest" (Ibn Kathir). The verse is addressed to the Jews who mocked the Muslims' victory and to anyone who doubts that Allah's religion will prevail; the proof is the battle itself.

"The believers saw their enemy twice their number" — the Arabic *yarawnahum mithlayhim ra'y al-'ayn* — "they saw them twice their number, with the sight of the eye." There are two classical explanations, both reported by Ibn Kathir. The first: the idolaters thought that the Muslims were twice as many as they themselves were, for Allah made this illusion a factor in the victory that Islam had over them. This is the explanation of Ibn 'Abbas as given in Tanwir al-Miqbas: the believers were 313 men, and Abu Sufyan's army was 950 — and the disbelievers saw the believers as twice their own number. Al-Jalalayn gives the numbers likewise: the disbelievers numbering almost a thousand saw the Muslims as twice their number, more numerous than themselves, as the eye sees. The second explanation: the Muslims saw twice as many idolaters as themselves, and still Allah gave them victory. Ibn Mas'ud said: "When we looked at the disbelievers' forces, we found that they were twice as many as we were. When we looked at them again, we thought they did not have one man more than we had" — and he cited 8:44, where Allah says He showed them to the Muslims as few in their own eyes and made the Muslims appear few in the eyes of the enemy.

Both explanations belong to the same divine purpose, and Ibn Kathir states it precisely: "When the two camps saw each other, the Muslims thought that the idolators were twice as many as they were, so that they would trust in Allah and seek His help. The idolators thought that the believers were twice as many as they were, so that they would feel fear, horror, fright, and despair. When the two camps stood in lines and met in battle, Allah made each camp look smaller in the eyes of the other camp, so that they would be encouraged to fight each other — so that Allah might accomplish a matter already ordained (8:42): so that the truth and falsehood are distinguishable, and thus the word of faith prevails over disbelief and deviation, so that the believers prevail and the disbelievers are humiliated." The numbers were managed by Allah, both for the encouragement of the believers and the terror of the enemy. The sign is the visible working of the invisible decree.

"But Allah supports with His victory whoever He wills" — *yu'ayyid bi nasrihi man yasha'* — He strengthens, supports, reinforces with His victory whom He wills. The victory is not the property of the larger army nor of the more experienced soldiers; it is the grant of Allah, and He grants it to whom He wills. The verse thus contains both the doctrine and the election: the power is Allah's, the choice is Allah's, and the believers are the recipients of the grant. The same point will be made at the end of the surah (3:160): "If Allah supports you, none can defeat you."

"Surely in this is a lesson for people of insight" — *la 'ibrah li uli al-absar* — a lesson, a clear instruction, for the possessors of vision, the people of insight, the ones who see through the event to the sign. The eye saw the numbers; the insight sees the decree. Ibn Kathir: "This should be an example for those who have intelligence and sound comprehension. They should contemplate about Allah's wisdom, decision..." The difference between the observer and the person of insight is precisely the reading of the battle: the observer saw a coincidence, a surprise, a lucky day; the person of insight saw the assistance of Allah and the wisdom of the management.

For the reader, Badr is the surah's central historical sign, and the verse is its summary. The lessons: (1) the cause of Allah is not measured by numbers — the believers were a third of the enemy's size and still won; (2) the victory belongs to Allah — the believers who attributed the result to their own strength were corrected later at Uhud (3:146–152), where the defeat taught the same lesson in reverse; (3) the sign is for the people of insight — the same event that hardened the deniers in doubt is the evidence of faith for those who see; (4) the wisdom is in the management — Allah arranged even the appearance of the armies so that faith would be tested, hearts purified, and the truth distinguished from falsehood. The reader who faces his own "Badr" — his own confrontation of a cause that is outnumbered, outfunded, and outmatched — is told: the support is from Allah, and the sign is in the outcome.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 14

Verse 14 says: *The enjoyment of ˹worldly˺ desires—women, children, treasures of gold and silver, fine horses, cattle, and fertile land—has been made appealing to people. These are the pleasures of this worldly life, but with Allah is the finest destination.*

In simple terms, the verse names the attractions of the world that are made appealing to people — women, children, hoards of gold and silver, fine horses, cattle, and fertile land — and then sets them against the better: these are the pleasures of the worldly life, but with Allah is the finest destination.

The verse opens with *zuyyina li al-nas* — "made attractive, beautified, adorned for people." The passive form is deliberate: the appeal is not entirely the people's own invention; it has been placed in the attractions themselves. Ibn Kathir explains: "Allah mentions the delights that He put in this life for people, such as women and children, and He started with women, because the test with them is more tempting." The list is not random; it is the catalogue of what the human heart, across all times, has been drawn to, and it is arranged deliberately: women, then children, then heaped-up treasures of gold and silver, then fine branded horses, then cattle, then tilled land. It is the whole grammar of worldly ambition — family, wealth, livestock, property — the things that men fight for, build dynasties over, and measure success by.

Ibn Kathir gives the classical treatment of each item, showing that the Qur'an's list is not a condemnation of the things but a diagnosis of the attachment. The desire for women and children is not evil in itself: "When one enjoys women for the purpose of having children and preserving his chastity, then he is encouraged to do so." He records the hadiths of the encouragement of marriage — "Marry the loving, fertile woman, for I will compare your numbers to the rest of the nations on the Day of Resurrection"; "This life is a delight, and the best of its delight is a righteous wife"; "I was made to like women and perfume, and the comfort of my eye is the prayer" — and the saying of 'A'ishah about the Prophet's love of women and horses. But: "The desire to have children is sometimes for the purpose of pride and boasting, and as such, is a temptation. When the purpose for having children is to reproduce and increase the Ummah of Muhammad with those who worship Allah alone without partners, then it is encouraged and praised." Likewise wealth: "The desire of wealth sometimes results out of arrogance, and the desire to dominate the weak and control the poor, and this conduct is prohibited. Sometimes, the want for more money is for the purpose of spending it on acts of worship, being kind to the family, the relatives, and spending on various acts of righteousness and obedience; this behavior is praised and encouraged." The famous hadith of the Prophet — "I did not leave behind me a test more tempting to men than women" — is cited as the reason women are mentioned first. The interest is in the heart's relation to the attraction, not in the attraction itself.

There is also a fine point in the word *qanatir al-muqantarah* — "heaped-up," "piled-up" treasures, the word *qintar* being a large unit of wealth. Ibn Kathir reports that the scholars differ on the amount of a qintar, all agreeing that it is a large amount; Abu Hurayrah said the qintar is twelve thousand uqiyah, each uqiyah better than all between the heavens and the earth (recorded by Ibn Jarir). The "heaped-up" repetition conveys the excess: not a coin or two, not a modest savings, but piles — the object of greed.

"These are the pleasures of this worldly life" — *mata' al-hayat al-dunya* — the enjoyment, the provision, the temporary goods of the present life. The word *mata'* is used in the Qur'an for provisions and enjoyments that are consumed and pass.

"but with Allah is the finest destination" — *wa Allah 'indahu husn al-ma'ab* — and with Allah is the beautiful return, the good final abode. The comparison is the point of the verse. The world's attractions are real and attractive; the verse does not pretend otherwise. The claim is that they are not the best, and the best is with Allah. The next verse (3:15) will name the better: Gardens with rivers, eternal life, purified spouses, and the pleasure of Allah. The structure is the Qur'an's characteristic method: it does not shame desire; it redirects it. The same appetite that runs after the world is not annihilated but reoriented toward the higher good, so that the love of woman, children, wealth, horses, cattle, and land becomes the love of the Garden, the eternal, the purified, and the pleasure of the Lord.

For the reader, the verse is one of the most practical passages in the surah. It diagnoses the two errors of the human relationship with the world: the error of asceticism that denies the goods are good (the verse begins by affirming they have been made appealing — the attraction is real and the goods are gifts), and the error of idolatry that treats them as the point of life (the verse ends by locating the finest destination elsewhere). The believer is invited to hold the goods as goods and not as God — to enjoy the wife and the child, to earn and spend, to ride and cultivate, and to do it all with the destination in view: the finest return is with Allah, and the proper use of the world's attractions is to travel toward it.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 15

Verse 15 says: *Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Shall I inform you of what is better than ˹all of˺ this? Those mindful ˹of Allah˺ will have Gardens with their Lord under which rivers flow, to stay there forever, and pure spouses, along with Allah’s pleasure.” And Allah is All-Seeing of ˹His˺ servants,*

In simple terms, the verse proposes a comparison: shall I tell you of something better than all of this? For the mindful — the people of taqwa — there are Gardens with their Lord, rivers flowing beneath them, eternity, purified spouses, and — above all — Allah's pleasure. And Allah is All-Seeing of His servants.

The verse is the direct answer to 3:14. The catalogue of the world — women, children, hoards, horses, cattle, land — is met with a question: *qul a'unabbi'ukum bi khayrin min dhalikum* — "Say: shall I inform you of something better than that?" The interrogative, Al-Jalalayn notes, is affirmative in intent: it is a statement dressed as a question, and the answer is yes. Ibn Kathir frames the whole passage as "The True Value of This Earthly Life": after listing the delights of this life, Allah presents the better of the two, and the better is the reward of the mindful.

"For those mindful ˹of Allah˺" — the reward belongs to the *muttaqin*, the people of taqwa — the same word used in 2:2 for the audience of the Book, and the same word at the end of this surah (3:186, 200). Taqwa is the guarding of the self against what displeases Allah, the God-conscious awareness that orders the heart.

"Gardens with their Lord under which rivers flow, to stay there forever" — the description is the Qur'an's recurring image of Paradise: *jannat tajri min tahtiha al-anhar* — Gardens beneath which rivers flow. The qualifier "with their Lord" (*'inda rabbihim*) is the Qur'an's way of placing the reward in the divine presence itself, not merely in a location. And the eternity follows: *khalidin fiha* — abiding forever. The contrast with the world of 3:14: the goods there are passing, the enjoyment is brief; the reward here is eternal and unending. The greater destroys the lesser — an eternity in the divine presence against a lifetime's possessions.

"and pure spouses" — *azwaj mutahharah* — purified spouses, purified mates. Al-Jalalayn: "purified of menstruation and other impurities." Ibn Kathir and the classical notes: the spouses of Paradise are free of the impurity, the defect, the friction that belongs to the spouses of this world. It is the completion of the world's best attraction: the verse of 3:14 listed the love of women at the head; the reward names the purified spouses at the heart. The same desire is not abolished; it is perfected.

"along with Allah's pleasure" — *wa ridwan min Allah* — and the pleasure, the good pleasure, of Allah. This is the crown of the reward, the item that the classical commentators single out as the greatest of the promised gifts. The phrase is the Qur'an's fullest statement of the goal: the Garden, the rivers, the eternity, the purified spouses — and above all of it, the pleasure of the Lord. Ibn Kathir and the commentators note this as the highest — the believer's aim is not merely escaping punishment or receiving gifts but drawing near and finding the Lord pleased.

"And Allah is All-Seeing of ˹His˺ servants" — *basir bi al-'ibad* — He sees the servants well, He is the Seer of the servants. The verse ends by connecting the promise to the evidence: the reward is for the mindful, and the One who judges who is mindful is the One who sees. Al-Jalalayn: "God is Seer, Knower of His servants, requiting each of them according to his deeds." The point is that the promise is not a reward for appearance. The mindfulness that earns it is known by the One who sees what is hidden.

There is a deep structure to the pair 3:14–15. The same components — women, children, wealth, land — are exactly the components of the reward in the language of the Qur'an's own imagery of Paradise. The believer who is tested by the beauty of the world and the desire for the family and the wealth is not asked to annihilate the desire; he is asked to receive its fulfillment from the right hand. The world gives the goods briefly; the Lord gives them purified, with eternity, with the presence, with His pleasure. The "better than that" is not a different good; it is the same goods, healed of their passing, their impurity, and their separation from Allah, and crowned by His pleasure. This is what makes the verse a reorientation rather than a rejection: the test of the world is whether the heart will take the promise and wait, or take the appearance and consume it now.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 16

Verse 16 says: *who pray, “Our Lord! We have believed, so forgive our sins and protect us from the torment of the Fire.”*

In simple terms, the verse describes the mindful people who are promised the reward: they are the ones who pray — Our Lord, we have believed, so forgive us our sins and protect us from the punishment of the Fire.

The verse is the first half of a description of the *muttaqin* that the previous verse promised. It begins with a pronoun connecting it to the reward: *alladhina yaquluna* — "those who say." Ibn Kathir explains: "Those who say: 'Our Lord! We have indeed believed' — in You, Your Book, and Your Messenger." The belief is stated in the prayer, in the address, in the confession. The reward of 3:15 belongs to people who are defined by their speech to Allah: "We have believed."

The prayer has two requests, and both are the requests of people who know what they are. The first: *fa-ighfir lana dhunubana* — "so forgive us our sins." Ibn Kathir: "because of our faith in You and in what You legislated for us. Therefore, forgive us our errors and shortcomings, with Your bounty and mercy." The logic is subtle. The believers do not say "we have believed, so we have no sins." They say "we have believed, so forgive us our sins." The faith is the ground of the request, not the substitute for it. The believer knows that his belief does not make him sinless; it makes him a candidate for forgiveness, the one who turns to the Forgiver.

The second: *wa qina 'adhab al-nar* — "and protect us from the punishment of the Fire." The request is not for the reward first; it is for protection first. The believer prays for distance from the Fire before he prays for the Garden — the order reflects the psychology of the faithful: the fear of the punishment is not the whole of faith, but it is the beginning of the security, and the one who is saved from the Fire is the one who is on the way to the Garden.

The verse is a model of the believer's prayer. It contains the confession ("we have believed"), the acknowledgment of need ("forgive our sins"), the request for protection ("protect us from the Fire"), and the address ("Our Lord"). There is no bargaining, no boasting, no negotiation — only the turning of the servant to the Lord. Al-Jalalayn and the classical notes align: the description of the mindful is their prayer.

For the reader, three things follow. First, the reward of 3:15 is not for a default population but for a praying population — the people of taqwa are those who say this prayer. Second, the prayer is the cure for the two central anxieties: the weight of the past ("forgive us our sins") and the fear of the future ("protect us from the Fire"). Third, the faith is the thing that makes the requests receivable: "We have believed" comes before "forgive us," ordering the prayer not as a transaction of good deeds but as the response of belief to the mercy.

The verse also stands in the surah's argument against the people who claimed the faith without the practice. The Christians of Najran and the Jews of Madinah claimed special standing with Allah. The Qur'an's answer throughout the surah is that the claim is not the currency; the prayer of the heart, the confession of belief, the requests for forgiveness and protection, and the marks of 3:17 are the evidence. The mindful are not defined by descent, tradition, or inheritance; they are defined by the words and the deeds of the ones who believe.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 17

Verse 17 says: *˹It is they˺ who are patient, sincere, obedient, and charitable, and who pray for forgiveness before dawn.*

In simple terms, the verse completes the portrait of the mindful: they are the patient, the truthful, the obedient, the charitable, and the ones who seek forgiveness before the dawn.

The verse is a list of five qualities, and the classical exegetes treat each one with precision. Ibn Kathir: "Allah describes the Muttaqin, His pious servants, whom He promised tremendous rewards." The five marks are the anatomy of taqwa — the inner life expressed in the outer character.

"patient" — *al-sabirin* — the patient, the steadfast. Ibn Kathir: those who are patient "while performing acts of obedience and abandoning the prohibitions." Steadfastness is not merely endurance under hardship but endurance in the two directions — the patience of doing the command and the patience of leaving the forbidden. Maududi: "they are those who remain steadfast in the cause of Truth; who do not lose heart when they either suffer losses or are subjected to afflictions; who do not despair when they encounter reverses; who are not seduced by temptations." The first mark is the mark of the tested people — the surah is addressed to a community that will soon face Uhud, and patience is the name of the quality that will be tested there.

"sincere" — *al-sadiqin* — the truthful, the sincere. Ibn Kathir: "those who are true concerning their proclamation of faith, by performing the difficult deeds." The sincerity is not in the tongue alone; it is in the match between the confession and the conduct. The truthful are the ones whose "We have believed" of 3:16 is followed by the deeds that testify.

"obedient" — *al-qanitin* — the devoutly obedient, the ones who stand in submission. Ibn Kathir: "they submit and obey Allah." Al-Jalalayn: "obedient, compliant before God." The *qanit* is the one whose obedience is not intermittent or reluctant but a settled posture of the soul — the standing of the servant before the Lord.

"and charitable" — *al-munfiqin* — those who spend, the givers. Ibn Kathir: "those who spend from their wealth on all the acts of obedience they were commanded, being kind to kith and kin, helping the needy, and comforting the destitute." The giving is not restricted to the formal charity; it is the whole disposition of the open hand — the obedience of wealth, the kindness of kin, the help of the needy, the comfort of the destitute.

"and who pray for forgiveness before dawn" — *al-mustaghfirin bi al-as-har* — the ones who seek forgiveness in the last hours of the night, at the *as-har*, the times before dawn. Ibn Kathir: "and this testifies to the virtue of seeking Allah's forgiveness in the latter part of the night." He records the tradition: when Ya'qub said to his children, "I will ask my Lord for forgiveness for you" (12:98), he waited until the latter part of the night to make his supplication. And he records the famous hadith recorded in the two Sahihs, the Musnad, and the Sunan through several Companions: "Every night, when the last third of it remains, our Lord, the Blessed, the Superior, descends to the lowest heaven, saying: 'Is there anyone to ask Me, so that I may grant him his request? Is there anyone to invoke Me, so that I may respond to his invocation? Is there anyone seeking My forgiveness, so that I may forgive him?'" The same sources recorded that 'A'ishah said the Prophet performed Witr in the first, middle, and latter parts of the night, and later in his life he would perform it only in the latter part. Ibn 'Umar used to pray during the night and would ask his servant: "Is it the latter part of the night yet?" — and when the answer was yes, he would begin supplicating and seeking forgiveness until dawn. Al-Jalalayn explains the choice of the time: "the last part of the night, singled out here for mention because it is the time of unawareness and of the joy of sleep."

The verse's five marks form a complete religion in outline: patience in the self, truth in the heart, obedience to the Lord, generosity with the wealth, and the night prayer of forgiveness. The first four are the daytime life of the believer; the fifth is the night. The reward of 3:15 — the Gardens, the purified spouses, the pleasure of Allah — is promised to the people of these five marks. The verse is therefore not a commentary on the reward but the specification of it: the reward belongs to the patient, the truthful, the obedient, the charitable, and the seekers of forgiveness before dawn.

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's portrait of the believer in action. The prayer of 3:16 ("we have believed; forgive us; protect us") is answered in the conduct of 3:17. The faith that prays is the faith that is patient, truthful, obedient, giving, and praying in the night. And the closing note — the last hours of the night — is the Qur'an's most intimate invitation: the world sleeps, the darkness is deepest, and the servant who wakes to seek forgiveness at that hour has found the hour when Allah Himself calls out for the askers.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 18

Verse 18 says: *Allah ˹Himself˺ is a Witness that there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—and so are the angels and people of knowledge. He is the Maintainer of justice. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Almighty, All-Wise.*

In simple terms, the verse declares the testimony of the Oneness from the three witnesses: Allah Himself, the angels, and the people of knowledge — the One who maintains justice — there is no god except Him, the Almighty, the All-Wise.

The verse opens with the strongest possible confirmation of the creed. *Shahida Allah* — "Allah bears witness." Al-Jalalayn: "God bears witness — that is, He has made it clear to His creation through proofs and signs — that there is no god in existence except Him." The testimony of Allah is not merely a statement; it is the whole apparatus of evidence — the creation, the revelation, the signs in the world and in the self, the scriptures, the messengers — the proofs that testify to the Oneness. The divine testimony is the ground of the human testimony.

"and so are the angels and people of knowledge" — the testimony of the angels and the testimony of the learned join the testimony of Allah. Ibn Kathir: "Allah then mentioned the testimony of His angels and those who have knowledge after He mentioned His own testimony." The angels — the inhabitants and administrators of the unseen — testify by their affirmation, their worship, their obedience; the people of knowledge — the scholars, the prophets, the believers — testify by their conviction and their words. Al-Jalalayn: the testimony of those of knowledge is "from among the prophets and the believers, through their conviction and in words." Ibn Kathir makes a notable point: "This ayah emphasizes the great virtue of those who have knowledge" — the learned are raised to the rank of witnesses alongside the angels, because the knowledge of Allah is the thing that makes a person a bearer of the testimony. The people of knowledge are the human link in the witness: the ones who read the evidence, understand it, and declare it.

"He is the Maintainer of justice" — *qa'iman bi al-qist* — standing, upholding, maintaining justice, equity, the right measure. Ibn Kathir: "(He always) maintains His creation in justice, in all that He does." The phrase places the justice of Allah as the standing posture of His governance: He does not sometimes maintain justice; He stands in it. The testimony of the Oneness is tied to the justice: the One who is worshipped alone is the One who deals justly, and the worship of Him alone is the restoration of justice in the affairs of creation.

"There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Almighty, All-Wise" — the creed is repeated at the close of the verse, and the two names return from the opening of the surah (3:2, 3:6): *al-'Aziz* — the Almighty, the One whose will prevails — *al-Hakim* — the All-Wise, the One whose acts are ordered by wisdom. The verse is thus framed by the shahada: it opens with the witness and closes with the creed, the names that support it.

The verse is also polemically loaded in the surah's context. The People of the Book — the Jews and the Christians — are the audience. The Christians had veered toward the elevation of Jesus; the Jews had their own conceptions of the elect. The Qur'an's answer is not a long argument but the testimony: Allah Himself, the angels, and the people of knowledge bear witness that there is no god except Him. The next verse (3:19) makes the explicit conclusion: the religion with Allah is Islam — and those who were given the Scripture did not differ except out of envy after knowledge came to them.

For the reader, the verse is an argument for the dignity of knowledge and a standing instruction. The dignity: the people of knowledge are named by Allah in the same sentence as the angels, bearing the same witness. The instruction: to learn the religion is to enter the rank of the witnesses — the believer who understands the Oneness and declares it is not merely an informed person but a witness with the angels. And the content of the testimony is not a private preference but the structure of reality: the One, the Justice, the Almighty, the All-Wise — and the religion of the One is the religion of justice.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 19

Verse 19 says: *Certainly, Allah’s only Way is Islam. Those who were given the Scripture did not dispute ˹among themselves˺ out of mutual envy until knowledge came to them. Whoever denies Allah’s signs, then surely Allah is swift in reckoning.*

In simple terms, the verse states the conclusion of the testimony: the only religion accepted with Allah is Islam — the submission to Him. The People of the Scripture only split and disputed after knowledge had come to them, and they did it out of mutual envy. And whoever rejects Allah's signs — Allah is swift in reckoning.

"Certainly, Allah's only Way is Islam" — *inna al-din 'inda Allah al-Islam* — the religion with Allah, the only religion in His sight, is Islam. The word *din* carries both "religion" and "way, judgment, obedience"; *al-Islam* is "the submission, the surrender, the peace of the surrendered one." Ibn Kathir: "Allah said, asserting that the only religion accepted with Him is Islam," and he cross-references 3:85: "And whoever seeks a religion other than Islam, it will never be accepted of him." Ibn 'Abbas (Tanwir): "Religion — that which is accepted — with Allah is the Surrender (to His will and guidance)." The verse does not name a tribe, a lineage, or a new historical community; it names the submission itself. The religion of every prophet was Islam in this sense — the surrender of the whole self to the One — and the Qur'an says so repeatedly (e.g., 2:132, Abraham's testament; 2:130; 3:52, the disciples' confession: "we have submitted").

"Those who were given the Scripture did not dispute ˹among themselves˺ out of mutual envy until knowledge came to them" — the verse turns to the People of the Book with a precise historical diagnosis. *Ma ikhtalafa alladhina utu al-Kitab* — those given the Scripture did not differ — *illa min ba'di ma ja'ahum al-'ilm* — except after knowledge came to them — *baghyan baynahum* — out of mutual envy, transgression, wrong-doing among themselves. The order is the point: the dispute did not precede the knowledge; it followed it. Ibn Kathir: "some of them wronged others." The division between the Jews and the Christians, the sects within each, the disputes over the messenger — none of it arose from ignorance of the truth. It arose after the truth reached them, and its engine was *baghy* — the envy, the rivalry, the desire to outrank and to hold, the refusal to submit to the truth that was known. This is one of the Qur'an's most important diagnoses of religious division: the primary cause is not the absence of evidence but the presence of envy.

"Whoever denies Allah's signs, then surely Allah is swift in reckoning" — *fa inna Allah sari' al-hisab* — Allah is swift in reckoning, quick in the account. The denial of the signs after knowledge is the precise charge: the signs are clear, the knowledge has come, and the denial is chosen. The swiftness of the reckoning is the answer to the apparent success of the deniers: the account is not delayed indefinitely; it is quick — not in the sense of haste but in the certainty of the settlement when the account is opened. Ibn Kathir: "their reckoning is with Allah and their return and final destination is to Him."

The verse is the surah's central declaration about the religion. For the People of the Book, the declaration is a call: the religion of Musa and 'Isa was the submission, and the submission is the religion of the messenger who has now come — so the dispute is not about the truth but about the envy, and the envy will not protect anyone from the reckoning. For the reader, the verse is the standard of religious truth: not the tradition of the ancestors, not the name of the community, not the comfort of the familiar — but the submission to Allah, the acknowledgment of the One, the acceptance of what the messengers brought, and the embrace of the truth wherever it is found. And the verse is the answer to the debate that runs through the whole surah: Islam is not a new invention but the original religion; the divisions came later, and they came from envy.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 20

Verse 20 says: *So if they argue with you ˹O Prophet˺, say, “I have submitted myself to Allah, and so have my followers.” And ask those who were given the Scripture and the illiterate ˹people˺, “Have you submitted yourselves ˹to Allah˺?” If they submit, they will be ˹rightly˺ guided. But if they turn away, then your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. And Allah is All-Seeing of ˹His˺ servants.*

In simple terms, the verse gives the Prophet's instruction for the end of the argument: if they dispute with you, say — I have submitted myself to Allah, and so have my followers. Then ask the People of the Scripture and the unlettered people: have you submitted? If they submit, they are guided; if they turn away, your duty is only to convey the message — and Allah sees the servants.

The verse is the conclusion of the opening section of the surah. Three things have been established: the Oneness (3:2, 18), the truth of the revelation (3:3–4), and the religion of submission (3:19). Now the instruction for the disputation: *fa in hajjuka* — "so if they dispute with you, argue with you, advance their proofs against you" — *fa qul aslamtu wajhi li Allah* — "say: I have submitted my face, my whole self, my direction to Allah." Ibn Kathir: "So if they dispute with you say: 'I have submitted myself to Allah, and (so have) those who follow me.'" The word *wajh* — face — is the Qur'an's word for the whole orientation of the person: the face turned toward Allah. The submission is the answer to the argument, and it is not a counter-argument but a declaration: this is my position, my person, my whole being — turned to Allah.

"and so have my followers" — *wa mani ittaba'an* — and those who follow me. The submission is not a solitary declaration; it is the declaration of the Prophet and the community. The claim of the Muslims is not that they have won the argument but that they are the ones who have submitted — and the next sentence turns the question to the opponents.

"And ask those who were given the Scripture and the illiterate ˹people˺, "Have you submitted yourselves ˹to Allah˺?"" — the verse addresses the two audiences of the call: *alladhina utu al-Kitab* (those given the Scripture — the Jews and the Christians, as Ibn Kathir glosses) and *al-ummiyyin* — the illiterate, the unlettered — which in this context is the Arab pagans of Mecca and the surrounding region, who had no revealed scripture before the Qur'an. Ibn Kathir: "say to those given the Scripture (Jews and Christians) and to those who are illiterates (Arab pagans): 'Do you (also) submit yourselves?'" The command reflects the universality of the call: the question is put to the people of the Book and to the people of no Book alike, because the religion is not the property of a lineage but the submission that every human is invited to make.

"If they submit, they will be ˹rightly˺ guided" — *fa in aslamu fa qadi ihtadaw* — if they submit, they have already been guided, they have attained the guidance. The submission is the guidance: the one who surrenders to Allah has found the way, and there is no further condition. Ibn Kathir: "if they do, they are rightly guided."

"But if they turn away, then your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺" — *fa in tawallaw fa innama 'alayka al-balagh* — but if they turn away, then what is upon you is only the delivery, the conveying. This is one of the foundational statements of the Prophet's role in the Qur'an: the duty is not to compel, not to coerce, not to convert by force, and not to answer for the outcome — the duty is the delivery. Ibn Kathir: "their reckoning is with Allah and their return and final destination is to Him." The message has been delivered; the response belongs to the one who receives it; the account belongs to Allah. The same principle is repeated throughout the Qur'an (e.g., 16:82, 42:48) and is the basis of the religion's teaching on the freedom of conscience.

"And Allah is All-Seeing of ˹His˺ servants" — *basir bi al-'ibad* — the verse closes with the same name that closed 3:15: the All-Seeing of the servants. The servants who submit and the servants who turn away are both seen by the One who will reckon. The seeing is the ground of the justice: nothing is missed, no submission unrecorded, no turning away unobserved.

For the reader, the verse is a complete model of the believer's relation to the disputer. The believer declares the submission first — the person, the community, the followers — rather than waiting to win the argument. The believer then asks the question — have you submitted? — making the claim a question, not a verdict, and inviting the opponent into the same surrender. And the believer then leaves the outcome: if they submit, guidance; if they turn away, delivery — and the account with Allah. The verse is the end of polemic and the beginning of the open call: the argument is closed by the submission, the call is made to all, and the rest is between the servant and the All-Seeing.
